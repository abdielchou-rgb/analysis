# Venture Vision: IPOs #3 - First Day Pops

1st day returns have become emblematic of "successful" IPOs. Yet, over a 3 year view, such pops tends to be uncorrelated with a company's success in listed markets. That said, it is a more useful tool than most in providing the catalyst for a snowball effect in restarting IPO markets after a trough.

First day returns are a leading indicators of IPO volumes: Looking at our IPO data back to the 1990s, using lagged 1st day returns shows a positive relationship between previous 1st day returns and subsequent IPO listings/proceeds both 6 months and 12 months thereafter. We note, however, this relationship has become somewhat more volatile since the 2008 IPO regime shift we have discussed in prior research. The change in the correlation below appears to be attributable to faster listings subsequent to changes in first day return performances. The market appears to be becoming more reactive (i.e. the time lag between rising first day pops and a rise in volume of activity is compressing).

Back to First Day return...: There have been two notable changes in first day returns over time: (1) Standard deviations of annual returns have decreased over time; the 1970s was the last in which average first day returns were negative on new issuances. Since then the trend has been for progressively higher but more volatile first day returns. (2) Despite the majority of IPOs performing well on day 1, the majority of IPOs also perform poorly on a 3 year view (>54%). This is an equal weighted conclusion, rather than a market cap weighted comment. By this we mean that the larger, more established companies with better fundamentals tend to have a lower one day pop but a better likelihood of positive 3 year returns than smaller listings where one day pops are more frequent/larger but tend to deliver negative shareholder returns over a 3 year period.

For long term IPO investing, "bubble" level 1st day returns have a high negative correlation to long-term returns. Not surprisingly, euphoric 1st day returns tend to come at significantly elevated multiples and should be a warning sign to investors looking to buy and hold. At 1st day close, peak level median multiples reached as high as \~45x sales (2020), according to data from Warrington University. In a public market context though, this number should be taken with a grain of salt, as a significant driver of this effect seems to be an outsized number of pre-revenue companies coming to market during peak IPO markets.

Profitable vs. unprofitable companies: Years with increased proportions of unprofitable companies also tend to have the best "trading IPOs", i.e. highest 1st day returns. This phenomena is also typically associated with peak market conditions. In this vein, the current cycle (and its recovery) looks more like dot-com than the GFC.

This type of assumed first day success could act as a psychological hindrance to listing for certain founders, waiting for more supportive markets. A high single-digit first day return would have been considered the norm - or even a "successful" float - in the 1980s. Mid teens returns/first-day returns in the 1990s or mid 2000s were signs of successful listings.

The problem for the current recovery in markets is the recency bias in the expectation that >30% average first day returns are the norm, rather than the exception. If we were to follow the dot-com phasing of recovery, founders/CEOs would have to settle for first day returns half the level seen over the past 5 years. Moreover, we would need to see an >3x increase in the number of companies being profitable before listing if the dot-com recovery were the playbook for 2024/25. Has the pipeline of c1200 unicorns/prospective IPO candidates become that "fit" on average over the past 3 years?

Given the above data and analysis, there is ample evidence to state that the strongest relationship in our dataset is between unprofitable companies coming to market and first day pops (albeit correlation vs causality is up for debate as both are a function of interest rates and wider equity markets). But as investor lock-ups roll off, the drag on 3 year returns - particularly after strong first day pops - becomes overwhelming in every year we have data for.

Given we are now bumping up on this 3 year buy-and-hold window for the latest period of IPO market euphoria, we can see this playing out in the performance since. Average first day returns of between 30-60% during 2020-2021 have now underperformed the S&P500 by 74% since January 2021 levels. The silver lining is that the IPO index is now beginning to pull away from unprofitable tech indices, which have delivered lackluster returns from the lows in the backdrop of a 5% interest rate market.

So what should IPO investors do? In a nutshell, across the decades of data we have looked at, companies with sales above \$100mn (inflation adjusted) perform better vs smaller peers. This simple heuristic has not diminished over time, nor has it seen the regime shift described above since 2008. This pattern is reflected across a number of different ways we looked at the data. This relationship holds for both profitable and unprofitable ( Exhibit 10 ) companies but also between Tech and Non-Tech firms. Whilst oversimplistic, 1 day returns and 3 year returns on a collection of companies >\$100m of sales is the most surefire way we have found to disaggregate IPO returns for investors.

## Real-time Valuation Monitor:

A simple top-down metric to look at is the Refinitiv Venture Capital Index, which provides an aggregated and rebased index of US venture capital-funded firms. The index:

• Leverages Refinitiv private company data of over 22,000 US firms;

• Estimates the value of each company over time by compiling known firm values, estimating missing values and interpolating values between events; and

• Aggregates month-to-month returns to compute a market cap-weighted index tracking the broad US venture capital universe since 1995.

While the log-scale sees a more modest pull-back in valuations of US VC-backed companies, the index as a whole is nonetheless nearly 18% down YoY and 40% off its highs.
