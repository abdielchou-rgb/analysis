## Another brick in the vol

Yields are lower this week thanks to a soft print on Retail Sales (-0.1% MoM for Retail Sales ex Auto and Gas, which was 0.3% below consensus expectations) and a consensus print on CPI (0.3% on core CPI). The lack of an upside surprise in CPI was helpful in forestalling fears of the Fed needing to signal an openness to further hikes, and sufficient to pressure yield levels as well as short-expiry implied volatility lower this week (Figure 1). But it is likely not sufficient to alter the broader backdrop of policy uncertainty. As we have often noted, the cone of future policy paths remains wide, and the Fed has been unambiguous in signaling that more corroboration of disinflation in future data will be required before it begins easing. As such, YE24 forward OIS rates are only modestly lower, to the tune of \~3bp.

For some time now, yield curves have become quite stable even as front end yields have been on the move. For instance, between early March and now, YE24 forward OIS is higher by 50bp, but the 2s/10s swap curve has traded in a tight range in highly mean-reverting fashion and is essentially unchanged over this period (Figure 2). This is noteworthy given the empirical significance of front end yields in driving yield curves, and merits a closer look. To do this, we turn to our empirical fair value model for the swap yield curve. As a quick reminder, we use a common set of five factors to model numerous different spot and forward swap curves. The factors we use are the level of short rates (3Mx3M forward OIS), the Fed expectations curve (15Mx3M minus 3Mx3M forward OIS curve), the size of the Fed's balance sheet, short term and long term inflation expectations (2Y and 5Yx5Y inflation swap rates). We also include a forward guidance factor as a control variable to account for historical periods where the Fed utilized forward guidance as a policy tool. As we have described elsewhere, these factors have served us well in explaining moves in swap yield curves (see Interest Rate Derivatives 2024 Outlook).

Recent history is no different, and this empirical framework has helped to characterize the contributing factors to yield curve moves in recent months as well. But it is instructive to look at the past four months as being divided into two different roughly equal periods. Specifically, we look at yield curve moves between 1/12/2024 and 3/8/2024, and the period since 3/8/2024, and use our empirical model for yield curves to decompose yield curve changes into portions that are attributable to each significant factor. This analysis is shown in Figure 3, and we make three different observations from this. First, these factors remain useful in explaining yield curve moves in virtually all spot as well as forward sectors. This is seen in the fact that over the first period (1/12 - 3/8) as well as the second period (3/8 - present), expected changes in yield curves are quite similar to actual observed yield curve changes. This suggests continuing effectiveness of these factors when thinking about the curve outlook. Second, the first period was characterized by all factors pointing in the same direction, lining up in favor of yield curve flattening. Short rates rose, the Fed expectations curve dis-inverted, short term inflation expectations rose while long term inflation expectations fell - each of these exerting flattening pressure on yield curves that added up to significant amounts of curve flattening. In contrast, the period since 3/8 has seen the steepening bias from a rise in long term inflation expectations being offset by a narrowing bias from short rates and Fed expectations. Thus, the stability of yield curves in the past 8 weeks or so is really the result of offsetting influences that have been roughly equal and opposite. Finally, a third observation we make from this table is that yield curves in most spot sectors appear too steep relative to fair value.

What does all this mean for yield curve strategy? First, we would expect the front end to remain significant in driving yield curves - the level of short rates (3Mx3M OIS) and Fed expectations (3Mx3M / 15Mx3M curve) are likely to remain significant drivers of the curve. The first of these factors (3Mx3M OIS) is likely to exhibit more stability, but the Fed expectations curve is likely to exhibit more data dependency (Figure 4) and volatility. Second, we would expect long term inflation expectations to moderate, and decline somewhat. Our inflation strategists think that details of the April CPI report strengthen confidence in disinflation going forward, and softer growth data as well as weaker oil prices (relative to expectations) all point to modest declines in 5Yx5Y forward inflation swap yields. To be sure, such declines are expected to be modest in nature - but given high partial betas of yield curves to this factor, even modest moves down in 5Yx5Y inflation translate into significant flattening in yield curves. Thus, given this expectation of a decline in long term inflation expectations, and given currently-steep valuations, we think that swap yield curves, hedged for Fed expectations, are likely biased flatter.

One such trade is a 12Mx3M / 3Mx10Y swap curve flattener, hedged with a 33% risk weighted receive fixed position in 3Mx2Y swaps. The 12Mx3M / 3Mx10Y curve has exhibited a minus \~33% beta with respect to 2Y yields, making this weighted flattener roughly neutral with respect to the front end. But more appealingly, recent market moves have brought back some carry in flatteners anchored at the front end. Currently, this weighted flattener offers \~15bp of positive carry and slide over 3 months, making the annualized carry-to-risk ratio (we measure annualized risk as 2 times the 2Y standard deviation of rolling 3M changes in the weighted yield spread) risk as about 1.0. Therefore, we recommend this trade (see Trade recommendations).

An alternative way to position for flatter near-spot swap curves, while hedging exposure to the front end, is to pair a spot curve flattener with a weighted amount of a forward curve steepener, where the weighting is chosen to offset front end exposure (since both curves will be exposed to the front end, but to possibly different extents). Ideally, such a package, after weighting to neutralize front end exposure, will have three desirable characteristics. First, the spot curve flattener should be in a sector that appears too steep relative to fair value. Second, the forward curve steepener should be in a sector that is either too flat, or at least less steep, relative to fair value. Third, the weighted package should offer attractive carry and slide characteristics. One trade that offers these characteristics is a 3M forward 3s/15s swap curve flattener, paired with 85% risk in a 2Y forward 3s/30s swap curve steepener. The 85% weighting is chosen to offset exposure to Fed expectations based on the partial coefficients from our fair value model. In addition, relative value considerations are favorable, as the 3M forward 3s/15s appears 16bp to steep to fair value, while the 2Y forward 3s/30s curve appears less steep, to the tune of 6bp. Finally, carry on the weighted package stands near 10bp over a 3M horizon, and this trade also offers an annualized carry to risk ratio (where risk is measured as mentioned above) of slightly over 1. Therefore, we recommend this trade (see Trade recommendations).

## Swap spreads

Swap spreads modestly widened this week, led by the long end. For instance, maturity matched swap spreads are flat in the front end, about 0.5bp wider in the 10-year sector, and 1.5-2.5bp wider in the 20- to 30-year sector of the curve (Figure 5). On one hand, the passing of the April CPI release is tactically supportive of resuming a bearish stance. The Fed has repeatedly and unambiguously indicated that it will be patient before deciding to ease, and will require upcoming data to increase its conviction in disinflationary trends. With growth data slowing and inflation data remaining the wildcard, Fed expectations will likely remain rangebound for the foreseeable future as markets await corroboration in future months<sup>’</sup> inflation data. Therefore, we continue to expect yields to be mean-reverting and ultimately rangebound, which is favorable for less-actively delta hedged short gamma positions. Thus, gamma-selling by carry seeking investors is likely to continue and pressure implieds lower relative to levels well below fair value, as is typical in such periods.

That said, short expiry implieds have declined significantly this week, well in excess of what would have been expected by moves in yields, and now appear considerably cheap to fair value (Figure 9). Thus, the question becomes, how cheap is too cheap to continue maintaining a bearish bias on gamma? One approach to answering this question is to build an empirical model for delta hedged long swaption straddle returns that uses the implied volatility mispricing (versus fair value) as one of the inputs. We show such a model in Figure 10, where delta hedged returns are modeled as a function of three ex-ante observable variables - the near term policy rate level, the level of real rates of the same maturity as the underlying swap, and the implied volatility mispricing. As expected, the coefficient on the mispricing is negative, meaning that long vol positions are projected to be more profitable when initiated at cheaper levels, all else equal. Importantly, such a model projects that delta hedged returns on 5- and 10-year tails will be profitable to a statistically significant extent, given current levels of the mispricing (and the current levels of the other drivers). This suggests that implieds in 5- and 10-year tails are now too cheap to warrant initiating short gamma exposure, and therefore, we remain neutral on 5- and 10-year tails, even as we resume a bearish stance on short expiry volatility in the wings of the curve.

## Treasury Futures June – September Rollover Outlook Summary

As we approach the period where the majority of Treasury futures positions will need to be rolled forward from June to September expiries, we discuss our outlook for the various Treasury futures calendar spreads. A detailed discussion may be found in US Bond Futures Rollover Outlook: June 2024 / September 2024, and we include a short summary here.

One, forward financing rates are likely to remain flat and stable given the macro backdrop of elevated policy uncertainty, data dependency, and our expectation for rangebound yields in the near term. Therefore, we don't expect financing rates to impact calendar spreads. Also, carry continues to be negative across all contracts as in the last few roll cycles thanks to the elevated level of financing rates. As a result, futures contracts are priced to early delivery. Early delivery is highly likely in all sectors and optimal where delivery optionality is low. We recommend investors who would like to avoid delivery to roll their positions ahead of the first notice day.

Two, technical positioning will likely play an important part in driving calendar spreads in this roll cycle as well. Net commercial futures open interest is elevated relative to history in the 2-year note, 5-year note and (most of all) ultra-10-year-note contract sectors (where it is at a record high) (Figure 11). In contrast, net commercial futures open interest is near 3-year averages in the TY and WN contracts, and significantly short in the US sector.

Thus, we expect these technical factors to pressure calendar spreads narrower in the UXY, FV and TU sectors, and wider In the US sector, all else equal. Even in the WN sector, net longs may lend some narrowing bias despite the fact that they are historic average levels, as wildcard exposes longs to the risk of significantly suboptimal delivery.

Three, delivery optionality and basis convergence will be a relevant factor in this roll cycle in the ultra-long bond and classic bond contracts. The back basis is likely to stay wide, reflecting the possibility of a higher wildcard option value since the Nov 49s remain a contender for CTD status, but the front basis is likely to narrow heading into the roll if yields remain rangebound as we expect and if the Nov 53s stay CTD. Altogether, this will likely pressure the WN calendar spread wider.

In the US contract, the front net basis is trading at \~1.25/32nds and the back contract is trading at \~3/32nds. On the margin, convergence in the front net basis could exert a widening pressure on calendar spreads.

In both the UXY and FV contracts, the maturity matched swap spread curve between the front and back CTDs appear too flat with respect to recent averages, even after accounting for the broader flattening in spread curves.
