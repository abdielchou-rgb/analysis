## Policy – and fundamentals – in a good place

Treasury yields continued higher and spreads widened following a week of hawkish Fedspeak and strong economic data. Control group retail sales rose 1.1% last month, the largest monthly gain in over a year, and the January and February figures were revised up. Accordingly, it looks like real consumer spending is tracking a solid 3.3% saar gain and we see some upside risk around our 2.25% real GDP growth forecast for 1Q24 (see US: March retail sales report beats expectations, Daniel Silver, 4/15/24). Additionally, commentary from a number of Fed officials emphasized a message of patience, suggesting the Committee is in no hurry to begin rate cuts. Notably, Chair Powell spoke on Tuesday afternoon, providing his first public remarks since the release of the March CPI, stating that recent inflation data “have clearly not given us greater confidence and instead indicate that it<sup>’</sup>s likely to take longer than expected to achieve that confidence.<sup>”</sup> Given continued strength in the data and the messaging from Fed officials, it is unsurprising that markets are now pricing only 38bp of easing this year and are not expecting a full cut until November, which would represent

the longest on-hold period following a hiking cycle in modern monetary history.

Looking ahead, we think yields should find more stability in coming weeks. Despite the Fed<sup>’</sup>s hawkish shift in rhetoric, we think the bar is rather high to price in tightening this year. Most Committee members appear to be characterizing policy as “in a good place,<sup>”</sup> and if the progress in returning inflation back to target becomes even more halting, most members lean toward staying higher for longer, rather than considering rate hikes. Additionally, intermediate yields appear 35bp too high relative to our fair value estimate, a level reached only on two occasions over the last five years (Figure 1). Moreover, looking at previous histori cal episodes of large rate sell-offs, we note a pattern for these aggressive moves to peter out after roughly reaching 100bp in magnitude and 3-4 months in duration. Lastly, in contrast to earlier this year when investor positioning appeared to be stretched to the long side, duration positioning now appears neutral. While these considerations suggest it is becoming more attractive to add duration, it<sup>’</sup>s also hard to identify a near-term catalyst that could drive yields materially lower. Thus, we will be patient before adding duration and remain neutral for now (see Treasuries).

Turning to inflation markets, breakevens continued wider this week, trading to new YTD wides, even as commodity prices found stability and risk assets weakened. Notably, we think there is room for TIPS to outperform further, given that they have lagged their beta to other markets in recent months and remain cheap versus our fair value models. We think real yields appear more attractive than breakevens at these levels, especially as the marketimplied 1Yx1Y real Fed funds rate has crossed above 2%, slightly higher than the spot real policy rate (Figure 2). Thus, we hold longs in 5-year TIPS (see TIPS).

Away from the macro narrative, rates markets have been closely following the evolution of April tax season, given that the resulting rise in Treasury<sup>’</sup>s cash balance would correspond with a drawdown in the other two main liabilities on the Fed<sup>’</sup>s balance sheet—RRP and reserves, with potential implications for money market rates. Overall, tax season has mostly evolved as expected. With most payments likely processed at this time, we are tracking 17% higher than at this point last year, but cumulative receipts remain below the exceedingly strong levels observed in 2022 (Figure 3). As such, in the week through Wednesday, the TGA rose by \$263bn, broadly in line with our expectations, alongside a \$286bn decline in reserve balances, while ON RRP balances proved stickier as expected, declining by only \$5bn. However, RRP usage as fallen further over the last two days, with balances now sitting at \$397bn. Meanwhile, money markets were left largely unaffected despite the sizeable liquidity drain, as overnight benchmark rates barely moved and overnight funding volumes did not meaningfully increase (Figure 4). Perhaps this week<sup>’</sup>s liquidity drainage was a good test in showing just how much the system can weather, which suggests that QT is likely to continue throughout this year (see Short-Term Fixed Income). We expect net T-bill issuance will remain negative in coming weeks, totaling -\$92bn over the coming three weeks, and estimate we are still tracking a \$1.51tn deficit forecast for FY24 (see Treasuries).

In derivatives markets, we maintain swap spread wideners at the front end of the curve, given expectations for continued negative net T-bill issuance, and we also add back swap spread wideners in the belly. The looming prospect of financial issuance was one reason we recommended unwinding belly spread wideners last week. In that context, the relatively modest price action this week despite significant issuance gives us more confidence that spreads should be biased wider in coming weeks. In addition, we expect healthy demand for Treasuries on a swapped basis from banks going forward. The messaging during earnings calls, as well as the evidence from H.8 data, all suggest that banks are growing securities portfolios in general, especially Treasuries on a duration-hedged basis, which should support spreads in the 2- to 5-year sector. Meanwhile we remain neutral on swap spreads at the long end of the curve as the potential for a stronger dollar and rising rates could hamper demand for Treasuries from foreign investors and weaken demand for bond funds. Additionally, we turn neutral on volatility across much of the vol surface. Although implieds remain below fair value, a significant amount of retracement towards fair value has already occurred, and the risk-reward proposition is weaker now for long volatility positions. Meanwhile, we maintain a bearish view on 30-year tails (see Interest Rate Derivatives).

Mortgage spreads edged slightly wider on the week as rates and implied vol bounced around their local highs. Broadly speaking, mortgages have weathered the move to higher rates somewhat better than in the prior two major episodes of the recent past (November '22 and October '23). The technical backdrop is better today, with money managers having started the year with strong inflows and banks with some deposit growth. Moreover, while the backup in rates has increased estimated mREIT leverage, it remains at more sustainable levels than in the prior two episodes, limiting convexity selling. That<sup>’</sup>s not to say that we couldn<sup>’</sup>t experience another deleveraging event with a sufficiently large move in rates. For the time being, we think that the lower coupons offer a decent amount of fundamental value (low 40s T-OAS), though there are some medium-term concerns around the potential unlocking of that float as the Fed talks more about the composition of their balance sheet. UIC still offers a decent amount of spread and high yields (in models adjusted for the recent slowness in speeds), while the belly is perhaps more exposed to technical pressure if we sell off more from here.

Away from spreads, mortgage markets were rocked by a surprise announcement from FHFA that Freddie Mac is looking to purchase closed-end second mortgages, and, if approved, this promises to reshape how borrowers extract equity from their homes. The proposal would have Freddie guarantee closed-end second liens with terms up to 20 year, backed by properties where Freddie already owns the first lien. If the proposal becomes reality, Freddie<sup>’</sup>s cash-window could start purchasing second liens in short order and securitizing them in 6-9 months; this could modestly dampen cash-out refis in discounts if more originators start offering seconds as an alternative, and if GSE execution proves to be better and allows for somewhat lower second lien rates. This new program threatens to remove \$5-10bn of cashout net issuance, which is marginal compared to our \$175bn of total Agency MBS net issuance forecast we have pencilled in for 2024. Fannie Mae is likely to follow, but there are greater hurdles for Ginnie to adopt a similar program, and this could increase the relative speed advantage of Ginnie discounts (see Agency MBS).

Notably, further expansion of the GSE footprint into the home-equity extraction sector promises to crowd out private capital at a time when new entrants are proliferating. Indeed, while we maintain our gross issuance forecast for \$10bn for home equity private-label MBS, the GSEs reentering the second lien mortgage space will absorb significant supply in the long run. Furthermore, the announcement is likely to have significant impact on CRT. Freddie acquiring second liens will help with data disclosure, help harmonize delinquency workout options to both liens, and could increase mortgage credit supply if we see CRT transactions reference second lien mortgages in the near future. We will need more information from FHFA an the GSEs to better understand how the capital relief for existing CRTs change with more second liens (see RMBS Credit).

Turning to commercial real estate, preliminary Q1 2024 data from Costar indicate that mul tifamily housing demand started the year off strong, with 107k of absorptions representing the second largest first quarter since 2000. In addition to a robust job market, we think demand is also driven by the influx of migrants and housing unaffordability, with nearly 75% of renters potentially being locked out of the home purchase market at today<sup>’</sup>s median home prices and mortgage rates (Figure 5). The pickup in absorptions have dampened the effect of heightened construction supply deliveries, cushioning the downward pressure in new lease effective rent growth. This is particularly true for more affordable options (class B/C) which benefit from more durable renter demand compared to the upper end of rental stock (class A) which cater to higher income (and more mobile) renters. Current cap rates (\~5.6%) and financing rates (\~6%) still imply negative leverage, but this dynamic should be less problematic for total returns over longer horizons with reasonable and predictable rent and NOI growth rates. Indeed, predictability is at a premium in today<sup>’</sup>s CRE landscape that is abundant in uncertainty and multifamily is one of the few CRE asset classes that can offer that given the generational unaffordability dynamic (see CMBS).

Within the corporate space, high grade credit spreads are 6bp wider on the week after achieving a new post-GFC tight of 99.5bp last Thursday. Indeed the 3bp of spread widening last Friday was the largest single-day move in the past three months, which highlights the low volatility of HG spreads recently. With HG bonds returning -1.7% over the past three months (-2.2% in April MTD), our prior analysis suggests that we are getting close to the +/-2.5% return threshold where we could see a material shift in fund inflows. However, our credit analysts address a separate question this week, showing that historically there has been no strong correlation between trailing three month returns and one month forward spreads. Meanwhile foreign demand has proven strong: TRACE data show net overnight selling to dealers last week rose to a \$390mn/day, 64% higher than the YTD average. Indeed our FAB index moved up to 90bp, a five month high, indicating that US HG credit is increasingly attractive to foreign investors despite the decline in HG yields as hedging costs have come down. Away from sources of demand, we have observed larger swings in dealer balance sheets, including a record \$5.5bn of dealer purchases of bonds from investors on Monday. We think these swings reflect an expanding credit trading ecosystem and the steady increase in secondary trading volumes—notably among the rising share of portfolio trading—should be supportive of liquidity and a positive for spreads (Figure 6). Looking forward, the focu will be on earnings with 32% of the S&P reporting results next week. Consensus earnings estimates are for 0.9% growth y/y, down from 4% reported last quarter as resilient growth is counterbalanced by the impact of a stronger dollar on exporters (see Corporates).

High-yield bond spreads are 27bp above last week<sup>’</sup>s 5yr tights at 364bp amid resurfaced geopolitical concerns, heightened VIX, strong equity market losses, elevated fund outflows, and higher Treasury yields. Lower quality credits led the way higher, with CCC spreads widening 57bp on the week, while BB and Single-B spreads rose 21bp and 23bp, respectively. Leveraged loan prices continued to consolidate this week (-\$0.11 w/w) amid geopolitical concerns and a more hawkish Fed backdrop. That said, loans<sup>’</sup> 193bp of outperformance versus HY bonds in April is a high since September amid steady inflows, robust CLO origination, light new issuance, and higher for longer policy rates (see High Yield). Indeed, with much of fixed income nursing cumulative losses over the last few years, we think CLO AAA floaters are an attractive addition to core bond fund strategies looking to boost returns and take advantage of the continued inversion of the Treasury curve as well as the compression in spread products. While not the most obvious fit for traditional strategies, our models indicate a modest allocation of CLO floaters (20%) in a core bond fund-like portfolio delivered higher illustrative returns (+1% YTD, compared to current -2.2% YTD). Furthermore, CLO AAA have carved out a high (\~4%) excess coupon to the US Agg, providing ample cushion when rates decline or if tranche spreads were to widen (see CLOs).
