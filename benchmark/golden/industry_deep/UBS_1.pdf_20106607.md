# European Economic Perspectives Switzerland quarterly: All you need to know

## A new quarterly publication

With this edition, we introduce a new quarterly publication on the Swiss macro and policy outlook. It summarises our current views, key charts and data points to watch, and includes a focus article, in which we dive deeper into a topic of current interest. This time, we delve into the tracking of SNB foreign exchange interventions.

## Macro outlook: Growth, inflation, labour market (p. 3)

Manufacturing activity remains depressed amid stagnating Eurozone growth, but the more domestic-oriented services sector partially compensates the weakness. We project real GDP growth of 1.2% in 2024, including international sport events. This should also support the labour market, where we expect only a modest softening in light of still prevalent recruitment shortages (unemployment rate end-2024 UBSe: 2.3%). Inflation has slumped to 1.3% in January and is now running well below the SNB's forecast from December. We expect some acceleration from rent increases in May, but project average inflation in 2024 and 2025 at just 1.4%.

## What next for the SNB? (p. 4)

We expect the SNB to start cutting rates in June, followed by two more cuts in September and December, to a terminal policy rate of 1%. We think the SNB will want to wait to ensure that domestic price pressures stemming from higher rents no longer pose upside risks to inflation (its latest forecast assumes inflation picks up to 2% in Q2). However, with the January inflation downside surprise, the SNB forecast looks too high to us, and the probability of a policy rate cut on 21 March has increased. To cut rates, we believe the end-point of the SNB's inflation forecast would need to decline below 1.5% from currently 1.6%, and the forecast path would have to become downward-sloping. We discuss arguments in favour and against a March rate cut on p.4.

## Focus article: Tracking SNB FX interventions − a closer look (p. 7)

In December, the SNB announced that it no longer focused on FX sales but remained willing to intervene in FX markets, as necessary. The strength of the CHF at the beginning of the year has opened up the question whether the SNB may now move towards purchasing FX again, if needed, and more broadly what the balance will be between FX interventions and policy rate cuts. Tracking SNB intervention activity has thus gained more interest among clients. The focus article describes our current approach to estimating monthly intervention activity, and discusses alternatives with more timely intra-month data.

## CHF rates strategy (p. 6)

We like fading the March SNB pricing by entering into a March vs December 2024 SNB meeting dates flattener. We acknowledge the risks of a March SNB cut, but with the March Fed and the ECB cuts likely off the table, we think that the threshold for the SNB is higher. Furthermore, we believe the divergence in policy rate expectations between ECB and SNB could persist, resulting in a further widening of the ESTR vs SARON spread.

## CHF FX strategy (p. 6)

Given that the CHF REER is trading back at levels seen in mid-December, we believe SNB FX interventions are unlikely to be behind further CHF weakness going forward. This leaves the pricing of the Swiss forward rate curve as the primary short-term driver. We are thus less inclined to trade CHF weakness vs EUR as the pair approaches the high of our Q1 range (0.92-0.96, with the end-Q1 target at 94.50¢) and instead use CHF as a funder vs high-yielding currencies, such as CAD and NZD.

## Swiss economy at a glance

## Growth

Manufacturing activity in Switzerland remains depressed, as Eurozone growth continues to be weak and CHF strength in December/January weighed on sentiment. With the January reading practically unchanged at 43.1 points, the Swiss Manufacturing PMI has now been in contraction for more than a year, and its forward-looking subcomponents do not point to an imminent recovery of manufacturing activity.

Meanwhile, the more domestic-oriented services sector is holding up relatively well, helped by robust private consumption and strong immigration. The latest Services PMI (54.6 points) remains at elevated levels last seen at the beginning of 2023. The January KOF leading indicator, which has a relatively high services weight, also climbed above its long-term average for the first time since March 2023.

We expect growth acceleration of 0.2% q/q in Q1 24 (from 0.1% in Q4 23) and annual GDP growth of 1.2% in 2024, including a positive impact from international sport events.

Key to watch: Q4 23 GDP (released 29 February), February KOF index (released 29 February), February PMIs (released 1 March)

## Labour market

Our growth forecast for Switzerland hinges on the assumption of a relatively resilient labour market. Rising income and job security are important determinants of household consumption, which accounts for 50% of Swiss GDP. A tight labour market is also a pull factor for immigration, which in turn contributes to private consumption.

After two years of unusually high labour shortages, recruitment difficulties have started to ease, but the labour market remains relatively tight by pre-pandemic standards. Employment growth has moderated but is still positive at 1.7% y/y. Meanwhile, unemployment has risen from 1.9% at the beginning of 2023 to 2.2% in January 2024. Manufacturing employment looks much more vulnerable than services.

As long as solid services momentum continues to compensate manufacturing weakness, we expect only a modest softening of the labour market, with unemployment rising slightly further to 2.3% by the end of the year. This would be a moderate rise of 0.4pp from trough to peak, not the sharp deterioration of past recessions when unemployment rose by 1.2 to 1.7pp.

Key to watch: February labour market data (released 7 March)

## Inflation

Swiss inflation fell 40bp to 1.3% y/y in January and is well within the SNB's target range of "below 2%". It is now also running well below the latest SNB forecast released at the December meeting, which assumes average inflation in Q1 of 1.8%. Core inflation (our definition excluding food and energy) was unchanged at 1.5% y/y.

Inflation is now solely driven by domestic price increases, with higher rents (the largest CPI component) becoming more important. We think the full impact of the rise in the mortgage reference rate on rent inflation is yet to come through in May.

Source: Haver, procure.ch, KOF, SECO, UBS

We expect inflation to slip to 1.2% in February and then reaccelerate slightly in March (1.3%). In May, we expect a 20bp increase in headline inflation. Overall, we expect inflation to average 1.4% in 2024 and 2025 (the SNB projects 1.9% and 1.6% in 2024/25).

Key to watch: February inflation (released 4 March) − we expect 1.2% y/y

## SNB

Next meeting: 21 March 2024

Market view: -14bp of cuts priced by March 2024, -39bp by June, -56bp by September (cumulative)

UBS view: First cut in June 2024 (-25bp to 1.5%), followed by two more cuts (Sep/Dec) to 1% by end-2024

Key to watch: January SNB balance sheet data (released on 29 February), February inflation (4 March), February FX reserves data (released on 7 March), speeches/ interviews by SNB board members

At the December meeting, the SNB left the policy rate unchanged at 1.75%, noting that, after rate increases of 2.5pp and significant exchange rate appreciation, monetary conditions were appropriate. In addition, it moderated the language on FX interventions, stating that it no longer focused on selling FX but remained "willing to be active in the foreign exchange market as necessary." The inflation projection showed inflation within the "below 2%" target range until 2026, with the end-point at 1.6%, well below the previous projection of 1.9%. Since the December meeting, inflation has come in much weaker than the SNB expected for Q1, and the CHF first appreciated substantially but has since fallen again. This has led to intense discussions about whether the first rate cut could come as early as on 21 March.

We expect the SNB to start cutting rates in June, followed by two more cuts in September and December, to a terminal policy rate of 1%. We see the following arguments. (1) While near-term inflation (1.3% in Jan) is running well below the SNB's near-term expectations (Figure 7), the SNB may want to wait for the impact of delayed rent increases lifting May inflation. After all, its latest inflation forecast still showed concerns about domestic price pressures with a pick-up to 2% in Q2. (2) What matters for the policy decision is the long-run inflation forecast, which stood at 1.6% for 2026 in December. In order to cut rates, we believe the SNB is aiming for the end-point of its inflation forecast to decline below 1.5% and the forecast path to become downwardsloping (Figure 8). Historically, Swiss inflation averaged 1%, and hence the SNB's implicit target is likely lower than that of other central banks. By March, the SNB may not be confident enough to make a large adjustment to its long-run forecast. (3) Following the latest depreciation, the trade-weighted CHF is now weaker than at the last meeting, reducing the need for rate cuts (although the CHF weakness may partly reflect increased rate cut expectations and could appreciate again if those were priced out). Also of note is that the SNB has two instruments, policy rate and FX interventions, and could implement easier monetary conditions through a combination of both, with less need for rate cuts. (4) With policy rates at 1.75%, the SNB has less distance to negative rates than other central banks. Hence, it may not want to rush into starting the cutting cycle, also considering it would likely be the first G10 central bank to start cutting rates.

At the same time, a March rate cut remains a risk scenario and the SNB may weigh the following arguments in favour of such a move. (1) Concerns about domestic price pressures have become smaller with the latest inflation downside surprises; with lower mortgage rates, rent inflation beyond May also seems no longer a risk. Hence, the longterm inflation projection may decline towards the desired level of below 1.5%. (2) While the SNB would likely be the first G10 central bank to cut in March, it has increased rates earlier and stopped hiking sooner than the ECB, demonstrating its independence. (3) While the SNB could ease monetary conditions by purchasing FX to weaken the CHF prior to cutting rates, it may be concerned about a rising balance sheet and hence rely more on policy rate cuts than during the hiking cycle (when substantial FX sales implied fewer rate hikes).

To weigh the arguments in favour of an earlier or later rate cut, we will closely watch February inflation (4 March) and the ECB meeting (7 March). We will also be watching whether the SNB has intervened to counter CHF strength at the start of the year following Chairman Jordan's remarks in various interviews that a strong CHF was a potential headwind to growth and would lower the inflation outlook. Of key interest will be the release of January balance sheet data on 29 February (see focus article below).

## Strategy views: CHF rates and FX Front-end SARON flatteners remain appealing

SARON March vs December flatteners have been our favourite expression for some time due to attractive valuations, dovish comments from SNB officials and, more importantly, the Swiss inflation outlook.

This segment of the SARON strip has flattened after the softer January Swiss CPI report. Although market pricing is now closer to our expectations of 75bp cuts from the SNB by year-end, there is still scope for some further flattening in this part of the curve, in our view. A March SNB cut cannot be ruled out, considering the inflation outlook and the risks of CHF appreciation. Yet with the March Fed and the ECB cuts likely off the table, we think that the threshold for a March cut from the SNB is higher.

A postponement of a March SNB cut could push pricing of cuts towards the end of the year. In contrast, an early March cut from the SNB, coupled with concerns about Swiss franc appreciation, could set the stage for further cuts at the upcoming policy meetings, leading to additional flattening in the March vs December curve.

Furthermore, we think the divergence in the policy rate expectations between the ECB and the SNB could persist, resulting in a further widening of the ESTR vs SARON spread. Despite the upward repricing in the ECB's policy rate expectations, the market is pricing in nearly 105bp of cuts from the ECB. President Lagarde warned last week against a hasty rate cut, while an ECB source's story set ECB cut expectations this year ranging between 50bp and 100bp, with the majority of GC members currently in favour of a cut in June. Earlier in February, GC member Isabel Schnabel also mentioned the recent pickup in demand, as reflected in the latest PMI survey, as a challenge for the disinflation process. All in all, we think there is still more room for a repricing higher in ECB policy rate expectations compared with those of the SNB.

## FX: Short CHF vs CAD and NZD

Our target EURCHF is at 94.50¢ for the end of Q1, with the pair likely to trade within the 0.92-0.96 range.

We recently mentioned that, even though there still seems to be strong attention on the SNB FX interventions with investors questioning whether the central bank has flipped again to selling CHF/buying FX in recent months (see our focus article on p. 7 for background), we believe that it is unlikely to be a main driver of further CHF weakness going forward. FX intervention is now a finetuning tool the SNB is ready to use in case CHF starts to overshoot again, as seen at the end of December and end of January. With CHF REER trading back to levels seen in mid-December (as per the latest SNB meeting, Figure 13), monetary conditions are now expected to be more balanced. ## In focus: How we track SNB FX interventions

FX interventions have always played an important role in the SNB's policy to achieve its inflation target. After abandoning the minimum EURCHF exchange rate in 2015, the SNB purchased FX in large amounts as it deemed inflation too low and the Swiss franc "highly valued". When policy rates began to rise in 2022, the focus turned to selling FX reserves to strengthen the Swiss franc and help lower inflation. In December 2023, the SNB moved to a more neutral position with regard to FX interventions, stressing that it remained willing to buy or sell FX, if needed. Tracking the SNB's intervention activity has thus become even more important. A key question is to what extent the SNB will rely on interventions alongside rate cuts to ease monetary conditions going forward.

While we do not expect a fully fledged reversal to aggressive balance sheet expansion, we would not exclude moderate and temporary interventions to counter sharp and sudden appreciation. We therefore monitor the data closely for any signs of interventions. Since the SNB publishes its actual intervention data only at quarterly frequency (and with a three-month lag), SNB watchers have to rely on alternative sources to gauge the extent of FX interventions in (more) real time. We discuss the two approaches we use below, and also discuss the pros and cons of more timely data.

We approximate FX interventions using monthly balance sheet data, which is usually released with a one-month lag. We use two approaches, looking both at the asset and the liability side of the SNB balance sheet.

1. Adjusting changes in FX reserves: Given that FX interventions involve purchasing or selling FX assets, the monthly change in FX reserves should be a good proxy. However, the SNB uses mark-to-market valuation of its balance sheet, and hence the monthly changes in FX reserves may also reflect currency fluctuations or changes in asset prices (e.g. 25% of the SNB's reserves are invested in equities). To adjust for these valuation effects, we subtract from the change in FX reserves and the change in gold reserves the change in "provisions and equity capital", which captures the valuation effects of FX reserves and gold on the liability side of the balance sheet. Note that this adjustment does not take account of dividend and interest payments, and hence tends to overestimate the amount of FX purchases.

2. Adjusting changes in sight deposits: FX interventions result in a change in sight deposits that banks hold with the SNB. For example, if the SNB purchases (sells) FX assets, it credits (debits) the amounts in CHF to the banks' sight deposit accounts. A change in sight deposit amounts is hence one indication of intervention activity. However, the level of sight deposits is also affected by several other factors so that we make the following adjustments to the monthly change in sight deposits: (a) sight deposits could be exchanged for cash, hence we subtract the monthly change in banknotes; (b) we substract the change in secured loans (asset side) that the SNB grants to banks through a credit on their sight deposit accounts − these were important during the Covid crisis when the SNB offered the so-called Covid loans via the newly created SNB Covid-19 refinancing facility, but it also played a role in Emergency Liquidity Assistance (ELA) during times of banking stress; they still influence the balance sheet today as the secured loans are gradually paid back; (c) we account for open market operations by substracting the change in claims from CHF repos and adding the change in liabilities from CHF repos (reverse repos) and the change in SNB bills. For March/April 2023, we also added the change in FX liabilities to try to account for emergency lending during the period of banking stress.

These two approaches track the actual FX interventions reasonably accurately. Since Q1 20 (the first date when SNB official intervention data was made available), the correlation coefficient amounts to 0.89 for the approach using adjusted sight deposits, and 0.82 for the approach using adjusted FX reserves. Over this period, our two approaches coincided with the direction of actual FX interventions in almost each quarter.

One drawback of our estimation approach is that − while the data is still more timely than the actual intervention data − it is nevertheless released with a one-month lag. While the overall amount is small and could merely reflect estimation inaccuracy, the fact that both estimation approaches point in the direction of FX purchases is noteworthy. ## Technical notes

To approximate the changes in the provisional FX reserves stemming from valuation adjustments, we try to recreate the SNB's FX portfolio based on as much available data as possible.

For local-currency equity returns, we use the respective national total return (i.e. including dividend payments) stock market indices but exclude banks, in line with the SNB's investment principles.

For local-currency bond indices, we use total return (i.e. including coupon payments) bond indices that roughly match the duration, rating and issuer profile of the SNB's bond portfolio.

We calculate the US equity share from the SNB's 13F filing with the SEC and derive the other country weights from the currency weighting published by the SNB. According to this methodology, the share of US equities in the total equity portfolio has increased from 35% in 2013 to almost 80% in 2023, likely due to the strong performance of US tech stocks over the period.

We update asset returns only at the end of each quarter, whereas we incorporate gold and FX valuation effects on a monthly basis, in line with our understanding of the SNB's accounting practice (see footnotes here and here).

The calculated returns match the annual returns published by the SNB reasonably well and could also be used to forecast the SNB's annual profit, for example. However, the accuracy is lower on a monthly basis, and the deviations are large enough to introduce significant variance to the monthly FX intervention estimate.
