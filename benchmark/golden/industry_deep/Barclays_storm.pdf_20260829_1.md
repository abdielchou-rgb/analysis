# Calm after the summer storm

CDS indices have fully retraced the summer weakness, while IG credit, and insurance hybrids, remains well of the tights. Though Main is tight to IG credit, the upcoming roll, cleaner investor positioning and a likely pick-up in supply could lead to Main staying tight to IG in the medium term.

After the sell-of in early August, markets have retraced a large portion of the move and have traded in a narrow range in the past five sessions. Surveying the spread changes since 31 July (Figure 1) is instructive: CDS indices have retraced fully, while €IG corp spreads are still 9bp wider.

Adjusting for the beta of each market, the sell-of has had a notable compression footprint, with higher-beta credit outperforming their historical beta. The most pragmatic reason for this is arguably that during a "summer-illiquid" sell-of, the easiest-to-sell assets were straight IG credit, so they moved the most. Another potential reason for the low-beta sell-of is that Bund yields dropped meaningfully, and with marginal demand seemingly yield-based, this made lowbeta credit more vulnerable. €IG yields are now back near the YTD lows. We will have to wait until September to get a sense for whether the low all-in yield yields will be a headwind to IG demand, and/or if it will lead to increased demand for higher-beta credit to meet earlier yield bogies.

The one major exception to the "high-beta outperformance" pattern is that of insurance hybrids, which have underperformed other parts of credit lately, see Figure 2 showing Inco T2 vs Bank T2 (RT1 vs AT1 shows a similar pattern.)

The other notable dynamic from Figure 1 is the outperformance of CDS vs cash. Even if this now leaves Main tight to €IG cash (7.5bp on a 3yr horizon), Main could remain tight to cash in the medium term due to a number of factors: Investor positioning is much cleaner in Main now, the upcoming roll on 20-Sep could lead to a reluctance from investors to buy protection, and IG supply could pick up meaningfully from here as well.

The earnings season for IG companies saw an incremental rise in the cautious tone around full year outlook from firms, and our equity strategists note that net guidance upgrades for the second quarter results in Europe were the weakest since 2020. Given that EPS growth for 2024 looks to be back loaded in Q3 and Q4 for Europe, estimates for coming quarters need to hold, in order for our and consensus estimates of mid-single digit EPS growth for the whole of 2024 to materialise. With persistent weakness in manufacturing PMIs, this could lead to more earnings disappointment later in 2024.

Bank earnings were generally strong, with our equity research team noting that profit before tax beat consensus by 10% in aggregate, helped by short-term NII upgrades and fees driving revenues higher. ## Investment Grade

# Idiosyncratic risk: wider for a reason

Buying the dip has proven a poor strategy for single names in €- IG. Only a quarter of underperforming tickers retrace their widening within six months: around half settle at their new trading level, while a significant tail continues to sell of. Performance is particularly poor for low-beta idiosyncratic names.

• Idiosyncratic sell-ofs aren't short-lived...: The majority of €-IG credits fail to fully retrace an idiosyncratic move wider in the following six months. This implies that single-name moves usually happen for good reason, and that disappointing earnings or name-specific headline risks should not be faded quickly, in most cases.

• ... especially for low-beta credits. For names that initially trade tight vs the index, an idiosyncratic move wider tends to be more damaging because full recovery is less likely. The picture is more mixed for high-beta names, as there is much greater scope for subsequent recovery once bad news has been more fully incorporated into spread levels.

• Buying the dip is not attractive for single names. Our analysis implies that it doesn't pay to simply buy the dip in €-IG names following an idiosyncratic sell-of. Not only is the widening only retraced less than a third of the time over a six month horizon, but the potential for a continued underperformance creates an asymmetric returns distribution and poor riskreward, as the worst performers fare more poorly than the best performers.

Note that in this report we specifically exclude 'dips' at the index level, and focus solely on namespecific widening in periods when the macro-context was relatively stable.

## Don't fight the idiosyncratic moves

Idiosyncratic risk in €-IG has been elevated since early 2022 as the weakest credits have struggled with the transition to a higher rates environment and investor tolerance for 'story' names has been low, as we've discussed previously (Figure 1). Though the frequency of idiosyncratic moves<sup>1</sup> is down from the 2022/23 peaks, it has remained high vs history for some time. Hence, it is natural to consider how to trade these moves; in particular, are single-name widening events an opportunity to buy the dip?

To address this question, we have looked at the cumulative spread performance of 3,542 idiosyncratic spread-widening events since 2010. Using this measure, a name has 'recovered' when it trades with the same spread to the index as before the initial widening event (ie, once the cumulative change reaches 0bp). More often than not, however, we find that credits do not recover within six months; more specifically, over the past 15 years, only c.30% of tickers fully

For example, after six months, the bottom decile ends up 50bp wider than the index, while the top decile is 35bp tighter (Figure 3). The outcomes are even less favourable if you include the initial move wider, not just the ex post performance (Figure 4).

## Low- vs high-beta names

Using starting spread versus the index as a proxy for beta, we find that low-beta names are less likely to fully recover within six months than the general cohort. Tight-trading low-beta names that idiosyncratically move wider have a lower probability of recovering vs the index and a higher probability of further underperforming the index. Hence, not only should idiosyncratic moves wider not be viewed as buying opportunities, they should especially be avoided for names that initially trade inside the index.

In contrast, high-beta names have more extreme outcomes: they have a high probability of fully retracing and a high probability of widening further – but they have a very low probability of settling at a new price level for an extended period of time. That high-beta names are less likely to track the index after a widening is not surprising, but we find it interesting that the split between outperformance and underperformance is much more evenly balanced for high-beta names than for low-beta ones.

## Variation over time

The behaviour of idiosyncratic credits has varied over time, in the size of moves and the frequency and speed of recovery. Though there is some variation, our conclusion that investors should not try to fade the widening does hold over time and in diferent market environments.

Figure 5 shows how long it took the top-performing quintile of idiosyncratic events to retrace in a given year. The recovery speed dropped of notably around 2017, and didn't rebound until 2022: 2017 also had the fewest idiosyncratic moves wider of any year, followed by 2021. The lower level of single-name risk combined with the slower recovery speed in these years implies that the bar to an initial sell-of was perhaps higher. In other words, credits that were most likely to quickly recover (ie, where the selling was least warranted) may not have sold of in the first place, signalling greater investor tolerance for single-name situations back then than today. That the ECB was running CSPP at this time may have been a factor.

While the data set for 2024 is somewhat limited by being only eight months into the year, we note that 2024 has been another harsh year for idiosyncratic underperformers. This indicates that buying the single-name dip has been a particularly poor strategy for €-IG managers this year.

# Drawdowns - blink and you'll miss them

Drawdowns occur on average every four months when spreads are <400bp. The majority of drawdowns are short-lived and spreads tend to be tighter most of the time one month later. But, spreads rarely retrace to pre-drawdown levels. Typically, "averaging-in" after a drawdown trigger ofers minimal spread tightening upside.

The move that took PE HY spreads from 326bp to 383bp over the course of a few days earlier this month proved to be extremely short-lived. At 338bp through the close of Wednesday, spreads have retraced around 80% of that widening while Xover is already back to trading below 300bp. Initially, the narrative around the move wider along with the broader correction in risk assets was based on worries of an imminent growth slowdown in the US, but that has been quickly put to bed based on economic data releases in the days and weeks that followed.

In our view, the market is right to downplay concerns. Growth in the US was and is expected to moderate through 2024 and into the first half of 2025, but only to trend or slightly-below trend levels. This would mark a soft landing, as opposed to anything resembling recession-like levels (and in our view there is no evidence from recent economic data to suggest that this is the case). Nevertheless, we do think that an important narrative change has been the shift away from a fixation on inflation data and rates as the market gets comfortable with the path of central bank policy rates, and toward growth again, and therefore corporate earnings.

And with that, we have seen spreads become much more correlated with equity volatility (in this case the V2X). For much of 2022 through 2023, the relationship was relatively weak based on an r-squared of only 17% (Figure 1), which can only be explained by the fact that rates dominated over everything else. However, since March of this year especially, that relationship has reverted back to the strong long-term trend, as evidenced by PE HY spreads and the V2X having an r-squared of slightly more than 50% during this period (Figure 2).

What is ultimately most informative is seeing how long these drawdowns last and also how long it takes for spreads to revert back to their pre-drawdown levels. Based on the 24 periods above, we find that one month later, spreads are tighter than the day the drawdown is triggered 78% of the time (Figure 4) - for the other 22%, it would be fair to classify them as more of a sustained spread widening. This success rate falls to 65% three months later but just 35% six months later, likely reflecting the fact that these drawdowns occur relatively frequently.

Put another way, for all instances when a drawdown is triggered and the starting spread is below 400bp, in only 1 in 5 such instances are spreads still wider one month later. Drawdowns are therefore short in nature.

While the historical trend shows that drawdowns tend to be short-lived, it's important to also highlight that spreads also rarely revert back to their pre-drawdown level. In Figure 5 we show that after one and three months, spreads only revert back 13% of the time. And in Figure 6 we show that after one month especially, spreads retrace on average 65% of the widening. That percentage falls considerably after three months but gets more complicated given overlapping drawdown cycles.

Nevertheless, it emphasises the point that drawdowns are short but also that spreads rarely fully retrace the move. In theory, this still justifies any strategy around "buying the dip" or buying on weakness. But this only works with immaculate timing. For example, if you bought all 24 instances when a drawdown is triggered, and on the day that it is triggered, then spreads are on average 34bp tighter one month later (Figure 7), meaning adopting such a strategy is likely worth it when considering what this translates to in terms of excess returns.

However, given a more realistic scenario of "averaging in" over the two weeks post a drawdown is triggered, suggests much more limited upside. One month later spreads are only 12bp tighter and three months later on average spreads are actually slightly wider (Figure 8). The reality is, after considering transaction costs, any potential spread tightening is probably likely to be largely eroded.

Clearly, this data is skewed by those instances where spreads are not tighter one or three months later post drawdown (as per Figure 4) but even if we limit this analysis to those situations where spreads are only tighter, the average spread tightening is only 19bp after one and three months on an "averaging in" strategy.

All of the above analysis is focused on the index level and the biggest pushback is that the equivalent is likely a lot more nuanced at the single name level. Indeed, in the IG section of this week's Alpha our colleagues argue that it doesn't pay to buy dips at the single name level.

This speaks to the fact that sharp moves wider at the index level are more often than not driven by some sort of macro shock. And given how few of those subsequently lead to the point of a material growth slowdown/recession, which would be accompanied by spreads continuing to widen beyond 400bp, it is perhaps unsurprising that spreads more often than not tighten quickly even if they do not fully retrace.

However, when we isolate what has happened to spreads of individual bonds that have widened significantly more than the index since the start of 2023, we find that 56% are still wider one month later and only a little less (43%) three months later (Figure 10).

While this is only limited to the sample of situations over the last 20 months, the justification as to why we see a diferent conclusion versus the index level is more likely reflective of the fact that these names have underperformed for idiosyncratic reasons related to concerns about their credit profile. Subsequently, spreads may "reset" to reflect a weaker credit profile and/or any retracement risks taking place over a longer period of time. Like what our IG colleagues find, it would suggest that a buy the dip strategy at a single-name level is also not a favourable strategy.

# The reopening of the primary market

After the usual summer break, the primary market restarted as expected this week, with three EUR benchmark CBs being priced. We take stock of these deals and look at which issuers could come next. We delve into Aareal's and PBB's H1 24 results, and take stock of Fitch's upgrade of two Spanish cedulas' programmes.

Previously published as The AAA Investor: The reopening of the primary market, 22 August 2024.

Covered bond supply: The second half of August saw a successful reopening of the primary market, with three EUR benchmark deals being priced in the first three days of this week. These first few deals after the summer break showed that demand remains solid, but is becoming increasingly selective.

Aareal and PBB publish results: Provisions remain high and the ongoing challenges related to the CRE market, particularly in the US ofice segment, still seem to be having a significant efect on both banks' profitability. However, liquidity and funding levels look good, particularly for PBB.

Cedulas upgraded: In July Fitch upgraded two cedulas programmes, as a consequence four bonds moved from the A-rated iBoxx index to the AA-rated index, with an impact on index spreads.

## Covered bond supply

## The reopening of the primary market

The market is traditionally quiet from mid-July to mid-August, and usually reopens shortly after the 15 August bank holiday in many continental European countries. This year has been no exception, with three EUR benchmark deals being priced this week.

The first deal was brought by Commerzbank, which hit the market as early as Monday, 19 August, with a 5y paper that garnered €2.2bn of demand for a €1.25bn deal, and tightened by 4bp between IPT and final pricing, still allowing for a c.1bp new issue concession (NIC). This was followed the next day by a 7y issue from BHH, which only attracted c.€600mn of demand for a €500mn social covered bond; the deal was priced flat to the secondary curve. Then, on Wednesday, 21 August, ABN came to the market with a 3y CB that attracted a staggering €3.9bn of demand for the €1.25bn deal (over 3x oversubscribed). The deal tightened by 7bp, pricing with little to no NIC.

These first few deals after the summer break show that investors demand remains solid, but is becoming increasingly selective. Both issuers and investors seem to be focusing on shorter tenors, as we expected, after a H1 24 that was driven by the reopening of the long end. In

## What's next?

August is usually a low issuance month, but in the past two years, issuers have taken advantage of good market conditions to come to market in the second half of the month. August 2024 has been no exception so far, and with these three new deals being successfully priced in just three days (for a total of €3bn, vs €15bn in August 2022 and €13.75bn in August 2023), we expect to see more to come next week as issuers try to price deals ahead of the usually very busy month of September.

Issuance YTD now stands at €121bn in EUR. We expect the post-summer re-opening to be relatively busy, particularly after sluggish primary market activity in June due to volatility in rates markets as a result of the French snap parliamentary elections. Factoring in the 'lost' time, we think issuers might want to complete as much of their funding as earlier on as possible, and even pre-fund for 2025 ahead of major central bank meetings in September (ECB: 12 September, Fed: 18 September) and the US elections.

## What about other currencies?

Most issuers have tended to tap the GBP market in September, although Canada's CCDJ and Germany's PBBGR issued FRN covered bonds in August 2023, and Clydesdale Bank priced a 4y FRN CB in August 2022.

The same is true for USD covered bonds. Also, USD supply has been very low this year (MUNHYP \$600mn 2y, LBBW \$600mn 4y, TD \$2.5bn 3y).

There are no GBP or USD covered bond redemptions in August or September 2024. But in October, RY has a GBP1bn CB maturing on 3 October, and PBBGR has a USD750mn CB maturing on 11 October.

## Aareal Bank and PBB publish results

## Aareal Bank

On 8 August, Aareal Bank published its Q2 24 results. The bank posted net income from continuing operations of €128mn (+42% y/y), operating profit of €181mn (+31% y/y) and NII growth of 11% y/y.

Loan loss provisions remained elevated, at €176mn, due to ongoing challenges in the US ofice market, but it declined 10% y/y. The bank has been active in managing its NPLs: “c. €300mn of NPLs are prepared for resolution in Q3 and are already fully provisioned in Q2”, and the NPE ratio (EBA methodology) stood at 3.3% in Q2 24, vs 2.9% in 1Q24 and 3.4% in FY23. US ofice exposure was €3.0bn, 81% of which has an LTV below 60%. LCR was 239%, vs 192% in Q1 24. Deposits remained relatively stable at €13.8bn, vs €13.9bn in Q1 24. Retail term deposits from the Raisin platform increased to €3.5bn (€3bn in Q1 24).

In terms of funding, Aareal successfully issued its first green senior non-preferred bond; the €500mn issue was more than 3x oversubscribed. Additionally, two benchmark Pfandbriefe were issued in 2024, the most recent being a €500mn five-year Pfandbrief placed at the beginning of July that was also c. 3x oversubscribed. One additional Euro-Pfandbrief benchmark is planned for H2 24, while no additional senior preferred funding is planned through 2026, and a T2 benchmark is planned for 2025.

Aareal Bank's latest covered pool report is as of end-June 2024 and can be found here.

## PBB

Deutsche Pfandbriefbank published its Q2 24 results on 14 August. Interestingly, the bank showed very strong liquidity figures, pointing to an overall solid funding profile.

PBB posted pre-tax profit of €13m, vs €49m in Q2 23 and €34m in Q1 23, and NII grew 10% y/y to €121m. Management reiterated its optimistic profit guidance for 2024: “PBT expected significantly above 2023 level”of €90m. Liquidity has been boosted by higher than expected growth in retail deposits, which totalled €8bn as of July, vs the peak of €8.1bn as of April and

compared with €6.6bn at end-2023. These are mainly term deposits with 3.6 years of WAL. PBB wants to reduce deposits to \~€7.5bn by YE24 due to its “excess liquidity” position.

Risk provisions were €56m, vs €47m in 1Q24, but significantly below levels recorded in 2H23. Stage 3 NPLs decreased to €1.6bn, vs €1.65bn in 1Q24, and the NPE ratio came in at 3.5%. The US NPE ratio was 18%, with absolute NPEs increasing to €761m from €672m in Q1 24. Additionally, the development portfolio was reduced by €0.5bn to €2.5bn. The CET1 ratio stood at 14%, and the bank started the transition to Basel IV F-IRBA. In the current transitional period, models are calibrated towards standardised risk parameters, and no further downside risk is expected on PD/LGD parameters.

In terms of funding plans, the bank said there was “No need for Senior Unsecured funding in 2024”, thanks to the €2bn Pfandbrief funding completed year-to-date, which is well ahead of 2024 needs, and “Funding exceeds 2024 needs; any further issuance is prefunding for next year”.

PBBGR also announced that it would host its capital markets day on 10 October.

## Cedulas upgraded

Fitch on 2 July upgraded five Spanish multi-issuer Cedulas Hipotecarias (MICH) transactions (tickers: AYTCED and CEDTDA) from A+ to AA. The upgrades reflected an update to Fitch's approach in terms of OC levels.

Interestingly, this means that these issues now have an Aa1 rating at Moody's and an AA rating at Fitch, but are still at BBB+ at S&P. However, as a consequence of the upgrade and due to iBoxx index methodology, four of the five covered bonds mentioned above migrated from the EUR iBoxx Covereds A-rated index to the EUR iBoxx Covereds-AA rated index: CEDTDA 4 1/4 04/10/31, CEDTDA 4 1/4 03/28/27, AYTCED 3 3/4 06/30/25, AYTCED 4 3/4 05/25/27.

Hence, as of 1 August, the iBoxx Covered A index is only made up of two Danish ship finance bonds (DANSKB 4 3/8 10/19/26 and DANSKB 0 1/4 06/21/28), and this explains the jump in spread observed in the iBoxx Covereds A-rated index from c. 44bp at end-July to c. 67bp in the beginning of August (Figure 9).

## Fundamentals in focus

## Real estate

## HEIMSTADEN BOSTAD AB: Progressing

HEIBOS reported positive valuations during H1 24, which along with disposals through privatisation, created a bufer on S&P D/(D+E) metric, ofsetting concerns of weakness in the ICR metric. We think HEIBOS can continue to take on bank debt to repay unsecured debt maturities and defend its IG rating. We remain Overweight.

## German residential real estate: Turning point

Landlords are calling for an end to valuation declines, which along with potential disposals, is likely to support LTVs going forward. However, we expect ICRs to decline with the refinancing of debt maturities as costs remain high. We remain Market Weight on ANNGR and DWNIGY and Underweight on LEGGR and GYCGR.

## Prologis European Logistics Fund: Growth opportunity

PELF is a pure-play logistics landlord which continues to grow with the support from shareholders, including Prologis. It remains one of the lowest leveraged landlords, ofering attractive spread pick-up compared to lower- or similar-rated ofice, retail and residential landlords. We initiate at Overweight.

## Alstria Ofice: Debt comes due

Alstria Ofice has €440mn of debt coming due in Q3 25. With c.€105mn cash and a €200mn RCF, short-term liquidity is constrained. If the company were to proceed with a debt exchange, now is the time. If not, the bonds could be at risk of downgrade by S&P.
