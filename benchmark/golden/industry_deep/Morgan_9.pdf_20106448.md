## United States

• Fed rhetoric continued to signal rate cuts this year, but not in March

• Jobless claims and business surveys point to ongoing expansion in 1Q24

• Fiscal year <sup>’</sup>24 federal deficit looking a little less bad, but very far from good

• Next week<sup>’</sup>s January retail sales expected weak, and core CPI expected benign

The news flow concerning the US economy was fairly light this week, and what little data was released suggest the economy is continuing to do fine early in the year. In this cycle the surveys haven<sup>’</sup>t been a great guide to where either GDP or employment is heading, but for what it<sup>’</sup>s worth the two national service sector surveys pointed to modest ongoing growth. The weekly jobless claims figure remained low, echoing last week<sup>’</sup>s January jobs report that indicated the labor market remains tight.

Signals were mixed regarding credit availability and credit quality. The Fed<sup>’</sup>s Senior Loan Officer Opinion survey for January indicated that lending standards were generally tightening at a slower pace than in the prior quarter. However, the survey took place before the recent return of market worries about the health of regional banks. Finally, the New York Fed<sup>’</sup>s quarterly report on household credit showed some developments worth watching. Healthy consumer credit metrics had been a strong point of this recovery, but more recently credit card and auto loan delinquencies have accelerated.

The Fed-speak kicked off early this week, with a televised Powell interview on Sunday (recorded the prior Thursday). In that interview the Fed chair doubled down on the message from the most recent post-FOMC press conference that a March rate cut is unlikely. More generally, most Fed speakers this week talked about taking their time in approaching the decision of when to first reduce rates. We<sup>’</sup>re feeling pretty good about our long-standing call for the first rate cut at the June FOMC meeting.

Also this week, the CBO released their mid-year FY24 deficit estimate. Their expected deficit of \$1,507bn is notably more optimistic than our prior \$1,675 forecast. The CBO<sup>’</sup>s estimate is predicated on current law, and normally we might expect the passage of the usual business tax extenders would boost their number by over \$100bn, bringing it closer to our prior estimate. However, with that bill now apparently stalled in the Senate, we are inclined to view the CBO<sup>’</sup>s number as a good estimate of where things will end up during the current fiscal year.

The data flow picks up next week, with both the January retail sales and CPI reports. We expect that seasonal adjustment factors—which may have flattered December retail sales—will present a headwind to January retail sales, and we look for total sales to slip 0.9% and sales in the important control category to be flat. For the CPI we expect declining energy prices will limit the headline figure to just a 0.1% increase, which would take the year-ago gain down to 2.9% from 3.4% in December. We also look for a high-side 0.2% increase in the ex-food and energy core measure, held down by an expected decline in used vehicle prices.

## Mixed credit news

The Fed<sup>’</sup>s Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey (SLOOS) showed less widespread tightening in lending standards for many of the different reported loan types in the most recent quarter (generally coinciding with 4Q). For example, a net 14.5% of banks reported tightening lending standards for C&I loans to large and medium firms in the most recent quarter, down from a net share of 33.9% in the prior quarter and a net share of 50.8% in the one before that (Figure 1). There also has been a similar large shift down in the net share of banks reporting tightening in standards for CRE loans lately along with more modest declines in the net shares of banks tightening for residential real estate and consumer loans.

In spite of the turn in the series for CRE, it was hard to get warm and fuzzy feelings about that sector of the economy this week. Markets remained focused on CRE-related losses at regional lenders, presenting a new risk to credit availability.

One of the hallmarks of this expansion has been pristine consumer balance sheets. Supported by ample fiscal support in <sup>’</sup>20 and <sup>’</sup>21, household debt loads have been modest and credit performance solid. Over the course of <sup>’</sup>22 and <sup>’</sup>23 those metrics have generally been normalizing back toward prepandemic norms. Recently, however, there have been pockets of credit that are starting to look a little worse than before the pandemic (Figure 2).

Figure 2: New delinquent balances (30+ days) % of current balance
![](images/6f193dc579ad5747cdcf60287cdf08dd32d4d3db1424027998e0e882321a97f8.jpg)

Credit card and auto loan performance has continued to deteriorate, with delinquencies rising above levels generally seen in the last expansion. Overall credit performance is still benefiting from good mortgage credit quality, which is being helped by high and rising house prices. The end of student loan forbearance may be an issue for some borrowers, but missed federal student loan payments are not being reported to credit bureaus until 4Q24, so the New York Fed<sup>’</sup>s data will not be showing any deterioration in related credit quality yet.

## Labor markets still looking healthy

Last week<sup>’</sup>s highlight (besides the FOMC meeting) was the blockbuster 353,000 gain in January nonfarm employment. This week<sup>’</sup>s jobless claims report indicates that the labor market likely remains healthy heading into February, with the latest report showing initial claims filings declining from 227,000 to 218,000 during the week ending February 3 (Figure 3).

## CPI revisions a dud

The annual update to the CPI seasonal adjustment factors was released this week. This normally mundane report was closely watched, in part because Fed governor Waller flagged it as important for this policy outlook (even though changes to the seasonal adjustment never affect over year-ago readings, and on Sunday Chair Powell indicated over year-ago readings are what matters). In the event, it was a non-event: while there were some minor changes to the recent monthly pattern,

4Q23 core CPI is still reported up at a 3.4% annual rate, as was the case before the revision (Figure 4).

![](images/6337f96295fed276770d98e40a4a8121fbdbe858b884ec1ac2ba0d64ecf0baaf.jpg)

The revisions to the CPI data will affect upcoming revisions to the related PCE price data, with the October-December PCE data subject to revisions in the BEA<sup>’</sup>s upcoming reports late in February. But with small revisions in the CPI data, we expect only marginal corresponding revisions to the core PCE data, with the December monthly change revised down by just 0.02%-pt but offsetting upward revisions to the earlier months.

## Looking back to 4Q GDP

The nominal trade deficit was close to expectations in December, at \$62.2bn that month. But revisions to earlier figures showed a narrower deficit during the quarter overall than we had been anticipating, and also relative to what the BEA incorporated in its 4Q GDP release on January 25. Since that earlier report from the BEA showing real GDP up 3.3% saar in 4Q, we have raised our tracking of growth in that quarter to 3.6%, with news from the trade release adding a tenth.
