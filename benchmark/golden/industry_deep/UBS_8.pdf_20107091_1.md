# US Economic Perspectives US population growth

## Population growth is economic growth

Population growth in theory feeds directly into the potential growth rate of an economy. Another tenth of growth in the population available to participate in the labor force means another tenth the economy grows, or should be able to grow, Congressional Budget Office,<sup>(3)</sup> as well writings by outside analysts such as ourselves, have raised optimism among economists, policymakers and market participants that the US economy may be able to sustain faster growth due to an increase in net international migration and help the FOMC achieve the historically elusive soft landing.

## US population growth rests largely on immigration

Let's start with some boring facts. Wait, facts? No, a better word is estimates. The US Census Bureau estimates the US resident population expanded 0.49% in the 12 months ending July 1, 2023, to 334,914,895. That represented acceleration from the 0.37% in 2022, but in line with estimated 0.46% growth from 2019.

As birth rates have trended down and deaths trended higher, population growth depends increasingly on immigration.

Figure 2: Population growth increasingly dependent upon immigration
![](images/ed97685bef13fe4516630b9b4e699a9a971e4bb059a1062fc33ed5421d2d5700.jpg)
Source: William H. Frey analysis of US Census Bureau population estimates: intercensal 2000-2010, vintage 2020, and vintage 2023 released December 19, 2023. Brookings Institution. Available: https://www.brookings.edu.

Despite similar rates of resident population growth in 2019 and 2023, the composition of that growth was quite different (chart above). The Census Bureau estimated over the 12 months ending July 1 2023, the resident population expanded 1,643,484. Of that, 504,495 was natural growth (3,653,356 births less 3,148,861 deaths) plus 1,138,989 was due to net international migration (immigration less exit migration). The Census Bureau calls these the "components of change". The vital statistics on births and deaths generate little debate. The estimates from the National Center from Health Statistics are periodically revised, updated and checked, but the slow moving components represent considerably less margin for error than the source of most of the uncertainty: net international migration.

The United States' births began moving down around the time of the financial crisis, and has failed to buck that downward trend. In addition, the large population bulge of the baby boom is aging. The first wave born 1946 is turning 78 this year. The peak year, those born in 1957, are turning 67. With births declining and deaths rising, US population growth has become increasingly dependent on net international migration.

Even with net international migration, population growth has generally been declining in the US for the last few decades. In the late 1990s, the resident population in the US was rising 1.2% a year. That's more than double the estimated pace in 2018 and 2019.

In addition to that, population aging driving structural declines in the labor force participation rate has been an important reason that potential GDP growth in the US has decelerated since the late 1990s.

## A wide range of current assessments

The recent debate focuses more on what is underway than longer-run projections for the US population. Because the pace of population growth is fundamental to understanding the structure of the economy and current economic growth, we considered it imperative to weigh the evidence of what is unfolding or risk failing to fully understand an important economic phenomenon. The estimates of population growth for 2022 to 2025 are sufficiently wide to have material implications for understanding recent and near-term growth.

The Census Bureau and the Congressional Budget Office have very different assessments of population growth at the moment.

The Census Bureau estimates that net international migration in 2023 (year ending July 1) was 1.139 million, as we noted above. When the borders closed during Covid, immigration fell sharply. Note that while many readers hear immigration and they think of the southern border, recall that many students come to go to school, and the Covid pandemic limited that flow. Legal visa granting collapsed, as even family reunifications fell. In January 2020, the Department of State recorded legal immigrant visas were running over 500K a month at an annual rate. That fell to 8K in May 2020, a drop of 98%. Immigration was estimated to have been generally declining since 2016. The composition of immigration to the US was changing in the decade before Covid too, with a higher share of immigration from China, and less from Mexico, for example.

The differences rest primarily on the assumptions for a category called "other foreign national," to use the CBO terminology. That mostly reflects immigration across the southern US border. Note that we are using language that hews very closely to that on page 6 of the CBO report. The category CBO calls "other foreign national" one of three categories of net international migration. One category reflects the growth in lawful permanent residents plus people who are eligible to apply (which can include asylees and refugees). The second category represents "nonimmigrants", or individuals admitted under the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), including students and temporary workers. The "other-foreign-national" OFN category consists of people not in the first two categories, and who have not become citizens, or received legal LPR, asylee, or nonimmigrant status. There are some differences across the federal government agencies in the estimation methodology for each category.

## Why isn't this shock seen in other metrics?

Looking at the immigration statistics from the US Department of State and Department of Homeland Security (DHS), we would agree a wide range of estimates are plausible. However, at this juncture we want to consider what other evidence might suggest before turning back toward consideration of the most compelling evidence supporting the high estimates of net internation migration: the surge in apprehensions and encounters being reported along the southern US border.

The magnitude of the CBO projections leads to other questions, some mentioned by Drs. Edelberg and Watson. Changing population growth should affect measures of housing and consumption. CBO is arguing an acute shock is underway, several million extra people in 18 to 24 months. That is a lot for a country where the resident population grew an estimated 1.7 million in 2018 and 1.5 million in 2019. If there were 4 or 5 million added people dropped on the US in two years, that should move some needles, so to speak.

## Our updated population assumptions

In weighing this evidence, a few observations are worth making.

One reason the surge in immigration may be absorbed into the broad array of economic data with so little impact in many series, could be because the level of the population is not as overstated as the recent growth rate might suggest. The population growth slowdown during the pandemic could be sharper than appreciated, and the population growth rebound potentially not putting as much pressure on rental supply, school enrollment, or consumption as a higher level might. We wrote previously the estimates of population growth in the household survey data may be too high for 2020. That could be because of not capturing the sharp decline in immigration adequately but also how when the population estimates are updated in the sample weights, how to distribute the new estimates over time is unclear. Plus, the 2020 decennial census results were incorporated, a change in population estimates that could have occurred anytime between 1990 and the 2020, where some of the results were delayed by the pandemic. This would be consistent with acute wage pressures during the population shortfall being alleviated after the level of population normalized. We wrote about this in 2022, in a piece titled "Where is the labor market tightest?".

We largely agree with the CBO analysis but would feel more comfortable if other data corroborated the magnitude.

Two, there is possible overlap and potential for double counting. Note that in theory someone entering the US unforeseen could then be someone who is apprehended and then could become an asylee. That one additional person entering the US could show up in three of the buckets used for estimating net international migration. Third, the heightened scrutiny of these issues may make assumptions less stable over time; in other words prior relationships may be less reliable for extrapolation. For example, the ratio between observed apprehensions at the southern border and unobserved crossings may change the more attention is paid to the issue. Even the magnitude of the flow might increase observability. CBP agents might observe a higher share of a border flow of 1 million than 200,000. Note, these possibilities are supposition, but worth keeping in mind as part of our attempt to reconcile conflicting evidence.

Third, the transition into legal status could have improved too. The Biden administration has attempted to clear backlogs of Visa applications from the pandemic, and step up the pace of approval of legal visa applications. That could also reduce so called "overstaying" a visa, for example. The administration has tried to speed up asylum claim processing and adjudication. The relative success of those efforts is the subject of debate.

Consider this exercise. Simply take the variation in Census Bureau estimates of net international migration and southwest border encounters reported by CBP and Office of Field Operations (OFO). That regression would give one a negative coefficient of -0.6 for the years 2013 to 2019, if we line up the monthly data to align with the July 1 to June 30 Census estimate basis. If we limit the data to apprehensions in the years pre-Covid the relationship is negative. Now, that fails to account for policy changes underway, but that’s partially the point. These relationships are inherently unstable. If we focus on ratios of reported border apprehensions to net international migration components in 2017 to 2019 we could argue ratios from zero to three could be appropriate. If we look at the ratio of apprehensions to the CBO’s estimates of net change in the “other foreign national category” the ratio would be negative in two of the three years prior to the pandemic. Now, this is not to say the CBO is doing something incorrect, quite the contrary, the work is admirable in its detail and well thought out in its assumptions. The point is the uncertainty.

Compare this to nonfarm payroll employment. In that case, the source data, sometimes called the ES-202 program, is based on establishment reports to state unemployment insurance systems. The insurance systems and their trust funds are paid for by taxes on employers who face fines for misreporting. As a result, incentives for reporting correct employee information are well aligned with researchers’ desire for an accurate count of employment. The system covers over 95% of employees in the US, representing a near Census. Each year, the BLS compares the establishment survey data to the unemployment insurance data, and realigns the level of the survey estimate of nonfarm payroll employment (NFP) in March of each year. In the case of immigration, where a large share is unobserved and undocumented, there is no such source data. There is no regression to run and say "ah-ha, that’s it". The question is how to reconcile the facts we do have.

In their paper evaluating the CBO estimates, Drs. Edelberg and Watson take each component of the “other nonimmigrants” (using their terminology), the source of the estimated surge in net international migration in the CBO estimates. The discussion begins on page 2 of their paper. They start with an estimate of “likely stayers.” “In FY 2023, almost a million people encountered at the border were given a “notice to appear,” meaning they have permission to petition a court for asylum or other immigration relief. Most of these individuals are waiting in the U.S. for the asylum court queue, which has a backlog of over a million cases. In addition, over 800,000 have been granted humanitarian parole,” write the authors. That 1.8 million estimate in FY2023 compares to the CBO estimate of 2 million for the calendar year, a shift of three months. The fiscal year begins in October. The calendar year begins in January.

For the 'not encountered crossing', the authors note the DHS estimate of 600,000 in FY2022. Acknowledging the inherent uncertainty, the authors note that flow staying the same could suggest the CBO estimate of 860,000 could be too high. The authors withhold comment on the overstayers and net calculation, simply noting that the CBO estimates 430,000 people overstayed a temporary visa. CBO estimates 660,000 of the overall category of “other immigrants” emigrated or moved to LPR. We would argue that these estimates also contain uncertainty. Transition to LPR could be faster than assumed, and a significantly larger inflow into the country, could imply a somewhat larger than normal outflow too, a possibility we noted above. Again, however, there is little evidence to bring to bear to add precision over and above the CBO methodology and assumption.

If we try to assess what has unfolded in the household survey data, the Current Population Survey, age 16 and over foreign born population has risen anywhere from 3.9 million over the last two years to 4.6 million. Why the uncertainty? One, it depends on how one corrects for the population adjustments to the sample weights, updated each year, and how that revision is wedged back (or distributed) to the introduction of the 2020 Decennial Census controls. Two, the base effects are painfully volatile. The year over year change in January in the published data series is 1.6 million. In February it is 2.3 million, and the same as December. In any case, these 3.9 to 4.6 million estimates fall short of the CBO's assessment, which would suggest a two year change well over 6 million.

If we take the Edelberg/Watson estimate "likely stayers" (200K below CBO) and the midpoint of FY2022 and CBO estimates of unobserved crossings, that alone would reduce the CBO estimate of net international migration by 370K. Any increase in exit migration, or acceleration in transitions to LPR, and the estimates could easily be 500K lower. Note that Drs Edelberg and Watson did not disagree with the higher CBO estimate. They said it was plausible based on the higher levels of border encounters at the end of the calendar year.

We estimate a net international migration assumption closer to 2.75 to 3 million would better reconcile the observed data than the 3.3 million 2023 pace assumed by the CBO, but we could be wrong. That still may not perfectly align all the facts in the data. However, when we shift to considering the age 16 and over population, we estimate a slightly larger shortfall in population in 2020 than the Census Bureau population estimates suggest. In rescaling the CBO assumptions, we also assume a higher rate of exit migration. We assume lower "likely stayers" and unobserved crossing. Overall we assume 2022 to 2024 annual net internation lower than the CBO assessment by roughly 300K to 400K in 2022 through 2024 in our updated population assumptions underlying our economic projections. In our view that at least moves the level estimates closer to a range that better reconciles the available evidence. We explain further below.

## Impact on labor force statistics and potential output

So what does this mean? First, the foreign born labor force participation rate is about 66.6%. It averaged that over the last 12 months and was 66.6% in February on a seasonally adjusted basis. The published level of the national LFPR the last three months was 62.5%. Adding 1% of 66.6% types to 99% of the 62.5% types would add 0.04 pp to the LFPR. Plus, some new immigrants face delays in transitioning into the workforce. In any case, the differential labor force attachment, to the extent LFPR might be raised 4 bps, we argue is negligible enough to ignore for the analysis below. The primary economic impact is simply the added growth in the population itself.

To a logarithmic approximation, the potential real GDP growth rate is the sum of the growth of potential hours worked (labor supply) and the sum of the growth of output per hours worked (productivity). ## Does this explain the gap between NFP and the unemployment rate?

No, the net international migration estimates do not appear to be able to reconcile the strength of nonfarm payroll employment and the weakness in the household survey and rise in the unemployment rate. As the next graph below shows, the 6-month moving average in household survey employment has been -46K. Over the last six months national employment in the household survey fell -283K. The labor force fell -138K over the same period. That is almost assuredly not a positive labor supply story. The unemployment has risen from a low of 3.4% last April, the unemployment rate has risen to 3.9% in February.

A burst in net international migration we do not think explains the difference between the brisk pace of NFP growth and the rise in the unemployment rate.

While the net international migration could support the growth of NFP. While net international migration may explain some of the gap between NFP and the household survey. One common supposition for example, employers may report employment for tax purposes that appears unaware the documentation status of employees, while in contrast new immigrated individuals may be less likely to participate in a government survey like the household survey. Such an explanation may explain why NFP can run faster than measured household survey employment. The weightings can also differ due to the population estimates used in weighting the household survey sample. However, at the moment, the unemployment rate has risen due to weakness being reported by the survey respondents. It's hard to construct a story for why net international migration would lead to outright declines in measured household survey employment.

## Where is the impact of net international migration showing up?

At this point readers may be wondering, how can this author push back so hard against the estimates and then ultimately agree? Our estimates of growth are closer to the CBO than what is seen in the household survey, but the level is lower too since the gap accumulates, and we expect the negative population growth shock in 2020 was probably larger than estimated.

But one place we think the potential impact can be seen is on nominal wage growth, seen in the differences across groups and occupations. We wrote a note on this two years ago, looking at where the labor market looked tightest. The LFPR of teenagers had posted a rebound not just back to pre-Covid levels, but above pre-GFC levels. The unemployment rate of teens was at the lowest levels since the 1950s. Plus, their wage growth was the most unusually strong. We looked across the labor market and assessed that was one impact of the shortfall in immigration during Covid. We also saw that in the differences in wage gains across different sectors of the economy.

The acute tightness in some parts of the labor market appear to be lessening, and that could be one disinflationary supply side repair resulting from the added net international migration.

What has unfolded since net internation migration has rebounded? The differences in wage gains across a number of sectors. The graph above is just one example. It shows data from the Employment Cost Index (ECI) wages and salaries, comparing the growth in leisure and hospitality, a sector with an estimated higher proportion of immigrants than professional and business services, or using the occupational data, management less obvious in construction, where the convergence has been less in the ECI but has converged to other sectors in the average hourly earnings data.

The estimates of the impact on wages from a surge in immigration is mixed. There are estimates of sizeable downward pressure on the wages of the original workforce. Harvard Professor George Borjas in his assessment of the experience of the Mariel boatlift crisis derives elasticities of between -0.5 and -1.5 on less educated workers. That work and earlier work by David Card spawned numerous papers on the topic, with impacts from large down to zero (see Michael Clemens and Jennifer Hunt, 2019) to potentially positive over time (see Gianmarco Ottaviano and Giovanni Perio, 2006) as general equilibrium benefits spread.<sup>(11)</sup> In the case of easing some of the pandemic supply constraints, the added labor supply does appear to have helped cool wage growth in parts of the labor market that appeared to be the tightest. However, that could be nonlinear, and Covid was an extreme supply shock. The impact on wage conditions going forward could be highly uncertain.

## More workers, a positive supply shock, but not the explanation for everything

To conclude, an acceleration in potential GDP growth in 2023 may have helped the economy expand with less inflationary pressure than the \~3% GDP growth might have previously implied. Not all of the evidence points to an increase in net international migration of the magnitude of the shock CBO estimates. However, it is difficult to ignore something afoot, and after weighing the estimation methodologies of the CBO, the review by Drs. Edelberg and Watson, and the available evidence, we have marked up our assumptions of population growth in our economic projections. Note that these estimates are highly uncertain. We intend to change or alter that assessment as new information arrives and as we continue to do more work on the topic. We will also continue to review what this implies for our detailed forecasts of real GDP and employment going forward.

One case we would argue strongly, the higher estimates of net international migration fail to fully reconcile what is happening in the US employment data. The unemployment rate is rising due to an outright weakening and deterioration being reported by surveyed individuals in the household survey. Weights would not explain why employment is outright declining. In addition, NFP is likely to some extent overstating employment gains at the moment. We would caution against extrapolating too far what the implications of the added net internation migration might imply for how that divergence is reconciled going forward and more broadly, how the economy evolves from here.

The faster population growth should help potential GDP grow faster, lead to a less tight output gap and a labor market that can run a little faster. But we would warn there is a lot else to consider, including what LFPR does going forward, and the limits of what this can explain. In particular, household survey employment has been declining over the last six months. That upward pressure on the unemployment rate does not seem explained by a positive labor supply shock.
