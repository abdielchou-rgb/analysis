New exploitation methods (most notably fracking in the US), the war in Ukraine and strong growth in global gas demand, driven by China and the US, have changed global gas markets structurally

Global gas production and demand for natural gas have been growing faster than other fossil fuels, mainly driven by the US (supply and demand) and China (demand)

Global LNG (liquified natural gas) trade is now more important than inter-regional pipeline gas trade, with global LNG exports being dominated by the US, Qatar and Australia

With the war in Ukraine, mutual sanctions between the EU and Russia and declining domestic gas production (e.g. in the Netherlands), the EU had to diversify its gas imports ➔ higher US LNG imports partly compensated for lower pipeline imports from Russia

Other countries (e.g. in Latin America, Asia, Africa) are likely to ramp up their investment spending in gas exploitation and LNG infrastructure in the future

In 2023, gas consumption in Europe was 19% below the 2021 level ➔ lower industrial gas demand, higher energy efficiency, fuel switch, mild temperatures; risk of shortages in gas supply very low

Total Russian gas exports fell by 43% between 2021 and 2023 as the EU was the major consumer of Russian pipeline gas. However, Russian LNG exports to China (and others) are on the rise ➔ the energy/gas collaboration between China and Russia is very likely to become closer

Gas prices in Europe remain elevated compared to pre-war levels and compared to the US ➔ structurally lower gas prices will increase the competitiveness of energy-intensive sectors in the US

Global gas production has more than doubled since 1990

Natural gas production, trillion cubic metres

![](images/a147dda4ac0c0bbdfe1f9871234635cb6e98f248c2b5f6b6b23310a307fa0e49.jpg)
Source: Energy Institute

With an average annual growth rate of 1.9%, natural gas was the fastest growing fossil fuel during the last 10 years (oil: +1.1%; coal: +1% p.a.)

Steady growth except for years of crisis (e.g. global financial crisis in 2009 or COVID-19 in 2020)

New extraction methods (fracking) have boosted gas production in North America (+3.9% p.a. between 2013 and 2023)

<sup>▪</sup> Middle East (+2.4% p.a.) and Asia Pacific (+3%) have also increased local gas production

<sup>▪</sup> Europe has lost ground (-1.8% p.a.); peak European gas production was back in 2004

## US is the largest gas producer (with increasing production) – Europe is falling behind

United States by far the largest gas producer

Natural gas production, trillion cubic metres

![](images/19b5f43d41f70d3b512100b949b4df2d70740e9d4f9d58729fbd6c1060ae6af2.jpg)
Source: Energy Institute

In the last 30 years, the US more than doubled its gas production; acceleration during the last ten years (fracking) with an average annual growth rate of 4.7%

Mainly driven by LNG exports to Europe and Asia, the Middle East has become the second largest gas producer

<sup>▪</sup> Russian production has slumped since the war in Ukraine (-16.4% vs. 2021)

China has increased gas production to satisfy domestic energy demand (+6.8% p.a. between 2013 and 2023) and is likely to expand its capacities further

European gas production likely to decline further (large gas fields exhausted, lack of political support for new projects) despite high domestic demand

## US in the lead, while Europe and Russia (for the moment) have become less important

Russia and Europe are losing market share

![](images/23d2adc4130250b398f826c667b3c1f759e08e681e910ed3e11410175f70c242.jpg)
Source: Energy Institute

The gap between US and Russian market share has widened during the last 15 years

However, Russia has the highest proved gas reserves in the world and potential (new) clients with enormous energy demand (China, India, other Asian countries) ➔ a recovery of Russian market share cannot be ruled out in the medium term

Middle East market share has remained quite stable during the last few years with Iran accounting for 6.2% of the total share

Europe’s market share dropped from 17% in the late 1970s to just 5% in 2023; Norway (2.9% global market share) is by far the largest European producer

## Global gas consumption has doubled since 1990 – Asia Pacific registered an increase of more than 500%

Asia drives global gas demand
![](images/a38c1e3b71eacdc05e27757a16778bfba46cee18aa1fc6e9479cc42759fe7355.jpg)
Source: Energy Institute

With a wide range of applications across sectors and a pivotal role in the energy transition, global gas demand doubled in the last 30 years (+2.3% p.a.)

Gas was the third largest energy source behind oil and coal with a share of 23% in total primary energy demand in 2023

The main driver has been Asia Pacific, where demand has increased by more than 500% since 1990

Global gas consumption is likely to increase further (roughly +1% p.a.) since gas is an energy multi-talent (can be used for power generation, heating market, industrial processes) and has a lower CO intensity than coal

In countries such as Germany, gas is supposed to be a bridge technology on the way to a climate-friendly future

Natural gas consumption, trillion cubic metres

## While US and European gas consumption was equal in 2008, US demand is now 90% higher than in Europe

Gas boom in China and the US

![](images/8c1bef12340189b799dc56b9e38b6e423bf1a9983325b7a39552b8056a6e9781.jpg)
Source: Energy Institute

With China’s closer integration into global industrial value chains after accession to the WTO in late 2001, gas demand started to increase massively (+13% p.a. since then); industry, power sector and heating will drive consumption in the future

Gas boom in the US during the last ten years (+2.3% p.a. between 2013 and 2023); gas replaced coal in the power sector and now accounts for 43% of total power generation; the US tries to attract gasintensive industries (from Europe)

In 2023, gas consumption in Europe was 19.1% lower compared to 2021 due to structurally lower industrial demand, higher energy efficiency, fuel switch as well as mild temperatures; German is the largest gas consumer in Europe, ahead of Italy

# Despite rising production and consumption, proved reserves of natural gas have increased

No lack of gas in sight

Proved reserves of natural gas, trillion cubic metres

![](images/95fc24d199e91a84805a0764ff73801b58e0ef0946a066b9004527108f48a9b8.jpg)
Source: Energy Institute

Rising gas prices and better and cheaper exploitation methods have led to an increase in global proved reserves

<sup>▪</sup> 40% of total (current) proved reserves are in Middle Eastern countries

<sup>▪</sup> With 20%, Russia is the country with the largest share of (current) proved reserves

Compared to the share in global gas production, the share of North America in proved reserves (8%) is relatively small

<sup>▪</sup> Europe’s share of proved reserves (1.7%) is negligible

Strong growth of proved reserves in China (more exploration projects)

## LNG trade has overtaken pipeline gas trade due to technological progress and geopolitical tensions

LNG trade now more important than pipeline gas trade

![](images/324da71709175219562cae04f2bae0ab485e59c99a18494a3e383357ccc453e2.jpg)
\* LNG exports include re-exports. Pipeline trade excludes intra-regional trade
Source: Energy Institute

LNG trade has registered an average annual growth rate of 5.3% since 2013 and is now more important than inter-regional pipeline trade

Investment in export infrastructure in the US, higher efficiency and cost reductions in liquification and transportation have been major drivers on the supply side

Geopolitical tensions and lower ${ \mathsf { C O } } _ { 2 }$ intensity compared to coal have supported the general trend of higher gas demand

European decoupling of Russian pipeline gas was the main reason for the decline in total inter-regional pipeline trade (2023 vs. 2021: -23%)

<sup>▪</sup> Global LNG trade is expected to continue to grow strongly

Russian pipeline exports have dropped significantly

![](images/37de49a2f4d0ef33c03811f6ad1b11e2a346344eb6d30baf0843870f20118968.jpg)
\* LNG exports include re-exports
Source: Energy Institute

<sup>▪</sup> Total Russian gas exports fell by 43% between 2021 and 2023 as the EU was a major consumer of Russian pipeline gas

Pipeline exports from Russia to Europe had already declined by 12.5% in 2020, largely due to reduced industrial demand during the pandemic

Russia compensated for lower pipeline gas exports partly with higher LNG exports (+176% compared to 2017)

Gas collaboration between Russia and China (and other new customers) is likely to become closer

<sup>▪</sup> Strong focus of Middle Eastern countries on LNG exports allows switch between clients

## US, Qatar and Australia account for 60% of global LNG exports

Three countries dominate global LNG exports

![](images/0700c316e3c0a93b460f9164ae85b79637034a98480b7277593417b2310f325b.jpg)
Source: Energy Institute

<sup>▪</sup> Qatar has the longest tradition as an exporter of LNG

With the fracking boom, the US has become the largest player in global LNG markets in just a few years

The rise in Australian LNG exports was mainly driven by Chinese demand, but mutual trade relations have cooled down

Russia had already started to expand LNG exports before the war in Ukraine

Other countries (e.g. in Latin America, Asia, Africa) are likely to ramp up their investment spending in gas exploitation and LNG infrastructure in the future

## Europe still the largest gas importer, but China is catching up – LNG is becoming more important

Europe replaced Russian pipeline gas with more LNG imports

![](images/468af0e98424654a77ae4cad7c5aa2d4c3cfb3f86ffa3156fbe5676e73fa3520.jpg)
Source: Energy Institute

Massive switch away from Russian pipeline gas imports to more LNG imports (mainly from the US) in Europe; absolute European gas imports were 18% lower in 2023 compared to 2021 – return to former highs seems unlikely

For the first time ever, total Chinese gas imports declined in 2022 (weak economy due to COVID-19 and high gas prices due to additional European LNG demand)

Still, Chinese gas imports were ten times higher in 2023 than in 2010 and will likely continue to rise

<sup>▪</sup> Japan and Korea will likely remain large consumers of LNG

<sup>▪</sup> Total US gas imports now less important

## Europe, China and Japan are the largest LNG importers, but ever more countries will ask for LNG

Europe and Asia dependent on LNG imports

![](images/143b21d492003bbf06263631b612dbe9ec5b1d6db4f5c50c8660fb652bcc6286.jpg)
Source: Energy Institute

Europe has become the largest LNG importer in just a few years; Europe aims for more diversification of LNG supply

By paying very high gas prices in 2022, Europe crowded out LNG demand from China and other (Asian) markets

China is likely to become the largest importer of LNG in the medium term (GDP growth, substitute for coal); Russia will likely deliver more LNG to China

Japanese LNG imports rose after Fukushima (2011) but have trended downwards since; a sustainable recovery seems unlikely given low GDP growth and a renaissance of nuclear power

<sup>▪</sup> India is likely to overtake South Korea as an LNG importing country soon

## Europe and OECD Asia have a structural disadvantage compared to the US regarding gas prices

Gas prices in Europe and Asia above their pre-war levels

Natural gas prices, annual averages, USD per million Btu\*

European gas prices skyrocketed in 2022, when Europe was willing to pay very high prices for LNG to avoid gas shortages ➔ impact on prices in Asia and the US
