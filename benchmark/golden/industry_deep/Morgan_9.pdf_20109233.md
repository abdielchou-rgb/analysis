# FX & EM Fixed Income Strategy: A Weaker USD Is Not Sustainable

James Lord

July has so far seen a weaker USD across the board. We don’t believe it is sustainable. The weakness of US data is clear, but so are the economic slowdown and soft inflation data elsewhere in the world. Expectations of policy easing outside the US should increase, offsetting Fed rate cut expectations, while a global slowdown is not typically USD-supportive – at least not for EM. As such, we feel more comfortable recommending receivers and long duration trade ideas relative to long EM FX. In fact, this week we add to long USD and EM duration positions. Below we also update our CEEMEA FX/rates trades.

## Is a weaker USD sustainable?

Global currencies have all had a pretty good run against USD so far in July, with USD one of the weakest currencies in the world. EMs in particular are outperforming, though GBP, JPY and AUD all feature in the top ten returning currencies month to date. Only NOK has weakened versus USD.

US data have been unquestionably soft, across both prices and real activity, and a 25bp cut from the FOMC is now fully priced for September. 10-year UST yields continue to fall. Notably, USD has weakened even as the market has judged a rising probability of a Republican win in November ( Exhibit 3 ), something that ought to put upward pressure on USD, at least according to our view on the effect Republican policies would likely have.

Investors have clearly put more emphasis on the softer US data, perhaps in part because we are some months away still from the US election. If the election were closer, we suspect that the rising probability of a Republican win in November may have had a stronger market impact.

However, we remain sceptical about the durability of a weaker USD. Growth and inflation trends in the major economies of the rest of the world hardly point to a strengthening of currencies versus USD either, while the impact of US election risk on markets should only grow as the date draws closer.

This suggest to us that a global move lower in bond yields is likely more sustainable than the move lower in USD.

In EM in particular, we continue to think that the better trade is to position for lower yields rather than stronger currencies.

It's clear that global growth is slowing: Economic surprise indices have been trending lower in unison since April. Most recent data from China showing a weak credit impulse, weak imports, continued deflationary pressures and a pullback in PMIs paint a weak domestic demand picture. 2Q GDP data released this week came in well below expectations at 4.7%Y, but it's the Q/Q SAAR figure of 2.8% that really shows just how weak the momentum currently is. Rate cuts are likely and we see more USD/CNH upside.

This week's ECB meeting may be fairly uneventful, but some dovish commentary could come on the back of growth tracking weaker in 2Q versus ECB expectations (Morgan Stanley tracking at 0.2%Q versus 0.4%Q expected by the ECB).

With activity data also suggesting a slowing US economy, global growth is decelerating, which is not typically an environment where USD weakens meaningfully. Investors should be cautious about assuming that a more dovish Fed alone will be enough to weaken USD.

Global inflation is also soft: US and EM core CPI continue their march lower, with US data turning sharply in the past few months. Fed commentary has turned more dovish, but the same should be the case over time in the rest of the world, where inflation is generally heading down as well. Across EM, core inflation readings for June have been soft, maintaining recent trends.

These global inflation trends, combined with recent USD and UST price signals, should help to give central banks space to adjust their commentary in a more dovish direction.

More dovish central banks outside the US should help to prevent rate differentials from moving in a USD-supportive direction for much longer.

The prospect of easier monetary policy may well take the edge off the growth weakness for markets, as investors should start to assume that any growth weakness will be moderate and short-lived. This could support risk appetite and risk-sensitive currencies. But for a sustained weakening in USD, we need rate differentials to move in a weaker USD direction. This is not guaranteed, and in fact we see UST-Bund yields moving in a USDsupportive direction.

US election risks for USD: Macro trends should increasingly be punctuated by US election risk. With former President Trump's chances of winning the 2024 presidential election in November around 65% according to betting markets ( Exhibit 3 ), perceptions of improved chances of another Trump term could cause investors to anticipate policy choices that have historically put upward pressure on USD, at least for a time, and encourage them to position for a steeper US curve (with more term premia in the back end).

So far, this trend has not been met with a stronger USD, likely because US data have had a bigger impact. But this does not mean that US election risk has not had any impact. We believe that USD could have been even weaker if the Democratic Party's chances were improving, for example.

The US election poses upside and downside risks for USD, then. Should former President Trump's chances of winning the presidential election in November fall for any reason, trades speculating on a Republican win may come under pressure, and could result in a lower USD. Should the Democratic Party's nominee change and polls become closer, for example, then the knee-jerk reaction in markets could be for a weaker USD, though for how long is unclear ( Exhibit 4 ).

UST yields would also likely move lower in this scenario, though, supporting our expectation for lower yields for EM local currency bonds.

Conversely, as the election draws closer, markets will likely place an increasing weight on its outcome and proportionally less weight on incoming macro data, at least for a while. Should polling trends remain unchanged, then this should support USD as investors start to anticipate and price in a Republican victory.

## Updating CEEMEA macro trades

South Africa – lowering our SAGB yield target: We published our mid-year outlook just after the South African elections and decided to enter a long position in the SAGB 2035 (see Global EM Fixed Income Mid-Year Outlook: The United States of EM, May 31, 2024). We've had a notable rally across the curve since then, a strong flattening trend and an outperformance of bonds relative to swaps. The SAGB 2035 hit out 11% target and we've decided to lower the target by another 50bp to 10.5%. Factors we are considering:

• The SARB should set up a September cut: We doubt that the SARB will be able to cut at the July 18 meeting, but a split vote with some members voting for a cut would be a good signal of a September cut.

• South Africa's growth story is improving, with load-shedding in the rear-view mirror, Transnet issues likely troughing and upside potential from a reformed visa regime. This should help to keep a lid on concerns about debt sustainability and thus risk premia in bonds.

• Discussions about a fiscal rule and a 3%Y inflation target should keep SAGBs in demand: Timing of the announcement and implementation of these two linked reforms is unclear, but the momentum and direction of travel are clear – these changes look likely to be made before end-2025. Discussion in this direction over the coming months should help to flatten the yield curve.

• The IRS curve remains steep: South Africa's curve remains one of the steepest in EM. Our excess term premia model places the fair value of 2s10s ZAR IRS at around 45bp, compared to the current level of 127bp. The model doesn’t take into account the issuance strategy of National Treasury which keeps the SAGB curve steep, which affects the IRS curve too. Yet we believe that there is room for the curve to flatten some more back to the July 2023 lows around 100bp.

• SAGB valuations are still attractive: At around 185bp, the 10-year ASW is around 10bp from our 175bp target, yet a move down to the 2023 low around 150bp is possible. Hence, another 50bp rally in SAGB 2035 is achievable.

Our main concerns are around technicals, with the real money community already overweight SAGBs relative to the benchmark to the tune of 2.5pp, with the gradual increase of India's weight in the benchmark pushing South Africa's neutral index weight down over the coming months as well. GBI-EM-benchmarked investors have been overweight by as much as 3.5pp in the past, but current positioning is above the long-run average. Local asset managers have scope to reduce offshore holdings of assets and repatriate, but our onshore team expects to see bias towards higher equity allocations rather than fixed income.

CBT Governor Karahan reinforced the hawkish message last week, which should help to push back against rate cut expectations, which remain substantial. We're not suggesting that a policy response is coming, just simply that investors should consider non-monetary options too.

We continue to see TRY outperforming the forwards, with USD/TRY ending the year at 36 versus 39 priced in the forwards.

Domestic central banks have had to raise interest rates substantially. Externally, the hits have come from wider credit spreads and higher UST yields leaving new debt more costly. ## 1) Fiscals improving or should improve, not priced: Argentina, Ecuador, Costa Rica, Uruguay.

• Argentina is clearly undergoing a huge shock fiscal adjustment, largely coming from the spending side. The results are coming through strongly, with Argentina likely to have run a nominal surplus in 1H24 once June data are released. Importantly, the passage of the omnibus and fiscal bill made some of the fiscal measures more permanent (see Argentina: Back on Track, June 14, 2024). While we don’t expect the government to fully deliver on a nominal fiscal surplus for the year as a whole, if it reaches a primary surplus of 0.7% as expected by our economist, this is still very positive and a huge change. That said, we also think that a lot of this is why bonds have rallied already and is mostly priced in. As fiscal results keep delivering, markets should trade positively. However, the more important driver now is the management of the FX, which is more difficult given the competing narratives of FX accumulation and inflation reduction. Given that we believe the government is still on track to make further progress on all

imbalances, we also still like the bonds.

• Ecuador had a very bad fiscal year in 2023, ending at a 3.6% deficit. So far 2024 is showing a reversal, with data coming in much better on a year-on-year basis. Oil revenues are ok but in fact not the main driver, with transfers instead the largest driver, which is likely due the lower subsidy payments. The IMF anticipates that Ecuador can consolidate to a 2% deficit this year, which seems optimistic considering that this is a period less than one year away from the next elections (see Ecuador: IMF Staff Report – Supportive but Also Optimistic, June 2, 2024). Yet getting closer to this would be positive and reduce funding needs, especially given that funding needs this year are already covered via multilaterals. If it looks like Ecuador can fully deliver on fiscals while President Noboa's popularity remains high, spreads should tighten. Yet we do not have high enough conviction on this to suggest a like stance.

• Uruguay's fiscals should improve versus 2023 throughout the remainder of the year, helped by the base effect of weaker fiscals on the back of its 2023 drought, which negatively impacted both revenues and expenditures. Despite elections in October, the finance ministry has been adamant about standing by its fiscal rule this year. We see its fiscal rule as helping to reduce concerns about fiscal slippage by the next administration. We do not see this as priced and expect Uruguay to outperform.

• Costa Rica is seeing weakness in its fiscal balances, compared with the past two years, when it registered an impressive fiscal consolidation with primary surpluses each year. Still, its fiscal balance, both nominal and primary balances and tax revenue remain elevated compared with most pre-pandemic years. The weakness year to date has mostly been driven by lower revenue compared with this point last year from weak income tax revenues, in part due to subdued economic activity. We see this as enough to remove our like stance and turn to a neutral view on the credit.

## 2) Fiscals improving, priced: The Dominican Republic.

• The Dominican Republic's improved fiscals are driven by improvements in revenues via higher income taxes, but also with some improvements in subsidy expenditure. We would expect this improvement to continue now that elections are out of the way, helped further in the medium term by the fiscal reform that the administration intends to pass this year (see Dominican Republic Sovereign Credit Strategy: Trip Notes: Gearing Up, June 7, 2024). We see the passage of this fiscal reform as priced given the administration's high popularity and the supermajority it will have in Congress beginning in August.

## 3) Fiscals worsening and not entirely priced: Peru, Chile, Brazil and Mexico.

• In Chile, weaker revenue is largely due to lithium prices, which were very high in 1H23. This underperformance has been driven by revenues coming in weak versus recent years. Going forward, we are expecting fiscals to improve, ending the year with a deficit of 2.3% of GDP, wider than the administration's budgeted expectation of 1.9% of GDP. We expect revenues to improve from higher copper prices in the coming months, and for expenditure to remain under control. Chile's 2Q24 public finance report should be released in the coming weeks, which should provide

additional colour on the fiscal slippage year to date.

• In Peru, much of the reason for lagging revenue is that the weak growth in 2023 resulted in weakness in tax revenue being paid on income from last year. Peru's finance ministry has pushed to change its fiscal rule multiple times already, now aiming for a deficit of 2.8% of GDP this year, compared with 2.0% of GDP originally. Despite signs of growth improving, President Boluarte is requesting the approval of S/3.0 billion (near US\$0.8 billion, or 0.3% of GDP) for economic reactivation programmes. This is on top of continued risks associated with Petroperú, which we see as an added layer of risks to fiscals that should remain in the medium term. Weakening fiscals alongside rich levels are key to our dislike stance.

• In Brazil, the market assumption was always that the government's fiscal targets were too ambitious and wouldn’t be met. Despite this, the market has reacted negatively to the fiscal uncertainty. In part, this is due to the longer-term targets for 2026-28 being adjusted wider, yet the fiscal data for this year have also been weak. The best case scenario sees one-offs come out and front-loaded expenditures help 2H24 to end with a primary deficit of around 0.5-0.6%. Given that this would be an improvement from 2023, it would be positive. However, this assumes additional fiscal measures actually being approved, which leaves risks to a wider deficit. At this point, both the IMF and Morgan Stanley assume that the primary deficit stays wide at around 0.5-0.7% in the coming two years. Without a more substantial improvement, we agree with our fiscal risk premia model which currently flags Brazil credit spreads as around 30bp rich. We also think that it means rating upgrades are unlikely at this point given that debt is unlikely to stabilise in the coming years.

• In Mexico, the 2024 budget back in September 2023 made it clear that fiscals would weaken materially and so far data are showing this. At this point, fiscal risk depends entirely on the ability of the new government to convince the market that 2025 will show a consolidation path, with the 2025 budget on November 15 thus key. If the deficit projection is around 4% and assumptions of where spending cuts are coming from are realistic, we think that the market will react positively, as it would still be a year-on-year consolidation of around 1.3pp of GDP. Then it will be down to fiscal results in 1H25 to see whether it's being implemented. For now our economist does see it as feasible. In terms of credit spreads, we see the fiscal risk as manageable given the assumptions above, yet we think that spreads should be wider on the back of the risk to the broader reforms likely being implemented in September. We think that markets should be pricing around 1-1.5 downgrades compared to 0.8 today.

## 4) Fiscals worsening and priced: Panama, Colombia and El Salvador.

• In Panama, data look like they are improving compared with last year, although from a weak base. The year-to-date deficit through June is now at 2.1% of GDP, which would mean that the remainder of the year would have to register a surplus of 0.1pp of GDP in order to make up for it. New Finance Minister Chapman has been vocal about his intention to meet this rule this year, which most see as not feasible. The prior finance ministry noted the likelihood of revenues slowing in the remainder of the year and even recommended the incoming administration

implement a spending freeze to limit this. We would expect the new administration to adjust the country's fiscal deficit targets alongside his presentation of his new government plan in the autumn (see Panama Sovereign Credit Strategy: Trip Notes: Balancing on a Tightrope, June 6, 2024).

• In Colombia, we have long been bearish on fiscals and the recent medium-term fiscal plan showed just how difficult the outlook is (see Colombia: 2024 Medium Term Fiscal Plan: A First Look, June 16, 2024). In short, we still see risks to wider fiscals compared to the authorities' new 5.6% deficit target due to still optimistic revenue assumptions and also difficulties in delivering all the planned spending cuts. Despite this existing bearishness, the April results were even weaker than anticipated, coming in at a substantial monthly nominal deficit of 1.2% of GDP, especially when compared to the 0.4% surplus in April 2023. Higher primary spending, including discretionary transfers, was the key driver but with tax revenues also weak. This leaves the year-to-date deficit at 2.4% compared to 0.5% by April 2023. Colombia has performed well recently, justifying the removal of our dislike stance that was predicated on fiscal risks being priced in, yet April's data point is worrying and we need to watch next month's fiscal data now.

• In El Salvador, the market is waiting for a further fiscal adjustment to take place, the so called "bitter medicine" noted by the president. We agree with the IMF that fiscals do need to adjust another 2-3pp of GDP. So far this is not happening, with year-to-date fiscals coming in slightly weaker than last year. On a worrying note, spending is up around 9%Y. Yet at least this is so far being offset by revenues also increasing at a similar pace. Capex is running at around 4% of GDP, higher than prior years, so if productively used this could also be positive. All in, we are not worried about fiscals yet, particularly given that funding needs are fine for the next 12 months given the recent external issuance. However, for markets to tighten more materially from here, the "bitter medicine" needs to happen. For now, we still see spreads as cheap enough to incorporate the risks of this not happening and we like El Salvador, both via ELSALV 2032 and the interest-only note.

## CEEMEA fiscals

## 1) Fiscals improving or should improve, not priced: Morocco and Hungary.

• Morocco has seen an improvement in its budget balance from relatively wide levels in 2024, but the market is paying little attention to the better out-turn, in our view. While the extent to which fiscals can improve is limited given larger capital expenditure ambitions by the government – aimed at removing bottlenecks to growth in the economy – continued better fiscal readings should help to anchor spreads and also keep the market focused on an eventual return to investment grade in the medium term, in our view. Certain areas of the Morocco complex, including the USD belly and the OCPMR long end, are not pricing this in yet, in our view. However, EUR looks comparatively tight now.

• Following weak fiscal data out of Hungary in the first few months of 2024, there are tentative signs that fiscals are improving and set to improve further in 2H24, where there is a window to consolidate prior to a spending re-acceleration from 2H25 onwards. The latest taxation announcement is an example of this and should allow the sovereign to record better fiscal readings into 2H, increasing the chance of meeting the deficit target. The 2025 budget may also show more expenditure restraint. Most importantly for credit, issuance is likely to come down substantially, and private placements/Samurai bonds could tide the issuer through until 2025 without a need for a revised FX borrowing plan. Upside surprises on fiscals are not fully priced into spreads yet, in our view.

## 2) Fiscals improving but priced: South Africa.

• South Africa's fiscal performance has been remarkable, seeing a 0.6% primary surplus in an election year. The driver of this has been corporate income tax and VAT receipts outperforming, while on the expenditure side a more prudent debtmanagement strategy delivered some cost savings. Our economist continues to expect fiscal deficits better than the consensus as we are more constructive on commodity prices and growth. The numbers are admittedly lower than National Treasury's as we see greater support needed for Transnet. Bond spreads have outperformed peers particularly post-elections, and the fiscal improvements have helped the market to get more comfortable with the reform agenda.

## 3) Fiscals worsening and not entirely priced: Angola.

• In Angola, preliminary fiscal performance worsened unexpectedly in 1Q24 with the deficit wider at AOA 318 billion versus a year ago. While oil revenue was up due to higher production and prices, current expenditure (including subsidies) was up in year-on-year terms. The officials and the IMF still see a path towards a budget surplus of 2.7% of GDP this year but this would require a reduction in recurrent expenditure and for oil prices to hold up. We are not yet worried about expenditure overruns as there was a deficit in 1Q23, but Angola managed to turn this around in the subsequent quarters to register a small full-year deficit.

## 4) Fiscals worsening and priced: Kenya, Turkey, Zambia, Romania, Israel and Saudi Arabia.

• In Kenya, the withdrawal of the Finance Bill will likely result in a wider fiscal deficit in FY25 relative to the IMF's assumption of 3.5% of GDP. The bill was meant to introduce a raft of new measures to deliver a revenue-based fiscal adjustment which would have seen a consistent decline in the debt/GDP profile. The authorities have agreed to reduce spending by US\$1.39 billion and borrow the rest (US\$1.31 billion) to cover the deficit. The combination of a larger borrowing requirement and high real interest rates will likely ensure that debt/GDP will keep growing beyond the 70% forecast by the IMF. We think that these risks are priced in as the 10-year and 30-year part of the KENINT curve are pricing in 1.7 and 1.4 downgrades, respectively. The risk to wider deficits now emanates from the implications of the cabinet reshuffle, where any delays in appointing the new government could act as a hindrance to much-needed expenditure reforms. On the upside, further concessional financing from the IMF and/or World Bank would help to reduce the need to borrow via expensive commercial financing (eurobonds or syndicated loans).

• Turkey's cash deficit widened significantly in June, coming in at TRY 431 billion on the month. As a result, the 12-month trailing cash deficit widened by TRY 224 billion, reaching TRY 1,176 billion. This implies a 0.5pp widening as share of GDP to

3.4% from 2.9% last month. This was largely driven by the increase in the primary cash deficit (+TRY 150 billion month on month) as primary expenditures increased by 85%Y. The increase in interest expenses was quite dramatic at 347%Y, reflecting the normalisation in rates and an increased borrowing requirement since last year. Revenues also posted a strong increase, driven by high inflation and resilient activity (88%Y). With this print, the gap between the headline budget deficit and cash deficit narrowed (4.8% headline in May versus 3.4% cash deficit in June). We think that the deterioration in the deficit is largely expected as this is a consequence of the economic rebalancing which has resulted in higher interest costs. Commitment to fiscal adjustments is still there given the recent decision to skip the June minimum wage hike which would have added to recurrent expenditure and put upwards pressure on inflation.

• In our view, risks to Zambia's fiscals are skewed to the downside owing to the ongoing drought. Lower growth and greater borrowing will likely be a headwind, as was shown in the supplementary budget. Additional spending is now expected to be ZMW 42 billion higher, financed with ZMW 17 billion more in domestic and external borrowing. Additional external financing will likely come from donor aid and the World Bank. The IMF projections contained in the Third Review see the deficit at 6.1% of GDP – unchanged from the Second Review given the expected reduction in recurrent expenditure to compensate. The relatively constructive outlook from the IMF sees us removing our dislike stance as this offers some upside to the 2053 bond (see Bringing Zambia and South Africa Closer to Home, July 12, 2024).

• Romania is perhaps the credit where fiscal consolidation expectations were lower, and deficit estimates for 1H24 sit at 3.6% of GDP already. The dual elections later this year mean that there is little chance of a turnaround in the fiscal trajectory this year. Consolidation will likely take until 2025, and recent newsflow suggests that numerous measures are already being discussed for the post-election period, including a potential adjustment to income taxation or VAT. As long as investors keep their conviction in post-election action on fiscals, we think that existing longs are unlikely to be cut substantially.

• In Israel, we think that budget underperformance is well understood by investors, with expenditures year to date already 3.4% of GDP higher than a year ago, mostly due to defence spending. Our economists maintain a 8.5% of GDP budget deficit forecast for 2024 and we think it is unlikely that the sovereign will be able to outperform against this forecast, creating high funding needs. While the US\$8.0 billion eurobond transaction helped to alleviate the need for larger external issuance over the past months, we think that further external issuance – via private placements or another eurobond deal – is likely in 2H24.

• Previously we were of the view that Saudi Arabia spreads did not accurately reflect the higher funding needs that resulted from fiscal deficits, both on-budget but also in the PIF. As we outline below, however, spreads have now underperformed following a rush of supply out of the complex, suggesting that the market is once again paying more attention to the funding picture. Hence, we think that there is some discount priced for fiscals but not enough to make us outright bullish on Saudi bonds.

Investors should recall that a similar print in 2Q23 caused the market sell-off and policy easing. We shall watch if the PBOC would be the first one to ease by cutting rates should data be soft.

• FOMC (July 31): Our economists don’t think that the Fed will cut rates at this meeting, but they think the Fed will not push back against the market pricing of a rate cut. This would be an indication of the Fed being comfortable about a September cut.

• US Democratic National Convention (August 19-22): This event could be more important than the FOMC itself given investors' focus on the US election.

• Jackson Hole Economic Symposium (August 22-24): This should serve as the last event for the Fed to hint at a September rate cut, assuming the jobs data and inflation path are consistent with our economists' forecast.

The weak US CPI print last week triggered some appreciation of Asia FX but more than half of the rally was already reversed last Friday. Our assessment is that events ahead could still pose downside risks to Asia FX, particularly if China data surprise the market on the downside while the Fed only delivers what is in the price in the market. The US election remains a wild card and tariff risk is real into the election, in our opinion.

Hence, we would like to hold long USD exposure via long USD/THB and long USD/PHP. We would like to add long USD/MYR 3m NDF and the recent MYR outperformance provides a good entry level. The outperformance has been driven by BNM's hawkish stance and the government's effort to encourage government-related companies to convert their FX into MYR.

However, we don’t think that BNM's hawkishness is different from other central banks in Asia, except BSP, which is more dovish. The effort to encourage FX conversion happened in March but at that time MYR was underperforming. Overall, we believe that MYR should continue to be a proxy for CNY weakness, especially should Chinese equities start to underperform again. We set the target at 4.80 with a stop at 4.57.

Our long USD exposure should provide a good hedge against our carry positions such as short CNH/INR and short TWD/INR. We also hold long USD/CNH 6m 7.40/7.60 call spread.

In rates, we continue to recommend pay 1-year HKD versus USD, pay 5-year SGD versus USD and long 10-year G-Secs with FX hedge. We would like to add a 1s5s THB steepener. 1s5s has flattened in recent weeks and the current level is close to the flattest in history. We don’t think that the curve would be inverted, especially when the bond curve still has a positive slope.

The curve could steepen should: 1) The BoT turn more dovish; 2) The US curve steepen in line with our US colleagues' view; or 3) Fiscal concerns rise again. We set the target at +20bp with a stop at -10bp.

## Korea: What might be a fair market pricing for rate cuts?

Bottom line: Given the BoK's pushback against market expectations of rate cuts, there could be room for market pricing of cuts to reduce. But we think that the room is modest at \~10-15bp, given the backdrop of data supporting a Fed rate cut and the BoK similarly moving closer towards a cut. Korea rates could underperform US rates but we see better opportunities to express this elsewhere, e.g., in Singapore and Hong Kong.

Last week's BoK meeting tried to send a balanced message, with the central bank signalling that it is moving closer to a rate cut but also highlighting a greater focus on financial stability given the renewed rise in house prices (see BoK Review: Not So Fast, July 11, 2024, for our full takeaways).

Market pricing going into the BoK: Of note, Governor Rhee highlighted during the press conference that market rates have eased more than desired by the BoK and market expectations for BoK rate cuts are somewhat excessive. Ahead of the BoK meeting, markets were pricing in close to 60bp of rate cuts for the BoK by end-2025. Given that the BoK is thinking about a rate cut but a pricing of \~60bp of cuts is deemed to be somewhat excessive, it suggests that market pricing of 1-2 rate cuts may be a more acceptable range.

Current market pricing... BoK rate cut pricing reduced post-meeting, but this was subsequently reversed by the lower-than-expected US inflation print. Market pricing is now back at pre-BoK levels, with the first cut priced for November 2024 and another 1.5 cuts priced for next year, i.e., a cumulative \~57bp of cuts priced by end-2025.

There could be room for markets to price out \~10-15bp of rate cuts, in our view. This would be closer to what markets are pricing in for other Asian central banks, including the BoT, which market participants expect would be one of the first to cut rates in Asia ( Exhibit 43 ). It would also bring market pricing to be within a more acceptable range for the BoK.
