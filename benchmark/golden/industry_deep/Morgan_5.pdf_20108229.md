## Can’t buy me love: A vibe check on G-4 consumers

• Improving consumer confidence should help ensure resilient final demand in 2024...

• …but the completeness of confidence<sup>’</sup>s recovery varies by economy

Modelling G-4 confidence reveals asymmetric inflation and income impacts (people hate inflation more)...

• ...and a consumer “malaise<sup>”</sup> that helps explain Western Europe spending weakness though not US strength

We have long held that the final demand supports that ensured above-trend 2023 growth would promote resilience in 2024. Strength in consumer spending is to spur improvements in regional and sectoral weak-spots (namely Western Europe, Japan, and the manufacturing sector) amid moderations in the US and China. We are seeing tentative signs of such a rotation taking hold. Both Euro area and UK GDP surprised to the upside in 1Q23, based on strength from household spending, and signals from our Capex nowcaster and the PMIs suggest improving manufacturing momentum. And while US growth moderated last quarter, underlying final demand growth remains robust.

Further supporting this conviction are recent gains in consumer confidence (Figure 1). However, the improvement in confidence has run well behind the recovery in macroeconomic fundamentals—an outturn that some refer to as the “vibecession<sup>”</sup> (depressed confidence despite improving economy). In this report, we estimate a strong relationship between confidence and inflation, nominal income, labor market outturns, and stock market returns. These four variables alone explain 50-80% of consumer confidence across the the major G-4 economies (US, Euro area, UK, and Japan).

Despite the recoveries in these fundamentals over the past two years, confidence remains depressed—suggesting an unexplained factor we define as “malaise<sup>”</sup> (or, adverse “animal spirits<sup>”</sup>). We estimate that malaise is depressing consumer confidence by 1-2 standard deviations through end-2023. While the latest data through 1Q24 points to some recovery, the recent monthly prints for April and May are a reminder that the recovery is unlikely to move in a straight line.

## Misery loves company

Underlying the recent recovery in consumer confidence is considerable variation across economies and even within economies. While US confidence was relatively resilient to shocks from inflation in 2022 (we use an average of the Michigan and Conference Board measures), this impact (as well as the outbreak of the Russia-Ukraine war in the case of Western Europe) drove large declines in confidence across the rest of the G-4.

Figure 2: DM consumer confidence
![](images/848f016eec7747b1ed6a39fe3ff65a4225306070e41655ea375971fc756038dc.jpg)

Since late 2022, US confidence has gradually recovered to its prior expansion average (though it came off a bit in April and the latest news shows a further dip in early May) even as the rest of DM remains negative (Figure 2). While the recent gains have been supported by slow upticks in Western Europe, much of the increase has been driven by rapid gains in Japan, where consumers have recently enjoyed a fading of real income drags (though weakness in the yen seems to have led to some moderation in confidence last month).

The gap between the two US confidence measures (Conference Board and University of Michigan) has widened consid-

erably since mid-2021. Although there are some differences in the nature of the survey questions, they have moved closely with each other for the bulk of the last four decades. The recent gap had narrowed some of late as the Michigan measure recovers and the Conference Board measure moderates, but the two continue to send differing signals on the state of US sentiment (with the preliminary May reading taking a significant step down). While the Conference Board measure remains 0.2 standard deviations above its prior expansion average, the Michigan index is still at a depressed 1.6 standard deviations below its average (Figure 3). To better understand this gap, as well as the moves in confidence across the rest of the G-4, we turn to a model to ascertain the macroeconomic factors driving household sentiment.

## Money actually can buy happiness

For a better understanding of the potential drivers of confidence, we consider its comovement with a set of macroeconomic fundamentals. This regression model takes as its inputs the over year ago rates of change of consumer prices, equity prices, and nominal household disposable income, as well as both the level and four-quarter change in the unemployment rate. The regression output suggests that consumer confidence is rooted in these fundamentals across economies. Not only does the model explain a substantial share of the movement in confidence (ranging from 81% in the US to 65% in Europe and 49% in Japan), but it also reveals relatively similar and stable coefficients across countries (Table 1).

The model helps explain part of the unusually large gap between the Conference Board and the Michigan surveys in the US, though not all of it. The Michigan survey is twice as sensitive to inflation as the Conference Board survey. Whereas a 1%-pt rise in CPI over the past year lowers the Michigan measure of confidence by 0.3 standard deviation, the effect is only half as strong in the Conference Board measure. The negative effects of inflation are felt even more strongly in Europe, with the model suggesting consumers in the UK and Euro area are twice and three times more sensitive to inflation, respectively, than the US average. This variation again helps explain the resilience of US confidence relative to that of Western Europe as year-ago inflation reached a peak in 2022. The effect in Japan meanwhile is not significant, likely reflecting its relatively unique state of price dynamics and deflation concerns.

The surge in inflation has come with equally impressive gains in nominal incomes. The model suggests the boost to confidence from rising incomes is quite significant across the US and Western Europe (particularly the Euro area), and marginally so in Japan. Notably, however, the inflation-versus-income impacts are asymmetric: a rise in inflation outweighs a similarly large rise in nominal income gain, and particularly so in the UK and Euro area. This uneven impact speaks to the damage that a period of heightened inflation can cause even when met with equal-sized income gains: households tend to feel they have “earned<sup>”</sup> their income gains but don<sup>’</sup>t “deserve<sup>”</sup> to be punished by inflation.

Whereas European confidence is driven by inflation, confidence in the US is more heavily swayed by the labor market. Increases in the unemployment rate damp confidence across the two US measures by more than in Europe: a 1%-point rise reduces confidence by roughly 0.4 standard deviations in the US, but only 0.15 in Europe. This effect is statistically insignificant in Japan. However, a four-quarter change in the unemployment rate—a gauge for shifts in labor market trends rather than the outright level—is statistically significant in Japan, suggesting respondents here are more concerned with labor market momentum. Surprisingly, equity market gains have similar impacts in the US and Europe despite equity exposure being much larger in the US. Even more surprising, the stock market impact is particularly large in Japan.

In the Euro area, by far the greatest driver of confidence over the last two years has been the inflation shock, which drove confidence down nearly five standard deviations at its end-2022 peak (Figure 5). Since then, however, this effect has ebbed considerably and has nearly faded completely in the current quarter (using available April data). This drag was only partially offset by income<sup>’</sup>s contribution. But encouragingly as of 4Q23, income<sup>’</sup>s positive effect greatly outweighed the inflation drag.

## Catch and release: Animal spirits

Based on the fundamentals alone, confidence in both the Euro area and US should have moved beyond its prior expansion average. The fact that it has not can be attributed to an underlying “animal spirits<sup>”</sup> (i.e. moves in confidence unexplained by macro fundamentals). Comparing animal spirits across economies reveals a broad-based malaise for much of the post-outbreak period, but with notable variation across economies (Figure 6). Malaise in the US, UK, and Euro area fully recovered from the post-pandemic (and in the US, post-2020 election) drop. Beginning in 2022, however, this recovery unraveled, likely reflecting the impact of Russia<sup>’</sup>s war on Ukraine and a more damaging effect from inflation than the average impact would imply.

Notably, though inflation had shown considerable progress back to target by the fourth quarter of last year, confidence in the Euro area slipped to its lowest point since the measure began in 2000. This fall (and to a lesser degree the UK<sup>’</sup>s) likely explains part of the puzzling weakness seen in Western

European consumer spending. Even as households see meaningful gains to real income as wages rise and inflation moderates, European consumers have not exhibited the same boost in spending that we<sup>’</sup>ve seen in the US. A fading of this subpar consumption is a key element of our expectation for Western Europe to turn from its 2H23 stagnation in coming quarters. So far, Euro area malaise remains in place. The pickup in GDP growth last quarter could invigorate households. If so, we would expect to see our “animal spirits<sup>”</sup> measure recovering smartly. Indeed, assuming no change in income between 4Q23 and 1Q24, we see an improvement in 1Q European malaise. This should, in turn, reinforce the growth pickup as consumer spending bounces back to its fundamental supports.

Persistent US malaise throughout 2023 is more puzzling given the strength of the US expansion. Although US malaise was at similar levels to that of the Euro area through 4Q, consumer spending has been a primary driver of the above-trend growth in recent quarters. Why US malaise has decoupled from actual consumer behavior is unclear. It could partially reflect an increasing political bias in measures of US confidence, with studies showing large swings in sentiment around a change in presidential power (and particularly so among Republican respondents). This malaise began to fade in the US in 1Q, but with the recent drops in US confidence, it remains to be seen whether this recovery continues.

Japanese malaise, meanwhile, was both hit the hardest by Covid and has shown the most persistent weakness in the ensuing years. Encouragingly the drag began to fade in 2023, and by the end of the year malaise had met the (albeit high) levels of the US and Euro area.
