# APAC Economic Perspectives ASEAN Inflation Monthly: A turning point for central banks?

## Inflation converging to target/trend across most of ASEAN

Core inflation remains tame, and is already at or below trend across the region. Headline inflation looks likely to crest in the Philippines and pick up gradually in Thailand, both converging towards their respective central bank targets. Taking into account weaker than expected outturns and/or policy changes, we lower our '24 inflation forecast for the Philippines, Indonesia and Malaysia by 0.4ppt/0.2ppt/0.1ppt, to 3.3%/3.0%/2.4% respectively. Yesterday, we raised our CPI inflation forecast for Thailand to 0.8% (from 0.7%) to factor in the recent spike in raw food prices (mainly vegetables). For Singapore, we remain below consensus on MAS Core Inflation (UBSe: 2.9% vs. Cons: 3.0%) and CPI All Items inflation (UBSe: 2.7% vs. Cons: 2.8%).

## A turning point for inflation as policies are tweaked

Last week, the Philippines government announced a cut in rice import tariffs from 35% to 15%, which is estimated to eventually lower rice prices by P6-P7 a kilo (down \~10- 15% from current prices). Assuming a 5% decline in rice prices by July, our revised forecast is for inflation to just cross the 4% threshold in Jun/Jul. Malaysia announced a >50% increase in the price of diesel, which we expect to add 10bp to the CPI. We expect RON95 price hikes in Q4 to RM2.50 (vs. 2.05 currently), which would lift inflation by at least 1ppt. We think RON95 subsidy rationalization goes ahead this year, or Malaysia's target fiscal consolidation to a deficit of 4.3% (FY23: 5%) might not be achievable. BNM's Deputy Governor said that it has yet to determine whether the impact of subsidy rationalization should warrant any response at all from monetary policy.

## BSP to cut ahead of the Fed

In recent weeks, the BSP Governor has thrice mentioned an August rate cut is on the table, downplaying peso weakness as a constraint. BSP was expecting the Fed to cut in September, by a total of 50bp in 2024. (See here and here.) Given the uncertainty on inflation (whether it will breach 4%), recent peso weakness with the majority of FOMC (and UBS) expecting just one cut, we maintain our call that the BSP will cut before the Fed in Oct/Dec (50bp in total), followed by another 100bp in 2025, which will broadly maintain rate differentials with the US. In our view, a couple of months delay to October represents a better policy trade off amidst still resilient, albeit sub-trend, growth. The BSP had previously noted that a 1% depreciation in the peso has a 0.04ppt impact on inflation in the short run, while PHP has already weakened 5% vs. USD over the past three months. This could in theory have a 0.2ppt impact on inflation, which should still be manageable given the broader disinflationary impulse. At the 27 June policy meeting, should BSP give clear forward guidance of expectations of sub-4% inflation by July, and/ or an August rate cut, we would revisit our forecasts.

## Other than BSP, no central bank in ASEAN to ease this year

Yesterday, we upgraded Thailand's GDP growth forecast from 2.5% to 2.8% on the back of faster fiscal disbursements and we no longer expect rate cuts this year. Meanwhile, BI's focus on currency stability suggests the central bank would not rush to ease. We expect BI to start cutting rates only in January 2025. Moreover, policy is not that restrictive yet, given the lagged pass-through to deposit and lending rates and strong credit growth. We don't expect BI to hike either. We think that MAS can sit tight amidst cooling inflation, although the jump in wages growth in Q1 suggests ongoing labour cost pressures remain. Amidst a closing output gap and a labour market at full employment, the latest MAS Survey of Professional Forecasters shows that a smaller minority expects the MAS to ease this year.

## Measuring the breadth of inflation in ASEAN

In this report, we include metrics of the breadth of inflation in ASEAN, such as CPI trimmed means and diffusion indices. Academic research (see list of references on page 15) has shown that conventional core CPI indicators based on exclusion based metrics ("ex food and energy") could be perceived to be less credible if, as in many emerging economies, they exclude important parts of the consumer basket such as food and energy. "Limited influence estimators" such as the trimmed mean have a higher correlation with key macro indicators such as the money supply. A description of the metrics can be found in the Appendix.

## Indonesia

In May, inflation fell to 2.8% y/y (3.0% in April). Food inflation slowed to 6.8% y/y (8.0% in April). Sequentially, food prices have fallen by 0.2% m/m in April and 0.6% m/ m in May, following sharp increases of 1.3% m/m in February and 1.8% m/m in March. Rice prices came down to around Rp15,400/kg from the peak of Rp15,900/kg in March. Prices of red chili pepper also moderated. Core inflation climbed to 1.9% y/y in May from a trough of 1.7% y/y this Jan. On a m/m basis, core inflation slowed to 0.2% m/m (Apr: 0.3). Prices such as clothing & footwear (-0.04% m/m) and transportation (-0.4% m/m) contracted. Price increases of services such as dining out services (+0.03% m/m) and healthcare (+0.04% m/m) also decelerated. Still, in the coming months, we expect inflation to head up to >3% due to base effects. That said, easing food inflation reduces the headwinds for consumption, especially for the lower income segment.

Food inflation slowed to 7% y/y in May. Base effects and elevated food inflation are likely to keep inflation above 3% in 2024

## Malaysia

CPI inflation accelerated to 1.85% y/y in April from 1.77% y/y in March and a trough of 1.5% y/y in January. Core inflation (excl. volatile items) eased to 1.46% y/y from 1.55% y/y in March but still higher than the trough of 0.9% y/y in January. Goods inflation remains mild, particularly in clothing and footwear (-0.3% y/y) and household equipment and furnishings (1.0% y/y). However, prices of housing and utilities accelerated (3.0%y/y). Services inflation has been relatively sticky, particularly for spending in restaurants and hotels (+0.3% m/m, 3.5% y/y), as well as health (+0.2% m/ m, 2.3% y/y). The breadth and the intensity of inflationary pressures remains contained, with less than half of measured goods and services prices experiencing m/m increases that are higher than average, and only less than a quarter of the CPI basket weight experiencing inflation above 2.5% y/y.

Starting from 10 June 2024, the government cut diesel subsidies and floated diesel prices at the market rate of RM3.35 per litre, up 56% from RM2.15 (see here). We expect this adds around 10bp to the CPI, given the weight of diesel is 0.2% in the CPI basket. With the diesel price rationalisation, the government is expected to save RM4 billion annually (see here). Whilst the government remains committed to reducing RON95 subsidies, the implementation time and amount of RON95 subsidy rationalization has yet to be pre-announced (to avoid hoarding). We expect the price of RON95 to be lifted by Q4 to an average of RM2.50 (vs. subsidized price at RM2.05 currently), but not fully floated. We believe this would add about a full percentage point to inflation. Overall, we think inflation is likely to be above 3.5% by the end of the year.

Inflation edged up to 1.8% in April. Subsidy rationalization expected to lead to an upward shift in headline inflation to be above 3.5% by the end of 2024.

## Philippines

Inflation edged up to 3.9% y/y in May (from 3.8% y/y in April), though it is far lower than the last peak of 6.1% y/y in September 2023. Core inflation, which excludes volatile food and energy items, fell by 0.1ppt to 3.1% y/y. In sequential terms, headline inflation edged up 0.1% m/m while core inflation was up 0.2% m/m. Food prices fell by a modest 0.08% m/m, driven by rice (down 0.2% m/m). Inflationary pressures are not widespread. Less than half of the CPI items are experiencing m/m price increases above their historical average, and the summed CPI weight of items experiencing >4% y/y inflation has been coming down sharply.

Rice price is expected to fall with import tariff cut.

On 4 June 2024 the Philippines National Economic and Development Authority (NEDA) announced it would cut tariffs on staple food from 35% to 15% to ease the elevated inflation. The Philippine Statistics Authority estimated that the tariff cut could bring down rice prices by P6 to P7 per kilo, about 10-15% of the current rice price. The new rule is under the new Comprehensive Tariff Program for 2024-2028. Given that rice makes up 9% of the CPI basket, the 22.9% y/y increase in rice price in May resulted in an addition of 2.1% y/y CPI inflation, compared to 3.9% y/y overall CPI growth. The Department of Agriculture commented that consumers are likely to start benefiting from lower rice prices as early as July. We now see inflation just barely hitting the 4% threshold over the next two months, assuming a 5% decline in rice prices by July.

## Singapore

CPI inflation remained flat at 2.7% y/y in April (vs 2.7% y/y in March), the lowest since the peak of 7.5% y/y in September 2022. Private transport prices edged up moderately to 0.3% y/y in April (from -0.3% y/y in March) while public transport inflation remained elevated around 7.0% y/y. Accommodation inflation dropped to 3.5% y/y in April (from 3.7% y/y in March). MAS Core inflation also stayed flat at 3.1% y/y compared with last month. Food prices moderated to 2.8% y/y in April from 3.0% in March. Food servicing services inflation dropped to 3.8% y/y in April from 4.0% y/y in March, led by fast food (4.1% y/y), hawker food (3.8% y/y) and catered food (3.3%y/y). Goods prices also dropped, as seen in clothing & footwear (-0.7% y/y, 2.7% m/m), household durables (1.3% y/y, 0.3% m/m), and recreational goods (0.1% y/y, -0.3% m/m), reflecting the fading of supply shocks and lower imported goods prices. Meanwhile services inflation remained elevated, as seen in healthcare (4.8% y/y, 0.6% m/m), holiday expenses (5.3% y/y, +1.25% m/m), and recreational & cultural services (5.7% y/y, +2.4% m/m).

Core and headline inflation stayed flat in April, due to lower accommodation costs and goods inflation.

In terms of underlying inflation, the breadth and the intensity of inflationary pressures are modest, with less than half of measured goods and services prices experiencing m/m increases that are higher than average.

## Thailand

CPI inflation rose for a second straight month to 1.5% y/y in May (vs 0.2% y/y in April), marking the first time that inflation returned to the BoT's target band of 1-3% y/y since April 2023. The acceleration in inflation was primarily driven by the faster growth of food prices, especially vegetables. The growth of food price rose to 1.5% y/y (from 0.1% y/y in April) and the growth of fresh vegetables price surged to 11.7% y/y in May (from an already fast pace of 7.5% y/y in April). In addition, the growth of electricity prices turned positive to 12.1% y/y (from -5% y/y contraction for 8 consecutive months) due to a low base in May. Core CPI, which excludes volatile food and energy prices, grew 0.4% y/y in May. The ministry of commerce maintained its inflation forecast at 0-1% y/y for 2024 and expected CPI would decelerate in June.

On a m/m basis, headline CPI was 0.6% m/m higher, driven by higher raw food prices (2.7% m/m). Prices of fresh vegetables accelerated at double digit growth for two months in a row (15.3% m/m in May and 17.0% m/m in April), while prices of meat rose 1.1% m/m and eggs price went up 3.9% m/m. Energy inflation remains very soft (0.1% m/m).Core inflation, whilst still stable at 0.4% y/y and in sequential terms picking up to 0.1%, remains very weak. The breadth of inflationary pressures is mild, with <40% of measured goods and services prices experiencing m/m increases that are higher than average. Goods inflation is subdued, particularly in clothing and footwear (-0.4% y/y), household equipment and furnishings (-1.2% y/y), and personal care items (0.2%y/y).

Vegetable prices are the main driver of inflation in Thailand.

But core goods and services prices remained weak in the first five months of 2024.

## Appendix

Diffusion index: Measures the cumulative weight of CPI components whose monthly y/y price change are above a certain threshold. We aggregate some items to maintain a constant basket over time. In this report, we select 1%y/y (the bottom of the acceptable inflation comfort zone for the lower inflation economies in the region), 2.5% (close to the mid-point of inflation comfort zones across the region), and 4% (the top of the range for higher inflation target economies) as thresholds. A higher share for the latter two categories implies a greater breadth of inflation outcomes.

Trimmed mean inflation: Is calculated by excluding a certain percentage of the distribution of m/m price changes at the higher and lower ends of the distribution. In this report, we begin with sorting monthly individual price changes in ascending order from "fell the most" to "rose the most", trimming 10% of the most extreme observations at both ends of the spectrum, and then calculated as a weighted average of the remaining components. This can be used as an alternative to core inflation, but eliminates the influence of outliers or data points to gauge the breadth of underlying price pressures.

The share of CPI components rising: Indicates the number of CPI items the recorded mom % increases as the total number of aggregated CPI items in the basket. Again, we aggregate some items to maintain a constant basket over time. The metric highlights how much of the CPI index is or is not in deflation.

Core goods and core services: We break core inflation down into two broad categories: core goods (commodities less food and energy commodities) and core services (services less household utilities and other services). Core goods inflation may be more influenced by external developments. Both elements of core inflation rising together may suggest broad-based pressures. A caveat with this analysis is the revisions to CPI data. On balance this has been helpful since the data revisions have increased the granularity of available CPI data in the region in recent years. However, this also makes for challenges in defining categories consistently over time. Most recently this caveat has been strongest for Indonesia and the Philippines, while the availability of detailed CPI data for Vietnam remains limited.
