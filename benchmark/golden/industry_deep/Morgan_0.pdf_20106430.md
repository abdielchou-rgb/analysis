# US: All signs point to slower job gains in 2024

• Stronger-than-expected GDP growth in 2023 kept labor demand high

• Supply side surprises made it possible to accommodate that demand

• Cyclical tailwinds from the aggregate employment cycle have likely subsided

• The few sectors that led the payroll gains in 2023 will have less room in 2024

The pandemic caused major dislocations in the labor market in a short period of time. Some changes from this episode, such as remote work and flexible work arrangements, might be here to stay, but otherwise the labor market seems to have recovered well and normalized. In fact, at first glance, the resilience we have seen over the past year was largely unexpected. In the Survey of Professional Forecasters (SPF) for 4Q22, many forecasters penciled in a significant moderation in the overall payroll growth for 2023. The average monthly pace of payroll gains in 2023 of 255K (Figure 1) was a significant step down from the 377K in 2022, but much higher than the median expectation of 143K in the SPF a year ago. Here we analyze the underlying drivers of US labor market resilience in 2023 and the prospects for 2024.

Figure 1: Monthly nonfarm payroll gains
![](images/ebc258e7531b2b73859ac7615b0ce1e98f8f482d2fc1464a1491950ef35485e1.jpg)

We conclude that the resilience in the labor market in 2023 was consistent with stronger overall activity than expected as well as supply side surprises, with significant support from immigration and a pickup in the participation rate. Digging deeper into sector-specific data, we further argue that tailwinds from the cyclical normalization and idiosyncratic strengths in certain sectors will be absent this year.

## Lost steam but kept going

It was not unusual to think that around the end of 2022, the labor market would have slowed amid a rapid policy tightening cycle. Economic theory tells us, all else equal, higher rates curb aggregate demand and increase the cost of investment for businesses. Moreover, it looked like the US economy had already recouped all the jobs lost during the pandemic by early fall 2022. In the end, businesses added about 3.0mn new jobs last year. What explains this surprising resilience?

First, the overall economy proved to be more resilient to higher interest rates than we anticipated a year ago. In fact, the payroll growth in 2023 was in line with what one would have expected given this performance (Figure 2). As we argued in our 2024 US Outlook, we do not think this performance was due to a changing dynamic in the monetary transmission mechanism. Instead, many households and businesses seem to have been well insulated from higher interest rates so far, having locked in low rates during the pandemic. Moreover, monetary tightening has been offset partly by the fiscal side. We also think that the full effects of the restrictive policy have not been felt yet. As a result, the annual growth in real GDP for 2023 was around 2.5%, a step up from the 1.9% pace in 2022. That was a significantly stronger performance than the 0.7% median expectation in the SPF a year ago.

Stronger-than-expected real activity kept demand for labor high, but without improvements on the supply side it would have been difficult to observe the solid hiring we saw in 2023. More specifically, the labor force participation rate and the civilian labor force rose sharply last year after solid gains in 2022. Together, these supply-side surprises have provided significant support for robust job gains over the past several quarters. The rebound in the participation rate over the past two years was sizable given the strong secular trend dragging the participation rate over the past two decades (Figure 3).

Similarly, the civilian noninstitutionalized population (age 16 and up) was up 1.2% in 2023, following a 1% rise in 2022. These numbers reflect a significant increase in growth rates from depressed levels during the pandemic, around 0.4% in 2020-21. This surprisingly strong boost from population growth was led by a robust increase in the foreign born population. The increase in the participation rate was mostly due to the strong rebound in labor force participation among women aged 25-54 years old.

## Accounting for monthly gains

To understand how each of these supply-side channels contributed to the overall strength in job gains, we can relate the payroll gains each month in a simple accounting framework to changes in the participation rate, civilian population, and the unemployment rate, measured from the household survey. We know that there are survey-related differences in sample and scope between the household survey designed to measure these labor market aggregates and the establishment survey our payroll estimates come from. Over the past three years, the establishment survey indicated around 100K more jobs created per month than was implied by the household survey, which is the largest discrepancy in this direction since 1999.

Keeping this recent divergence in mind, this accounting framework can shed more light on sources of the resilience. Changes in household employment every period equal the sum of three individual components in logs: change in the employment rate (1 – the unemployment rate), changes in the participation rate, and changes in the population level. All things equal, a higher unemployment rate is associated with lower employment gains in a month. By contrast, in this accounting framework, a higher participation rate and population growth would be positive.

Based on this framework, significant improvements in the participation rate and civilian population growth accounted for a large fraction of the average gains per month in 2023 (Figure 4). The combined contribution of these two channels led payroll gains in 2022 as well, but their contribution in 2023 was more pronounced. Note that the difference between the line and the horizontal bars in Figure 4 corresponds to the survey-related difference we alluded to above. This decomposition is informative about what to expect in 2024. We do not think there is a lot of room for improvement in terms of participation and population growth, as the participation rate is not far from our estimate of its secular trend and the growth in the foreign-born population likely normalized.

## Employment cycle and reallocation

Our analysis explains the strength in the aggregate payrolls, but not necessarily the significant variation across sectors.

Figure 4: Factors contributing to monthly employment gains 000s, sa monthly average.
![](images/00e30b4c6ddfa84edf366892fa107e665bd1c659f17b7289f6b73241f11d3f95.jpg)

For instance, the bulk of the overall change in 2023 can be attributed to a handful of industries: education and health, government, and leisure and hospitality. Although these three sectors combined account for around 40% of nonfarm payrolls, they accounted for about 75% of the 3.0 million job gains in 2023. To systematically understand sectoral differences in employment growth, we write down a simple model that can help us identify cyclical movements from reallocation across sectors due to idiosyncratic changes at the industry level over time.

More formally, we assume that employment growth in sector i at time t, $\mathbf { g } _ { \mathrm { i t } } ,$ can come from three distinct components: a long-run average, $Z _ { \mathrm { t } } ;$ an unobserved cyclical component, $\mathrm { C _ { \mathrm { i } t } } ,$ and an unobserved idiosyncratic component, $\mathrm { X } _ { \mathrm { i t } } ,$ that potentially reflects other noncyclical factors. We use data on 14 individual sectors going back to 1939 and aggregate the monthly payroll data to a quarterly level for this exercise. In this formulation, which follows Rissman (2009) closely, we assume that the cyclical response by each sector $\mathrm { C _ { \mathrm { i } t } } ,$ will be different and will depend on the aggregate cycle, $\mathrm { C _ { \mathrm { t } } } .$

This structure can help us identify unobserved cyclical and idiosyncratic components with the Kalman filter, using data on industry employment growth over time. Note that this parsimonious model is already quite flexible in that different industries could respond to the aggregate cycle differently.

Given how the recent gains have been concentrated in certain sectors, our approach should be able to capture these underlying differences. Moreover, this decomposition can inform us whether these underlying differences in sectoral strength come from differences in sectoral cyclicality or idiosyncratic differences that cannot be associated with the usual cyclical pattern of these sectors.

## Cyclical tailwinds mostly subsided in 2023

Our estimates suggest that the aggregate cyclical adjustment over the pandemic recession was not only unprecedented in magnitude, but also unusual in terms of the boost it provided to payroll growth during much of the recovery. This was a significant change from the prior “jobless recovery<sup>”</sup> episodes (Figure 5) in the recent past. The cyclical components of sectoral payroll growth, $\mathrm { C _ { \mathrm { i t } } } \mathrm { s } ,$ , ceased to provide any support by 3Q. None of the 14 individual sectors were getting a boost from this cyclical tailwind by the end of the year.

Figure 5: Estimated aggregate employment cycle
![](images/dc69ab124e1118946e9c7c858fdf9a58f615d55ae8c9e085c20024915eba2697.jpg)

Of course, this does not imply that a contraction in employment is imminent; instead, it suggests that the aggregate cycle is not a driving force behind individual sectoral gains anymore. In fact, the median cyclical component was hovering around levels consistent with what we observed at the end of the previous expansion, 2017-2019, when average payroll gains were still around 170K a month. Given that our outlook calls for below-trend growth, we think, if anything, cyclical forces will turn into headwinds.

## Pandemic: also a reallocation shock

The pandemic was also a reallocation shock for the labor market. Shutdowns forced consumers to direct their consumption away from in-person services and towards goods. Increased reliance on online shopping and deliveries presented a positive relative demand shock, boosting some sectors like transportation and warehousing. Our estimates for ${ \mathrm { \mathbf { X } _ { \mathrm { i t } } ^ { \ \prime } { \bf s } } }$ capture these variations across sectors. Since the aggregate shock (the pandemic) was partly behind these sector-specific effects, we find more cyclical variation in the median $\mathrm { X } _ { \mathrm { i t } }$ over the current recovery in contrast to the recent past (Figure 6). More importantly, variation in these non-cyclical components across sectors shot up to levels that are an order of magnitude larger than the sample average (Figure 7), suggesting an unprecedented level of churn in the labor markets.

## Leaders in 2023 were laggards in 2021

Even though the median sector had a positive $\mathrm { X } _ { \mathrm { i t } }$ during 2023, most of the gains came from three sectors. We have argued in the past that this was due to “catch-up hiring.<sup>”</sup> We find a negative correlation between how strong a sector<sup>’</sup>s performance was during the early recovery and how it performed later in 2023 based on our estimates.

We think that among those, leisure and hospitality might still have some room to grow at a disproportionately higher pace in 2024, but the combined three sectors have recovered a lot relative to their estimated trend over the last year. Estimating individual trends based on sectoral growth between 2007 and 2019 and extrapolating them implies that the relevant employment gap closed to a large extent (Figure 8).

We think fading cyclical tailwinds and idiosyncratic strength in certain sectors will make continuing the trend from 2023 unsustainable. Given the slowdown we expect in economic activity later in the year, average gains would fall below the breakeven rate of approximately 100K a month, as the unemployment rate slowly climbs up later in the year. Supply-side surprises we have seen last year can of course happen again, but given the strong secular trends in the participation rate and the normalization in the immigration channel, we do not think they would come to the rescue in 2024.
