# European Economic Perspectives France: 2025 budget - what to watch and OAT views

## Political uncertainty continues

Following the hung parliament resulting from the parliamentary elections and the resignation of the previous government, Prime Minister Attal continues to lead a caretaker government. Uncertainty remains about the composition of the next government and who will be appointed prime minister by President Macron. Should attempts to form a grand coalition fail, a technical government remains an option. As we have argued before, the most important task for a new government will be the adoption of a budget for 2025. This will be challenging for at least two reasons: (1) adhering to the requirement of adopting a budget and following the process laid out in the Constitution without having a majority in parliament; and (2) adopting a budget that satisfies the EU fiscal rules. With France set to enter an Excessive Deficit Procedure, and the new EU fiscal framework applying, this requires fiscal consolidation. We have argued before that France is in a challenging fiscal position: France: Fiscal challenges ahead. In this note, we focus on the budget approval process, what to watch over the coming weeks and months and discuss implications for OAT spreads.

## 2024 growth outlook better than expected

Before delving into the budget discussion, we note that GDP growth this year is holding up better than expected. GDP growth amounted to 0.3% q/q in Q1 and Q2, and the latest indication is that Q3 could benefit from a (likely temporary) Olympics-related boost to consumption (see the latest update by the Banque de France). Overall, we are lifting our 2024 GDP growth forecast by 30bp to 1.2%, but lower our 2025 projection by 10bp to 1.1%.

## 2025 budget process in focus

Article 47 of the French Constitution imposes the obligation to pass a budget in time for the following year by the end of the current year, with a strict time frame (see Box 1 for a detailed discussion). This requires the government to send a budget draft to parliament by 1 October 2024 to leave enough time for parliamentary discussion. The current caretaker government under Prime Minister Attal is working towards a draft that aims at keeping state spending in 2025 at the 2024 levels, while recognizing that this would be a "reversible budget" and a new government could make substantial changes. With the uncertain political situation in mind, and notably doubts whether a working majority can be achieved to pass a budget, we note that the Constitution includes various safeguards to ensure budget continuity and to prevent a US-style government shutdown, whereby the government would no longer be allowed to pay its bills. These apply if there are delays in submitting the draft budget in time, if parliament rejects the budget or if the Constitutional Council rejects a budget (after it has been passed by parliament but before it can be promulgated in time for the following year). However, these procedures only serve to ensure that either a 2025 budget is in place or to ensure budget continuity, i.e. that the state can continue to collect taxes and implement necessary spending.

## New fiscal rules require consolidation

The challenge for the next government will not just be to ensure budget continuity; the contents will matter as well, as budget cuts are required to adhere to the EU fiscal rules. Given the spending promises made in the various party manifestos ahead of the election, this could be challenging. The next budget will need to abide by the restrictions of the reformed Stability and Growth Pact (which is applied again this year after having been deactivated during Covid and the energy crisis). ## Strategy: No return to pre-election levels for French spreads

We still expect French 10y spreads versus Germany to trade \~70 bps or higher the longer the formation of a new government takes. Leading into the 1 October deadline for a draft budget to parliament, we expect 10y spreads over Germany to trade in a range of 70-80 bps. Even if a caretaker government submits a budget with spending at 2024 levels and flexibility in EDP rules reduce required fiscal consolidation to 0.2% of GDP for 2025, we expect investors to remain focused on budget continuity and long-term debt sustainability. Note that French 10y spreads over swaps are trading close to 2024 highs (Figure 15). In a scenario where a technocratic government emerges, we see 10y France versus Germany spreads falling to \~60 bps.

## Box 1: The budget process in France

Article 47 of the French Constitution imposes the obligation to pass a budget in time for the following year by the end of the current year, with a strict time frame. However, the Constitution includes various safeguards that aim to ensure budget continuity and to prevent a US-style government shutdown, whereby the government would no longer be allowed to pay its bills, see here, here and here for details.

To start, a draft budget needs to be sent to parliament in time to allow for approval, and parliament has 70 days to vote on the budget bill (40 days for a first reading in the National Assembly, 15 days in the Senate, and the remaining time to find agreement between both chambers). To ensure a budget is in place at the start of the following year, this time frame requires this year's budget draft to be sent to parliament by 1 October 2024 (first Tuesday in October). This would allow parliament to adopt the budget by mid-December. Before being promulgated, Art. 61 of the Constitution allows the budget bill to be referred to the Constitutional Council at the request of 60 deputies or 60 senators (as well as the President of the Republic, the Prime Minister, or the presidents of the assemblies).

In case the 70-day period is not respected, and no bill is voted before the end of the year (this has never happened before), the Constitution distinguishes between whether this delay is due to parliament or the government.

If a delay can be attributed to parliament, i.e. if parliament fails to reach a decision, Art. 47(3) allows the government to bring the provisions into force by a "budget ordinance", which is adopted by the Council of Ministers and does not require parliamentary ratification (unlike the usual procedure in Art. 38, whereby ordinances require parliamentary approval).

If instead the delay is caused by government, i.e. if a budget bill is not presented to parliament in time for promulgation by the end of the year or if parliament rejects a budget draft within the 70-day period and leaves too little time for government to present an alternative draft, the provisions of Art. 47(4) and Art 45 of the organic law apply. These allow government to:

(1) Ask parliament before 11 December to adopt the first part of the budget bill (dealing with revenues) first, and the second part (dealing with expenditures) at a later stage; or

(2) Request parliamentary authorisation before 19 December for a special law to collect existing taxes and – once the special law has been passed – adopt decrees to implement the appropriations established in last year's budget, essentially ensuring budget continuity. The same option to request a special law applies if the Constitutional Council were to reject the implementation of a budget (that had been adopted by parliament) before 1 January.

Lastly, in case of any other rejection of the budget law not foreseen by legislation, such as a systematic rejection of the budget bill or emergency measures, a Constitutional Council ruling from 1978 could apply. This requires the government and parliament to find a solution to ensure the functioning of the state, in essence by extending the previous year's budget.

As a very last resort, the Constitution's Art. 16 grants the president exceptional powers in case "the institutions of the Republic, the independence of the Nation, the integrity of its territory or the fulfilment of its international commitments are under serious and immediate threat, and where the proper functioning of the constitutional public authorities is interrupted". However, it is unclear whether a budget crisis would qualify for such measures.

Another way of ensuring the passing of a budget in time is that the government makes use of Art. 49 (3) of the Constitution. This states that a bill can be passed without a vote in parliament unless parliament puts forward a vote of no confidence. If this is passed with an absolute majority in the National Assembly, the government has to resign (Art. 50). This procedure was used several times during the last government under President Macron, which did not have a parliamentary majority. The use of this procedure requires that the president appoints a new prime minister first, as a caretaker government would not be able to resort to Art. 49 (3).

## Box 2: How much fiscal adjustment is required by the EU fiscal rules?

Under the rules of the Stability and Growth Pact, countries that are in an Excessive Deficit Procedure are required to lower their structural budget deficit by at least 0.5% of GDP as a benchmark. However, the regulation was amended under the new fiscal framework and now states that, during "a transitory period in 2025, 2026 and 2027", this benchmark may be adjusted to "take into account the increase in interest payments when setting the proposed corrective path relating to the first medium-term fiscal-structural plan for the years 2025, 2026 and 2027 within the Excessive Deficit Procedure".

How this will be implemented in practice has not been specified explicitly. However, the latest EC forecasts for France show interest payments increasing from 2% of GDP in 2024 to 2.3% in 2025. If this increase in interest payments were taken into account and excluded from the consolidation requirement, this could potentially imply that the required extent of annual fiscal tightening is reduced from 0.5% of GDP to 0.2% of GDP by 2027.

## Box 3: The new EU fiscal framework

## The background to the reform

Reforming the Stability and Growth Pact (SGP) has been on the EU's agenda since 2019, as the rules were seen as too complicated, ambiguous, procyclical, and hard to enforce (see our notes here, here, here and here). The reform momentum had stalled during the Covid pandemic as the SGP had been deactivated under the socalled escape clause since 2020, which essentially suspended enforcement of the SGP over 2020-23. With the deactivation of the escape clause in 2024 and European Parliament elections on 9 June 2024 looming, the EU Council agreed on a reform in December 2023, which the EU Parliament approved on 23 April 2024. The implementation of the new rules is currently ongoing, with member states set to publish their fiscal-structural plans (see details below) by 20 September.

## The new fiscal framework

The agreed framework (see here) maintains the 3% of GDP budget deficit and the 60% of GDP debt target, but with two important changes to the old rules.

First, the new rules differentiate debt reduction strategies across countries − thus moving away from a "one-size-fits-all" approach − and strengthening national ownership of budgetary plans. Specifically, the new framework replaces the old debt reduction rule (which required countries with debt above 60% of GDP to reduce the difference to 60% by 1/20<sup>th</sup> per year) with a medium-term fiscalstructural adjustment plan based on debt sustainability analysis (DSA). The aim is to put debt on a declining path, with the 60% reference value as a distant target, but with more flexible and realistic adjustment steps. After the end of the adjustment period, debt/GDP should be on a "plausibly and continuously downward trajectory" and the budget deficit brought to and maintained below 3% of GDP. The minimum adjustment period is four years, but this can be extended to seven years if a country commits to a "relevant set of reforms and investments". The EC sent member states a reference trajectory for a four-year adjustment in June, which formed the basis for the "technical dialogue" with the EC. The eventual publication of the adjustment plan by the respective member state (which may extend the adjustment period to seven years) is expected by 20 September. Overall, this procedure is assumed to enhance national ownership. In case of a change in government, a member state can submit a revised plan to the EC.

Second, fiscal adjustment will be based on net primary expenditure ceilings, which replace the focus on structural budget deficits (which are seen as prone to revisions and less suited as a policy guide). Net primary expenditures is defined as total expenditures minus interest costs, expenditures fully matched by EU funding (e.g. grants from the EU recovery fund), the cyclical component of unemployment benefits, and increases in net revenue due to discretionary revenue measures. Hence, this indicator is not affected by automatic stabilisers and, as such, provides leeway for counter-cyclical policies.

As before, countries with budget deficits >3% of GDP in an Excessive Deficit Procedure will need to correct the budget deficit by at least 0.5pp of GDP. This correction relates to the structural primary balance in 2025-27 and the structural balance afterwards.

## Safeguards to limit discretionary leeway in budget discussions

Following requests by some countries (notably Germany) that have argued for less discretionary leeway in the bilateral discussions between member states and the EC, the framework includes minimum quantitative requirements ("safeguards").

On debt, the safeguard requires that debt/GDP declines by 1pp per year for member states with debt/GDP >90% in 2024, and 0.5pp for member states with a debt ratio between 60% and 90%.

On budget deficits, the safeguard requires that there is a 1.5% of GDP safety margin in structural terms to the 3% of GDP threshold. This implies that once a country falls back to within the 3% of GDP budget deficit, it still requires further annual improvement of 0.4pp of GDP in the structural primary balance in case of a four-year fiscal structural plan, and 0.25pp in case of a seven-year plan.
