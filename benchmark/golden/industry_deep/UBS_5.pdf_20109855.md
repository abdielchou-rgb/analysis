## US Economic Perspectives Fed & US outlook update

## Updating our rate call to reflect FOMC risks reassessment

We expect three 25 bp rate cuts this year, one at each of the September, November and December FOMC meetings. We expect this to be well signaled in the September FOMC meeting materials and the Summary of Economic Projections. We expect Chair Powell will lay out a case for an orderly withdrawal of monetary policy restrictiveness in a speech at Jackson Hole the morning of Friday, August 23, and by orderly, we mean 25 bp rate cuts, rather than 50 bp. A table of our rate projection by meeting is below.

We view what is unfolding amidst the Committee akin to a reassessment of short-run rstar, or a reassessment of the appropriate degree of restrictiveness. Chair Powell highlighted the risks to the labor market in his testimony to Congress last month, and raised the issue in the July FOMC meeting press conference, despite his contention the labor market expansion remains strong. He also did little to push back against the market pricing sequential rate cuts at the time of the July FOMC meeting.

Our forecast has been that we expected the growth slowdown underway to shift the narrative among the FOMC. Seeing growth actually slow, and the labor market slow on the back of that, would convince FOMC participants that monetary policy was restrictive. The concerns that monetary policy was not that restrictive, and the sense of an expansion resilient to rate hikes would melt away. The r-star is higher narrative would be replaced with FRB of New York President John Williams's assessment that it is not that much higher, and the higher-for-longer narrative would give way as a result. Combined with improving news on inflation, the FOMC would want to pick up the pace and get out of restrictive policy more quickly than previously signaled.

We thought it would take more slowing than what has actually occurred to motivate that shift, however. But, that FOMC shift may be underway.

This morning's CPI report points to a core PCE price increase in July a bp less than we were previously tracking. Despite the pick-up in owners' equivalent rent, the two-month annualized pace has stepped down to 3.9%, closer to where we think it needs to run (3.5% to 3.8%) for PCE inflation to run sustainably at 2 percent. Nominal wage growth slowing continues, while the increase in average hourly earnings in July was a little ahead of our model estimates (0.23% vs. 0.21%) that could be due to the hurricane depressing the workweek, and in any event, the year-over-year change slowed to 3.6%. ## The FOMC has long signaled a slow removal of policy restrictiveness

The FOMC has long signaled a slow removal of policy restraint. In the FOMC's Summary of Economic Projections, the clumps of dots (the assumptions of appropriate policy) moved lower generally at a quarterly 25 bp cut pace in the last several projections. The terminal rate a little over 3% has not changed much. And this pace of policy restraint removal came with an unemployment rate at 4.2% (it is currently 4.25%) and real GDP growth ranging from 1.8% to 2.1%. Real GDP growth in H1 was 2.1% annualized. However, the risk assessment appears to be shifting. The July increase in nonfarm payroll employment was not far below our expectations, nor the broader slowdown we have been expecting. We also have been noting the weakness in the household survey over the last year, as well as the mismeasurement and potentially misplaced comfort of poorly measured, elevated job openings. The rise in unemployment has now gotten some notice. The FOMC has done little to push back against market pricing. And even if the data is arriving close to their projection, a reassessment of the risks or degree of restrictiveness might prompt a reconsideration of the policy outlook.

The incoming data has not been that far from projections. Looking ahead, inflation seems likely to look a little stuck with core PCE inflation around 2.6% or 2.7% for the remainder of the year.

## Policy rules said the FOMC should have lowered rates in June

The figures above are policy rules. For a given level inflation and the unemployment rate, they compute an implied federal funds rate. They are described in the Federal Reserve's own Monetary Policy Report (see this link), and rely on historical experience. In the report the Fed sent to Congress last month, their own rules showed the implied setting of the federal funds rate should be below where the FOMC had voted to leave rates at their June FOMC meeting.

Why? The intercept of those rules is the neutral rate of interest, or r-star. The FOMC median saw the longer-run real funds rate at 0.8%, or 2.8% nominal, in the June SEP. However, the FOMC has been acting and setting policy as if that real neutral rate was higher, akin to short-run r-star being above their longer-run assessment. The FOMC raised rates rapidly, but the economic expansion remained resilient. Many FOMC participants appeared to question whether monetary policy was all that restrictive. Our forecast was that a growth and labor market slowdown would reveal policy was that restrictive. That setting also reflected the assessment of the risks to the outlook. As the resilience and risk assessment shifts, that implies a setting of policy closer to the rule and lower r-star might be more appropriate.

## The policy outlook should get an update next week at Jackson Hole

We expect the Chair to explain the policy outlook at Jackson Hole, which could undo this whole forecast. We expect him to make the case to take out a little more restrictiveness in the next few meetings than previously signaled, to better position policy, something of a recalibration. We expect 25 bp rate cuts and not 50 unless the data signal more ominous threat to the expansion. At face value, a 114K increase in nonfarm payroll employment or Sahm Rule triggered by two months of improvement in the household survey, would not seem that ominous, when the backdrop of final demand is slowing but real GDP growth still a touch over 2% annualized in H1. We expect Chair Powell to remain data dependent, and caution that ongoing rate cuts after any recalibration should depend upon ongoing progress on inflation toward 2%, weighed against the risks to the labor market expansion.

## If our economic outlook is right, the policy rate likely continues to move lower

In terms of policy rules, three 25 bp rate cuts this year would roughly catch the FOMC down to an inertial policy rule, one which removes policy more gradually than the instantaneous adjustment of the traditional Taylor rules. Now, for readers who recall on the way up, we noticed the degree of inertia shifted rapidly as inflation rose. A risk we do not dismiss, and revisit below. We left our 2025 funds rate forecast little changed, though we pulled one 25 bp cut from 2026 into 2025, assuming that if the FOMC is getting worried with a 114K increase in nonfarm payroll employment, they are essentially starting a march to neutral. ## Slowdown underway: taking stock

In times of high uncertainty it is good to take stock. Oddly, the uncertainty at the moment appears to be more about the FOMC reaction function than the incoming data.

Since our March projection overhaul we have been expecting real GDP growth to slow from over 3% in 2023 to something closer to 1-1/2% in 2024 (Q4/Q4). Roughly 1 pp of that forecast was because we thought the fiscal boost to 2023 growth of over 1 pp was turning to neutral or slight drag in 2024. That appears to be unfolding. Figures 4 and 5 show the slowdown in public sector construction spending, essentially flat year-to-date, and government spending and investment, which rose 4.6% in 2023 and slowed closer to 2% in H1. We expect government spending (and employment) continues to slow in the second half of this year.

The two big components of the slowdown underway have been government and consumer spending, and both continue to unfold.

Second, we have been expecting consumer spending to slow. As Figure 9 shows, real personal consumption expenditures slowed to 1.9% annualized in the first half of this year. The monthly composition has been different from 2023 so we doubt this is residual seasonality, repeating a weak first half followed by stronger second half. We project the 1.9% annualized pace in H1 is followed by a 1.8% annualized pace in Q2. With a few other less important pieces, those two segments (government and consumption) represent the bulk of the slowdown to \~1-1/2% real GDP growth we project in 2024. We expect that softness continues into 2025 until the impact of rate cuts takes hold.

Real GDP of over 3% in 2023 appears to be stepping down closer to 1- 1/2% in 2024.

Our projection for consumer spending slower has rested on Figures 10 and 11. While upper income households remain awash with high levels of liquid assets and equity market wealth, the bottom two-thirds of the income distribution look to be under more pressure. Delinquency rates have moved up. Covid benefits have ended. Balance sheets have deteriorated. Those households paying higher net interest are not the households receiving the net interest.

High income households remain awash in high levels of cash and equity market wealth.

The business sector of the economy has generally looked somewhat soggy for the last year and a half, net of the fiscal stimulus, in our view. The ISM surveys have been low. Investment has generally been weak outside of the segments with fiscal support. Some rolling improvement and ups and downs have offered some support. But the forward looking orders and shipments data continue to just head sideways. One good example of the rolling ups and downs is residential investment, which was an outsized contributor to the growth in final demand in Q1. However, as Figure 13 shows, single family permits, a good predictor of residential investment, have been declining the last several months, pointing to a weak H2.

## Slowdown underway and recession risks elevated

Last week we updated our recession probabilities in a note "Recession probabilities elevated but stable". With fiscal support falling away, and the business sector outside of the IRA and Chips Act support having acted as though interest rates were elevated, what is left? Consumption. The expansion looks increasingly narrowly driven we have written, as the upper income households benefit from a positive wealth shock from rising equity prices, and elsewhere saving rates remain low, consumption elevated relative to income. If consumer spending stumbles, that's a big part of final demand and would be expected to feed upstream and across sectors.

The expansion has looked narrowly driven and vulnerable for some time. Our assessment is that recession risks remain elevated, but little changed in recent months.

We expect the growth slowdown underway to see the labor market slow on the back of that. As Figure 14 shows, output and the labor market have been well aligned for the last several quarters. If output growth slows, the labor market should slow with it. Even prior to the July employment report, we were projecting an average pace of nonfarm payroll employment of 165K in Q3 and 130K in Q4. The current 3-month moving average is 170K. If our growth outlook is right, job gains should slow further from here. We'll know more Friday when the state employment data is released, but we forecasted the June gain was held down by Hurricane Beryl by 20K, and could be more. We also published a detailed take on the Sahm Rule being triggered last week (link here).

## This should not be expected to be a straight line

One forecast difficulty the last few years has been the new era we are in, and that includes new seasonal patterns in the economy. Are they here to stay? An echo of the pandemic? A shift in the center of economic gravity south? We suspect some of the latter. We have seen these patterns in both retail sales data as well as the employment data. As Figure 15 shows, last year we saw relatively soft seasonal inflows into employment in summer. That held down the seasonally adjusted change in employment. However, slower seasonal inflows implied slower seasonal outflows too. That meant that in September, when seasonal employment falls, it fell by less than the seasonal factors expected, pushing up the seasonally adjusted increase in employment. The slowdown everyone is worrying about now could look different in October, after the release of the September employment report.

Diminished seasonal amplitude from activity moving south during Covid could be depressing summer employment gains and boosting September.

## Will we know the recession when we see it?

Probably not. At the moment, initial and continuing claims look relatively range-bound. A falloff in hiring would be expected to push up continuing claims as the outflows slow and for any given inflow the stock would rise. Initial claims should rise too, not necessarily because layoffs are rising but because if finding a new job is not as easy, people become more likely to file for benefits.

One measure that we have pushed back on as not useful has been job openings, since their cyclicality changes over the course of the business cycle. ## 25 bp vs 50 bp ….A more activist regime?

In a note last month we walked through the election implications (see The US Election Compendium), so we skip that here. One takeaway, many of the impacts to come we expect to affect the economy in 2026, rather than the 2025 outlook.

We see 25 bp cuts as more likely unless there is more deterioration than currently evident.

In another note at the end of last year, "A New Era of Macro Vol III", we continued to discuss why we could emerge into a more activist regime for monetary policy. Heightened uncertainty, and reasons beyond the pandemic, pointed to a new regime, which we first described in 2021 in "A New Era of Macro Vol". With heightened data dependence, accompanied by higher volatility in that data, together that could imply many more ups and downs in policy rates decisions resting on those data outcomes. In addition, what does the structure of the economy look like? That leaves assessments of neutral rates and the responsiveness of the economy open to new uncertainty too.

But forecasting has been pretty humbling, in this regime of heightened volatility.

We already appear to be in an era where tightening cycles are not 25 bp increases, but 50 bp or 75 bp. The FOMC could want to course correct down faster than we expect. Our sense is some FOMC participants assume they are not out of the inflation woods yet, and while the risks in the FOMC's assessment may be shifting, we expect not enough for them to signal they potentially made a policy mistake. We do not really know if they are behind the curve, either. Our guess is that there is still a little hawkishness lurking, but we could be wrong. We also appear to be taking the July employment report more in stride than others, awaiting more details on potential hurricane impacts. But we are sympathetic to other arguments. Another potential implication could be, if the July employment report cleared the bar to generate enough concern the FOMC starts cutting 50 bp per meeting, that has implications for the likelihood of being back to the zero lower bound if conditions deteriorate more meaningfully.

While we expect a 25 bp rate cut at each of the September, November and December FOMC meetings, and still sense some hawkishness lurking on the FOMC, we admit they went up a lot faster than we thought. They could go down faster too.

## New rate projection

Our new meeting-by-meeting rate path is below. We do not have strong conviction whether the FOMC may want to do a series of cuts to recalibrate and then pause to reassess conditions, or just start an orderly march to neutral. Of course the path would also depend on the data and ongoing improvement on inflation. We think the FOMC might not want to race straight to neutral until they see evidence the base effects on inflation in Q1 are being lapped and the year-over-year rates are more clearly headed lower. Then, given our economic outlook, if concerns over the expansion are already emerging, they are more likely to get worse than better as we head into 2025 and the labor market slows with a lag. Real GDP growth of 1-1/2% and nonfarm payroll employment below 125K (our projection for H1 2025) per month might sound ok in theory, but in practice it might raise concerns the economy has slowed too far, or the expansion has become too vulnerable. Our base case at the moment is the FOMC wants to get out of restrictive policy more quickly, in an orderly way, and once inflation is close to 2%, as we project in Q2 of next year, there would be no reason to not to do so quickly.
