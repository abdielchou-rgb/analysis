## Executive summary

Although post-reopening recovery remained the major theme for China in 2023, the path has been bumpy and uneven. Activity rebounded strongly in 1Q (10.4%q/q saar) after the rapid ending of COVID-related drags, but then slumped in 2Q (-0.8% q/q saar). Encouragingly, economic activity has been bottoming since August, with policy shifting to become growth-friendly in 2H. Meanwhile, performance has been divergent across sectors. The housing market witnessed the worst downturn in China<sup>’</sup>s history. By contrast, beneficiary sectors in China<sup>’</sup>s structural transformation, e.g. new energy and NEV (new energy vehicles) sectors, made remarkable progress, and China is on track to becoming the largest auto exporting country.

As we turn towards 2024, we think the government will likely set the annual growth target unchanged at “around 5%<sup>”</sup>. While the government is unlikely to introduce a massive policy stimulus (Question 1), the growth-friendly policy stance will continue into 2024, with strengthened coordination between fiscal and monetary policies (Question 2). As the impact of modest easing measures will be reflected in the coming quarters, we expect the economy can maintain its recovery momentum in 1Q24 (5.1%q/q saar), before moderating to trend growth in 2H24 (4.3%q/q saar). Our forecast of 2024 growth stands at 4.9%.

An important task in 2024 is to manage the downside risk in the economy, particularly from the housing market correction and its spillover risks. Housing faces problems on both demand and supply sides, which will persist in 2024 (Question 3). The government<sup>’</sup>s housing stabilization efforts may not be sufficient to stabilize the housing market in the near term, yet public housing can partly offset the housing drag. In the absence of a housing market recovery, policymakers will step up efforts to mitigate risks arising from local government hidden debt. There may also be increasing risks associated with the banking sector, especially for regional banks, e.g. credit quality concerns, NIM compression and capital adequacy pressure (Question 4).

China also faces a problem of demand-supply imbalance and consequent deflation pressure. Even though consumption was the primary growth driver in 2023 (contributing about 80% to GDP growth in 2023), the consumption recovery has been lagging behind that in production. Income growth and adjustment in household savings jointly contribute to consumption recovery, but the absence of policy support for consumption has been a caveat (Question 5). Deflation pressure will likely fade in 2024, with the turnaround in global commodity prices and domestic pork prices, but low inflation will stay along with insufficient domestic demand (Question 6).

The shift in policy focus to “stability<sup>”</sup> does not mean the government will abandon other policy objectives. In fact, structural transformation has contributed to divergent performance across sectors. The government prioritizes technological innovation and modernizing the industrial system (Question 7). New economy sectors have grown rapidly, but not sufficiently to offset the decline in headwind sectors (most notably the housing market). The CEWC called for a balance between the two, i.e. maintaining the lift from new sectors but reducing the drag from the downturn sectors.

While China<sup>’</sup>s economic performance is mainly domestic driven, external conditions matter. Exports (in USD terms) declined in 2023, but in volume terms maintained positive growth. We expect export performance to be stable in 2024, with mitigated pressure on portfolio outflows and CNY exchange rate (Question 8). Nonetheless, global supply chain relocation seems to have accelerated since the Ukraine war, as resilience-based considerations seem to prevail efficiencybased considerations amid rising geopolitical tension, in addition to the change in comparative advantages and the US-China relationship. In response, China has made efforts to diversify exports, to invest overseas and promote self-sufficiency in technologies (Question 9).

2024 also brings a record number of elections across the world, including Taiwan<sup>’</sup>s election in January and a US presidential election in November. While elections often have little impact on underlying macro trends, markets have typically been volatile when facing geopolitical uncertainties. US-China strategic competition will persist. The competition in technology, financial and human rights areas may become more complicated into the election year (Question 10).

## 1. What is the government’s policy framework?

Throughout 2023, a general comment from many market participants was that policy responses to shocks have been “too little, too late<sup>”</sup>. Facing various challenges in the economy (e.g. deflation pressure, insufficient domestic demand and housing market correction), the July politburo meeting put growth as a policy priority. Yet the series of pro-growth measures has been piecemeal and gradual from the market perspective. The Central Economic Work Conference did not mitigate that policy disappointment: markets perceive that the probability of bolstered stimulus remained low although the growth target likely will stay unchanged at “around 5%<sup>”</sup> in 2024.

Such a policy disappointment reflects multiple discrepancies of views comparing markets and policymakers.

The first discrepancy arises from different assessments of current economic situation. Markets generally view the post-reopening recovery as “weaker than expected<sup>”</sup>. Average real GDP growth in 2020-2023 (incorporating JPM forecast of 2023 growth at 5.2%) is 4.6%, lower than the trend growth in pre-pandemic years. At the beginning of the this year (after a quick post-COVID restart), the market was expecting a strong recovery and perceived the 5% growth target as too conservative.

This is different from the government<sup>’</sup>s perspective. The CEWC statement recognized difficulties and challenges in the economy, yet “overall the favorable conditions are stronger than unfavorable factors. The basic trend of economic recovery and long-term improvement has not changed.<sup>”</sup> The official media praised this year<sup>’</sup>s economic work as meeting expectations (on track to achieve the annual growth target), and criticized the market<sup>’</sup>s “weaker-than-expected<sup>”</sup> assessment as being due to expectations which were too high.

The second discrepancy arises because of different benchmarks used by policymakers and investors in assessing the appropriate magnitude of policy easing. A typical argument from investors is that the US government has quickly adopted a massive stimulus package after the COVID outbreak, which is critical to the solid recovery in the US economy (the US is the only major economy that has returned to above pre-pandemic trend growth).

Yet policymakers tend to draw policy lessons more from historical lessons than cross-country comparison. The different policy responses between China and the US since the pandemic outbreak mirrored the different policy responses after the 2008 Global Financial Crisis, though the policy choices and policy lessons are the opposite. China<sup>’</sup>s 4-trillion stimulus package in 2008 successfully led China out of the global crisis with strong economic performance in 2009-2011 (average annual growth 9.9%), but the side effects were also obvious. This explained the reluctance of Chinese policymakers for another massive policy stimulus package.

The third discrepancy arises from different priorities of policy objectives. Compared to market investors, policymakers not only focus on macro objectives (growth, inflation, unemployment) but also structural transformation, social (e.g. common prosperity) and security (e.g. security in supply chain, energy and food) objectives. For instance, the top task for 2024 economic work is to develop a modern industrial system via science and technology innovations. For the same reason, the government<sup>’</sup>s tolerance for housing market correction is higher than the market has expected.

The fourth discrepancy comes from the debate about the room for further policy stimulus, and whether now is the time to fully deploy the room policy has to maneuver. Some investors and renowned policy advisors have argued that China should shift to a “whatever it takes<sup>”</sup> strategy. In particular, China could take advantage of healthy balance sheet of the central government to introduce a massive fiscal stimulus, and meanwhile cut policy rates massively to restore domestic demand and resolve deflationary pressure.

Policymakers think differently. Yes the central government<sup>’</sup>s balance sheet is relatively healthy, but only because local governments have taken disproportionately high fiscal responsibility. Taking together central and local government debts, including local government hidden debt, the total has reached about 100% of GDP. This has exceeded the threshold of government debt level (75-100% of GDP), above which academic studies show negative relationship between debt level and economic growth. On the monetary policy front, policy rate (7-day reverse repo rate) has fallen to a historical low at 1.8%, and is not far away from the zero lower bound if the PBOC shifts to an aggressive rate cut strategy.

The fifth discrepancy relates to the assessment on policy transmission channels. Path-dependence suggests that China<sup>’</sup>s policy response will prioritize the supply side, especially to support manufacturing upgrade and green technologies. By contrast, we have argued that demand-side policy support is more effective to deal with the deflation pressure, and equal treatment between manufacturing and service upgrade is important to resolve the structural unemployment problem. These differing policy recommendations are associated with different assessments of the transmission mechanisms of fiscal and monetary easing measures and their effectiveness.

# 2. How will fiscal and monetary policy coordinate?

The CEWC stated that policy adjustment should be strengthened in 2024, that proactive fiscal policy should be appropriately intensified with improving quality and efficiency, while prudent monetary policy should be flexible, appropriate, precise and effective. Meanwhile, policy tool innovations and policy coordination should be strengthened. What do these high-level and rather vague policy statements mean for fiscal and monetary policy in 2024?

In our baseline forecast, fiscal and monetary policy will better coordinate with each other in 2024 than in 2023. Improved coordination has two-fold meaning. First, both fiscal and monetary policy will be accommodative in 2024. This is different from the observation in 2023 that expansionary monetary policy and contractionary fiscal policy coexist. Second, the central bank<sup>’</sup>s liquidity management is important to support expansionary fiscal policy (government bond issuance). More directly, the PBOC<sup>’</sup>s structural monetary policy instruments will play a more active quasi-fiscal role in 2024.

To understand China<sup>’</sup>s fiscal policy stance, it is important to distinguish three concepts of fiscal deficit. The narrowest definition is official fiscal deficit in the fiscal budget. It increased from 2.8% of GDP in 2022 to 3.0% of GDP in 2023, and further increased to 3.8% of GDP in October (though only 500 bn yuan out of the 1 trillion additional fiscal deficit was deployed in 2023). The utilized budgetary fiscal deficit increased by 0.6% of GDP in 2023 (from 2.8% to 3.4% of GDP), a key argument for proactive fiscal policy from the government<sup>’</sup>s perspective.

The second definition is aggregate fiscal deficit in the fiscal budget, which also includes the quota of special government bond that is not part of fiscal deficit but part of government debt management. In 2023, the quota of special local government bond increased from 3.65 trillion yuan in 2022 to 3.8 trillion yuan. However, because actual utilization of special local government bond in 2022 was much higher at 5.23 trillion yuan (including around 1.1 trillion yuan carry-over from the previous year and an addition of 500 billion yuan in August), the aggregate fiscal deficit was smaller in 2023 (6.4% of GDP vs. 7.1% in 2022).

The third and broader definition is the augmented fiscal deficit that we have used in our analysis, which further includes off-budgetary items such as LGFV borrowing, net land sales and special refinancing via policy banks. Our estimates show that the augmented fiscal deficit narrowed from 12.9% of GDP in 2022 to 12.1% in 2023. Net land sales and LGFV borrowing were both smaller in 2023, while special refinancing bonds provide a meaningful offset.

What will happen in 2024? In our baseline assumption, the official fiscal deficit will increase to 4.2% of GDP (including 3.8% in 2024 fiscal budget and 500 billion yuan carry-over from 2023), the aggregate fiscal deficit in the fiscal budget will increase from 6.4% of GDP in 2023 to 6.9% in 2024. Meanwhile, augmented fiscal deficit will be marginally higher at 12.3% of GDP in 2024, as off-budgetary items tend to be contractionary (Table 2.1).

Our assumption incorporates important changes in fiscal policy. First, fiscal policy will truly become accommodative, though fiscal thrust is still modest. Second, there is a shift from less transparent off-budgetary items to more transparent budgetary fiscal deficit, which is a positive change. Third, central government will increase its fiscal deficit to partially mitigate fiscal difficulties by local governments.

Meanwhile, we expect fiscal support will continue to prioritize investment, especially infrastructure investment for new economy sectors and R&D for manufacturing upgrade.

On the monetary policy front, the PBOC cut policy rates twice (from 2% to 1.8% for 7-day reverse repo rate and from 2.75% to 2.5% for 1-year MLF rate) and RRR twice (25bp cut each) in 2023 (Figure 2.1). Credit policy was generally modestly accommodative, in that TSF growth was relatively stable (9.4%oya in November 2023 vs. 9.6% in December 2022). Credit impulse (the gap between TSF growth and nominal GDP growth) widened to 5.5%pts in November (vs. 4.3%pts in December 2022), modestly above the neutral range of 2-3%pts (Figure 2.2). In addition, the PBOC also expanded its structural monetary policy instruments (outstanding balance from 6.4 trillion yuan at 4Q22 to 7 trillion yuan in 3Q23), with new instruments created to support carbon emission reduction and science / technology innovations (Figure 2.3).

We expect the 2024 monetary policy stance will be similar, and the PBOC will further expand structural monetary policy instruments.

The PBOC will adopt similar attenuation approach in policy rates, with two 10bp rate cuts (in 1Q and 3Q) in our forecast. We believe policy rates will continue to move lower in the coming years, due to the slowdown in China<sup>’</sup>s potential growth and higher debt in the economy. However, the PBOC is reluctant to cut policy rates aggressively, reflecting doubts about policy effectiveness, the asymmetric political pressure in policy rate decision (wide support for rate cuts but strong resistance for rate hikes from other government agencies) and the concern that lower interest rates will encourage soft-budget-constraint entities (e.g. local governments and SOEs) to further lever up.

Regarding credit policy, the CEWC has an interesting nuance change by stating that “TSF growth should be in line with economic growth and inflation targets<sup>”</sup>, instead of “in line with nominal GDP growth<sup>”</sup>. The break-up implicitly recognized that inflation is now well below target, hence the new statement will tolerate above-normal credit impulse in the near term. Our forecasts expect TSF growth at 9.7% in 2023 (with average credit impulse at 5.0%) and 9.5% in 2024 (with average credit impulse at 5.1%), i.e. credit policy will be modestly accommodative in both 2023 and 2024.

The third element of the PBOC<sup>’</sup>s monetary policy instruments, so-called structural monetary policy, likely will play a more important role in 2024. The PBOC had 7 trillion yuan structural monetary policy instruments outstanding as of 3Q23 (including 2.9 trillion yuan PSL outstanding), in addition to an aggregate of nearly 10 trillion standard liquidity operations as of December (including 2.845 trillion OMO, 7.075 trillion MLF and a small amount of SLF). Structural monetary policy instruments serve as quasi-fiscal measures to supplement fiscal operations. In 2024, our baseline assumption incorporates a 1-trillion yuan package funded by PSL to support public housing and urban village developments, as well as an expansion of re-lending facility to support debt restructuring as part of local government hidden debt resolution. In fact, the PBOC injected 350 billion yuan of PSL funds into three policy banks in December, a policy move in line with our assumption.

In addition, coordination between fiscal and monetary policy is also evident in PBOC<sup>’</sup>s liquidity management with increasing scale of government bond issuance. For instance, net increase in government bond issuance totaled about 3.6 trillion yuan in 4Q23, in response the PBOC increased the scale of OMO/MLF operation by 2.1 trillion yuan. The liquidity operations led to a modest decline in CGB yields despite increases in issuance (Figure 2.4).

## 3. Will the housing market stabilize in 2024?

China's housing market continued to decline in 2023 and, in our view, has entered an overshooting stage. In the first 11 months of 2023 (Figure 3.1), new home sales fell 16.3%oya (after the 26.8% decline in 2022), new home starts fell 22% (after 40% decline in 2022), and real estate investment fell 9.4% (vs. -10% in 2022). The only housing activity indicator that registered positive growth is new home completion, which rose 17.7% (vs. -14.3% in 2022). Meanwhile, new home prices stabilized in 1H23 but started to decline again in 2H23, falling 1.7% between May and November. The dramatic fall in new homes sales and new home starts has taken them below our estimates of fundamental volumes to be expected over the long term, and there is no sign of bottoming despite the latest round of housing policy relaxation since August (Figure 3.2).

The challenges in the housing market come from both demand and supply sides. On the demand side, housing demand is weak both because of structural and cyclical reasons. Changes in demographic factors (e.g. new household formation, birth rate, Figure 3.3), urbanization, home ownership and purpose for home purchase jointly suggest that new home demand has been, and will continue to be on a downward trend, with home upgrade demand steadily replacing

new home demand. From the cyclical perspective, home demand is weak because of weak income expectations, concerns about home delivery and further house price decline. This is why recent demand-side easing measures (including relaxation of mortgage policies and administrative controls) are insufficient to restore market confidence and bring back market demand.

On the supply side, funding difficulties for private real estate developers remain a major obstacle. The regulators have called for bank support for real estate developers with equal treatment among different types of ownerships. However, banks have good reasons to be more cautious about lending to private developers. Private developers (distressed or surviving developers) usually have much weaker home sales (the most important funding source for developers, Figure 3.4), and they have refrained from making new investments. However, the bulk of bank loan support is developer loans to support new projects with collateral assets. It is not necessarily discrimination between SOE and non-SOE developers, instead a risk-based lending decision given the different business performance between SOE and non-SOE developers. In addition, an anti-corruption campaign in the financial industry will make loan officers more reluctant to lend to non-SOE sectors.

Another supply-side problem is a serious inventory overhang. Despite a sharp deceleration in new home starts and continued efforts of prioritizing home delivery, new homes under construction are still more than 5 years of new home sales (Figure 3.5). The issue is more concerning if we take into account additional supply from the secondary home market.

In such an overshooting environment, it will take excessive rather than “just enough<sup>”</sup> policy responses to stabilize housing activity and market expectations. Policymakers are not prepared for such a dramatic policy shift. Hence, we expect housing market activity to continue to decline in 2024, in other words, the probability of housing market bottoming in 2024 is relatively low.

We expect new home prices will be relatively stable in 2024, as observed in the past two years. The government has adopted the strategy to protect new home prices at the cost of massive volume correction. So far the strategy has been effective. Nonetheless, secondary home prices have been more volatile and the price decline seems to be accelerating in recent months. If secondary home prices fall below new home prices, the risk is that the administrative control on new home price may not work any longer, and the negative feedback loop between new home price and secondary home prices, as well as between home prices and housing transactions, may intensify.

This implies that the housing market will continue to drag on economic growth. Real estate investment as % of GDP has fallen from the peak of 13.9% in 2020 to an estimated 9.6% in 2023. In value-added terms, the property sector contribution to GDP (including both construction and real estate services) has declined from 14.4% in 2020 to 12.7% as of 3Q23. Our estimates suggest that housing market correction lowered real GDP growth by 1.2-1.4%pts in both 2022 and 2023.

This is why public housing is important in the macro-stabilization efforts. First of all, there is massive demand for public housing and rental housing in the new urbanization process, i.e. to grant migrant workers who reside in the city but without official residence status (known as hukou) the same privi lege as other urban citizens (e.g. social security benefits, home purchase rights, education). The beneficiary group can be as large as 18% of total population (the gap between 65% urbanization ratio by residence and 47% urbanization ratio by hukou), or 250 million people. The huge replacement demand (from rural to urban homes) in this new urbanization process is the way out of current real estate dilemma, and the answer lies in public housing and rental housing.

On the supply side, the scale of public housing has fallen notably in the past decade (Figure 3.6). The peak time of public housing construction was 2011-2015, when 36 million units of public housing were constructed, followed by the large-scale shanty town renovation (funded by the PBOC<sup>’</sup>s PSL) in 2015-18. Since then, the scale of public housing has come down notably to 1.1 million units in 2021. Although the scale increased again after 2022, it has stayed around 2 million units per annum in recent years.

![](images/b537e7b3575709a579ee091f5dc4c397f951ac5eb02348daae792422653eb3ce.jpg)

The major constraint of public housing development is funding. In the past, public housing is mainly financed by special local government bond and net land sales. In the current situation, a desirable policy action is to use fiscal (special government bond) or monetary (e.g. PSL) sources to establish a housing stability fund. Our baseline scenario assumes a 1-trillion yuan package funded by the PBOC to support public housing. It is equivalent to 8% of total real estate investment in 2023, and can reduce the decline of real estate investment in 2024 from 8-10% to 2-4%. This policy initiative can also serve a second purpose: it can accelerate the inventory correction in the housing market, if public housing purchases undeveloped land and incomplete projects from exiting developers.

In the longer term, the funding support for public housing can come from rural land reform. In the current two-tier land system in China, urban land is owned by the state, and housing is a commodity through land sales by the government and construction by developers. Rural land is collectively owned, and farmers get free land for residential purpose but it is not transferable (this explains high home ownership ratio in the rural area). In the new urbanization process, when the replacement demand of new urban citizens will be resolved by large scale of public housing and rental housing, it will also involve the transfer and change of use of rural land. The importance of rural land reform, if implemented, can be compared to the housing market reform in late 1990s.

# 4. What to watch for on financial stability, other than local government hidden debt?

Since November 2022, we have emphasized the LGFV (local government financing vehicle) risk in the aftermath of a major housing market correction (see here, here, here and here). The early warning has been confirmed, with increasing stresses faced by LGFV entities. It has also triggered a new round of local government hidden debt resolution efforts. In 4Q23 alone, about 1.5 trillion yuan re-financing local government bonds were issued. The new round of debt-swap arrangements, along with large-scale debt restructuring between banks and LGFV entities, aims to mitigate liquidity pressure and avoid massive defaults among LGFV entities.

The market has reacted positively to these measures. Credit spreads of LGFV bonds have come down significantly in 4Q23, with strong perception of implicit government protection. This is encouraging, but risk is still significant. We have warned about the risk that an unexpected LGFV default would have an out-sized impact on financial markets, and such a default cannot be ruled out as local government hidden debt is not transparent and local liquidity stresses are divergent. In addition, the current measures can mitigate liquidity stresses but are short of providing a fundamental resolution for the local government hidden debt problem. The local government hidden debt issue does appear, however, to being managed so that is less of a systemic risk in the near term.

So what should we watch for on financial system vulnerability? The CEWC statement listed three sources of risks: housing, local government debt and small and mid-sized financial institutions. The diagnosis is right. While housing and local government hidden debt have attracted most attention, the banking system, especially regional banks, will likely face increasing pressure (see link).

The risk associated with regional banks is not new. In fact, the problem for regional banks materialized in 2019, as exemplified in the failure of Baoshang Bank, Jinzhou Bank and Hengfeng Bank. In the 4Q19 regulatory assessment, the regulatory assessment identified 545 banks as high-risk (8-D category), about 12.4% of total banks (by number).

Regional banks are more risky than large banks, in part due to geopolitical restrictions on their business operations and higher concentration risk. For instance, areas with larger housing market correction or more stressful situation for local government hidden debt will naturally add more pressure on regional banks in the region.

Regional banks also tend to have more problems in their business operations, e.g. stronger influence from local governments on business decisions, higher liability cost (competing for deposits), more fragile funding sources, and higher-risk assets (competing for high-quality assets or borrowers). A lack of professional expertise also adds to these risks.

The size of regional banks, if including city commercial banks and rural financial institutions, totalled 108.5 trillion yuan as of 3Q23, i.e. 26.5% of total bank assets or 87% of GDP. The size is smaller than large commercial banks (173.2 trillion yuan) but larger than share-holding commercial banks (69.6 trillion yuan). By number, the regional banks dominate the banking system. In the PBOC<sup>’</sup>s Financial Stability Report 2023 (released on December 22), the stress testing covered 19 domestic systemically important banks (D-SIBs, including all large banks, most share-holding commercial banks and a few city commercial banks) and 3966 non-D-SIBs.

For instance, LGFV loans reached 44 trillion yuan as of 3Q23 (18.8% of total bank loans). The standalone credit risk could be high, in that 76% of LGFVs have cash over short-term debt ratio below 1, and 11% of them have interest coverage ratio below 1. But the reported NPL ratio for LGFV loans is very low (well below 1% based on individual banks<sup>’</sup> annual reports), given the absence of defaults due to strong implicit government guarantee. While the latest local government debt resolution measures can mitigate liquidity stresses and reduce bad loan write-off, the cost comes in alternative formats such as haircut losses in debt restructuring, crowding out of new lending, distortion in credit allocation, weaker bank profit and erosion in bank capital, etc.

Similarly, the PBOC has expanded its structural monetary policy operation to encourage bank lending to targeted sectors. As of 3Q23, SME / inclusive financing loans rose 24.1%oya to 28.74 trillion yuan, green loans rose 36.8%oya to 28.58 trillion yuan, and rural-related loans rose 15.1% to 55.8 trillion yuan. Even taking into account any overlaps in these loans, the total size of these policy-encouraged targeted loans may account for 30-40% of total bank loans. The question is whether these loans are business viable or can be justified by conventional lending practice. The National Financial Regulatory Administration reported that the average lending rate for new SME loans fell to a record low of 3.68% (vs. 7.9% in 2017), which is positive from policymakers<sup>’</sup>s perspective but banks may be taking excessive risks. So far, the NPL ratio for these loans is low, but perhaps only because of the very strong loan growth and regulatory forbearance of evergreen loans in these areas after the COVID outbreak. An end of regulatory forbearance may lead to a notable deterioration of credit quality in these areas.

Compared to regional banks, large banks are in a stronger position. But they are not immune to risks, either. A warning signal is that net interest margin has fallen from 2.2% at end-2019 to 1.725% in 3Q23 (Figure 4.5), and for large banks the NIM has fallen from 2.12% to 1.66% during the same period (Figure 4.6). This has fallen below the conventional 1.8% threshold, below which banks will face the risk of negative feedback loop of credit cycle (lower profits, weaker capital, lending constraints, economic slowdown and further lower rates). The constraint in capital adequacy could be a problem for major banks in China, as five Chinese banks (ABC, BOC, CCB, ICBC and Bank of Communications) are included in the GSIB list (global systemically important banks), and the estimated shortage in TLAC (total loss absorbing capacity) is about 2.9 trillion yuan in the next few years. The pressure for further lowering the funding cost of the real economy also imposes challenges for the banking sector.

## 5. What will drive consumption?

Consumption performance has been volatile since the pandemic. In pre-pandemic years (2018-19), Consumption contributed an average of 61% (or 3.9%pts) to GDP growth. The contribution to GDP growth turned negative in 2020 at -7% (or -0.15%pt), the first and only time on record, and rebounded to 58% in 2021 (or 4.9%pts). In 2022, consumption<sup>’</sup>s contribution to GDP growth fell again to 33% (or 1%pt) due to strict COVID restrictions. After reopening, consumption was a primary growth driver in 2023, contributing 83% (or 4.3%pts) to GDP growth in the first three quarters (Figure 5.1). Nonetheless, the four-year average contribution of 2.5%pts is much lower than in pre-pandemic years.

A key component for consumption is retail sales, which registered negative growth in 2020 and 2022. The average cumulative annual growth of retail sales in 2020-23 was only 3.7% (or 2.2% in real terms), much lower than the average growth in 2018-19 (8.4% in nominal term and 6.4% in real term, see Figure 5.1). The downshift in consumption has dragged on China<sup>’</sup>s economic recovery.

Figure 5.1: Retail sales and consumption contribution to GDP growth
![](images/4454d435512e9e3c973dcc812f3d96dde74df7076b15de26a22f7cef78d74222.jpg)

The slow consumption recovery after the reopening was mainly due to two reasons. First, households<sup>’</sup> income expectations remain weak, hence the propensity of consumption has not fully recovered. Second, the government has not rolled out meaningful consumption stimulus measures.

The NBS consumer confidence survey showed that consumer confidence, including income expectations and employment expectations, collapsed in 2022 (after the Shanghai lockdown), yet it has continued to stay at weak levels after economic reopening in 2023 (Figure 5.2). Field investigation suggests that a number of regulatory headwind sectors (e.g. education tutorials, internet, financial services and housing) and government employees are facing job security concerns and pay-cut pressure. As a result, the consumption upgrade trend observed for the growing group of upper middle income households in the past decade has been replaced with con-

sumption downgrade. This also raises the question on the creditability of official statistics that household disposable income has cumulatively grown 28% in the past four years, of which urban households registered 22% increase and rural households registered 35% increase (Figure 5.3).

One possible explanation is a structural imbalance in the labor market and uneven income growth across households. For instance, some “white-collar<sup>”</sup> sectors may have observed much slower income growth or even lower income, but “bluecollar<sup>”</sup> sectors and low-end services may register stronger income growth given the return of job opportunities but limited labor supply in these sectors.

A consequence of weak income expectations is the slow recovery in consumption and persistent excess saving in households. The dynamics of household saving rate in China is more similar to Europe, in that saving rate is still above pre-pandemic levels. This is in sharp contrast to the US

households, where the household saving rate has fallen below pre-pandemic levels and spending of excess saving has contributed to strong consumption recovery (Figure 5.4).

The slow recovery in the propensity to consume is also reflected in the large increase in household deposits. In 2022, household deposits in the banking system increased y 17.8 trillion yuan, much higher than the increase of 9.9 trillion yuan in 2021 and previous peak of 11.3 trillion yuan in 2020. The large increase in household deposits continued in 2023, rising 14.7 trillion yuan in the first 11 months (in line with 14.9 trillion yuan increase in the same period of 2022). The large increase in household deposits was in part due to reduced spending in home purchase and relocation of financial assets from the equity market or wealth management products (Figure 5.5), and in part due to precautionary saving especially from upper-middle income households.

What will determine the consumption outlook in 2024? Our baseline forecast is for a 6% increase in consumption (in real terms), of which 5% from income growth and 1% from adjustment of household saving rate to pre-pandemic levels.

The risk might be biased to the downside. First, income growth might have been weaker than official statistics, which may occur if the sample is biased toward employed workers or biased in employment sectors. Second, weak profit growth in the corporate sector in 2023 may point to a delayed/weak improvement in labor market conditions, which will affect income growth. Third, there is no signal that consumer confidence will improve soon, especially among upper-middle income households.

On the other side, there could also be source of upper-side risk. Our baseline forecast does not assume any meaningful consumption stimulus measures in 2024, as we have observed in the past several years. The highly recommended policy option, i.e. consumption voucher or e-CNY payout, has very low probability to be adopted. But we should not rule out the possibility of other consumption support measures. In 2023, there are a few indirect measures to support consumption, such as lowering the mortgage rate for outstanding mortgages and increasing tax deductible for elder- and child-care. The possible policy options that the government may consider

include: (1) product-specific consumption stimulus, e.g. auto, home appliance, cultural tourism as laid out in the NDRC guideline in late July; (2) enhanced social security coverage to reduce precautionary saving and to encourage new birth (though the scale tends to be limited and the impact tends to be gradual); (3) a crackdown on employers building up arrears of wage payments.

## 6. Will deflation end in 2024?

China is an outlier in its inflation dynamics. Instead of facing the common inflation pressure after reopening as observed in other countries, China has been facing intensified deflation pressure. As of November 2023, China<sup>’</sup>s headline CPI fell 0.5%oya (Figure 6.1), and PPI fell 3.0%oya. The GDP deflator was negative at -1.4%oya in both 2Q and 3Q23, and likely widened to -1.7%oya in 4Q.

Figure 6.1: China CPI inflation
![](images/a6f2487d3280517562a13c0d1bdda0d565fe3b2279ab5e0e391a206fbabc0a61.jpg)
Source: NBS, J.P. Morgan

Deflation is a result of various factors. On the external front, global commodity prices were in a deflationary cycle after China<sup>’</sup>s reopening, falling 7.9%oya in November (with the bottom of -24.9%oya in June, Figure 6.2). That lead to PPI deflation and lowered the energy price component in CPI. On the domestic front, food prices fell 4.2%oya in November (vs. +6.2% in January), mainly due to pork price declines (from 11.8%oya in January to -31.8%oya in November). This is the main contributor to CPI deflation.

Figure 6.2: China PPI vs. global commodity price index %oya, both scales
![](images/c4628d52d427d491724bb1a692995128b85c8c92f4bb31b76147a4b39d659db7.jpg)

The imbalance between domestic demand and supply (earlier and faster recovery in production than consumption), in part policy-induced, has contributed to lower core CPI inflation (from 1.0%oya in January to 0.6%oya in November). In the breakdown, core consumer price inflation fell from 1.0%oya in January to 0.6%oya in November, and services inflation fell from 0.8%oya in January to 0.5%oya in November. In addition, declining home prices (hence downward pressure on home rent) and a slack labor market (slow wage growth) are also dis-inflationary.

We expect that deflation will end in 2024, as the above-mentioned factors likely will move in opposite directions. First, global commodity prices likely will move up. JPM Commodity Research forecasts a 15% increase in Brent oil in 2024, while base metal prices are more mixed (copper: 5%; aluminum: 11%; iron ore: -19%). China<sup>’</sup>s PPI may gradually climb out of deflation by mid-2024, with full-year average at around 0.2% (vs. -3.0% in 2023). The energy price component in CPI will no longer be a drag. Second, pork supply has stabilized, hence the pork price likely will bottom near current low levels. With a diminishing pork price drag (which contributed -0.6%pt to November CPI), headline CPI will likely turn positive in 1Q24.

While deflation may end in 2024, the low inflation environment will continue into 2024. Our forecast of average CPI in 2024 is 0.9%, still much lower than the long-term average of 2%. First, the imbalance between domestic demand and supply will continue, as policy still prioritizes investment over consumption. Core CPI inflation will likely stay at low levels (0.5-1%) in the foreseeable future. Insufficient demand has led to intensified price competition among producers, both domestically and internationally. While China<sup>’</sup>s export of disinflation has helped global disinflation dynamics (mainly consumer goods prices), it has also triggered criticism about unfair competition, triggering anti-dumping concerns (e.g. trade dispute from the US and EU on electric vehicles). Second, the drags from weak housing market and slow wage growth will not mitigate.

Lastly, the central bank<sup>’</sup>s inflation objective has been biased on the lower end. Although annual inflation target has been set at “around 3%<sup>”</sup> in almost every year, the PBOC has been tolerant (and comfortable) of undershooting the target. Both medium and long-term average CPI inflation stand at 2%. The fear of high inflation is still dominant among central bankers, even though the current inflation is significantly below the target and there is policy debate whether the PBOC should also introduce a lower bound of target inflation.

Our 2024 inflation outlook has two important implications. First, with the end of deflation, China<sup>’</sup>s nominal GDP growth will be higher than in 2023 (JPM forecasts: 5.5% in 2024 vs. 4.1% in 2023). Second, recent development in domestic inflation dynamics and changes in global financial condition may trigger earlier and more policy rate cuts in 2024. We recently moved forward the forecast timing of next rate cut to 1Q (likely in March, previously mid-2024), with an additional rate cut in 3Q (likely in September).

# 9. Will China’s role in global supply chains change?

There are growing concerns around geopolitical risk and a consequent push for “de-coupling<sup>”</sup> between the US and Chinese economies, amid a broader global supply chain relocation. How will this affect China<sup>’</sup>s supply chain, and how will it impact the globalization process?

Over four decades of fast-growth, China has become a global manufacturing hub. In 2022, China accounted for 18% of global GDP (vs. 2.7% in 1980), 15% of global merchandise goods exports, and 30% of global manufacturing value-added (Figure 9.1).

Figure 9.1: China's global share %of global aggregate
![](images/0a9a8a525cf17831b6716c8534329a4c60cb7c5ebee19aa57e0cf78512b0863e.jpg)

Supply chain relocation out of China is not a new story. It has taken place over the past decade, driven by different factors. There are three important structural trends behind this shift.

Figure 9.2: China's share of global exports by sector
![](images/1f979b0c234f971a4fc8476213cce4cbd1797876da493438b0b748a800c0ae21.jpg)
Source: WITS, J.P. Morgan

First, supply chain relocation is driven by relative change in comparative advantages, i.e. the rise of production cost in China with economic development. The turning point started around 2010: China<sup>’</sup>s working age population peaked in 2011, and new Labor Laws became effective in 2008, jointly pushing up wage costs in China. Land costs also increased along with the housing market boom. In addition, environmental costs also increased with the release of Environment Protection Law in 2014 and focus on green developments. China<sup>’</sup>s share of global exports peaked around 2010 in labor-intensive sectors (Figure 9.2). However, along with the declining share of low value-added products, China has moved up in the value-added chain, gaining export share in higher value-added sectors, such as electronic products and machinery equipment.

The second change is the US-China relationship, especially after the tariff war in 2018. Higher tariffs and intensified US-China conflicts have led to a significant decline of China<sup>’</sup>s share in the US market, benefiting ASEAN economies, Mexico and Canada (Figure 9.3). As the US-China conflicts shifted from trade to technology under the Biden Administration, the negative impact also expanded from high-tariff industries to emerging fundamental technologies and hi-tech areas affected by national security concerns. The combined effect has reduced the bilateral trade between China and the US, but it is more of a re-route rather than de-coupling in that China has increased exports to ASEAN/Mexico/India (see Question 8).

Figure 9.3: US imports by country of origin
![](images/f8e32ccb0f795bb802ace4f443d71e92f449f4c0e29d287462bb1df0e825ec36.jpg)

The third change is associated with geopolitical tension in recent years, especially after the Ukraine war (2022) and Middle-East conflict (2023), and the impact is broader than the US-China strategic competition. Global supply chains are paying more attention to security and resiliency than efficiency. With a shift from “just-in-time<sup>”</sup> to “just-in-case<sup>”</sup>, multinational corporations are seeking a “China+1<sup>”</sup> or “China+N<sup>”</sup> strategy to diversify their supply chain (Figure 9.4).

Figure 9.4: China ranking in global investment plan
![](images/2497445565228070eb679eef5aee9465741a965b0f10de25b4ceaea731570396.jpg)

These structural changes, along with domestic adjustments in policy priorities (e.g. from high-growth to high-quality growth), have led to important changes in China and global economic landscape.

First is diversification in China<sup>’</sup>s trade. By destination, the share of exports to G3 fell from 41.5% in 2018 to 34.3% in the first 11 months of 2023, largely driven by the declining share of exports to the US (from 19.2% to 14.9%). By contrast, the share of exports to Asia ex. Japan rose from 41.9% to 43.1%, and exports to other EMs (including Africa, Latin American and Russia) rose from 12.2% to 15.7% during the same period (Figure 9.5). By products, exports of autos, solar cell and lithium-ion battery have grown exponentially in recent years.

Figure 9.5: Breakdown of China's exports by destination % of total exports
![](images/e440e354aec1c7630917892748016ddef1af8e87a4259ccad1a60171bc9cdfe3.jpg)

China has also stepped up its efforts to promote innovations and technology upgrade. Increases in geopolitical risk and US-China strategic competition have reinforced China<sup>’</sup>s efforts to seek self-sufficiency in its supply chain, from “Made-in-China 2025<sup>”</sup> to priority focus on structural transformation. China<sup>’</sup>s R&D spending, the number of new patent applications, and the size of new economy sectors, have all grown rapidly in recent years (see Question 7). Industrial policy has played an important role, and has received the support from structural monetary policy as well as regulatory policies (differentiated treatments between policy tailwind and headwind sectors). The policy efforts have paid off in some areas (e.g. break-through in new energy and NEV sectors), but the progress is less significant in other areas (e.g. semi-conductor). One side effect is that strengthened government-led industry policy and differentiated treatment across industries have caused policy uncertainties and weak confidence among private entrepreneurs, especially in policy headwind sectors.

The adjustment in global supply chains also led to changing dynamics in cross-border FDIs. Inward FDI reported in the SAFE<sup>’</sup>s BOP data registered the first negative reading in 3Q23. This is a consequence of multiple factors: production cost increase in China; geopolitical concerns and acceleration of global supply chain relocation (adoption of “China+1<sup>”</sup> strategy) after the Ukraine war; reversal of US-China interest rate differentials and CNY as a low-yield funding currency (hence repatriation of cumulative FDI profits to finance global investment); etc.

Meanwhile, Chinese companies have also sped up the pace of overseas investment. For instance, China<sup>’</sup>s ODI to ASEAN

economies in the manufacturing sector increased from US\$ 4.5bn in 2018 to US\$ 8.6 bn in 2021 and US\$8.2bn in 2022 (Figure 9.6). The increase in overseas investment is driven by multiple factors: lower production cost in ODI recipient economies; bypassing the higher tariff from the US; support the government<sup>’</sup>s Belt-and-Road Initiatives; risk diversification of private entrepreneurs (both business risks and policy risks).

![](images/3e22fc8a3b2482ab37ba76b9037d901c35d26b67c7acdf84ae9333c045f45dc4.jpg)

China<sup>’</sup>s response is also reflected in its foreign policy. Recent expansion in BRICS to include oil-producing countries (Saudi Arabia, UAE and Iran) and African countries (Egypt and Ethiopia) can help China to improve energy security and diversify trade relationships, along with the continuation of Belt-and-Road Initiatives.

Overall, China<sup>’</sup>s role as a global manufacturing hub will weaken, but global supply chain relocation cannot happen overnight, hence the process will take time and China will remain an important manufacturer as well as a major market. De-globalization is not yet reality, but traditional trading patterns are being disrupted and new trade corridors are emerging. Emerging Asia (including ASEAN, India and northern Asian countries) are benefiting the most from shifts in global value chains, due to their cost competitiveness, industrial developments, supportive policies, linkages to existing manufacturing hubs and rising middle-income consumers (link).

# 10. How will the US-China relationship evolve in 2024?

Strategic competition between China and the US has been a key driver of China<sup>’</sup>s changing external environment. Since the start of the tariff war in 2018, competition has dominated the bilateral relationship between the two largest economies in the world, despite limited areas of cooperation (e.g. climate change).

The Bali Summit between President Biden and President Xi in November 2022 raised the hope of bilateral dialogue and mitigation in US-China tension, but it quickly faded after the balloon accident in February. The impasse lasted until June when Secretary Blinken visited Beijing, followed by a series of bilateral high-level meetings and another Summit in San Francisco in November. Throughout 2023, the competition in technology and financial areas continued. The US expanded sanctions on Chinese companies and strengthened restrictions on technology and human capital, imposed an investment ban in sensitive technology (high-performing semiconductors, quantum and AI), and the US Congress passed a bill in December that would lead to the closure of Hong Kong<sup>’</sup>s US representative offices. In response, China placed export restrictions on 14 gallium and germanium items.

Market investors are concerned about the geopolitical risk into 2024, as there is an election in Taiwan in January and the US presidential election in November 2024.

Taiwan<sup>’</sup>s election will be held on January 13. The three candidates include Mr. Lai Ching-Te from the traditional pro-independence Democratic Progressive Party (DPP), Mr. Hou Yu-Ih from the main opposition party Kuomintang (KMT) that traditionally holds a more benign cross-strait policy, and Mr. Ko Wen-Je who runs for the Taiwan People<sup>’</sup>s Party. There was an effort of coalition between Mr. Ho and Mr. Ko in November but it fell apart at the last minute. The latest opinion polls suggest a lead by DPP<sup>’</sup>s Lai, though the KMT has narrowed the gap, while Mr. Ko stays in third place.

The market<sup>’</sup>s concern is whether cross-strait tension will escalate significantly, with the war risk increasing, if DPP is reelected. That outturn would strain the cross-strait relationship, however, the November summit suggests that the war risk is very low in the foreseeable future: the US reiterated the one-China policy and China implicitly stated not to take a military option in the near term. Even so, the immediate impact could be on the economic front. Taiwan<sup>’</sup>s exports to mainland China/HKSAR as share of Taiwan<sup>’</sup>s total exports have come down to 36% (from 41% prior to the US-China tariff war) under the DPP administration, and it may further shrink with DPP<sup>’</sup>s re-election. Alternatively, if KMT wins the election, the cross-strait economic relationship may stabilize or

improve.

Another hot spot of geopolitical concern is the South China Sea. China<sup>’</sup>s economic ties with ASEAN economies have been strengthened, but the territory dispute and unstable geo political relationship have persisted. President Xi<sup>’</sup>s recent visit to Vietnam is a positive step, but China<sup>’</sup>s relationship with the Philippines has deteriorated recently. In general, the presence of both Chinese and US military troops in the South China Sea, and regional territory disputes, suggests that geopolitical risk in South China Sea remains significant.

Entering the US election year, the political atmosphere in the US is generally not in favor of any improvement in US-China relationship. However, this does not necessarily mean that the US-China relationship will get much worse from here. Looking back in 2020, for example, it was a year full of rhetorical attacks but saw relative few policy actions undertaken.

On the trade front, the US-China tariff war has been part of the status quo since early 2020 (Figure 10.1). China has been pursuing for tariff reduction, but tariff reduction has high political cost on the US side especially in the election year.

Technology has moved to the center of US-China strategic competition. The US has used sanctions and restrictions, or “small-yard, high fence<sup>”</sup> strategy to maintain its technology advantage over China. Into 2024, China will continue to build a higher ground, while the US fence may be higher.

The geopolitical and national security concerns have also affected the flows in the financial sector. Despite some tentative improvement (e.g. PCAOB postponed de-listing plan for China<sup>’</sup>s ADRs after gaining access to audit documents in December 2022), investment restrictions have generally been tightened. From China<sup>’</sup>s side, market access, cross-border data management, and managed capital accounts will also limit the attractiveness for foreign investors.
