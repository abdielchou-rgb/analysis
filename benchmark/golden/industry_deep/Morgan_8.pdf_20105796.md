# Gold: A New Trendline

Gold's relationship with real yields evolved rather than broke down in 2023, ending the year with a weaker sensitivity to yields but a growing safe haven premium. We see gold fairly priced for now, but prices could move higher as rate cuts approach.

## Key Takeaways

■ Gold's relationship with real yields saw several iterations in 2023, as geopolitical and economic risks and rate cut expectations evolved.

■ The current trendline implies less sensitivity to real yields but a rising safe haven premium leaves the price at a higher absolute level.

■ Gold looks fairly priced for now, but as we get closer to rate cuts, we think the direction is more likely up than down.

Relationships breaking down? At first glance, gold's relationship with real yields appears to have broken down in 2023 ( Exhibit 1 ), with an R2 of just 6%, suggesting real yields were becoming less helpful in analysing gold. This was widely attributed to rising physical demand, particularly from central banks, offsetting the headwinds from rising rates.

Or changing? If we split 2023 into three periods, the R2 between real yields and gold improves significantly ( Exhibit 2 ). January to mid-March saw a steep trendline, with gold reacting to changing real yields as market expectations were that Fed hikes would soon be ending. From mid-March, concerns around the health of the US banking system and global economic growth escalated (see 2011 Parallels?), and central bank purchases accelerated. The trend line shifted higher but also flattened as gold held up well against rising real yields. Finally, from mid-October onwards, as geopolitical tensions escalated in the Middle East, the trendline has shifted up further to incorporate more safe haven premium. However, the slope remains shallow – 10 year real yields fell almost 100 bp between the 25th Oct peak to the late Dec trough, but gold rallied just 5%, well below the +16% and +29% moves that the 1Q24 and 2018-2021 regressions would have implied.

Where next? In our Price Deck , we argued gold is fairly valued around \$2,025/oz. Non-commercial net longs are the highest since May'22, gold's sensitivity to real yields is fading and geopolitical risk remains elevated, but seems largely priced in. However, as the year progresses, if gold's safe haven premium holds, the riskreward is probably more skewed to the upside. Our economists expect four Fed cuts this year, starting in June, and 200 bp in 2025. Looking back to 1990, gold has been on average 6% higher 30 days after the first rate cut. On top of this, even with a shallower regression line, a 100 bp drop in real yields would imply another \~5% upside for gold, while we would expect central bank buying to remain strong, supporting physical demand too.

## Week in Review

Base Metals: Base metal prices were down WoW, weighed down by weak China macro data and a stronger dollar. China's December manufacturing PMI came in at 49, lower than investor expectations, weighing on the outlook for commodities demand. Aluminium rallied at the start of the week on bauxite supply disruptions, but gave back gains through the week – see our view that the rally was likely overdone. Nickel, which was the worst performer in 2023, was down 4% on the week, as LME inventories rose more than 60% since November, while SHFE stockpiles are at three-year highs. Elsewhere, Chile's copper output was down 3.7% MoM, mainly driven by Codelco.

Precious Metals: Precious metals moved lower last week, although gold remained above \$2,000/oz. US Non-Farm Payrolls and the US ISM came in stronger than expected, strengthening the USD, raising questions whether the market moved too fast in terms of pricing Fed rate cuts. That said, the complex was supported by the increased geopolitical tensions. The worst performer last week was palladium, down 8% on the week, as a softening long-term demand outlook weighed.

Bulks: Iron ore remained stable at \$140/t, despite weaker steel output. CISA member mills' production was 1.93 mnt per day for the mid-December period, down 0.1% from the preceding 10 days and down 1.8% YoY. Australia hard coking coal rose to \$333/t last week, as India's steel output remains robust. However, Newcastle thermal coal fell to \$131/t, as Newcastle shipments rallied strongly at the end of the year, combined with weaker coal-fired power output in Japan due to the recent earthquake. The Northwest European (ARA) thermal coal price was broadly flat on the week, in line with the TTF price.
