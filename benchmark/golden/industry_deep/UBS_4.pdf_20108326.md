## US Economic Perspectives US Inflation Monthly: More slowing ahead

## Core inflation continues to gradually slow

 As noted last week, we project a 23bp increase in core PCE prices in April, which would see 12-month core PCE inflation edging down to 2.7% (2.73%) through April, though the risks are that core PCE inflation remains at 2.8% for another month.

We currently project the headline CPI to rise 9bp in May (seasonally-adjusted; 27bp not-seasonally-adjusted) with core CPI prices rising 25bp. This would put the 12-month headline CPI inflation at 3.38% and core CPI inflation 3.54%. That May level of core CPI inflation would be down 7bp from April and also down a solid 39bp from December despite the widely-held view that disinflation has stalled in recent months. We will review these projections as additional data becomes available over the next few weeks.

 A detailed forecast spreadsheet is available on our website.

## We expect more slowing to come

Much like a year ago when we got a big slowing in inflation in the late spring/early summer we expect another notable slowing over the next couple of months.

 Much of the expected slowing is from seasonal factors that have swung around since the start of the pandemic. Current CPI seasonal factors pushed up the change in the core CPI ex vehicles in April by 8bp more than using pre-pandemic seasonal factors. In the May data released next month that influence will swing to a 5bp push down. Problems with seasonal adjustment are a key reason we suggest focusing on 12-month changes (or comparing not-seasonally-adjusted monthly changes to the same month a year ago).

 A couple other factors should also help push down monthly core price changes in the next few months: First, beyond the specific problem of the current seasonal adjustment factors there is the more general pattern observed pre-pandemic (and also last year) of residual seasonality (where early-year price changes tend to be larger than changes later in the year for initially-released seasonally-adjusted data). Second, housing rents (OER and tenants' rent) are projected to slow over the next handful of months based on examining the various OER panels and the new tenant rent indexes.

 In May the expected slowing in the monthly core changes will be partially offset by a pickup in used car prices that will contribute around 4bp more to the core CPI monthly change than in April.

## Core PCE inflation forecast on the low edge of consensus at 2.5% for 2024

 We continue to expect that moderating demand, easing supply conditions, slowing rents, and a softening labor market will push down inflation over much of the next year.

 Following the upward surprise to PCE prices a few weeks ago, we boosted our core PCE projection for this year as a whole (Q4/Q4) to 2.5%. Consensus inflation measures released over the past month have moved up more than our projection, putting our inflation forecast at the low edge of consensus.

## Slowing ahead

The April CPI showed some slowing as the seasonally adjusted core CPI increase eased from 39bp in January, and 36bp the February and March, to 29bp in April. We expect notably more slowing in the next few months — much like a year ago when the the strength early in the year gave way to a step down in the late spring/early summer. Why do we expect the slowing?

Seasonal adjustment issues: Seasonal adjustment issues are likely to be the biggest part of the slowing in the core CPI over the next couple of months. As we have noted many times in past, we think the current seasonal adjustment factors used by the BLS for the CPI have been overly influenced by the pandemic and we recommend focusing on the 12-month changes or simply comparing the current not-seasonally-adjusted (NSA) core CPI monthly change to the one 12 months prior. On that basis the April CPI was a resounding positive. The April NSA core CPI increase was 28bp — 19bps below the 47bp increase last year, as well as 25bp below the April increase in 2022 and 55bps below the April increase in 2021. The 12-month core CPI change dropped from 3.80% in March to 3.61% in April — considerable progress, but still too high.

The April NSA core CPI increase was 28bp — 19bps below the 47bp increase last year

If one decides to look at the monthly seasonally-adjusted data, we suggest using data constructed using pre-pandemic seasonal factors. For most CPI items the BLS uses the past 8 years (currently 2016 to 2023) to construct seasonal adjustment factors. However, the pattern during 2020-2022 had a completely different pattern than seen in the pre-pandemic data. Last year the not-seasonally-adjusted pattern of core CPI price changes returned to the pre-pandemic pattern (though shifted up). (Figure 1 and Figure 2 The first four months of this year have continued to roughly follow the pre-pandemic pattern. Therefore we think the pre-pandemic seasonal factors are more relevant than the seasonal factors the BLS is currently using, which are constructed using data that includes the 2020-2022 years.

If one decides to look at the monthly seasonally-adjusted data, we suggest using data constructed using prepandemic seasonal factors.

Overly-sensitive seasonal factors likely pushed up the core CPI ex vehicles by 8bp in April and will push it down by 5bp in May

A few other things to note on the revisions to seasonal factors since pre-pandemic: First, June should also see a downward push from the seasonal factors, but a bit less than May. Second, revisions to seasonal factors since pre-pandemic were not a cause of the strength in the core CPI in the first few months of this year, and third, using the final prepandemic seasonal factors suggests that the April increase in the core CPI excluding new and used vehicles was quite similar to the increases seen from June to December last year. (Figure 4) Nonetheless, that does not suggest that April was back to the prepandemic pace. Even using the pre-pandemic seasonal factors the April increase in the core CPI excluding new and used vehicles remained about 8 bp above the average monthly increase in 2019.

There are a couple other factors likely to assist in the slowing in core price changes over the next few months, but the timing of these factors is less clear cut than the seasonal adjustment issue above:

Residual seasonality: Beyond the swings in the seasonal factors since pre-pandemic there is a tendency of monthly price changes to be higher early in the year rather than late in the year even after seasonal adjustment — a phenomena called "residual seasonality". The residual seasonality is most apparent in the initially released data, and is moderated in later data revisions. Even with subsequent data revisions, core PCE price increases were higher early in the year than later the year (except for October) in the 20 years prior to the pandemic. (Figure 5)

Figure 5: Core PCE price increases tend to slow over the year
![](images/49eb693930e6a543cc0e0dd265ec0fa207c13d21c92998eba8f8e46691bcf856.jpg)
Source: BEA, UBS

Slowing rents:A third factor that we expect to push down the inflation data in the next few months is slowing housing rents (owners' equivalent rent (OER) and tenants' rent). Part of the slowdown in housing rents is a reflection of revision to seasonal adjustment factors noted above. (Current seasonal factors were a push up of 5½bp relative to prepandemic seasonal factors for the April change and will swing to a 2½bp push down in May.)

Even beyond the impact of the seasonal factors we expect housing rents to slow. Twelvemonth changes for each of the six OER panels do not show the same flattening out visible in the monthly OER data since August. (Figure 6) New tenant lease rents that have been running at or below their pre-pandemic pace for a year and a half suggest that these twelve month changes should continue to trend down, which implies a notable slowing in OER in the next handful of months. Indeed, that slowing would have been more visible in April if not for the upward push in the seasonal factor relative to prepandemic: the not-seasonally-adjusted change in OER in April was the smallest increase in 2½ years. Similarly, the April increase in tenants' rent was also the smallest in 2½ years on both a seasonally-adjusted and not-seasonally-adjusted basis.

There is a lot of uncertainty in the path of OER going forward

However, there is a lot of uncertainty in how much slowing in OER is likely to occur. Data from Zillow suggests that OER needs to increase around another 6½% to catch up with the increases in new lease rents since the start of the pandemic. On the other hand, Apartment List data and the BLS' new tenants' rent index suggest OER has already overshot the increase in new tenants' rents by roughly 3½% to 4%. (Figure 10) As a result the range of forecasts in our models based on alternative rent indexes is quite wide. (Figure 11) Based on our examination of the OER panels, noted above, and our expectation that the downtrend in the 12-month change by panel will continue, our forecast for OER is below the median and mean of our models (which do not fully incorporate the panel structure), but we are likely to know in the next few months if that slowing is correct.

Upside from used car prices: The downward push from these various factors is likely to be at least partially offset in May by a swing in used vehicle prices from a 1.4% decline in April to roughly a ¼% increase in May, which should add about 4bp to the core CPI compared to April.

Overall, we were encouraged by the April CPI report, and, while inflation remains too high, we see continuing signs of progress. As noted by the San Francisco Fed, the share of PCE prices that are increasing, both unweighted and weighted by their share of expenditures, has returned to pre-pandemic levels, and, as we showed last month, most of the remaining excess inflation is from housing rents. If our forecast is correct, and housing rents slow over the next handful of months, it will have a major impact on the tone surrounding the inflation conversation.

## Near-term inflation forecast April PCE inflation

April headline PCE prices, released late next week (Friday, May 31), are projected to have increased 25bp with core PCE prices rising 23bp. With these increases the 12-month change in core PCE prices is projected to edge down to 2.73% through April — roughly 80% of the way back to the FOMC's 2.0% inflation target from its 5.6% peak in February 2022.

## Upcoming CPI prints

We are currently forecasting a 9bp increase in the headline CPI in May (seasonally adjusted; 27bp not-seasonally-adjusted) with 12-month inflation little changed (going from 3.36% in April to 3.38% in May). For the core CPI we forecast a 25bp increase in May as overly-aggressive seasonal factors are projected to swing from a upward push in April to a downward push in May (as described in the front section of this note). 12- month core CPI inflation is forecast to move down from 3.61% to 3.54% — continuing its gradual downward trend and it slowest pace since April 2021. As additional data becomes available over the next few weeks, we will refine this projection.

We currently forecast that in May core CPI inflation will fall to 3.5%

Looking further ahead than the May print, over the next handful of months we forecast seasonally-adjusted core CPI increases will begin to look like more like pre-pandemic monthly increases as rents slow, used car prices generally decline, and ebbing demand and easing supply conditions lead to a slackening in the pace of monthly increases for core non-rent services. ## Drivers of consumer inflation Inflation expectations

Long-run inflation expectations rose in the preliminary University of Michigan survey for May. However, looking more broadly, those long-run inflation expectations are at levels they were when core PCE inflation averaged 2% (as they also are in the Atlanta Fed survey of businesses). Over the past 25 years inflation expectations in the Michigan survey are positively correlated with past inflation, but negatively correlated with future inflation.

## Resource utilization and the labor market

The unemployment rate rose to 3.9% in April — still a very low level. We project labor slack to rise to about 4% by the end of this year.

The overall employment-population ratio remains below its pre-pandemic level and most of that difference is from older workers as the prime-age ratio is roughly in line with the February 2020 peak. We expect a weakening economy will lead to no further rise in the employment-population ratio.

The unemployment rate remains quite low

A normal Phillips curve coefficient where 1pp in unemployment leads to anywhere from 0.1pp to 0.3pp of inflation would suggest that the low level of unemployment can explain little of either the pick up or the slowing in inflation — particularly as the current unemployment rate is only 30bp above the rate in the year prior to the pandemic. However, alternative labor market indicators, particularly the job opening rate (shown later in the "Wages" section) suggest a bit more labor market tightness.

## Box: Rules of thumb for impacts of key economic shocks

We usually rely on estimated time-series models to bring in the impact of various factors on our forecast. However, rules of thumb are often useful to give a rough idea of how important a shock is likely to be. Below are a few key rules of thumb that we use:

 1 percentage point increase in the unemployment rate pushes down core inflation by 8bp.

 Permanent \$10 per barrel level shift in the price of Brent crude pushes up the level of CPI by 40bp and core CPI by 4bp.

 A one percent decline in the dollar increases consumer import prices by 0.15 percent.

 A one percent increase in import prices results in a 0.12 percent increase in core consumer prices.

A one percent increase in CRB spot food index equals about 8bp in prices of food at home with about 2/3rds in the first six months and 1/3rd in the six months after that. (CPI food at home is 8% of the CPI basket.)

## Components of core PCE inflation

Figure 56 through Figure 60 show 3- and 12-month changes for the main components of our core PCE projection along with our forecasts and model projections. The models are estimated taking 2024Q1 as data and forecasted starting 2024Q2.

1. Core goods prices (25% of core PCE, Figure 56) have slowed considerably over the past year. We expect essentially no further disinflation, but for core goods inflation to remain at about its current level (which is close to its pre-pandemic pace).

Goods price inflation is projected to remain at about its current level.

Our Phillips curve model for the category has adjusted down considerably over the past few quarters and now the model is roughly in line with our projection though the middle of this year and below it further out.

2. We forecast monthly housing services increases (essentially owners' equivalent rent plus tenants' rent, 20% of core PCE, Figure 57) will continue to slow over the next few quarters as the easing in rents for new leases filters into CPI/PCE housing rent inflation.

Our model for housing services inflation based on fundamental rent market factors (the unemployment rate, rental vacancy rate, and housing affordability) also shows slowing, but a little bit more gradually.

The 12-month change in shelter rent inflation is past its peak, but still very elevated.

3. Price inflation for PCE health services (19% of core PCE, Figure 58) is projected to run a little above the pace over the past year as the price reductions from the ending of Medicare Covid reimbursement rate increases is past. However, the upward push is only moderate as wage growth in health care has been slowing for the past year and Medicare adjustment for wage increases (which appear in October for hospitals and January for physicians) are estimated by the Center for Medicare and Medicaid Studies to be a similar to recent years.

4. PCE food services inflation (6% of core PCE, Figure 59Fod servic) has been strong through most of the pandemic. We have seen slowing in recent months and expect further easing of the pace of increases with moderating wage increases.

5. Inflation for other core PCE services (31% of core PCE, Figure 60) has slowed in recent months and we expect the pace will ebb further as wage growth slowly eases. This is in sharp contrast to the model that has taken more signal from the earlier strength in these core services prices.

## Key risks to our modal forecast

We view our projection as the most likely (modal) scenario and see risks on both sides of the inflation forecast with the overall uncertainty surrounding the inflation projection remaining larger than usual over the past 20 to 30 years (though down from a year or two ago). Over the next few months we see the risks as two-sided around our forecast though skewed somewhat to the downside now that the early-year surge is likely past.

We think that near-term inflation risks have shifted and are now skewed to the downside over the next few months

## Upside risks

1. Rents remaining strong: While we are not seeing a lot of strength in new tenants' rents, it is possible that Zillow's estimate of the amount of catch-up that CPI rents still need to do (around 6 ½ %) is more appropriate than taking the average across various measures, which suggest only a little additional catch-up. If Zillow is correct, CPI rents could continue to increase at roughly 45 to 50bp per month for the next year and a half to two years.

2. Continued near-term momentum: We think that the recent strength in price increases was largely consistent with the early year noise and will fade in the next couple of months. However, it is possible that the recent strength was not seasonal and will lead to somewhat less slowing than expected over the next few months.

3. Continued tight labor market passing into services prices: The labor market remains tight. If the economy does not gradually ease (though we no longer expect a recession) and wage growth remains elevated, it is likely that non-rent services inflation will be higher than we have forecast over the next year.

4. Higher inflation expectations/trend inflation: Estimating trend inflation is extremely imprecise and it is possible that inflation expectations and the inflation trend is notably above where we have it.

## Downside risks

1. A slowing economy: The recession that we had previously forecast starting this summer could still come to pass and have a greater slowing effect on inflation than we have anticipated. In particular, in recessions prices for PCE financial services, non-profit services, and travel-related services generally fall quite notably.

2. Rent weakness: Instead of the Zillow index being correct, it is quite possible that either the BLS NTR or Apartment List's index are right. If so, then OER and tenants' rents in the CPI/PCE prices could be slowing sharply in coming months and be negative in CPI reports by next summer.

3. Stronger productivity growth: The past couple quarters have seen a pickup in productivity growth. While we see this pickup as a return to the to the long-run productivity trend after some time below that trend, it could be the start of a longer-run productivity boom. If so, such a move could lead to lower price inflation for the same level of wage growth. In the late-1990s, as productivity growth picked up, price inflation fell.

4. Lower natural rate of unemployment: Additionally, it is possible that the ability to work remotely has led to better employment opportunities pushing down the natural rate of unemployment. If so, the natural rate of unemployment could be closer to 3.7% than the 4.0% we assume. If so, this would subtract close to a tenth percentage point off our or inflation forecast.

## Comparison to the forecast models

Our forecast models have revised downward notably since peaking in the second half of 2022. The models expect core PCE inflation around 2.9% to 3.0% this year (Q4/Q4) as they project a pick up of inflation in the second half of the year. Our forecast is notably lower.

As with most Phillips curve style models, they were unable to forecast the inflation as it surged upward and, as expected, they have not been a good guide as inflation has fallen. (Note that in the early part of 2022 the three higher models below in Figure 61 projected core PCE inflation remaining above 4% throughout the forecast horizon.) In particular, the models do not include an effect for changes in the mix of demand and the supply constraints that hampered efforts to satisfy this changing mix. We believe the combined effects of the changing demand mix and supply issues were far more important in driving the inflation surge over the past 3 years than were the standard Phillips curve drivers of aggregate resource slack.

Our Phillips curve models have performed poorly during thus inflation experience.

Nonetheless, over the next couple of years we believe those unusual factors in this inflation surge will continue to diminish and and the models will continue to revise down. Our forecast is only slightly below the lower range of models by 2026.
