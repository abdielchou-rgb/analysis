• China’s economic growth decelerated in recent months. Despite a rebound in exports, domestic demand has softened somewhat in Q2. As a result, GDP growth slowed to 4.7% in Q2 from 5.3% in Q1. The drag mainly came from the services sector, led by an underperforming property sector. These developments have led us to adjust down our 2024 GDP forecast to 4.9%.

• Looking at the key economic sectors: (1) The property sector outlook is still unfavorable despite recent policy easing, owing to a large inventory of finished and unfinished homes. (2) Household consumption spending continued to recover at a moderate pace, but it was constrained by slower income growth and still-weak sentiment. (3) Exports will likely remain a bright spot of the economy in H2, but how trade relations unfold beyond that will be a key focal point.

• Inflation has gradually improved to positive territory, but lower housing and car prices are preventing a stronger inflation recovery. We expect consumer inflation will keep on improving to slightly below 1% by end-2024 and further to 1.4% in 2025, yet below its 2% long-term average level.

• Policymakers may need to rethink their near-term policy mix to achieve the 5% annual growth target. In particular, a budget revision might be necessary to keep up the pace of spending against a continued decline in local government revenues. The Politburo meeting in late-July will be a key event for the signaling of additional policy support.

• The PBOC will cut rate only modestly, facing multiple constraints. We expect a 10bps rate cut by the PBOC before end-2024, likely in Q4. A rate cut remains necessary considering the softening credit demand and low inflation, but the PBOC also needs to keep a stable currency against a still-strong US dollar. Perhaps, there is a risk of more easing if (a) the Fed was to ease faster and sooner and/or (b) pressure on RMB weakness was to abate.

• Capital outflows persist. Despite the property package rolled out in May, property sales continue to disappoint, leading to a reversal in equity inflows from earlier in the year. The rate differential vs the rest of the world, meanwhile, continues to support ‘hot money’ outflows, rising FX deposits, and low exporter conversion ratios. Concerns around worsening US-China relations have also led to a notable widening in net direct investment outflows.

• The PBoC is tolerant of a weaker RMB vs the USD for now. The drift in the USD/CNY fix, and the limited nature of FX intervention, suggests the PBoC is (for now) allowing some RMB weakness, though it is keen to manage the pace to minimize the risk of adding to capital outflow pressures. Its FX policy bias is likely informed by: (a) the need to alleviate some of the depreciation pressure in the system, (b) maintaining export competitiveness; and (c) opening up space for further policy easing. Hence, we still like to be long AUD/CNH.

• Hedge RMB weakness via long-dated calls in onshore market. For corporates, we believe USD/CNY calls are the best instruments for corporates to hedge RMB weakness, while maintaining some exposure to RMB strength. Moreover, the cost of 12M ATMF calls can be offset by USD/RMB carry, making the net hedging PnL always positive regardless of where the spot ends up trading

• Bond curve to steepen. In the absence of further easing, we expect front-end rates to be rangebound. On the other hand, we believe the back of the bond curve should steepen. Why? (a) The supply of both ultra-long CGBs and special LGBs is set to pick up; (b) demand from banks is likely to be slower given the ongoing warning from the PBoC; and (c) the potential start of the PBoC bond-selling program. As such, we continue to recommend paying CNY NDIRS 2/10 steepener.

• Low funding cost. The onshore funding cost is likely to remain low against the backdrop of subdued loan growth and the PBoC’s supportive monetary stance. Hence, China is currently the only market in Asia that offers cost saving for corporates via RMB bonds and swapping back to USD.

• Notable pick up in Panda bond issuance. As of July, the finance cost saving has increased up to 83bp. Given that the China economic outlook has remained gloomy, we believe that the cost advantage could sustain and hence expecting more Panda bond to be issued in 2H 2024. A further pick-up in Panda bond issuance could also push the CNH CCS rates lower.

## The 3rd plenum of the 20th CPCCC, a key policy meeting focusing on medium-term reforms, was held on July 2024

60 categories, 300+ reform measures to be implemented in 5 years. The number of measures is comparable with the milestone 18th CPCCC third plenum in 2013, but the implementation period is 2 years shorter. We could see a lot of policy changes in the next 2-3 years.

The urgency of fiscal reforms. Local government finances are clearly under pressure owing to a drop in revenue, especially from land sales. In response, the third plenum proposed to expanding local government fiscal resources and tax base. A consumption tax may be introduced, which could increase the fiscal revenue (and spending) of local governments in higherincome regions.

Urban-rural integration to narrow the gap between urban and rural areas. Migrant workers should enjoy equal healthcare and education services as urban residents, social safety net to be strengthened rural areas; and restrictions on rural land transactions should be eased. These measures should improve rural household income and spending, which could become an important driver of China's consumer market.

Bolstering private sector confidence. The rule of law is the best business environment, according to Han Wenxiu, a senior party official on economy policy. The third plenum emphasized on the importance of promoting entrepreneurial spirit and build more world-class enterprises. It will formulate and implement a law on promoting the private economy and will prevent the use of administrative and criminal means to interfere in economic disputes.

Opening up and attracting foreign investment remains a key policy focus. The government aims to expand FDI inflows, which won't be an easy task under today's global economic and geopolitical environment. The government aims to bring domestic rules and institutions to international standards; provide convenient residence, medical care, payment and other services to foreigners coming to China; and opening up China's services sector for foreign investment in education, IT and healthcare businesses.

China’s trade surplus has consistently been the largest contributor to the current account surplus. In fact, over the past few quarters, the trade surplus is now 30% larger than the pre-pandemic period (2015-2019), due to: (a) a strong export recovery, and (b) the ongoing import substitution, as China looks to reduce its dependence on global goods.

• However, with the rise in protectionist sentiment from both the DM and EM world, China exports could slow, especially given rising concerns over its overcapacity issues. Indeed, in recent months, we have seen the US, the EU, Canada, Turkey, LatAm countries and Indonesia introducing tariffs on Chinese imports.

The reopening of the border has seen China’s tourism deficit returning to pre-pandemic levels. This is likely driven not just by rising outbound tourism, but also rising ‘hot-money’ outflows. Historically, the service deficit (where the tourism contribution is the biggest) recycles \~60% of the trade surplus.

• In the past few years, China has also seen a notable increase in its income deficit. Why? (a) The widening of the US-China interest rate differential leading to a preference of holding USD rather than RMB, (b) Poor China-US relations resulting in MNCs repatriating more of their earnings out of China; and (c) Domestic corporates establishing additional supply chains outside of China due to rising geopolitical concerns.

Exporters’ FX conversion continues to decline while….

… importers’ FX conversion continues to increase as both actively accumulate USDs

The wide US-China interest rate differential and ongoing RMB weakness against the USD has seen exporters reducing their FX conversion. As of June, exporters’ conversion ratio is \~44% on a 3M moving sum and remains below 50% for the fourth consecutive month. Importers have also been actively buying USDs.

With RMB weakness vs the USD likely to remain, we do think exporters’ conversion will remain low, while importers’ conversion is likely to remain elevated. Hence, despite the sizeable trade surplus that China is experiencing, the lack of USD selling would keep the RMB on a weaker footing.

Despite the ongoing outflows, the PBoC has been tolerant of some RMB weakness vs the USD. Since April, the PBoC has been allowing the USD/CNY fix to rise. However, the pace of the further weakening is likely to be gradual and orderly. Why? Its FX policy bias is likely informed by: (a) the need to alleviate some of the depreciation pressure in the system, (b) maintaining export competitiveness; and (c) opening up space for further policy easing

• To manage the process and to ensure the CNH-CNY spot basis does not widen too significantly, the PBoC has been tightening CNH short-end liquidity to curb any excessive weakness.

Given the poor growth outlook and the fact that the US-China interest rate differential is likely to remain wide, we believe USD/RMB calls are the best instrument for corporates to hedge RMB weakness while maintaining some exposure to RMB strength. Moreover, the cost of 12M ATMF calls can be offset by USD/RMB carry, making the net hedging PnL always positive regardless of where the spot ends up trading.

Onshore or offshore market? The onshore CNY options market theoretically offers a better hedging outcome due to higher USD/CNY carry and lower FX vols. However, the prevailing FX risk reserve deposit requirement adds to the cost of onshore CNY options, especially for shorter tenors. Therefore, we recommend corporates hedge RMB risk via long-dated onshore USD/CNY calls.

The PBoC has kept the seven-day interbank reverse repo rate above the OMO rate to discourage banks from excessive borrowing to buy bonds. YTD, the PBoC has withdrawn \~RMB630bn via OMO and MLF. In addition, China has not injected any liquidity via MLF for five consecutive months.

To avoid tightening monetary conditions, however, the PBoC has occasionally provided liquidity via OMO in order to smooth any interbank funding tightness ahead of month-end and long holidays. The introduction of the temporary overnight repo and reverse repo operations (which take place in the afternoon between 4-4.20pm Beijing time) should also ensure liquidity conditions do not become overly tight.

• In the absence of an interest rate cut or active injection of liquidity into the interbank market, we expect front-end rates to be rangebound. Why? (a) Limited upside risk. Domestic growth has remained subdued, underscored by weaker 618 sales this year (- 7% YoY). The ongoing decline in property prices will also keep consumer confidence subdued. (b) The PBoC has shown a strong desire to keep liquidity stable.

Apart from OMOs, over the years the PBoC has started to increase the number of toolkits that it has for managing liquidity. Apart from PSL, MLF and SLF, there are ten active rediscount and relending facilities for various purposes, and six targeted lending facilities to support activities and firms of national importance. These have been critical, not only to inject liquidity into the system, but also to support the key national economic agenda.

• As of May, the total outstanding size of the PBOC's structural monetary tools is RMB7.5trn, with half of these from the PSL program. However, with China’s desire to provide more targeted support, we believe that it will increasingly provide more credit by expanding the other collateralized liquidity facilities, such as relending and targeted lending facilities. This changed after 2014, with the PBoC having to actively cut the RRR and use other facilities to inject liquidity into the system

• A new addition is the CGB purchasing program. In 2014, with growth slowing and RMB starting to weaken, the PBoC started to actively inject more permanent liquidity into the system. Not only to achieve its economic target, but also to replenish the RMB liquidity that it took out during its FX intervention activities to lean against RMB weakness. This started with RRR cuts and reverse repo operations, but the toolkit was gradually widened to various collateral-based liquidity facilities.

However, after a decade of RRR cuts, the room for reductions has diminished. Indeed, we think 5% is potentially the floor for how low the RRR can be. Therefore, a CGB purchasing program would likely be the next tool used to provide. (for more details, see our report, Asia Thematic Strategy Notes: RMB: PBoC restarts bond trading)

## Recent developments suggest that the bond trading program can start earlier

The back end of the yield curve has reached a record low

The PBoC’s CGB holding is mostly short-dated bonds

![](images/9719537b63ef20233829351a0009541601953d6411e7823c9a7598d5fd2bab1b.jpg)
Source: Deutsche Bank, Wind

The strong rally of long-dated bonds (30-40bp in 1H 2024) has caught the attention of the PBoC, and it is now aiming to: (a) push back on the negative view on the growth/inflation mix; (b) maintain a sufficiently upward yield curve slope to incentivize investment in the economy; and (c) reduce FI’s speculative behavior on long-term bonds.

The PBoC first tried to guide long-dated bond yields higher through verbal intervention. This, however, was successful only for a short period of time. The market doubted the PBoC’s capacity to move the bond yields higher. Based on the PBoC’s balance sheet, it only holds \~ RMB1.5trn of CGBs on its balance sheet. This is equivalent to 5% of the total outstanding CGBs, with 75% of the holdings maturing within two years. Hence, to drive longer-dated yields higher, the PBoC lacks the necessary bond inventories.

Around half of the banks' holdings are over five years in duration

The large HTM government bond books of the Big 4 banks could support the PBoC's bond borrowing

![](images/d9269150900770458c404d019db9ec98a2d2d8d3d7151001aec9e720f1df3f6e.jpg)

To show its commitment and to increase its capability to manage the CGB yields, the PBoC has signed agreements with several major financial institutions to borrow bonds in an open-ended manner and on a credit basis. In other words: (1) the size of borrowing will not be limited by the PBoC's own balance sheet; instead, the constraint will come from how many bonds primary dealers are able/willing to offer; and (2) once the PBoC sells the bonds that it borrowed, it will not face any time constraint regarding when it will need to buy back these bonds.

• How much can the PBoC borrow? Of the RMB30tn CGB market, commercial banks account for two-thirds of outstanding CGBs. Breaking it down by tenor, around half of the government bonds held by banks are over five years in duration. We estimated that the PBoC should be able to tap \~RMB3.5trn in bonds from the Big 4 banks alone (for more details, see our report, Asia Macro Strategy Notes: RMB: PBoC getting ready to sell bonds).

Long-dated bond supply in Q1 was relatively low

![](images/6224d06d0446cd81938e3daf6bb494a05740da36d29430a4acb6df1c73ffaf31.jpg)
Source: Deutsche Bank, Wind
Net government bond supply is likely to peak in Q3

![](images/8e81ccdc0931cbc74bb6a648bc5857c8d42f1570dc2e1c3657b211593724bba9.jpg)
Source: Deutsche Bank, Wind \*DB estimates

Into 2H, we should see a steepening in the CGB curve. Why? (a) The supply of both ultra-long CGBs and special LGBs is set to pick up; (b) Demand from banks is likely to be slower given the repeated warnings from the PBoC and (c) The potential start of the PBoC bond-selling program.

On the first point, although the issuance of special LGBs has been slow, we think that the pace will pick up in the coming months, assuming LGB issuances are to be completed by October (which is the case traditionally). If so, the monthly average special LGB supply will be RMB605bn, of which \~70% will be in the 15-30Y bucket (RMB420bn). Combined with the monthly average of RMB170bn worth of ultra-long CGBs, the total duration supply (>10Y) could add up to RMB590bn per month, much higher than the monthly average of RMB240bn in 1H 2024.

• Hence, we think that the 10Y CGB would slowly grind higher, potentially to \~2.5% by the end of 2024. As such, we continue to recommend paying CNY NDIRS 2/10 steepener.

Foreign bond flows have increased notably

![](images/ac569692b392dcc8265a340340cc589a63b740800972813f3e98900ac440cb2f.jpg)
Source: Deutsche Bank, Wind
Yet, most have gone into NCDs

![](images/33ab05969c518acf291c200d5df3544b55b8a523c9cd5bab68da426ea44371cd.jpg)
Source: Deutsche Bank, Wind

Although in recent months, we have seen the return of foreign flows, we expect the flows into longer-dated Chinese bonds to slow. Why? (a) With global monetary policy pivoting, other DM and EM countries’ bonds are likely to offer a better return on duration; (b) The start of the PBoC’s bond-selling program should take some shine out of Chinese bonds; and (c) Inclusion flows are likely to come to an end soon. CGB inclusion into the WGBI will be completed in September 2024. As passive inflows fade, we believe that foreign outflows from CGBs could be larger.

Majority of the inflows are likely to be invested into shorter-dated debts like NCDs. Monthly average net NCDs purchasing reached RMB90bn as of June 2024 (vs. the monthly average of RMB23bn in 2023). Indeed, among its global peers of similar credit ratings, China offered the largest pick-up in yield, due to the low funding costs.

Net Panda bond issuance has picked up since 2023…

![](images/eb3346a5a44714e6b372bf29568f806ea8fad2c60d3bed435891b682b7d26f35.jpg)
Source: Deutsche Bank, Wind
…as low lending costs have increased finance cost savings

![](images/380b9b7cfef41dc368ad384d31748f73e03e17beb1d0c2c0ef38f905421ab6d3.jpg)
Source: Deutsche Bank, Bloomberg Finance LP, Wind

The net Panda bond issuance in 2023 has increased to RMB37bn, four times higher than that in 2022 (RMB9bn). Among which 60% of the net issuance was done in 2H 2023 as the finance cost saving has picked up since then. This was a result of the combination of (a) the elevated USD bond funding cost and (b) the low interest rate in China amid of the accommodate monetary environment.

The pace of Panda bond issuance has quickened, with a total of RMB45bn Panda bonds issued between January and May 2024. This was likely due to the larger finance cost saving (via issuing a 3Y Panda bonds in onshore market and swapping back the funds to USD using the cross-currency swap), which has risen from 21bp in January to 65bp in May.

• As of July, the finance cost saving has increased up to 83bp. Given that the China economic outlook has remained gloomy, we believe that the cost advantage could sustain and hence expecting more Panda bond to be issued in 2H 2024.

Similarly, net issuance of dim sum bonds has also increased

• Like Panda bond issuance, the net issuance of Dim Sum bonds also increased notably in 2023 since the wide interest rate differential also allow onshore larger enterprises to access cheaper USD funding by swapping out the CNH proceeds raised via Dim Sum bonds. Besides, given the government's agenda to deleverage LGFVs and discourage offshore USD debts, Dim Sum bond has then become an alternative for LGFVs to diversify their funding.

So long as the interest rate differential between US and China to remain and the CNY forward points to stay low, the CNH CCS rates would remain low. Hence, the USD financing cost advantage is likely to sustain which could give rise to more Dim Sum bonds.

Macroeconomic fluctuations often account for most of the risks associated with exposures to instruments that promise to pay fixed or variable interest rates. For an investor who is long fixed-rate instruments (thus receiving these cash flows), increases in interest rates naturally lift the discount factors applied to the expected cash flows and thus cause a loss. The longer the maturity of a certain cash flow and the higher the move in the discount factor, the higher will be the loss. Upside surprises in inflation, fiscal funding needs, and FX depreciation rates are among the most common adverse macroeconomic shocks to receivers. But counterparty exposure, issuer creditworthiness, client segmentation, regulation (including changes in assets holding limits for different types of investors), changes in tax policies, currency convertibility (which may constrain currency conversion, repatriation of profits and/or liquidation of positions), and settlement issues related to local clearing houses are also important risk factors. The sensitivity of fixed-income instruments to macroeconomic shocks may be mitigated by indexing the contracted cash flows to inflation, to FX depreciation, or to specified interest rates – these are common in emerging markets. The index fixings may – by construction – lag or mis-measure the actual move in the underlying variables they are intended to track. The choice of the proper fixing (or metric) is particularly important in swaps markets, where floating coupon rates (i.e., coupons indexed to a typically short-dated interest rate reference index) are exchanged for fixed coupons. Funding in a currency that differs from the currency in which coupons are denominated carries FX risk.
