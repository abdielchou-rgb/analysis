# Global Inflation Strategy Global Inflation Monday

## Long breakevens on risk momentum in a high stakes week

Inflation pressure means either higher breakevens or real rates: central bank attention largely determines which and policymakers have not been validating market optimism on rates this week. January CPI in the US and the UK might shift that, but the pipeline pressures that drive inflation models won't come quickly. Despite the more challenging environment for real rates, we stay long while emphasising that real rates are ideally balanced with an overweight in breakevens. For global breakevens, momentum in risk sentiment is enough to keep a long bias because breakevens tend to lag. Our main expressions are long US 5y CPI against UK RPI or on the US CPI curve against 10y. We close cross-market recommendations to be long EUR 5y5y HICPx vs US CPI and long UK rates against US. As a new trade idea we suggest looking at fading the steepening in the RPI curve through 5y5y/10y20y.

## Inflation pressure feeds back into real rates

Central bankers have not been validating market optimism about rate cuts. Their concern is sensible, but other than the weight they put on the arguments we know well, there hasn't been much to learn about the key issues. We have touched on all of them here in the past few weeks: wages, frameworks for assessing the cyclical strength of demand, pricing power, goods-services prices rebalancing, geopolitics, surveys of firms' price expectations. Depending on how strong ongoing disinflation as measured turns out to be, a supportive enough picture from all this might be quite slow to emerge.

UK and US data on Tuesday will provide a little new information on the big issue of wages. In Europe, evidence will be piecemeal and quite slow to arrive. Felix discusses the schedule in Germany here. Philip Lane shows an updated wage tracker in his speech here and speaks of the hope that firms will '[buffer] rising labour costs with a slowdown in profit growth', but unless weaker demand puts more of a dent in pricing power further, we're not optimistic.

Catherine Mann (BOE) picked up on goods price behaviour in her talk (as you'd expect for an inflation hawk, since rebalancing most naturally happens upward) and there's another decent summary of the mechanism here. The Chinese consumer durables PPI fell again this month, so there is a bit of a disinflationary story there, but we probably should not put too much weight on that passing through easily to western prices until recession arrives. It's also worth just reminding ourselves that correlation between this closely-watched and most-upstream of pipeline indicators and actual consumer price outcomes has been very unimpressive (Figure 2).

Finally, geopolitics is dispatched by every policy speech as an upside risk. Alongside a little tailwind from stronger economic data, the handful of basis points this is worth so far probably explains most of the rise in global front end breakevens this year.

What isn't being talked about officially is domestic politics. Sidestepping the possibility of a Trump presidency might just be because there isn't enough policy detail to evaluate, but it clearly matters for all regions. In Europe, farmer strikes/processions/protests are also hard to evaluate, but look clearly like an upside risk, partly because they are simply disruptive, and partly because a key issue is government pressure on food prices.

## Hawkish central bankers. Higher real rates ...

The point of all this week's policy-preamble is that we have been more real-rate bullish and all this central bank hawkishness has been bad news for that. (The expressions involving overweight breakeven exposure that we have preferred have done OK, however. That still looks like the 'all weather' way to proceed.)

## … and tougher going for breakevens, but risk sentiment keeps us long breakevens

Perhaps the best reason to be long breakevens still this week is momentum in risk sentiment: higher stocks and higher rates both tell us that breakevens are likely to trade well. Our best-performing model for TIPS returns over the long term simply tells us that risk appetite is a good leading indicator for breakeven performance (Figure 3).

Breakevens have corrected over the early part of the year for the better cyclical picture and geopolitical risks. But fundamentally we still feel that market levels, which project near-target inflation over the next two years, deserve a little more risk premium. Even in a central case, UBS bottom-up projections are 20-30bp above the market over the coming months (Figure 15) for the US. On the more negative side, natural gas is collapsing everywhere (our Europe energy team discusses here), but liquid fuels balance that for now. The UBS projection for CPI next week is also marginally below the market at 307.864 (market 307.90). Alan Detmeister's projection for 0.08% m/m SA (updated here this morning) is below the economist consensus, which is grouped around 0.2%.

## CPI seasonals didn't upset the profile for disinflation in the US. The January release is more important.

The much-awaited changes to the CPI seasonal adjustments made very little difference to any conclusions about the strength of disinfation momentum. The charts below illustrate. Alan Detmeister discusses in much more depth here.

The US CPI details for January on Tuesday will be much more important. The preview is here. US inflation is highly front-loaded in the year: 70% of the total seasonal increases for the year are in January to March (the rest is by June). Because Q1 is a key period for price resetting, the likelihood of change in tendency is higher.

## Price are also fluid in Europe in Q1. But discounting isn't the same as price resetting.

January is a highly disinflationary month across subseries in the UK and Europe. But January sales don't necessarily say much about how price rises will look through the year. In the UK, April is the key month. In Europe (details for January next week) it tends to be March.

## Why should long end UK outperformance continue? Fade as RPI 5y5y/10y20y flatteners.

The dynamic RV signals this week are unexciting and unsurprising: only UK RPI stands out as appearing expensive, particularly in the front end (Figure 8). Long end RPI has also been doing well, although that is a little obscured by the dynamics identified by PCA, which sees this as 'normal'. Last week we suggested sticking with the theme of UK rates outperformance but switching into nominals. That was not the right decision. We have now fallen out of love with the UK long end again altogether – partly motivated by Figure 8, and partly by a range of other charts that fail to stand out and because we can't identify a reason for outperformance here. The conventional gilt 63s auction next week may be an interesting gauge of pensions demand. For now fading 5y5y weakness into the 9y IL33s supply this week is worth a relative value look (Figure 9).

Trade idea: Receive 5y5y RPI and pay 10y20y RPI at +32.5bp. Target 40bp. Stop 27bp. Review on supply this week (IL33s and conventional 63s supply next week if held.) The trade behaves similarly to 5s10s30s (long middle) for those looking for a simpler expression. We take off the recommendation to own UK 15y15y IRS vs US.

Weakly bullish trades in US CPI. We still like being long US CPI as the 5y against UK RPI and the 5y against 10y CPI. Both trades have covered about half the distance to the targets we had in mind last week though and we taper our conviction accordingly. Our recommendation to fade US CPI outperformance against EURi in 5y5y nearly reached its target of 25bp last week and has pulled back a little since then. The January US anomaly (TIPS extension?) has played through now and we call an end to this theme for now.

## French supply and demand for European inflation

For this week's French supply the RV is also less important than the temperature-take for broader demand. We are not optimistic on pensions demand. Livret-A demand is more of an open question. We will learn about January inflows next week. Recall that the coupon was frozen at 3% until January 2025. Although French banks are paying up for term deposits, instant access, tax free savings at 3% is hard to beat. The OATei March-29s look expensive; the others marginally cheap.

## Spain should be overweight in any linker portfolio

At times like this it's probably a good moment to reflect that with Germany's retreat from the Euro inflation market, Spain, where government debt completed its longanticipated adelanto in Q3 last year, has a growing claim to being the credit leader. 15y SPGBei pick up 15bp over equally new (and hence tappable) OATi/ei for the same iota volatility (about 20bp/year).
