## A Wider Range of Outcomes and Opportunities

Since the COVID-19 pandemic, growth and inflation data have been more volatile and unpredictable than in the prior 10-20 years, a distinct period of financial repression where economic metrics were more muted and predictable. Much of this is directly due to the extraordinary policy reaction to the pandemic itself which has ushered in a new regime of fiscal dominance, which we discuss in more detail in our various scenarios below. The most distinguishing feature of this regime change has been higher and more volatile inflation ( Exhibit 1 ), not to mention other economic variables. The more important outcome for investors is the bifurcation these dynamics have created between the haves and have-nots which applies to both consumers and corporates. This increased uncertainty around the data and unique policy mix is one reason why quality and related factors continue to outperform ( Exhibit 2 ), and why this leadership is unlikely to change until we complete the cycle and move from late cycle, to recession, to recovery. High quality indices like the S&P 500 have been the beneficiaries of this environment as investors clamor for assets that can outperform inflation amid fiscal policy that shows limited signs of restraint under various political outcomes. We include more detail on the quality theme in the sections that follow.

In addition to the unprecedented combination of monetary and fiscal policy support postcovid, we have also experienced de-globalization, a secular rise in interest rates/cost of capital, an increase in geopolitical tensions (multi-polar world), a shift in power from capital to labor and a potential increase in taxes to pay for the accumulated debt of the past 30 years. Finally, the data releases themselves have become a bit more unreliable with the revisions larger and more frequent compared to history. This has also been the case with much of the soft data, too, given the lower response rates to traditional surveys. As a result, forecasting has become more challenging with a wider band of potential outcomes emerging, in our view. Ironically, despite this less predictable world, forecasters have actually huddled closer together with fewer outliers. This is true at both the macro and micro level ( Exhibit 3 and Exhibit 4 ). We attribute this dynamic to the fact that when uncertainty is high, humans are less inclined to go out on a limb and more inclined to coalesce around the consensus view. For macro investors, this guiding light has been

the Fed and other central bank authorities. For stock investors and traders, it's company guidance.

Tighter forecasts combined with a wider range of outcomes is a recipe for more volatile prices, in our view. As a result, we think it makes sense to present a wider range of bull and bear case price targets than usual. Furthermore, we think the probability of the tail outcomes is higher than normal as well, while our base case is less certain. We also believe a wider range of potential outcomes and greater uncertainty should lead to lower valuations via a higher equity risk premium, while strong earnings are a positive offset for price (more on this below). The question is really how much should valuations compress in this type of environment. At \~20.5x forward 12 month EPS, the S&P 500 is currently in the top decile of its historical range. Further, this is based on earnings expectations that are still well above trend thanks to the pandemic induced inflation that companies were able to capture in 2020-21. Adjusting for this over-earning makes the current valuation look even more unattractive—i.e., the cyclically adjusted P/E is in the top 4% of all readings since 1881 and it's only been surpassed in 2021, 2000, and 1929 ( Exhibit 5 and Exhibit 6 ).

It's very hard to predict exactly when valuations will normalize, but we remain confident that valuation matters in the end and that we are not in a new paradigm that justifies permanently higher P/Es. Our forecast for lower multiples over the next year, except in our bull case, is a reflection of the wider uncertainty bands around potential outcomes over the next 12 months (more on this below). As noted, we believe this will be reflected through a normalization in the equity risk premium though lower rates are a partial offset. In the end, we forecast the forward P/E multiple at 19x in 12 months which is \~7 percent lower than today but still in the top \~25% percent of valuation levels over the last 10 years. The other driver of multiple compression in our base case is the mechanical shift lower in P/Es that often happens as earnings reaccelerate in a mid-to-late cycle environment. Truth be told, our ability to forecast the P/E over the last year has been poor and while we are confident valuations are too high, we have little confidence in our ability to predict the exact timing or magnitude of this normalization. This adds to the higher than normal uncertainty in our outlook for equity prices.

At the beginning of the year, we cited 3 possible macro outcomes for 2024: 1) a soft landing/"Goldilocks" outcome described as slowing growth and decelerating inflation aided by Fed cuts this year, 2) a "no landing" where growth reaccelerates back to potential, but inflation remains stickier than expected, and thus, we see fewer Fed rate cuts, and 3) a hard landing/recession. Since then, markets appear to have moved from a "soft landing" outcome in January to a "no landing" in March and now back towards a "softer landing". Markets even wrestled briefly with a slower growth/higher inflation outcome in April. Chair Powell addressed the "stagflation" concerns at his latest press conference saying "I don't see the 'stag' or the 'inflation'." Bottom line, markets are fickle and will trade the data as it's released, particularly when the outcomes are so uncertain.

To this end, the data has been mixed with something for every scenario. This is not a new phenomena as markets have gyrated materially on changing views about the macro over the past year ( Exhibit 8 ). This year, the markets have bounced between the "no/soft

landing" scenarios, trying to decide if we can get a true broadening out, while essentially removing the risk of a "hard landing"/recession completely. With seemingly all of the attention over the past few months around the hotter than expected inflation numbers, last week brought some relief on that front with the latest release of the Consumer Price Index on Wednesday. The markets took solace in the data as a sign that inflation is not accelerating further; while we are still far away from the Fed's stated goal of 2%, Powell can maintain his latest guidance that the Fed's next move will be a cut even if he can't commit to when. First, from the bond market's perspective, we are now back to \~2 cuts being priced into the curve by December. Most importantly for stocks, 10-year UST yields are back to the break out point of 4.35-4.40 we identified as critical for P/Es, and are near support at the 200-day moving average. Equities have taken their cue from this fall in yields, and P/Es have pushed higher once again back toward \~20.5x, suggesting to us that this CPI release may ultimately prove the be the top for the year in terms of positive sentiment. From our perspective, this data point does very little to change our probabilities on the ultimate outcome. Of course, it doesn't matter what we think but what the market thinks and it's clear that last week's data has given the markets more confidence in the "soft landing"/"Goldilocks" outcome. That argues for leaning toward our bull case in the short term on a tactical basis even though it doesn't change our probabilities or targets for the next year as laid out below. In fact, it is likely that we will be directionally shifting our tactical views toward our various cases over the course of the next 6-12 months and it would not surprise us if markets do the same. In short, get ready for more rotations.

In the base case, we forecast a 19x P/E multiple on 12-month forward EPS (June 2026) of US\$283, which equates to a 5,400 forward 12-month price target ( Exhibit 11 ). Our 2024 and 2025 earnings growth forecasts (8% and 13%, respectively) assume healthy, midsingle-digit top-line growth in addition to margin expansion in both years as positive operating leverage resumes (particularly in 2025). This earnings path is based on our economists growth forecasts, output from our earnings models, and our view that AI diffusion will boost margins starting in 2025—see the Leveraging AI to Drive Efficiency section for more. In terms of industry drivers of AI-fueled productivity gains, our framework highlights that services-oriented pockets of the market possess a more significant opportunity with respect to AI-driven efficiency gains; these groups include Software & Services, Consumer Services, Health Care Equipment & Services, Financial Services, and Media & Entertainment. Just these groups alone represent over 25% of expected 2025 net income for the S&P 500, which speaks to the potential margin opportunity.

## Bull/Bear Case Scenarios

Our primary bull and bear cases (i.e., "Bull Case 1" and "Bear Case 1") map to the forecasts in Exhibit 11 though given the greater uncertainty around potential outcomes, we offer an alternative bull and bear case ("Bull Case 2" and "Bear Case 2") for investors to consider. We view the bull and bear case price targets in Exhibit 11 as reasonable outcomes in these alternative bull and bear cases as well. The write ups below offer more detail.

Bull Case 1 (More of the Same—i.e., No Landing via Heavy Fiscal funded by RRP, End of QT and TGA): In our bull case, fiscal policy support remains robust and potentially increases into the election, but without any negative implications for the bond market. Instead, immigration continues to provide a tailwind to labor supply while commodity prices are contained as China remains stagnant and even exports more deflation in certain areas. The dollar stays strong relative to other currencies but depreciates significantly against gold much like we have witnessed over the past 6 months. In this scenario, stock prices increase significantly in nominal terms but could continue to churn in real terms relative to gold. Nominal GDP and earnings end up being stronger than expected. Investors recognize this policy/macro backdrop as one where they need to own more

assets that can inflate faster than the purchasing power of the dollar—i.e., high quality equities. As we discuss in our bear case, such policy may be part of the larger plan to inflate away the excessive debt on the government’s balance sheet and to assist stressed parts of the economy. In the bull case, we assume policy makers are very successful with this strategy and the markets tolerate it. More specifically, we assume the bond market does not push back in a way that is destructive for equity multiples and/or forces fiscal discipline upon Congress. Early warning signs this is not the case will likely show up in the term premium.

In order to get this outcome, we assume the government has enough liquidity to fund itself without having to pay higher interest rates. The sources for this liquidity are already in place which is why this outcome is plausible for the next 6-12 months. There appears to be significant funding available to service the government deficits that could keep interest rates lower than they would be if it all had to be funded from private sources. More specifically, the reverse repo still has \~\$450B that could be drained via bill issuance. Second, the \~\$800B Treasury General Account could be cut in half as a way to reduce the net borrowing needs of Treasury. Finally, there is the tapering of QT which could be felt by the market in the second half of the year based on the Fed’s recent commitment to begin this tapering in June. The bottom-line result for the S&P 500 is higher multiples and earnings, though the biggest delta versus our base case is the upside in multiples on healthy earnings numbers (driven by solid top line growth, positive operating leverage and AI diffusion—see Leveraging AI to Drive Efficiency for more). The end result for lower quality stocks may not be as bullish as investors are only willing to own high quality companies that should benefit disproportionately from this kind of policy and economic backdrop. In short, it’s a continuation of the multiple expansion and earnings recovery we have been experiencing. The challenge with this scenario is that inflation may get out of control again and force the Fed to hike, but given its recent predisposition to cut rather than hike even in the face of bumpy inflation data, it appears the Fed may already not be as focused on its 2% target. In fact, policy makers may need inflation well above 2% if they are going to successfully inflate the Debt/GDP ratios back to more sustainable levels over time. This syncs with our longer standing view that the notion that the Treasury can fund itself without stress and at a reasonable cost is a primary consideration of policy makers.

## Bull Case 2 (No Landing Driven by Better Organic Growth and Higher Productivity):

Just like with the bear case outcome, given the uncertainty in today's backdrop, we offer an alternative bull case narrative for investors to consider. This second bull case scenario is not as likely, in our view, but is certainly plausible and worthy of consideration. In short, this is an outcome where growth is better for organic reasons—better global growth and a more rapid China recovery, higher capital spending, and a virtuous wage/income circle that drives sustainable consumption growth from the low- and middle-income cohorts that appear to have run out of steam. Fiscal support fades as it is no longer necessary and the risk of a rapid term premium rise in the Treasury market dissipates considerably. This outcome requires much higher labor productivity that allows companies to pay higher wages while expanding margins. In other words, we assume this productivity impact can be much greater in the short term primarily via technology diffusion and rival the kind of increase we saw in the late 1990s. Such a rise could lead to greater optimism that earnings growth could remain above the long term trend of 10% while also producing the kind of wage/income growth for workers to maintain and even increase their real level of spending without further fiscal assistance. In this outcome, both earnings and multiples are higher, much like under our first bull case, but returns are greater in real terms. This is a healthier outcome for markets and the economy than the bull case discussed earlier and we could see leadership broadening out to lower quality areas and small caps in this scenario.

Bear Case 1 (Hard Landing/Recession): As discussed in our base case outlook, our economists are forecasting a soft landing outcome. However, under our bear case we assume that 2023 was simply a deferral of the recession that many forecasters (including the yield curve) expected to occur last year. In short, the extraordinary fiscal expansion, immigration boost, massive liquidity provided by the reverse repo facility (delayed QE) as well as the injection of reserves to bail out depositors post last year's regional banking failures led to a much stronger economy than expected although earnings growth proved to be disappointing for most companies and even the S&P 500 overall—i.e., 0% EPS growth with 6% nominal GDP growth. As we have discussed in our research for most of this year, this unusual policy mix of heavy fiscal and robust government liquidity to pay for it, combined with high front end rates has crowded out many consumers and smaller businesses. These economic participants are suffering from a cost of capital and borrowing rates that are simply too high while simultaneously struggling with price levels they can’t afford. The end result of this policy mix has been a hollowing out of the economy and, in our bear case, it ultimately leads to an unemployment cycle and recession. Bear in mind that as muted as earnings growth was last year for the best companies (S&P 500), it was much worse for small caps, with S&P 600 earnings shrinking by 8% last year and Russell 2000 earnings declining by 12%. While the expectation for earnings growth is much better in 2024 and 2025, EPS upside is still likely to skew toward large cap, quality companies and indices such as the S&P 500.

To take this a step further and conclude that this crowding out could lead to a recession, it's worth recognizing that small businesses account for close to half of all jobs in the US. They have also created the majority of new jobs over the past 25 years. We have heard some suggest that the economy will be fine so long as the top 20 percent of consumers and larger companies remain strong. However, to say that small cap companies or mid-tolow income consumers don’t contribute meaningfully to the economic backdrop may end up being misleading, in our view. In many ways, the market agrees with the notion that these types of companies are struggling—small caps and low quality stocks have underperformed significantly over the past 3 years, a dynamic we attribute to the policy mix cited above, among other headwinds post pandemic. This has also been one of our most consistent calls/views for the past 3 years—we downgraded small caps in March 2021 after upgrading in early April 2020. This underperformance can also be traced to the negative earnings growth for small caps and many lower quality large cap companies that are finding this economy unfavorable for many reasons—high costs relative to pricing power, saturation in demand from Covid era over-consumption, limited access to capital at affordable rates, etc. In short, to assume the economy is safely out of the reach of a recession would be misguided at this stage. The fact that the consensus view has completely reversed on this possibility makes the potential risk for investors greater. Meanwhile, Morgan Stanley's quantitative recession model which can be found on

Bloomberg (MSRIREC) is elevated relative to history ( Exhibit 16 ).

Using market measures of economic risk like the yield curve, we come to a similar conclusion—i.e., the risk of recession is historically elevated and therefore a plausible bear case probability for our equity outlook. Looking at Exhibit 15 , the New York Fed's recession risk model is based almost exclusively on shape of the yield curve across different tenors. The combination has proven to be a good measure of recession risk over the last 50 years. More specifically, the risk of recession increases as the yield curve inverts but doesn't become acute until the curve re-steepens. Historically the time between the peak in this measure and the onset of recession has ranged from 3-13 months. Today, we are 12 months past the peak inversion and counting. This one measure would suggest that recession risk is elevated assuming the yield curve is still a valid tool/ signal. The fiscal dominance regime may explain why this signal has not yet led to recession as government intervention during the pandemic was historically large and created a stock of money that has just taken longer to work through than normal. However, with excess savings fading and many consumer companies sounding more concerned on demand, those stimulus tailwinds may finally be waning. Once again, given the nature of this recovery and policy mix, we are open to the idea that this typical time lag between yield curve inversion and recession can extend even further, particularly with the still flush pools of liquidity available to the government from a funding standpoint (RRP, TGA and now the tapering of QT). This is essentially our bull case as discussed. However, as that excess pool of money gets drained, we are also on close watch for how funding markets react and how risk assets trade.

In addition to small business/consumer weakness, the yield curve and other quantitative measures of recession risk, we also think there's reason to be a bit more skeptical about the strength in labor markets. First, the boon from immigration has been an unequivocal positive for labor supply that came along right when there was an increase in services demand as the economy finally reopened from the lock downs. This has helped businesses provide these services at a more affordable cost. However, with services demand now cooling, particularly in restaurants, this excess labor may not be needed to the same degree. More notable to us is the very distinct downtrend in the Conference Board Employment Trends Index.

In our view, because many forecasters and investors were wrong about the recession outcome last year, there is little willingness to trust these types of signals now. There is also the classic hope now that the Fed can save the cycle from a hard landing. Our view has consistently been that we are in the midst of a later cycle expansion, the length of which has been aided considerably by unprecedented fiscal spending as well as delayed and creative sources of liquidity to pay for it. As we discuss in our bull case, this dynamic may continue to extend the cycle even further and lead to bubble type valuations for asset prices much like the late ${ \displaystyle 1 9 9 0 s } ,$ a period when policy was arguably too easy. As is typically the case, a recession gets priced when there is a shock that the market perceives is just too much for an already fragile economy to handle or for policy to overcome—i.e., Iraq invasion of Kuwait in the summer of 1990 and subsequent oil spike, 9-11, the Lehman bankruptcy in 2008, and the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020. Keep in mind that in all of these cases, we were very much late cycle with the yield curve already signaling we were in the window of elevated risk. In other words, had these shocks happened at healthier periods there is likely a good chance recession would not have arrived, except in the 2020 episode where we actively locked down the economy and drove unemployment immediately to 15%.

The point of this discussion is to remind readers that we are still in that window of risk where large swaths of the economy remain vulnerable to additional shocks and/or monetary policy that has now been objectively tight via the yield curve inversion for the longest period in recorded history. Furthermore, the recessions of 2001 and 2008 were posthumously deemed to have started 6-12 months prior to the events noted above. In other words, for most of 2001 and 2008, the economy was officially in a recession and investors/markets did not know it, or fully price it, until the event arrived. We think it’s fair to say that with stocks trading in the top decile of their historical valuations, such a risk is far from priced.

From a data standpoint, there is also growing evidence that the economy may not be as strong as advertised. At the macro data level there has been a significant downtick in consumer confidence. Furthermore, the University of Michigan survey tends to lead the Conference Board measure because the Conference Board survey is more directly tied to the labor markets than to how consumers feel about the economy and other variables, like inflation. The labor market is the ultimate lagging indictor. By the time it turns, it's too late to reverse.

Importantly, the Conference Board measure has recently started to "catch down" as it typically does later in the cycle. In short, we think this is confirming our general view that the labor market is beginning to soften albeit from a very strong place. This view has also been corroborated by the Conference Board Employment Trend Index which looks at the “internals” of the employment data as opposed to the headline NFP data—i.e., initial claims, job openings, temporary hiring, etc.

Bottom line, the uncertain backdrop we face warrants an investment approach that can work as market pricing and sector/factor leadership bounce between potential outcomes. ## 3) Long Utilities

We recommend Utilities as an overweight rated sector given its defensive and AI exposures as well as its re-rating potential as a result of lower interest rates. Utilities offer downside protection in what we view as a later cycle environment, while also offering upside to the AI power theme. Both traditional and alternative energy providers have upside revisions potential amid the increasing need for AI data center power and more favorable data center power deals. Our Power & Utilities team projects data center electricity consumption to rise from 3% of total US consumption in 2023 to \~10% by the end of the decade. ## 5) Large over Small

We hold a preference for large caps over small caps. We think large caps hold an advantage with stronger balance sheets, less interest cost exposure, and historically better performance in a later cycle environment. Small caps have high economic exposure which should benefit from an earnings acceleration but, ultimately, lower quality balance sheets and volatile cash flows should hold the group back, in our view. On this front, small caps typically see sustained outperformance in early cycle periods; their relative performance tends to be weaker in a later cycle backdrop, like we're in today ( Exhibit 48 ). We recognize the 5.5x P/E discount between small caps and large caps, but we would wait until an early cycle environment for a sustainable re-rating.

## Equity Performance Around Presidential Elections

Interest in the US Presidential Election's impact on markets has picked up as November is drawing nearer. However, we are still too far away from the election to see much market volatility from it. We looked at the VIX around election cycles going back to 1990 and found that equity market volatility doesn't typically pick up until September and then peaks just before the election in November ( Exhibit 64 ). We also looked at 30-day realized volatility around election cycles going back to 1928 and found a similar pattern ( Exhibit 65 ).

In order to understand how the market performs during and after elections we looked at the average returns of the market and industry groups around presidential elections going back to 1992. In the 6 months leading up to the election, which is roughly where we are today, the US equity market typically returns 50 bps. Quality, defensives and large caps tend to outperform during this time period as the market adopts more of a risk off posture. Market returns then become modestly negative (-100 bps) in the 3 months leading up to the election. Post election, the market typically posts strong, positive returns. Value tends to show particularly strong outperformance in the 12 months following the election ( Exhibit 66 ).

It is important to note that performance does vary cycle to cycle. During the 2016 election cycle, we saw value, cyclicals, and large caps outperform during the 6 months leading up to the election and the market was up 4%. In the 6 months after the election we saw growth, low quality, and cyclicals outperform as the market tilted more risk on ( Exhibit 67 ).

Our analysts see three potential phases of generative AI-driven productivity: (1) generic language AI tools (i.e., information retrieval apps), (2) custom models with access to company data (i.e., proprietary Q&A and summarization apps), and (3) custom models that take action on command and become agents/assistants (i.e., process automation apps). The progression of these phases can be characterized by a shift from task augmentation, such as a basic writing assistant, to process automation, such as an agent that can perform work processes on behalf of employees. They see us as currently being in Phase 1 as companies adopt generic off-the-shelf AI tools such as ChatGPT, Gemini/ Bard, and Github Co-Pilot. Investment in more proprietary tools/models is expected to ramp throughout '24 and into '25 (i.e., Microsoft Co-Pilot, Customized Llama models, etc.), and they expect a gradual slope of adoption due to the inherent frictions (i.e., ROI, Data/ Skill Sets, Legal/Regulatory) and technology hurdles (i.e., hallucinations, lack of a standardized tooling layer, etc.). As a result, our analysts believe we're not likely to see broad based productivity gains until 2025 and beyond.

Importantly, the path forward is unlikely to be linear nor will the productivity gains be consistent across sectors/industries as evidenced by the dispersion across industries shown in Exhibit 72 . Our analysts already see anecdotal and empirical evidence that

indicates varying degrees of productivity gains across use cases and industries during this Phase 1. There are strong fundamental reasons why coding productivity gains tend to be larger and more consistent today compared with more general knowledge based work flows (more on this below). As a result, our analysts believe there is a higher likelihood that we see broad based efficiency/productivity gains within Software/Internet first, before the broader economy and non-tech industries outlined in Exhibit 72 . See Leveraging AI to Drive Efficiency for detail on how AI has the potential to drive operational efficiency and productivity across segments of the market/economy.

On the macro front, our economists believe that the diffusion of AI may eventually sustain the recent increases in the level of productivity, and potentially the growth of productivity in the economy. Between the goods and services sectors, the growth in productivity in the services sector may sustain the growth in aggregate productivity. Traditionally, the services sector has been characterized by lower average productivity than the goods sector. However, in the recent data, the productivity in goods appears to already be flattening, while our economists think the productivity in services can sustain its upward trend. In fact, our work in today's note suggests that the more significant potential opportunities from AI are likely to be in the services sectors. On the employment front, labor market disruptions will likely require reskilling among the workforce. In terms of central bank policy paths, AI-led increases in productivity could also lead to lower inflation in the near-term, thereby allowing the central bank to set a lower policy rate. In the long-run, however, increases in r\* would likely push interest rates higher.

While AI-driven efficiency gains are likely to evolve over a multi-year time horizon, we don't believe it's too early to start thinking about potential single stock beneficiaries from this theme. On this score, we asked our equity analysts to consider Overweight and Equal-weight companies under coverage that they see incorporating AI to drive operational efficiency and productivity gains over the next 2 years. Where possible/ applicable, these companies have pricing power and can maintain it on a relative basis even as marginal costs theoretically come down as AI is successfully implemented.
