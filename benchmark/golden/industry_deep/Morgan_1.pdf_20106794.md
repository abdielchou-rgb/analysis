China<sup>’</sup>s National People<sup>’</sup>s Congress (NPC) will begin on March 5, one day after the Chinese People<sup>’</sup>s Political Consultative Conference (together the two meetings are usually referred to as the “Two Sessions<sup>”</sup>). The NPC meeting is the annual event to approve the government<sup>’</sup>s work report, fiscal budget and other policy targets, and hence closely watched.

We expect the NPC will keep its annual growth target unchanged at “around 5%<sup>”</sup> in 2024, which will be a challenging task as the reopening dividend has largely faded. To achieve the target, we have argued that both fiscal and monetary policy will be more growth-friendly than 2023, but not a “bazooka<sup>”</sup> style stimulus. On fiscal policy, we look for a 3.8% of GDP budgetary deficit, which could be in the form of 3% official deficit plus 1 trillion yuan special central government bonds. In addition, we expect the quota of special local government bonds at 3.5 trillion yuan this year (vs. 3.8 trillion yuan in 2023). We expect the government will maintain its inflation target at 3% (a ceiling concept in practice), which is not binding as our forecast of average CPI this year is 0.6%. We expect the government will reiterate its monetary policy narrative with expanded use of structural monetary policy.

What could surprise us? If Premier Li Qiang<sup>’</sup>s first work report shares market concerns of economic challenges, including deflation risk, the structural unemployment issue and damage from the housing market correction, it will be a positive surprise for us. Another area of positive surprise could be the budgetary deficit. If the number surprises on the high side, this will suggest that the fiscal authority, especially the central government, will take more responsibility in growth stabilization efforts. If it is announced that the 1 trillion yuan special CGB scheme will become a multi-year program, or the NPC lifts the debt ceiling for local governments to create more room for local government hidden debt swap, it will be a positive surprise as well.

For the 3rd Plenum of the 20<sup>th</sup> Party Congress, it has been delayed and there is no clarity on a possible date. Our expectation for the 3<sup>rd</sup> Plenum is relatively low for two reasons.

First, there is no signal that the party will choose to deviate from the high-quality growth framework as laid out in the 19<sup>th</sup> Party Congress. Second, the market is looking for more action rather than policy reiteration, or looking for a directional change rather than continuation of current policy measures. From that perspective, the probability of upside surprise is low.

## PMIs point to steady growth momentum

China<sup>’</sup>s February manufacturing PMIs were in line with expectations, with a decent uptick in the NBS non-manufacturing PMIs suggesting steady near-term growth momentum. The NBS manufacturing PMI eased modestly by 0.1-pt to 49.1 (Figure 1), with the output component down 1.5-pt, largely reflecting the LNY seasonal factor. The Caixin manufacturing PMI, which tends to show less LNY seasonality, rose 0.1-pt to 50.9 in February (the highest reading in six months). The third consecutive monthly increase in the export orders component of the export-oriented Caixin manufacturing PMI is worth noting, hinting at somewhat constructive near-term outlook on export sector activity.

Figure 1: China manufacturing PMIs
![](images/266e5a803fc9a3140482c397b357f300d157f6cb72be921efa3a010051e1ca72.jpg)

The NBS non-manufacturing PMI shows solid gain of 0.7-pt to 51.4 in February (the highest reading in five months, Figure 2). The service activity component ticked up further by 0.9-pt to 51.0, largely reflecting solid pickup in travel-related activities during the Lunar New Year holiday. On the other hand, the construction activity index fell 0.4-pt to 53.4, reflecting impact of cold weather, as well as seasonal factor.

Putting together the manufacturing and non-manufacturing components, the NBS composite PMI output stayed unchanged at 50.9 (highest level since October), suggesting steady overall economic activity momentum. On the cautious side, housing market activity appears to remain sluggish. The employment component of the PMIs remains subdued, suggesting ongoing labor market sluggishness which will drag on household sentiment. Lingering softness in pricing power and general deflation concerns continue to stress corporate profit and cash flow, restraining the private investment outlook.

## Hong Kong: Exports beat expectations

Hong Kong<sup>’</sup>s January merchandise exports came in above expectations on LNY seasonal effects, rising 33.6%oya to HK\$388.7bn, following an 11.0%oya rise in December. Seasonally adjusted, we estimate that the city<sup>’</sup>s exports rose 2.0%m/m sa. Imports rose 21.7%oya on a 2.2%m/m sa fall. The monthly merchandise trade activities realized a surplus of HK\$3.6bn, the first time since January 2022.

Mainland China is the largest trade partner of HK SAR, accounting for 55.6% of the city<sup>’</sup>s exports and 43.5% of the imports in 2023. By our estimate, exports to mainland China rose 54.2%oya or 7.9%m/m sa in January while exports to Asia as a whole rose 45.7%oya. Exports to Japan fell 7.4%m/ m sa, and exports to the US fell 1.6%.

## The 2024-25 Budget

The Financial Secretary for Hong Kong delivered the FY24/25 budget speech on February 28, with newly announced economic supportive schemes. Current fiscal year FY23/24 is expected to record a deficit of HK\$101.6bn, larger than the original estimate of HK\$54.4bn. The government revenue was revised down by HK\$87.8bn to HK\$554.6bn, mainly due to the shortage from land premium and stamp duty. In particular, revenue from land premium is HK\$46.2bn lower than the original estimate, while revenue from stamp duty is HK\$35bn lower than the original estimate.

Given the trade-offs between supporting economic growth and restoring fiscal balance, Hong Kong will not rush to fiscal balance in 2024/25. The government announced to cancel all demand-side management measures for residential properties with immediate effect, as an effort to revive the property market. The Government anticipates FY24/25 total government expenditure to increase by 6.7%y/y to HK\$776.9bn, while revenue will increase to HK\$633.0bn. Considering the proceeds from issuance of government bonds of \~HK\$120bn, FY24/25 will post a fiscal deficit of HK\$48.1bn, or 1.5% of GDP based on our forecast (Figure 3).

## Retail sales fell 1.2% on LNY seasonality

Hong Kong<sup>’</sup>s retail sales volume came in significantly below expectations in January, falling 1.2%oya (Figure 4). Seasonally adjusted, we estimate that retail sales volume fell 1.9%m/m sa in January, adding to the 2.8%m/m sa decline in December. The weakness in retail sales growth is partly due to the Lunar New Year seasonal effect. According to the HK C&SD, local consumer spending normally attains a seasonal high before the Festival. Historical data also suggests that retail sales tend to be weaker in January if the LNY starts in February. Thus, it would be more meaningful to examine the situation when both Jan and Feb data become available.

Figure 4: Hong Kong retail sales and private consumption
![](images/11351e71033c7cb3fd7c046b702dcb5648a2d48d101a4c1354a6693aaa10a3b3.jpg)

Looking ahead, we believe inbound tourism will still be the driving factor of consumption growth in HK, yet the imbalance between inbound and outbound visitor recovery may continue to weigh on HK<sup>’</sup>s consumption. In February, visitor arrivals from the Mainland averaged at 93.7% of the pre-pandemic level (2015-2018 average), while outbound HK residents averaged at 127.8% of the pre-pandemic level. On the other hand, the low unemployment environment, household income growth, together with various promotional campaigns and activities launched by the government, should continue to benefit the retail sector. In the near term, the LNY seasonal factor may have somewhat boosted retail sales growth in February. However, retail sales are not likely to get back to the pre-pandemic level soon.

## Taiwan: Export orders rebounded on tech

Taiwan<sup>’</sup>s export orders peaked in 1Q22 and trended down persistently through 1H23, reflecting the fading of the Covidrelated WFH tech demand boom, downturn in the global manufacturing sector, as well as de-stocking pressure in the global tech sector. From June 2023 onwards, the level of export orders appeared to have stabilized somewhat, followed by volatiliti in the past few months. In January, export orders showed a significant sequential rebound, rising 24.6% m/m sa, following the 13.9% drop in December. It was led by tech orders, which jumped 30.3% m/m sa in January, with a broadbased rebound in orders for electronics products as well information and communication products. Non-tech orders also showed a decent gain, rising 6.8% m/m sa in January (vs. -2.8% in December). By source of demand, the rebound was led by orders from US (+32.0% m/m sa) and the ASEAN region (+53.1%), along with a broad-based rebound in orders from China/ HKSAR (+12.1%), Europe (+10.6%) and Japan (+4.7%) (Figure 5).

![](images/a02c1ef831bca468b5367b3a89c51b1886c51e32ee0acf6b68aad8bb53e4f806.jpg)

Going forward, we look for moderate growth in tech exports. That includes the structural boost for AI-related products, HPC-related demand, and likely improving consumer demand in the coming quarters. Meanwhile while the tech sector<sup>’</sup>s destocking pressure has begun to ease. Taiwan<sup>’</sup>s tech export orders have underperformed actual tech exports since early 2023. This likely reflects the boost of AI-related tech demand (largely produced in Taiwan manufacturing plants), while Taiwan manufacturers<sup>’</sup> production lines in mainland China (orders for which are included in Taiwan<sup>’</sup>s overall export orders compilation) seem to be restrained by regional supply chain adjustments. As such, the significant rebound in January export orders is encouraging, suggesting near-term solid performance in tech exports for Taiwan and possibly mainland China as well.

## January IP beat expectations

Taiwan<sup>’</sup>s IP beat expectations in January, rising 16.0% overyear-ago. Seasonally-adjusted, we estimate that IP rebounded 5.7% m/m sa in January, reversing the decline of 4.5% m/m sa in December. The breakdown shows tech-related manufacturing IP rebounded 5.1% m/m sa in January, following the decline of 4.7% m/m sa in December. Non-tech manufacturing IP also showed solid gain of 5.0% m/m sa.

Regarding the major trends in Taiwan<sup>’</sup>s export and industrial production cycles, merchandise exports returned to positive sequential growth starting from 2Q23, led by tech exports. This was followed by the sequential upturn in industrial production which began to come through since 3Q23, with the time lag between export and IP recovery reflecting the drag of inventory adjustment.

Following the aggressive inventory de-stocking by the Taiwan tech manufacturers since early 2023, manufacturing (tech) inventory has been turning up in recent months. It may hint at manufacturers<sup>’</sup> constructive outlook on near-term demand conditions. Tech inventory rose 3.3% m/m sa in December (or up 43.5% 3m/3m saar). The tech sector<sup>’</sup>s inventory to shipment ratio, having fallen consistently since early 2023, seems to turn back up again lately. Tech inventory to shipment ratio came in at 1.50, sa in December, compared to 1.36, sa in November (Figure 6).

## 4Q GDP shows moderate demand growth

Following solid 3Q GDP growth, Taiwan<sup>’</sup>s economy continued to show strong growth in 4Q. Q GDP grew 4.9%oya (compared to 5.2% in the advanced 4Q GDP report). Seasonally adjusted, GDP grew 9.7% q/q saar in 4Q, following 3Q growth of 7.9% q/q saar. Details in 4Q GDP show moderate growth in exports of goods and services (4.2% q/q saar) and private consumption (2.6%), while capex remained sluggish (fixed capital formation contracted 6.4%). Upturn in the inventory cycle has provided some support to overall growth. For full-year 2023, Taiwan GDP grew 1.3%yoy.

Merchandise trade (27 Feb)

On the domestic front, the recent easing in employment growth, and hence household income, will likely restrain the pace of domestic demand growth this year. Overall, we expect the Taiwan economy to show moderate growth going ahead, with average 2.2% q/q saar growth in 1H24 and 2.6% q/q saar in 2H24, reflecting moderate growth in industrial activity and domestic demand. Our forecast for 2024 GDP growth stays unchanged at 4.3%yoy.

## Manufacturing PMI eased modestly

After tracking a significant downward trend throughout 2022, Taiwan<sup>’</sup>s manufacturing PMI readings appear to be on a rather volatile, moderate upward trend (from subdued level) since early 2023, with decent uptick through 4Q23 and going into early 2024. While the manufacturing PMI eased modestly in February, down 0.2-pt to 48.6, it followed the notable gain of 1.7-pt in January, and still registered the second highest reading in 12 months (Figure 7).

Figure 7: Global and Taiwan manufacturing PMIs
![](images/2a319dfd2e754359759c89cae4dad0e119fa0c1efe01e81a4ca4bec1f09fc940.jpg)

Regarding the cyclical momentum in the industrial sector, it is somewhat comforting to note that while the headline manufacturing PMI eased modestly in February, down 0.2-pt to 48.6, it followed the solid gain of 1.7-pt in January, and still registered the second highest reading in 12 months. On the cautious side, signals from the export orders component have been rather mixed, with a decline of 0.7-pt in February, following the 2.2-pt gain in January: this seems to suggest the encouraging, significant rebound in January export orders may reverse course in February.

In addition, we have noted the recent upturn in Taiwan<sup>’</sup>s inventory cycle as mentioned above, and here the recent trend in the manufacturing PMI<sup>’</sup>s inventory component seems to send a similar signal: the finished goods inventory indicator picked up 2.0-pt to 49.4 in February, highest reading since April 2022. As such, the manufacturing PMI order to inventory ratio fell to 0.96 in February, lowest reading in six months, which may somewhat restrain the room for industrial activity growth going forward.
