## A cautious approach to the upcoming easing cycle

Policymakers are taking a cautious approach to the upcoming cycle compared to both market pricing and previous easing cycles. ECB GC member Schnabel pointed to uncertainty about r\* and a cutting cycle with small steps and potentially even a pause. Fed vice chair Jefferson emphasized that the July 1995 easing cycle was a "perfect soft landing", where the FOMC started to ease but then left rates unchanged for three meetings as it waited for more information before easing again. Despite the frontloading of hikes earlier in the cycle, growth has been relatively robust, especially in the US, as the pass-through of hikes has varied across countries and sectors (Figure 2 and Figure 3). Stronger-than-expected US NFP and CPI data this month suggest that our earlier arguments for a wide 10y US Treasury vs Bund spread will hold for longer.

## Later Fed start requires higher confidence on disinflation elsewhere

A later start to the Fed hiking cycle will require stronger confidence that inflation is falling back to target for the ECB to cut. Nevertheless, we think that pricing on the Dec'24 meeting (at 2.99%) is close to fair again with 100 bps of cuts priced for 2024 after the market priced out 40bps of cuts since early Feb, when we recommended a payer. Inflation in the Euro area is tracking below the ECB's December macroeconomic projections. UBS and the market expect the disinflation process to continue in the euro area, with inflation (HICP xT) projected to fall below 2% in the summer before a moderate rebound in the second half of 2024. We think that the ECB is likely to lower its 2024 headline HICP forecast by 0.3pp to 2.4%, but leave 2025 and 2026 unchanged at 2.1% and 1.9%, respectively, as well as the timing of the return to 2% (Q3-25). We think the downward revision will be driven mainly by a significantly lower gas price assumption (c.-40%) and by weaker-than-expected inflation in recent months. The significant market repricing since the December projections implies a c. 90bps lower policy rate assumption for end-2024.

## The starting point of this tightening cycle was different

The sharp growth in monetary aggregates and asset price gains following the coordinated fiscal-monetary response to the pandemic boosted net financial assets of households, especially in the US, but as a percentage of GDP we are closer to 2019 levels again (Figure 4). Some point to Dornbusch and Wolf (1990) to argue that a big one-shot inflation episode was necessary to restore unusually elevated real monetary balances back to equilibrium (see lessons from the great reflation). In its Winter 2024 forecast, the European Commission estimates excess savings in the euro area at €1 tn but also makes the point that a significant “unleashing of excess savings to provide an extra boost to consumption appears less likely than previously thought.”

## BTP Valore to support BTP spreads

We anticipate that the demand for the third BTP Valore issue next week will remain high (expected €15-17bn), resulting in tighter BTP spreads approaching the 140bps level. The first two issues (issued in June and October of last year) attracted a total of \~€35bn, alleviating the funding pressure on the Italian Treasury to issue more conventional bonds to finance the revised budget deficits. Despite the upward repricing in the ECB policy rate path and the record EGB issuance, BTP spreads continued to remain supported. The 10y BTP-Bund spread, currently at c.150bps, is trading closer to the lower end of the recent trading range.

Nevertheless, BTP spreads still appear cheap when compared to closely correlated risk factors such as the iTraxx Europe Index (Figure 5). We do not anticipate any immediate political event in Italy that could increase pressure on BTPs. Furthermore, UBS credit strategists have recently updated their projections for credit spreads in Europe. They remain optimistic about credit spreads in the first half of 2024 as the disinflation process in the Eurozone continues.

## UK: Continue to see value in front end

Current market pricing, which suggests 66bps cuts from the MPC this year, remains below our baseline forecast of 100bps of BoE cuts. The inflation and wage growth outlook in the UK, along with the volatility of US data, are likely to continue influencing the extent and timing of the BoE's cutting cycle. However, we still find value in the UK front end and maintain our SONIA March 24 vs December 24 curve flattener vs EURIBOR recommendation (entry: 28bps, current: 21bps, target: 0bps, stop: 38bps).

Besides the monetary policy developments, market focus in the coming weeks will be on the Spring Budget, which will be presented on March 6 by Chancellor Jeremy Hunt. While our economists expect the fiscal policy stance to become more restrictive in the coming years, the Spring Budget is likely to contain some fiscal giveaways ahead of the general election later in the year. Coupled with the already high gross gilt issuance, this could keep the pressure on Gilts. The PSNB for FY 23-24 is tracking around £9bn lower than the OBR projections in November. In addition, the fiscal headroom that the OBR estimated after the Autumn Statement in November was nearly £13bn. The additional fiscal space is widely expected to be spent on tax cuts, which we think could result in an upward revision of the FY 24-25 remit by nearly £4 billion, assuming that PBNB and fiscal headroom estimates remain at these levels until the end of FY 23-24.

Speaking to the Treasury Select Committee on Tuesday this week, Governor Bailey said that inflation "had come down very rapidly" in the UK, while he downplayed the significance of the technical recession that the UK economy officially entered after the release of the Q4 2024 GDP data last week. His comments, overall, were leaning towards the dovish side, in our view. The Governor stressed that he would like to see more progress on wage and services inflation and the state of the labour market. However, he also mentioned that market expectations of the BoE easing cycle during this year were not "unreasonable."

Ben Broadbent expressed a more hawkish stance at the same hearing. He mentioned that services inflation and wage growth were still increasing at “probably almost twice the rate” consistent with stable 2% inflation. The services inflation increased by 0.1pp in January to 6.5% y/y, which was lower than expected. Meanwhile, the BoE's preferred measure of private sector regular pay decreased by 0.4% to 6.2% y/y, the lowest rate since August 2022. This implies that services and wage growth levels should move closer to 3% to be consistent with stable 2% inflation, according to Broadbent's views.

Despite the rebound in activity, as reflected in February's PMI released this week, and expectations of a “shallow” recession, Citi's economic surprise index shows a divergence in economic surprises between the UK and the Eurozone and the US economies. The challenging UK inflation outlook and the resilient labour market could explain the divergence in BoE policy rate expectations compared to other major central banks. UBS economists expect inflation to return to a declining trajectory in February, while Ben Broadbent mentioned that wage growth was encouraging. He said that he is "reasonably confident" that wage growth would edge down as inflation continues to stabilize.

## CHF: ESTR vs SARON widening could persist

We think that the divergence in the policy rate expectations between the ECB and the SNB may continue due to strong data, leading to a further widening in the ESTR vs SARON spread. Despite the upward repricing in the ECB's policy rate expectations, the market is pricing in nearly 97bps of cuts from the ECB in 2024. President Lagarde warned last week against a hasty rate cut, which could prompt the ECB to take further actions if inflation rebounds. GC member Isabel Schnabel also mentioned the recent pickup in demand as a challenge for the disinflation process. The Eurozone PMI Composite rose by 1 point to reach an 8-month high of 48.9, although more pronounced variations were observed across sectors and countries.

We anticipate that the SNB will start its cutting cycle in June, followed by two additional cuts in September and December, ultimately reaching a terminal rate of 1%. A March SNB cut cannot be ruled out, considering the inflation outlook and the risks of CHF appreciation. Inflation in Switzerland came in weaker than expected in January, falling by 40bps to 1.3% y/y, which is well within the SNB's target range of "below 2%". UBS forecasts inflation to average 1.4% in 2024 and 2025, while the SNB projects 1.9% and 1.6% in 2024/25.

Against this backdrop, we struggle to identify significant structural factors that could apply tightening pressure on ESTR vs SARON spreads. From a valuation perspective, the 10y spread is trading 15bps below the peak levels seen in November of last year. Although the levels appear attractive, we think that European rates will continue to face pressure in the near term. This, combined with the Swiss inflation outlook and the dovish stance of the SNB, could potentially push the spread towards the peak levels in this cycle.

In the money markets, the approximately 63bps of cuts from the SNB priced into December do not appear excessive in our view. We have been recommending flatteners in the SARON March vs December curve. Following the Swiss CPI data last week, this segment of the curve flattened by nearly 10bps and it has been range trading since then. We continue to see room for further flattening in this part of the curve.
