# US and EM Credit Strategy

# Looking Across Borders

While US IG and EM sovereign spreads are highly correlated, we think dislocations are creating opportunities for investors. We provide a detailed comparison of the two markets and see an unusually good opportunity to switch into EM Sovereigns out the curve.

We think investors in DM and EM markets can benefit from looking across the way. This report offers a roadmap. We start with a fact sheet comparing the two asset classes today, with charts on key metrics. Then we go deeper, comparing (1) supply, (2) flows, (3) investor bases (4) duration/curves, (5) ratings, and (6) fundamentals. After reviewing the trajectory of IG and EM index valuations, we focus on opportunities in steeper EM curves, especially in higher quality.

Key differences between the markets: For US investors exploring opportunities abroad, EM IG is a smaller market but has grown in the last decade as issuance has increased, particularly from the Gulf. It has a higher duration and average life than the US credit market and an improving ratings profile.

Where investors can find value when spreads are tight: In US long-end IG, spreads to Treasuries are in the first percentile of the last 20 years, while the rest of the curve trades wider versus historical tights. Although EM sovereign spreads are also on the tight end, they've lagged the US corporate rally, partially on account of heavier net issuance. With much of this issuance out of the way and the spread pick up in long-end EM IG at its highest since early 2022, we see value in switching to select EM IG credits.

We identify pockets of opportunity for reallocation from US IG into EM IG, especially further out the curve. Within the AA/A-rated space we prefer the Saudi Arabia (KSA) sovereign long end. Within the broader Abu Dhabi complex, we also think the Mamoura quasi-sovereign curve offers value. In the BBB space, the Mexico curve offers above-average steepness versus US BBB issuers.

## Executive summary

## Overview

Global IG credit markets have been remarkably resilient this year in the face of sticky inflation data, Fed cuts getting pushed out, and negative total returns. Notably, both DM and EM credit have held up despite their different sensitivities to a higher rates regime. While the two asset classes represent very different underlying risks (in this report, US IG is corporate credit while EM IG is sovereigns), spreads have been highly correlated.

Under the surface we see some dislocations between the two markets that create opportunities. Along these lines, we think investors in both markets can look at the other either for overall signals on technicals or for pockets of relative value. Today, the EM IG market offers a compelling opportunity for DM credit investors looking for better valuations or simply diversification.

## What EM IG offers to DM investors:

• The quality of the EM IG index has improved over time. In aggregate, the average rating is now in-line with US IG.

• For natural long-duration buyers looking to hedge against long-dated liabilities, EM IG is among the few credit markets with a substantial stock of outstanding long duration debt. EM is also seeing significant issuance, providing US credit investors a liquid avenue to layer in exposure.

• Valuations in EM IG are cheaper than US IG adjusted for ratings quality. While fundamentals in EM can be driven by very different factors than US corporates, ultimately spreads are strongly correlated in the two markets, hence compression is highly likely, in our view.

• In particular, the 10s-30s curves in high quality EM IG are much steeper than in comparable US IG names. For active US investors or yield-buyers that find 30Y spreads in IG to be too rich, we like looking at EM AA/A credits as an alternative. In contrast to AA/A, BBB curves in EM are actually flatter with the exception of Mexico, Panama and Romania.

• In a sharply higher rates regime, EM is likely to underperform on (1) fiscal/ external considerations and (2) a less diversified demand base. However, our top picks in this space are higher quality names that we believe are likely to hold up much better than the index.

## Highlights of our comparative deep dive

Supply: US and EM IG both saw a slowdown in issuance trends post the 2020 peak, but 2024 is off to a strong start with record issuance in both asset classes YTD.

Flows: US IG is experiencing a strong streak of inflows YTD despite negative total returns. By contrast, EM IG has been unable to attract meaningful flows back into dedicated funds, but portfolio flow data suggest somewhat more broad-based buying of EM.

Investor bases: US IG benefits from strong dedicated yield buyers in the form of insurance companies and pension funds as well as Bloomberg Aggregate (Agg) investors that provide a baseline of strong sponsorship for the asset classs. EM investors include some dedicated and cross-over funds, banks, sovereign wealth funds, and retail.

Duration & curves: Strong demand from yield buyers has driven the US IG 10s30s curve to its flattest levels since the GFC. Among large, liquid EM issuers, we see a flatter 5s10s curve but a steeper 10s30s curve vs US IG.

Ratings: Both US and EM IG ratings have improved in recent years at the index level, with EM IG largely converging to the US following the inclusion of Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) sovereigns in the index in 2019.

Fundamentals: US IG company balance sheets weathered the higher interest environment over the last two years with resilience. In EM IG, the rise in commodity prices from early 2022 onwards has been a key driver. Oil exporters saw fiscal and external balances improve markedly as hydrocarbon revenues spiked and often used the windfall to delever.

## Index-level valuations

US IG spread levels compressed below 100bp in late 2023 and have remained there since January. YTD excess returns are positive but total returns are negative. EM IG outright levels are also towards the tights with a slight spread reprieve over the past month. Dispersion in EM IG is high, however, with oil exporters at the tights whilst Europe, Israel and Panama trade wide. Compared to its longer-term history, EM IG cheapened up notably versus US IG in 2024, following a two-year period of relative richness to US IG. This holds even when adjusting for ratings differentials.

## Opportunities for duration buyers in steeper EM curves

Across the largest US IG issuers vs liquid EM IG names, US IG tends to show steeper 5s10s curves, while EM IG shows steeper 10s30s curves. AA/A EM IG 10s30s (current avg. 32bp) curves are steeper than AA/A US IG names (current avg. 18bps).

Within the AA/A-rated space, we prefer the Saudi Arabia (KSA) sovereign long end, which offers a steep curve, strong fundamentals and attractive valuations versus peers. Within the Abu Dhabi complex we also think the Mamoura quasi-sovereign curve, which provides an extra 20-25bp over the sovereign, offers value. In the BBB space, the Mexico curve offers above-average steepness versus US BBB issuers (for more detail, see How Mexico's Elections Matter, 28th April 2024).

US IG: US issuance peaked in 2020 given the access to easier funding but has since moderated with the start of the rate hiking cycle. However, gross IG issuance roared back to life at the beginning of 2024. The IG market saw record breaking gross issuance this year in January and February followed by another solid March number, bringing year-todate totals to US\$665bn through April end, up 33% y/y. Notably, long-end supply, which shrunk in 4Q23 following a back up in rates and high all in yields, started making a recovery in February. Between October and January, long-end supply accounted for less than 10% of total monthly gross issuance; that number rebounded to close to 20% in February and remained in the mid-teens in March and April.

EM IG: The arrival of Gulf issuance in 2016 changed the supply picture permanently: In EM, IG issuance was catapulted by the emergence of Gulf issuance (Saudi Arabia, Qatar, United Arab Emirates) starting in 2016, as lower oil prices drove fiscal deficits wider. Following the cycle low in 2022, 2024 issuance is off to a strong start..: Following some recovery in 2023, EM sovereign IG issuance in USD is off to a strong start this year, mirroring the strength in the DM primary markets. Just four months into the year, EM IG sovereigns have issued a combined US\$54bn. This constitutes the fastest run-rate recorded in well over a decade, except for 2020, and a 23% y/y increase.

In EM, long-end issuance remains muted versus history, but still above US IG levels as a fraction of total issuance: With the rise in core rates and funding costs starting in 2022, EM saw a collapse in long-end supply in favor of shorter-duration issuance. US IG: Demand rebounding. Flows into US IG were tepid in 2023 after record outflows in 2022, but the dynamic has shifted in 2024. ## Investor Bases

The divergence in investor profiles and natural demand from benchmarked buyers plays a role in creating EM-DM valuation gaps and divergence in curve steepness, leaving opportunities for investors that are able to invest across both markets. We outline the demand profile of both markets below.

US IG: For US corporate credit, a distinguishing feature is the strong presence of yield buyers. Insurance and pension funds own 35% of the US IG market (over \$2tn), according to the US Federal Reserve. Additional players include mutual funds, overseas investors

and financial institutions. Within mutual funds, many funds are benchmarked to the Agg index and even if investors have a somewhat cautious view on credit, this creates a baseline level of strong sponsorship for the asset class.

US IG: Approximately 33% of US IG is in the long end (10y+), while the front end (<5y) accounts for 39%, with the remainder in the belly. The composition of the index has shifted following the start of the hiking cycle, especially with the longest part of the curve (30y) representing a smaller share of the index, down to 15% from a peak of 17% in 2021. Meanwhile, the shortest part of the curve (1-3y) is now the second-largest sector of the asset class, increasing share from a low of 16% during Covid to now 20%.

The shift in the maturity composition of the index has also impacted spread curves. Over the last decade, both the 5s10s curve and the 10s30s curve traded on average in the 30bp to 50bp range. However, curve behavior started diverging in 2022, with curves in the long end (10s30s) becoming increasingly flat and inverting for sustained periods since 2022. This curve inversion has been driven by strong demand from yield buyers, such as insurance companies, as mentioned earlier, who have been eager to bid the more attractive yield levels following the rise in rates. Additionally, rates volatility in 4Q23 meant higher all-in yields, but much lower supply as issuers decided to shy away from issuing at such expensive levels. This imbalance of strong demand but low supply drove the 10s30s curve even flatter, to levels last seen during the GFC. While long-end supply has recovered somewhat YTD in 2024, there is still robust demand in the space to keep curves flat to inverted.

Meanwhile, the 5s10s curve widened after the Fed started hiking, reaching a local peak of 65bp in September 2022. And while curves have flattened over recent months, they remain only slightly below the 10-year average rather than the extreme inverted levels in the long end. Front-end IG curves also remain well in the range of historical averages.

The flatness of the long end remains true, though to a lesser degree when we construct curves from a population of large single A and BBB non-fin IG issuers with stable broad ratings since 2019. The 10s30s curve for these 15 issuers have compressed to the 15bp range, down from 45bp in December 2019 ( Exhibit 21 ).

EM IG: EM IG is a slightly higher-duration asset class than DM IG, driven partially by strong demand for long and ultralong issuance from the higher-quality segment of EM. This demand is a result of involvement by pension and insurance funds, and of EM funds' preference for duration in IG, as these funds use EM IG long-ends to balance out shorterduration spread risk in the higher-risk HY bucket.

Another driver of index duration is that EM IG sovereign supply mainly focuses on the benchmark 5y, 10y, and 30y points, while Latin America also has added some 20y bonds (see EM Sovereign Credit Strategy: Back to the Roaring Twenties, 29 April 2021). With rising rates and funding pressures, some ultra-short (3.5y and below) issuance is emerging but this remains small compared to the corporate space (2% in EM IG versus 20% in US IG in 2023). Also, this is largely driven by private placements in non-USD currencies for EM. Combined with a stronger recovery in long end issuance in EM IG, average life has not fallen as much in US IG.

Exhibit 27: EM IG and US IG average ratings are improving and converging

US IG: Index quality is concentrated in A-/BBB+. Single-As account for approximately 45% of the index and BBBs make up just under half (47%) by market value. The remainder is in the AA/AAA bucket. Notably, the quality of the IG index has improved in recent years and now represents the highest quality index in close to a decade. In 2015, BBBs overtook single-As as the largest cohort of the index, and they have maintained their dominant share since. However, the shares of BBBs and single-As have started converging again since 2021, with the share of BBBs down from a peak of 52%. Single-As have increased their share, up from lows of 38% in 2017. While BBBs remain the largest cohort, the gap in share between single-As and BBBs has narrowed significantly.

Ratings migrations skewed toward upgrades. Following a wave of rating downgrades during the Covid period, US IG has since shown steady positive ratings improvement at the index level. In both 2022 and 2023, US IG credit saw more upgrades as opposed to downgrades, with the upgrade versus downgrade skew by debt outstanding staying above 2x in both years. The trend is continuing in 2024. Year-to-date through March, more than US\$240bn has been upgraded within IG compared to the US\$100bn that has been downgraded. Fallen-angel volumes since 2022 have also remained relatively muted, accounting for less than US\$30bn in both 2022 and 2023. These volumes are dwarfed by the rising-star volumes as issuers moved out of HY into IG, driven heavily by the Energy sector.

The bulk of the improvement came in 2019 via the inclusion of Gulf credits in the EMBI suite of indices, which pushed average ratings higher by nearly a full notch.

While ratings have trended upwards, this is also due to fallen angels: The continued improvement in ratings more recently may surprise EM investors that have become accustomed to downgrades, but two reasons likely explain much of the improvement.

First, three issuers (Colombia, Morocco, and Sharjah) have fallen out of the IG index since 2020, versus no IG upgrades on the USD side (Croatia attained IG on the EUR side), meaning that some of the recent improvement would have been mechanical.

Second, hydrocarbon exporters have also generally seen upward momentum in ratings as higher oil prices saw external and fiscal improvement. Kazakhstan, Qatar, Saudi Arabia have all seen upgrades. Beyond that, Uruguay has also seen upwards momentum despite not being an exporter.

We see more scope for upgrades to IG than downgrades to HY over near term: Looking ahead, we think upgrades to IG will likely outweigh downgrades to HY in EM over the coming years. On the upgrade front, Oman, Serbia, and Morocco all have BB+ rating with at least one positive outlook now. ## Fundamentals

US IG company balance sheets weathered the higher interest environment over the last two years with resilience. Covid caused a spike in leverage metrics but companies were able to build significant cash balances during that time period which helped maintain balance sheets in the face of the hiking cycle. Gross leverage, which peaked during Covid, has since moderated and remains steady, holding in the 2.2x range since late 2H22. EBITDA margins appear to have bottomed in 2Q23, showing two quarters of improvement since.

Cash-to-debt ratios rose sharply during 2020-2021 but saw declines once the hiking cycle started. However, companies have been showing an improvement in cash-to-debt ratios since 3Q23. Interest coverage ratios, on the other hand, continued to show sharp declines in recent quarters. But we note that this metric is down from historically high levels given lower interest costs during 2020-2021 and currently remain in line with historical averages (see US Credit Strategy: 4Q23 Fundamentals).

• US IG spread levels compressed below 100bp in late 2023 and have remained there since January. YTD excess returns are positive but total returns are negative.

• EM IG outright levels are also towards the tights with a slight spread reprieve over the past month. Dispersion in EM IG however is high, with oil exporters at the tights whilst Europe, Israel and Panama trade wide.

• Compared to a longer-term history, EM IG cheapened up notably in January 2024, following a two-year period of relative richness to US IG. This holds even when adjusting for ratings differentials.

US IG: US IG spreads (using the Bloomberg Barclays Corporate Bond Index) reached cycle peaks above 160bp in both October 2022 and March 2023 following the UK LDI and regional banking crises but compressed shortly after fears of a bigger impact subsided. We brought in our year-ahead spread targets to account for the better-than-expected growth in the US, resilient IG fundamentals, and strong technicals that have prompted IG to reach cycle tights of 85bp in May. While the recent upside surprises in inflation prints have pushed spreads wider, the index remains below the 100bp mark.

Risks to our view

Our view, outlined above, is that the pick-up available in select AA/A EM IG long ends is attractive enough to warrant switches. Investors considering these switches should however be mindful of the risks associated with investing in EM IG in absolute terms, but also across macro scenarios that might hurt US IG less in relative terms. We outline those we see as most relevant below.

## 1) What happens if rates spike higher?

The strength in growth and inflation this year has had investors focused on the path for Fed policy going forward. While the FOMC meeting last week suggested that the bar for a rate hike is quite high, we nonetheless see a significant minority of investors concerned about Treasury yields getting unanchored. And the worry isn't necessarily just about the possibility of more tightening, but also a potential bear steepening of the yield curve on the back of higher inflation expectations (driven by a combination of high deficits, a premature dovish pivot etc.).

To be clear, in their central scenario, our economists do not expect these fears to materialise. They think inflation resets lower substantially in the coming months as shelter inflation cools, and the residual seasonality that put upward pressure on prices in 1Q moderates. Our rates colleagues have also written about The Problem with Steepeners, highlighting that the possibility of 'insurance cuts' (similar to 2019) makes it difficult for the curve to bear steepen markedly. Our forecast into year-end remains for meaningfully lower yields.

Nonetheless, it is worth outlining what happens to our views on EM IG vs. US IG in the event that the higher long-end rates scenario materializes. This would clearly be a risk to our preference for select AA/A rated EM long ends, although the magnitude of any underperformance would depend on what drives rates higher.

If inflation is accelerating to the point that the market loses confidence around peak rates and a renewed hiking cycle is priced in, that should hurt risk assets overall. EM IG is likely to underperform US IG as the higher beta asset class, but directionally US credit will not remain immune, as technicals turn and the range of outcomes around future risks is broader.

On the other hand, if rates rise because of 1) very strong US growth, and 2) inflation expectations going marginally higher (without a hawkish policy response), we think EM may be able to remain more aligned with US performance. For one, a greater spread cushion is available in EM IG now to absorb a moderate re-calibration higher in core yields. Another offsetting factor here is that EM IG curves at the long-end should flatten in such a rates move. And considering that our preference for EM AA/A sovereign curves over US IG is at the long-end, the underperformance there might be less pronounced than at shorter/intermediate tenors.

Taking a more bottom-up approach, for US IG we have argued that the sensitivity of corporate balance sheets to higher rates is quite low. This is a function of many factors that aligned post Covid.

a) Companies were forced to term out maturities meaningfully. This means that refinancing risks are relatively low. In addition, as more debt gets refinanced into higher rates, the interest coverage should decline. However, this process will take years not quarters. As we show in Exhibit 52 , the ICR for the median IG company will get to pre-Covid levels by 2027, assuming all debt in the coming years is refinanced at current coupons.

## 2) EM Exposure to external tightness

EM IG issuers can have significant dollar funding needs, arising from redemptions, current account deficits and the need to fund fiscal deficits. Filling these funding needs relies on stable access to the international capital markets including, most importantly, the USDdenominated primary market.

As a result, a tightening external environment, including higher rates and a stronger dollar, poses a risk to EM IG issuers. At worst, for EM issuers with under-developed local bond markets, an over-reliance on external financing in a tightening external environment can result in an inability to stabilise external debt levels, risking eventual downgrades.

Much like in the US, we think EM IG has sufficient buffers to absorb a higher-for-longer environment, if the market does not price in additional tightening. A pivot towards the pricing of further hikes - or in other words, a real yield-driven increase in US treasury yields - would however create a clear risk for EM IG given issuer exposure to external tightness.

## 3) Oil linkage for EM economies

EM IG issuer fundamentals are often closely linked to developments in commodity prices, in particular in the hydrocarbon markets.

Much of the AA/A-rated EM IG space is oil-exporting, resulting in a close linkage of both current account and fiscal balances to oil export prices and volumes. In the Gulf, for instance, hydrocarbon receipts constitute 40-90% of total government revenues (see EM Sovereign Credit Strategy: Taking a Closer Look at MENA Exporter Resilience, October 31, 2023). This creates both downside risks in the form of exposure to oil price shocks (such as in 2014/2015) and upside risks in periods of rising oil prices (from March 2022 onwards).

The BBB space, meanwhile, includes a selection of large commodity importers in both Central & Eastern Europe and Latin America. For these, commodity prices largely constitute a downside risk. Rising commodity prices hurt trade balances, with the resulting deficit requiring funding from inflows elsewhere. Fiscals are also impacted as EM importers tend to shield domestic populations from some of the impact via subsidies, even where fiscal space is limited.
