# Economics, Banks and Payments | Europe

# Digital Euro: Work In Progress

The digital euro could be launched in 2027, and the ECB is currently in the first preparation phase – we examine the potential impacts for monetary policy, banks, and payments companies.

## Key Takeaways

We do not expect the digital euro to have a material impact on the conduct of monetary policy, but it could lead to an enlargement of the ECB balance sheet.

On the banks side, we think the key feature to watch is the maximum amount of personal holdings in digital euro, with a €3,000 limit apparently the frontrunner.

We estimate that if everyone aged 15 or older were to convert €3,000 from deposits into digital euro, banks could lose 7% of their deposits.

Digital euro | The reasoning: The share of transactions for which cash was used as a means of payment declined from 79% to 59% between 2016 and 2022 in the Euro Area. At the same time, cryptocurrencies/stablecoins emerged to present a risk of becoming a new means of payments or store of value, as did payment providers that operate in the euro area but are headquartered outside of the European Union. These developments represent challenges to the monetary sovereignty of the ECB. As a result, one of the main goals of the digital currency is to ensure that the euro remains the monetary anchor in the euro area during the digital era. The ECB is currently in the first preparation phase until October 2025, which could eventually lead to the launch of the digital euro by 2027, at the earliest.

Economics | No immediate impact: We do not expect the digital euro to have a material impact on the conduct of monetary policy, so we focus on implications for the ECB balance sheet. The main risks to monetary policy transmission from digital euro would come from large and sudden conversions of euro area bank deposits to digital euro. Safeguards being proposed for the digital euro, in terms of a cap on personal holdings, are aimed at minimizing this risk. In addition, the ECB has already outlined that the digital euro will be a non-interest bearing instrument, which removes it from the equation of having an active role in monetary policy setting. However, the digital euro will impact the composition, and most likely also the size, of the ECB balance sheet. We present a step-by-step process as to how the digital euro may be introduced to the economy, and how that could impact the ECB balance sheet.

Depending on how the digital euro is designed, we think its (potential) introduction could impact demand for bank deposits, which are the cheapest form of funding for a bank. In this note we look at how this could affect banks' profitability. ## Economics: No Immediate Impact

Gabriela Silova

Purpose of the digital euro: One of the main stated objectives of introducing a digital euro into circulation is to provide central bank money for the digital age, while protecting the strategic autonomy of European payments and monetary sovereignty (see Lagarde). Usage of cash has declined over the years as consumers started to prefer a digitalized form of payment. In a lot of cases, this is facilitated by private as well as non-European payment providers. The digital euro could therefore help to reduce Europe's dependence on private and non-European payment providers (see here). Secondary motives are around financial inclusion and providing a cost-efficient form of payment. The important factor here is that the digital euro is not aimed at replacing banknotes but instead should be a complementary means of making payments, in digital form.

Current state of the project: The ECB has carried out a two-year investigation phase ahead of deciding in October 2023 to proceed with the first preparation phase, which will also take two years (see press release). During this phase, further testing will be conducted related to design, user experience, privacy, financial inclusion, as well as the environmental footprint of the digital euro. Further work will also be done on the rulebook (the latest update here) and a selection process for potential service providers will be defined. Based on the outcome of the above and the legislative process running in parallel, the Governing Council will decide whether the ECB will take step two of the preparation phase. This next step would work towards achieving operational readiness for possible future issuance and roll out. Still, the move to the preparation phase does not mean that the decision on issuance has been made, and the final decision will be taken by the Governing Council once the legislative process is completed. The earliest date noted for launch has been between 2026-2028, with 2027 being mentioned the most prominently (see here or Lagarde).

What do we now know about the design? The cap, access and rate...: While the design already refers to a cap as a preferred measure to control uptake and prevent excessive outflows of deposits from the banking sector, it does not specify the amount. The threshold is to be calibrated closer to the digital euro release. However, what is important is that despite the proposed limit on holdings, transactions of a higher value will be possible, both in and out of the digital euro, due to its linkage to a bank account. All euro area citizens will be able to get access to the digital euro, and in addition residents and foreign visitors with an account at a European payment service provider (PSP) should get access. An important feature that has also been previously discussed is that merchants will not be able to accumulate digital euro holdings. All of these measures will limit the total amount of digital euros that can be present in the system. In addition, the October decision made clear that the digital euro will not be interest bearing, like cash, in order to minimize its use as investment vehicle and hence to contain its use to mainly transactional purposes and a limited function as a store of value.

...and others: Another feature that has been confirmed is the ability to use the digital euro both offline, to mimic cash, and online. The form that the digital euro wallet will take has also been made more clear. Users will be able to either use their respective banking apps, the digital euro app developed by the Eurosystem or get access via a simple card, if they do not have an access to a bank account. The use case currently being explored is for individual users to be able to do peer to peer (P2P) and e-commerce payments first, with point of sale (POS) payments and government transitions likely following in this order. For more details on the features that have been proposed, please see the ECB summary report on the investigation phase, 2023.

The EUR 3,000: A figure of €3,000 has been referred to most frequently in speeches by ECB officials and related notes when talking about limits on holdings of the digital euro (for example see here, here or here). However, this has not been confirmed and is therefore to be only used as an example amount. To size the aggregate impact on the euro area banking sector, another assumption is needed. We assume that every euro area citizen above 15 years old takes up the whole amount. This leads us to a potential take-up of around €873 billion digital euros. With an individual limit of €3000 for digital euro holdings, this quantity can be seen as an upper bound for total digital euro holdings. We think the income distribution and variation in payment preferences will lead to significantly fewer digital euros in circulation. For the ease of computation, we do not take into account the potential holdings of non-euro area citizens.

## Impact deep-dive

Monetary policy implications: We do not expect the digital euro to have a material impact on the conduct of monetary policy in the short term. The earliest the digital euro could be introduced is between 2026 and 2028. Yet, even after that, we would not expect the digital euro to have a material impact, as one of the design objectives is to precisely to minimize its influence on financial stability and monetary policy (see the ECB summary report on the investigation phase, 2023). The main risks to monetary policy transmission from the digital euro would originate from large and sudden conversions of euro area bank deposits to digital euros – basically if demand for digital euros were to become too high. Large deposit outflows to digital euro could therefore in the adverse scenario lead to a potential reduction in bank lending to the real economy, and hence have a negative impact on growth. The safeguards proposed for the digital euro, in terms of a cap and the zero remuneration rate, should work to minimize this risk.

The rate discussion: The digital euro should not be thought of as a monetary policy instrument since the ECB already outlined that it will be a non-interest bearing instrument. Discussion of the concerns (e.g. increased use as a store of value) or benefits (e.g. improved transmission of rate hikes) of introducing an additional key interest rates has therefore been eliminated for now (see the ECB Report on digital euro, 2020). However, even with a non-interest bearing digital euro, there are couple of issues related to the monetary policy pass-through. First, the pass-through from policy rate to deposit rates can be softer (on the way down), as there would likely be a larger share of liquidity remunerated at zero. On the other hand, in the case of rate hikes, banks might be more motivated to pass on the rate increases faster in order to attract those deposits sitting within the digital euro. Second, non-interest bearing digital euros could force the effective lower bound closer to zero. However, both of these concerns are partially alleviated by the cap, as it limits the transformation of reserves to digital euro holdings, in the latter case in order to avoid negative interest rates. In addition, as only households will be able to hold digital euro balances, the pass-through remains intact for the other sectors of the economy.

Implications for the ECB balance sheet: Still, the introduction of the digital euro will have an impact on the ECB balance sheet, which could lead to some implications for monetary policy effectiveness and transmission. Therefore, in the rest of this chapter, we focus on this aspect of the digital euro introduction. The impact of the digital euro on monetary policy is inherently linked to the rates environment within which it is introduced, and the remuneration tied to it. While we now know that digital euro will be non-interest bearing, we do not know yet in what interest rate environment it will be introduced. Initially, given the timing of the start of the project, a negative interest rates environment was largely considered. However, less than two years later, the rates environment is very different. Our current baseline is that the digital euro will be introduced when interest rates are in the positive territory -- and close to the neutral rate of 2%. This is one of the ingredients that will determine what share of the digital euro wallet will come from banknotes or deposits.

Step 1: Impact on banknote levels: Due to the similarity of the digital euro to cash, a certain degree of substitution with banknotes can be expected. One could also argue that the digital euro would have inherent benefits over cash (i.e. safety or storage costs). Therefore, since the remuneration on both is zero, in a positive interest rate environment we could see a larger share of the digital euro wallet coming from cash due to the lack of interest rate income loss. In our current approach to forecasting the evolution of banknotes (see our methodology here), we assume stability of the environment that we currently operate in. However, if we assume that euro area households substitute 50% of banknotes they hold to digital euro, we would see a potential decline in banknotes of €461 billion. Another liability item - the digital euro - of the same size, would appear on the ECB balance sheet and hence there would be no impact on excess liquidity levels. There would also be no implication on the size of the ECB's balance sheet as it would be merely a swap between two types of liabilities (see ECB Occasional Paper, May 2022). We label this as Step 1 but the substitution happens simultaneously with Step 2.

## Arriving at banknotes substitution level

Around 57% of banknotes in circulation are held by euro area households. Applying this share to our forecast of banknotes in circulation in the last quarter of our forecast horizon (4Q25), we estimate that households will hold around €922 billion worth of banknotes.

Reference period: It is important to note here that the earliest target date mentioned is between 2026 and 2028, and even then the substitution would not be instantaneous. However, our ECB balance sheet forecast horizon runs until the end of 2025, and hence we use the latest snapshot available for illustration (4Q25) with an instantaneous effect. It is however expected that the ECB balance sheet will be a touch leaner at the time of introduction than as outlined here.

Why 50%? The ECB estimated (see ECB Economic Bulletin, 2/2021) that in 2019 around 20% of banknotes in circulation was held for the purpose of domestic transactions (€310 billion) and that an additional 30-50% was held for the store of value (€465 billion to €775 billion). Given the benefits of digital euro over cash (i.e. safety or storage costs), we assume that the substitution will happen not only from the cash used for transactions but also from that kept as a store of value. At the same time, there will be a proportion of the population that will still prefers to hold physical cash due to personal mistrust in digital forms of payment or savings (i.e. the original reason why cash is still used as a store of value).

Step 2: Impact on excess liquidity: The remainder of the substitution into digital euros would come from bank deposits. Based on our estimates of the total amount of digital euro allowed in the circulation by the population size, this could be around €412 billion. We do not expect that term deposits or deposits with agreed maturity would be impacted as the digital euro is non-interest bearing and to be used for transactions, rather than as a store of value. Hence, the outflow would primarily happen from overnight deposits. Similar to the substitution channel with banknotes, commercial banks will have to "pay" the ECB to obtain digital euro, in this case with reserves - their excess liquidity holdings. Assuming that banks have sufficient excess liquidity levels, $( \mathrm { i } . \mathsf { e } . > \mathsf { E } 4 7 2$ billion), they reduce their holdings of excess liquidity equal to the amount of digital euros they need to obtain for their customers <sup>1</sup> . The size of the ECB balance sheet does not change, only the composition. However, this time around the substitution towards digital euro exerts downwards pressure on excess liquidity in the system.

Why would lower excess liquidity matter?: As we have explored in detail in the Understanding the ECB Balance sheet: Part 3 and our 2024 European Economics Outlook, the ECB is currently undertaking the Operational framework review for steering shortterm interest rates in the era of lower excess liquidity. The Review is likely to determine whether the ECB will continue the floor system, or return to the corridor system. Therefore the decision will steer how much excess liquidity has to be present in the system. This is because the operation of the floor system, where €STR trades below the DFR, is dependent on a large amount of excess liquidity. A prudent calibration of the critical level beyond which €STR rises towards the middle of the corridor would likely feature a level of around €2.0 trillion of excess liquidity left in the system.

Step 3: The response of the ECB: Should the ECB decide to maintain the floor system, it would need to ensure that the chosen future menu of refinancing operations and asset purchases caters for digital euro demand, and does not allow drops in excess liquidity beyond the required levels and hence upward pressure on €STR. In this case, the ECB balance sheet would increase in total size, with the beta equivalent to the size of the expansion on the asset side. However, even if the ECB moves towards a corridor system, it would still need to supply the banking system with additional reserves if reserves in the system are insufficient to cover the digital euro issuance. To be clear, we do not expect this to be mentioned in the Operational framework review, which is likely to be completed at the beginning of 2024, but likely to be addressed only in the later stages of the digital euro project. However, even if the ECB decides it does not want to actively provide an adjustment on the back of the digital euro introduction (e.g. the total amount of reserves is sufficient), it can be driven by banks under the condition that fixed-rate full allotment policy procedure remains. The digital euro would therefore be an additional autonomous factor, beyond the control of the ECB and would have an impact on banks' need for reserves. Due to the heightened demand, commercial banks would draw on the regular refinancing operations to satisfy this demand, either as a funding source or to obtain the

digital euro from the ECB, leading to an expansion of the ECB balance sheet.

# Banks: Digital Euro could weaken banks' deposit franchises

Giulia Aurora Miotto, Kerry Shaw

Why a cap is critical. Financial Stability is a key concern for central banks around the world when designing CBDCs. In previous times of heightened financial stress and diminished confidence in the banking system, depositors would queue up at the bank to get their cash out. The modern version of the same phenomenon, would be to switch from bank current accounts to a CBDC wallet on a mobile phone, computer or using a card, draining the banking system of stable funding. This is why having a cap on how much users can hold in CBDC is a mission critical feature in the design of the digital euro. A cap would therefore help prevent excessive outflows of deposits from banks, preserving financial stability. Users wishing to pay more than the set limit would be able to do so by linking their digital wallet to their bank account. The amount that has been most commonly referred to is EUR3,000, and we run our analysis with this. That said, the figure is not set in stone, and some banks have been calling for this amount to be lower (for example BNP mentioned that €500 would be a more realistic cap given households' cash usage).

Which areas of a bank's balance sheet does the digital euro impact? Loans and deposits. Exhibit 8 below highlights the main moving parts: CBDC 1 and CBDC 2 are the same digital euro, but they are marked differently to denote that part of the demand comes from replacing cash, and part from replacing deposits. If households substitute bank notes with the CBDC, then the sizes of central bank and commercial bank balance sheets do not really change (although components change for the central bank). However, if households substitute commercial bank deposits with the CBDC, implying loss of deposits (as the most stable funding) for commercial banks, banks could decide to adjust their balance sheet in some of the following ways:

a) by shrinking their assets, for example reducing their holdings in central bank deposits, corporate or government bonds (as shown for example in Exhibit 8 below, which assumes that banks reduce holdings in corporate / government bonds and the central bank could (although doesn't have to) step in to replace this funding (captured in Exhibit 8 by S1 and S2);

b) by increasing remuneration on deposits or by bundling them with additional services (for example, payment services, mortgages, etc.);

c) by replacing lost deposit funding with central bank borrowing, provided that they have adequate collateral (in terms of both quality and quantity). This would imply an increase in demand for collateral, which might ultimately have an impact on market interest rates for safe assets;

d) by replacing deposit funding with other instruments - covered bonds, or, in a worse scenario, senior unsecured bonds, given they would be more expensive.

Key impact: impairment of the deposit franchise. When rates are in positive territory, as they are today, replacing current accounts with wholesale funding would increase the funding costs of banks, as deposits represent the cheapest source of funds. This could lead to higher loan rates, as banks pass the increased cost of funding through, raising a question about credit supply to the economy. This may also lead to lower multiples on banks' valuations, as the CBDC potentially limits growth in the banking system. Importantly, if banks are forced to replace a part of their funding from deposits to wholesale (or potentially central bank funding), then the value of the deposit franchise (which is a key differentiator amongst banks), i.e. the ability not to pass through a high rate on deposits, would be impaired.

Looking at a worse case scenario: LDR up 8pp (manageable)… Given that payments currently work already fairly well in the Eurozone, it is unclear what the incentive would be for households to start using the digital euro, and hence we think more likely that households would end up using only a part of the €3,000 limit, and replacing part deposits and part banknotes with digital euro. However, in order to size the potential maximum impact to banks' P&L, a scenario we think would be unlikely but possible, for example in times of financial distress, we have assumed that everyone older than 15 years in the Eurozone decides to convert €3,000 from deposits to digital euro. This could theoretically reduce euro area total deposits (defined as households' and non financial corporations' deposits) by €873bn, or 7%, and increase the loan-to-deposit ratio (LDR) from 98% to 106%. Banks in aggregate would hardly notice this change: LDR was last at 106% in 2019, and the significant surge in deposits brought about by the pandemic led it to fall below 100%.

Yet, looking at the detail by country shows that banks in smaller countries (in particular

Latvia, Lithuania, Estonia, Slovakia, Slovenia and Greece), could theoretically be impacted harder than the average. We think, however, that our bear case scenario is unlikely for these smaller countries because converting €3,000 of deposits into digital euro would be equivalent to converting 30% to 45% of total household deposits (hence including term deposits) into CBDC. Although we don't have data available, we suspect the median deposit is actually lower than €3,000 in some of these countries. If instead we assume that the maximum amount any citizen above 15 yrs would want to convert from deposits into CBDC would not be higher than 12% of their deposits (which is what €3,000 represents for the euro area in aggregate), then the impact on total bank deposits wouldn't be higher than 9% (in the case of Greece), and the change in LDR would not be as large for lower GDP per capita countries.

A digital euro could also impact NSFR and LCR. Beyond impacting loan to deposit ratio and profitability, reducing deposits would also lead to a lower NSFR (Net Stable Funding Ratio). Retail deposits are valued highly from an NSFR perspective; with retail demand deposits carrying a 95% ASF (available stable funding) factor. Hence, any loss of retail deposits as the adoption of the e-euro increases would have an adverse effect on NSFR, ceteris paribus.

The digital euro could bring higher investment costs. The ECB is keen for banks to be very involved in offering the digital euro to end customers, and banks will likely be responsible for the technical infrastructure of e-euro, user interface, customer identification (KYC and AML requirements) and customer support of the e-euro wallet. While the ECB / Eurosystem will cover its own costs as it does with banknontes, PSPs will have to cover distribution costs. # Payments - more complexity, limited disruption

Alastair Nolan

Worldline and Nexi selected by ECB for Digital Euro prototype testing: In 2022, both Worldline and Nexi were selected as two of the five companies (CaixaBank, EPI and Amazon the other three) to provide front-end prototypes to test different payment use cases. In particular, Nexi was chosen for the point-of-sale payments initiated by the payee use case. Worldline was selected to build out the P2P use case, allowing individuals to make payments using the digital euro, even in offline scenarios.

In May 2023, the ECB published a summary of its findings. The ECB's prototyping exercise ran from July 2022 to February 2023, focused on what paying with a digital euro could look like for different use cases. The exercise included the integration of five user interfaces developed by different providers for each use case (front-end prototypes) and a settlement system designed and developed by the Eurosystem (back-end prototype). The tests confirmed that it is possible to "smoothly integrate a digital euro into the existing payment landscape, while still leaving scope for the market to use innovative features and technologies when distributing a digital euro." Additionally, the tests confirmed that a digital euro could work in both online and offline environments, although there were some questions remaining as to whether an offline solution that fulfils the Eurosystem’s requirements and achieves the necessary scale can be delivered in the short to medium term with the existing technology.

We see Digital Euro and other CBDCs as adding to overall complexity within payments ecosystem...: For our merchant acquirer payments coverage, we think the digital euro will be treated as another APM (alternative payment method), just like a BNPL method or mobile wallet. Our general view has been the more APMs in existence, the more complex the payments landscape becomes for a merchant, something merchant acquirers are in the business of solving for. As an example, Adyen offers its merchants one single integration, which provides access to all of the payment methods integrated onto Adyen's single platform. This single integration results in a much more simplified back-end when it comes to settlement and reconciliation with accounting and ERP (Enterprise Resource Planning) systems, avoiding multiple direct integrations for each payment type. We think the digital euro would be treated in this case as just another APM by acquirers, which will aim to reduce this complexity for their merchants.

...with similar economics for acquirers vs. other payment methods...: Additionally, the ECB has outlined its core principles for the digital euro compensation model. In the case of acquirers and merchants, the report specifically outlines that PSP/acquirers should be able to charge merchants for digital euro acquiring services, in-line with other payment methods, with pricing to be set by market forces. ...while some questions remain around economic model for payment schemes/issuers: On the issuing side, the ECB has outlined that financial institutions distributing the digital euro and offering wallet/account management to individuals should have a similar level of economic incentives vs. other electronic payment methods, again with a focus on the importance of distribution. The digital euro is intermediated, meaning there will be "issuing banks" which provide the consumer with a hosted wallet, similar to a consumer getting a bank card but instead having a bank’s digital euro wallet.

As part of the existing card based system, issuers receive an interchange fee, which is ultimately funded by the merchant. Interchange on consumer transactions in the Eurozone is currently capped at 20bps for debit card transactions and 30bps for credit. While the fee paid to the issuing institution may not officially be referred to as interchange, it appears the ECB recognises the importance of maintaining incentives and the regulation on interchange that exists currently suggests a more limited fee pool in any case.

The picture is less clear with regard to payment schemes like Visa and Mastercard, which don't appear to be involved in an illustrative digital euro transaction. With the compensation model, the ECB outlines that the Eurosystem will "bear its own cost", reflecting the 'public good' element of the digital euro (the same logic which currently applies to cash). Payment providers will not be charged Eurosystem costs such as those "related to scheme management and settlement processing". So it appears Visa and Mastercard will not have a direct role in which they can charge traditional "scheme fees". That said, both players are likely to still have an indirect role, since they are involved in developing wallet technology and digital currency AML, but the economics are less clear in this case.

## Cross-border payments - compatibility/holding limits suggest limited risks to Wise:

Cross border payments are currently expensive and slow for many, as the banking systems in each region do not connect well with each other (as we have previously written in our deep dive on cross-border payments here). CBDCs don't necessarily solve for that as they are also currently being developed independently by each region/country. The Bank for International Settlements (BIS) has been working on many projects to improve cross border connectivity between CBDCs. The digital euro will of course enable free transfers intra Europe, but we would argue that the existing SEPA payment system already enables this. Indeed, given discussions around limiting digital euro holdings per capita, we would argue a digital euro might not be all that suitable for larger transfer amounts within the cross-border space.

Within our coverage, we think there will be limited impact on Wise in terms of the introduction of the digital euro. As with other proposed CBDC initiatives, we expect freedom for cross-border players to innovate and support the adoption of the digital currencies. Given the high threshold to achieve universal alignment of cross border policies, participation requirements and regulatory frameworks, third party players will still be relevant for facilitating these payments.
