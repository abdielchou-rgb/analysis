# European Economic Perspectives SNB: FX interventions and the balance sheet

## Summary: What you need to know about SNB balance sheet data

We heavily rely on SNB balance sheet data to inform our monetary policy view. First and foremost, we use the data for estimating the SNB's foreign exchange interventions at a monthly frequency - but the balance sheet is also a useful resource for broader monetary developments beyond that. In this note, we review our various estimation approaches for FX interventions, expanding on an article we recently published in our Switzerland quarterly. This includes our take on today's provisional FX reserves data. In addition, we discuss what we learn from the balance sheet about the SNB's liquidity management as well as the SNB's portfolio performance and YTD profits.

## 1) FX intervention estimates and today's FX reserves data (p. 2)

Despite the CHF appreciation between mid-December and end-January, we see little signs for FX purchases in December and January balance sheet data. Our most reliable monthly estimate indicates around CHF 1bn of FX purchases in both months but the magnitude is small compared to past intervention activity and could merely reflect estimation inaccuracy. With the CHF depreciating back to late-November levels in February, we do not expect to see signs for FX purchases in February data either. Provisional FX reserves data published today give a first confirmation of this notion. The CHF 15bn increase to CHF 678bn can be largely attributed to currency valuation adjustments. Please scroll down to the Appendix for a detailed explanation of our different estimation approaches.

While we do not expect a fully fledged reversal to aggressive balance sheet expansion, we would not exclude moderate FX purchases alongside rate cuts in 2024. This implies the balance sheet reduction likely came to an end in late-2023, after it had contracted by 20% over the past 1.5 years to a level last seen in 2017. Details on page 2.

## 2) Liquidity management (p. 3)

As a result of the SNB's FX sales and liquidity-absorbing operations, excess liquidity in the banking system has fallen from its peak of CHF 649bn in April 2022 to CHF 444bn in December 2023. A further reduction of excess liquidity is no longer necessary for the effectiveness of monetary policy implementation, and the volumes of the SNB's openmarket operations over the last months appear to merely roll over maturing operations. We expect this to continue, provided the SNB does not have to sterilize FX purchases. Details on page 3

## 3) FX portfolio returns and YTD profits (p. 4)

We see signs that the SNB is trying to optimize the performance of its FX portfolio. First, the SNB used its stock of highly liquid FX deposits for its FX sales, reducing the amount of securities that it had to liquidate and thus also lowering the realized losses of the FX bond and equity portfolios. Second, we notice a shift to lower-rated and thus higheryielding bonds. Third, the share of US equities in the equity portfolio has steadily increased over the past years, indicating that the SNB does not rebalance its equity holdings to maintain specific country weights but decided to hold on to highperforming US equities at the expense of EUR equities.

Thanks to the SNB's mark-to-market accounting, balance sheet data is also an early indication for the central bank's profits and losses. YTD developments have been beneficial for the SNB. We estimate the balance sheet profit YTD would amount to almost CHF 30bn if the SNB conducted a full valuation of its balance sheet items at the end of February. Details on page 4.

## Last FX sales in November …

(1) FX intervention estimates

Since official intervention data is published only at quarterly frequency and with a threemonth lag, we rely on monthly balance sheet data to estimate the SNB's FX interventions (see Appendix for details). The latest data provide estimates up to January 2024, and official intervention releases are available up to Q3 2023. We know that the SNB sold around CHF 160bn of foreign currency between June 2022 and November 2023 (Figure 1), thereby strengthening the CHF and reducing imported inflation. From December onwards, it has shifted from a focus on selling FX to a neutral stance as it judged monetary conditions (i.e. the interest rate and exchange rate level) to be appropriate. Accordingly, the SNB stopped selling FX in December. Going forward, we would expect FX interventions to become less one-sided than in the past 1.5 years and would not exclude that the SNB also purchases FX, at least temporarily, should it be concerned about CHF strength. This is also important for the policy rate outlook: We would expect that the more the SNB uses FX interventions to set monetary conditions, the less it has to do with policy rates.

## … and likely no substantial FX purchases since

The 2-3% appreciation of the CHF between mid-December 2023 and end-January 2024 (in real and trade-weighted terms) has thus opened up the question whether the SNB has moved towards purchasing FX again. However, we see little signs for that in December and January balance sheet data. Our most reliable monthly estimate (based on adjusted sight deposits) indicates FX purchases of c. CHF 1bn in December and January (Figure 2). However, the magnitude is small compared to past intervention activity and could merely reflect estimation inaccuracy. With the CHF depreciating back to late-November levels in February, we do not expect to see signs for FX purchases in February data either (published on 28 March).

## Today's FX reserves data do not signal interventions in February

In fact, provisional FX reserves data published today gave a first confirmation of this notion. The published FX reserves number for February increased by CHF 15.4bn to CHF 678bn, but we cannot interpret this increase as FX purchase because it also contains valuation adjustments for exchange rate developments (see Appendix for details). We therefore have to subtract the valuation effect from the increase to get an actual FX intervention estimate. Using our portfolio return model (see Technical Notes for details), we calculate that the valuation adjustment should amount to CHF 15.7bn. Accordingly, the increase in the provisional FX reserves can be entirely attributed to currency valuation effects. We therefore tentatively conclude the SNB did neither buy nor sell foreign currency in February and look for confirmation in the monthly balance sheet data published on 28 March. <sup>(1)</sup> Official FX intervention data for Q1 will be released by end-June.

## Further balance sheet reduction unlikely

As a result of the CHF 158bn FX sales throughout the 2022/23 tightening cycle, the size of the SNB's balance sheet has contracted by 20% to a level last seen in 2017 (Figure 3). It now stands at 100% of GDP, compared to c.50% for the ECB, 28% for the Fed and 127% for the BoJ. Absent any unexpected depreciation pressure on the CHF, we do not expect the SNB to sell material amounts of FX throughout this year. Given that the asset side of the balance sheet predominantly consists of FX reserves, this implies that the balance sheet is unlikely to shrink further from here. If anything, we consider it more likely that developments will turn once again in the other direction. While we do not expect a fully fledged reversal to aggressive balance sheet expansion, we would not exclude moderate and temporary FX purchases to counter sharp CHF appreciation alongside rate cuts in 2024.

Another notable balance sheet development over the past 1.5 years have been the SNB's efforts to absorb excess liquidity from money markets. The motivation to do so resulted from the adjustment of monetary policy implementation to positive interest rates in an excess liquidity environment.

## Signs for optimizing portfolio returns

Aside from last year's decision to no longer remunerate minimum reserves, we see a number of other indications that the SNB is trying to minimize losses at the margin. These are mainly efforts to optimize the performance of its FX portfolio - within the narrow scope that the prioritization of monetary policy objectives allows.

## (3) FX portfolio returns

First, the SNB has more or less depleted its stock of FX deposits in the past 1.5 years (Figure 8 . These deposits are the result of previous FX purchases that have not been invested into FX securities but are held in deposit accounts at other central banks or the BIS, plus proceeds from liquidated or matured FX securities, plus inflows from interest and coupon payments. When the SNB started selling foreign currency to fight inflation in June 2022, it was able to sell these highly liquid FX deposits first before it had to realize any losses on its FX bond or equity portfolio.

Second, we notice a shift to lower-rated and thus higher-yielding bonds: The share of AAA-rated bonds in the FX portfolio has dropped from 62% in 2022 Q3 to 32% in 2023 Q4, whereas the share of AA-rated bonds has increased by the same magnitude (Figure 11). We doubt that this merely reflects rating downgrades as the SNB is unlikely to have a sufficiently large exposure to individual issuers that would justify this drop. Instead, it is more likely that when the SNB sold FX assets in the past 1.5 years, it sold more AAArated than AA-rated bonds, resulting in a higher weight of the latter.

Third, we see a similar development within the equity portfolio with respect to US equities. Using the SNB's 13F filing with the SEC, we can derive the weight of US equities in the portfolio and see that it has steadily increased over the years from 35% in 2013 to 80% now (Figure 7 . The SNB did thus not rebalance its equity holdings to maintain specific country weights but decided to hold on to high-performing US equities at the expense of EUR equities. Accordingly, when the SNB started divesting equities for its FX sales, it disproportionately did so with other equities while only selling a small share of its US equity portfolio.

## FX portfolio has generated a profit YTD

These efforts might have helped to optimize the SNB's portfolio returns and will likely continue to do so, but they will only have a marginal impact on the SNB's annual profit/ loss. Given the size of the FX portfolio and monetary policy considerations in the currency allocation, the annual result is dominated by the developments on global bond and equity markets and the EURCHF resp. USDCHF exchange rate moves.

In that regard, YTD developments have been beneficial for the SNB. Up until the end of February, the equity rally (+7% YTD in local currency for the approximated SNB equity portfolio) and CHF depreciation should more than compensate for the losses from bonds (-0.8% in local currency for the approximated SNB bond portfolio), gold and interest expenditure. Using our portfolio return model, we estimate the balance sheet profit YTD would amount to almost CHF 30bn if the SNB conducted a full valuation of its balance sheet items at the end of February. Yet to allow for a profit distribution to cantons and the Federal government, we calculate this profit would have to grow by at least another CHF 37bn until year-end, while adverse market developments could also easily erase it.

## Appendix: How we track SNB FX interventions

FX interventions to achieve the inflation target have played an important role in the SNB's policy for a long time. After abandoning the minimum EURCHF exchange rate in 2015, the SNB purchased FX in large amounts as it deemed inflation too low and the Swiss franc "highly valued". When policy rates began to rise in 2022, the focus turned to selling FX reserves to strengthen the Swiss franc and help lower inflation. In December 2023, the SNB moved to a more neutral position with regard to FX interventions, stressing that it remained willing to buy or sell FX, if needed. Tracking the SNB's intervention activity has thus become even more important. A key question is to what extent the SNB will rely on interventions alongside rate cuts to ease monetary conditions going forward.

While we do not expect a fully fledged reversal to aggressive balance sheet expansion, we would not exclude moderate and temporary interventions to counter sharp and sudden appreciation. We therefore monitor the data closely for any signs of interventions. Since the SNB publishes its actual intervention data only at quarterly frequency (and with a three-month lag), SNB watchers have to rely on alternative sources to gauge the extent of FX interventions in (more) real time. We largely rely on monthly balance sheet data but also take movements in more timely data into account.

The importance of FX interventions for SNB policy

We approximate FX interventions using monthly balance sheet data, which is usually released with a one-month lag. We use two approaches, looking both at the asset and the liability side of the SNB balance sheet.

1. Adjusting changes in FX reserves: Given that FX interventions involve purchasing or selling FX assets, the monthly change in FX reserves should be a good proxy. However, the SNB uses mark-to-market valuation of its balance sheet, and hence the monthly changes in FX reserves may also reflect currency fluctuations or changes in asset prices. To adjust for these valuation effects, we subtract from the change in FX reserves and the change in gold reserves the change in "provisions and equity capital", which captures the valuation effects of FX reserves and gold on the liability side of the balance sheet. Note that this adjustment does not account for dividend and interest payments (which are also included in the provisions and equity capital position), and hence tends to overestimate the amount of FX purchases.

2. Adjusting changes in sight deposits: FX interventions result in a change in sight deposits that banks hold with the SNB. For example, if the SNB purchases (sells) FX assets, it credits (debits) the amounts in CHF to the banks' sight deposit accounts. A change in sight deposit amounts is hence one indication of intervention activity. However, the level of sight deposits is also affected by several other factors so that we make the following adjustments to the monthly change in sight deposits: (a) sight deposits could be exchanged for cash, hence we add the monthly change in banknotes; (b) we substract the change in secured loans (asset side) that the SNB grants to banks through a credit on their sight deposit accounts − these were important during the Covid crisis when the SNB offered the so-called Covid loans via the newly created SNB Covid-19 refinancing facility, but it also played a role in Emergency Liquidity Assistance (ELA) during times of banking stress; they still influence the balance sheet today as the secured loans are gradually paid back; (c) we account for open market operations by substracting the change in claims from CHF repos and adding the change in liabilities from CHF repos (reverse repos) and the change in SNB bills. For March/April 2023, we also added the change in FX liabilities to try to account for emergency lending during the period of banking stress.

These two approaches track the actual FX interventions reasonably accurately. Since Q1 20 (the first date when SNB official intervention data was made available), the correlation coefficient amounts to 0.89 for the approach using adjusted sight deposits, and 0.82 for the approach using adjusted FX reserves. Over this period, both approaches coincided with the direction of actual FX interventions in almost each quarter.

One drawback of our estimation approach is that − while the data is still more timely than the actual intervention data − it is nevertheless released with a one-month lag. In the following, we hence look at more timely intra-month data: provisional FX reserves in IMF SDDS Plus data and weekly sight deposits. In theory, both indicate FX interventions

Weekly sight deposits: The release (published Mondays for the previous week) contains the change in domestic and foreign banks' sight deposits at the SNB, which may give some indication of intervention activity, but the data release does not include the other balance sheet items (banknotes, secured loans, open market operations) that are required to single out the impact of FX interventions on sight deposits. Up until the pandemic, weekly sight deposit data was a relatively reliable approximation of FX interventions, but since secured loans and open market operations have gained importance, the correlation with actual interventions has broken down (see Figure 14). We do not have sufficient real-time data on the volumes of secured loan repayments and open market operations to approximate these components, and can therefore not rely on the weekly sight deposit data alone for estimating FX interventions. This is likely to be a particularly important limitation going forward, given that the SNB would likely sterilise its FX interventions through open market operations to maintain money market conditions. This means it has to absorb the liquidity that it has created with its purchases, otherwise SARON may deviate too much to the downside from the policy rate. Hence, the created liquidity would show up in increased SNB bills or repo volumes rather than sight deposits.

as well as interest and dividend payments in order to be able to estimate the extent or direction of FX interventions. This requires some modelling of the SNB's portfolio returns, which can be a rough approximation at best, given that the SNB provides only a high-level asset class and currency breakdown of its reserve assets (see Figure 9 to Figure 13). While our calculations allow us to approximate the valuation effects to a reasonable degree (Figure 14), the approach faces an additional hurdle: The provisional FX reserve data is published according to IMF standard and hence in a different accounting metric than the SNB balance sheet. This results in a level difference between both series (Figure 15), which in our understanding reflects that the provisional FX reserves exclude (respectively, include) some securities that the SNB is using (respectively, receiving) as collateral in repos. These are unlikely to be constant over time, and we do not have timely information about the amounts. This adds a further layer of volatility to the change in the provisional FX reserves number and can cause estimation errors in the magnitude of CHF4bn, on average (and as much as CHF20bn in some occurrences in the past), which might change the direction of the signal. The provisional FX reserve data is hence not a reliable indicator for FX intervention estimates, but we can nevertheless draw some conclusions from it. Importantly, the quarterly sum of our approximations (which is available three weeks earlier than balance sheet data) tracks the official intervention data very well − the correlation coefficient of 0.92 is the highest across all approaches. This likely reflects the fact that we approximate the valuation effects with total return indices, which include dividend and interest payments (see "Technical notes" box for details).

## Technical notes

To approximate the changes in the provisional FX reserves stemming from valuation adjustments, we try to recreate the SNB's FX portfolio based on as much available data as possible.

For local-currency equity returns, we use the respective national total return (i.e. including dividend payments) stock market indices but exclude banks, in line with the SNB's investment principles.

For local-currency bond indices, we use total return (i.e. including coupon payments) bond indices that roughly match the duration, rating and issuer profile of the SNB's bond portfolio.

We calculate the US equity share from the SNB's 13F filing with the SEC and derive the other country weights from the currency weighting published by the SNB. According to this methodology, the share of US equities in the total equity portfolio has increased from 35% in 2013 to 80% in 2023, likely due to the strong performance of US tech stocks over the period.

We update asset returns only at the end of each quarter, whereas we incorporate gold and FX valuation effects on a monthly basis, in line with our understanding of the SNB's accounting practice (see footnotes here and here).

The calculated returns match the annual returns published by the SNB reasonably well and could also be used to forecast the SNB's annual profit, for example. However, the accuracy is lower on a monthly basis, and the deviations are large enough to introduce significant variance to the monthly FX intervention estimate.
