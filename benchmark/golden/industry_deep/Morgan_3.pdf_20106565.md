## United States

• January CPI was a brusque reminder that the path back to 2% inflation will have bumps on the way

The trend in retail sales growth looks to be moderating after a strong 2023

• Disappointing prints on January manufacturing output and housing starts

• Fed minutes next week could shed light on QT plans

The story of 2023 was one of downside surprises on inflation and upside surprises on growth. This week<sup>’</sup>s news about the start of 2024 flipped that around: the January CPI came in hot while retail sales stumbled out of the gate. A broad-based firming in prices supported a 0.4% increase in the ex-food and energy core CPI, the biggest gain since last April. Additional data from the PPI suggest that the Fed<sup>’</sup>s preferred core PCE metric will also show a firm increase. Meanwhile retail sales fell 0.8% last month, indicating that real consumer spending likely modestly declined last month.

For both inflation and consumer spending, one swallow does not a summer make (particularly with January data). The ugly inflation number came after a reminder that broader inflationary psychology remains in check, as the NY Fed<sup>’</sup>s 3-year ahead inflation expectations measure released this week dropped to a low for the series that began in 2013 (February moves in the University of Michigan survey inflation expectations were pretty minor). Meanwhile, the still-low level of jobless claims reported this week is an indicator that the most important support for consumer spending—labor income—is likely continuing to grow.

The tone from Fed speakers this week continued to echo speeches from last week indicating an intent to eventually ease, but also not rush into the first rate cut. So far, the January data do not appear to be having a big impact on their rhetoric. We continue to look for the first cut of the cycle in June. While this week<sup>’</sup>s CPI report has clearly hawkish implications, it<sup>’</sup>s worth recalling that there are effectively three more CPI reports before the June meeting, which is plenty of time for the disinflation to get back on track. Perhaps more importantly, there are four more employment reports between now and then, which is plenty of time for boomy job growth to get derailed.

Fed chair Powell already has indicated that there was some discussion about plans for the balance sheet at the January meeting. The minutes to that meeting (released on Wednesday) could give us more insight into these plans, and we continue to expect a more formal announcement to come following the March meeting. Apart from the minutes, we don<sup>’</sup>t

expect major news coming from the economic reports in the upcoming holiday-shortened workweek.

## The undead CPI report

The January CPI data were firmer than expectations, with the headline up 0.3% during the month and the core index rising 0.4% (0.392% to three decimals). The broad trend in headline inflation continued to moderate, with the related year-ago rate cooling from 3.4% to 3.1%oya between December and January. The year-ago rate on the core also continued to trend lower into January, but it is hard to feel confident that the broad trend for core inflation is moderating when the January increase was the largest monthly gain since April 2023 and the related three-month run rate picked up to its highest level since June 2023, hitting 4.0% saar in January (Figure 1). While the January strength in the core CPI marked a change from what had been a softer trend over the prior few months, that month on its own may not be indicative of the new underlying trend.

Figure 1: Core CPI run rates
![](images/5637880e58b454996ba15d7dfc8bd1a39e92b026ff09ac0e16bfbebb52ad47fa.jpg)

There were only a few major categories of the CPI with weak readings in January. Energy prices fell 0.9% in January, continuing their recent downward trend. Apparel prices also continued their recent soft run, declining 0.7% in January. Used vehicle prices, meanwhile, dropped 3.4% that month, mark ing their largest monthly decline since 1969. This plunge in used vehicle prices kept the broad core goods aggregate trending lower in January (-0.3%), but away from used vehicles, core goods prices ticked up 0.1% in January, marking their largest monthly increase since early 2023. The food price index in the CPI, meanwhile, rose 0.4% in January, which was the largest increase since January 2023.

And the core services price index jumped 0.7% in January, posting the largest monthly increase since September 2022 and showing strong readings throughout many of the related subcategories. The supercore index (core services ex. rent measures) jumped 0.8% in January, marking one of its largest monthly increases in recent decades. And owners<sup>’</sup> equivalent rent also looked firm, rising 0.6% (although the tenants<sup>’</sup> rent

measure looked much softer).

We also saw firmness in the other major January inflation reports, with upside surprises in the PPI and import price data for the month. Factoring in the related relevant data in hand, we look for strong January increases in the PCE price data. We estimate that the headline PCE price index rose 0.4% in January (2.4%oya) while the core PCE deflator increased 0.47% (2.9%oya). As mentioned above, the strength in inflation in January does not appear to have meaningfully pushed consumer inflation expectations higher yet.

## A slow start for retail spending

The January retail sales data were weak, and there were also downward revisions to some earlier relevant figures. Overall, the news was a significant disappointment relative to the consensus expectation and a more modest downside surprise relative to our own forecast. Before the report, we had argued that the seasonal factors would be “harsh<sup>”</sup> for this January and that these would generate weakness in the seasonally adjusted figures for the month and also that we should look across some of the recent volatility in the data (December was strong) to get a better sense of the underlying trend.

Total retail sales dropped 0.8% in January (Figure 2), and sales of the important control group declined 0.4%. There were also downward revisions for the control group for earlier months, although December still looked solid, with a 0.6% gain that month (down from 0.8% previously reported). With the available data in hand, we think that real consumer spending declined about 0.2% in January (a weather-related surge in demand for heating consumption prevented a weaker outcome). Through some of the monthly ups and downs, it appears that the trend in consumer spending is moderating, and we think that real spending is on track for an increase of around 2% saar in 1Q.

## Other activity measures weak as well

There was some other disappointing news for January this week with respect to manufacturing production and housing starts and permits. The harsh winter weather may have been at least partly to blame here, with the Fed<sup>’</sup>s IP press release specifically noting weather as a drag on manufacturing (and mining) output in January and housing construction indicators often influenced by weather as well. Manufacturing output dropped 0.5% in January, and while we might want to discount the signal from this decline on its own, the broader trend has looked weak lately (particularly when excluding motor vehicles and parts for which production has been influenced by strike-related issues in recent months).

Meanwhile, housing starts fell 14.8% to 1.331mn saar in January and permits declined 1.5% to 1.470mn saar. The relatively good news in the report was that single-family permits—an important leading indicator—continued to trend higher, rising 1.6% in January. But most of the other major details were weak, including a 4.7% drop in single-family starts and even large declines in the multifamily data (starts: -35.6%, permits: -7.9%). The multifamily data are often very noisy from month to month, but the broad trends for these measures have been weakening lately while the single-family data generally have been trending higher (Figure 3).

But some other recent reports give us confidence that the weak January prints discussed above are not indicative of a clear downward break for the economy. Monthly indicators can often be noisy and the special issues associated with seasonal factors and winter weather may make the recent figures particularly distorted relative to the underlying trends. Moreover, jobless claims filings have continued to come in at low levels into February, suggesting that the labor market has remained strong lately. The few business surveys that have released February data so far (covering manufacturing, services, and housing specifically) also point to improvement that month relative to January. While the link between these surveys and the “hard<sup>”</sup> economic data is not particularly strong, the firming in the surveys does not point to intensifying weakness.
