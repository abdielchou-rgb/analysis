## All about France

1. Will a new French government be inflationary? There is a tug-of-war between risk sentiment and inflation analysis. Risk sentiment has dominated so far. We think consensus is that either a Rassemblement National (RN) or new Front Populaire (NFP) government would raise inflation expectations. We agree but are less concerned about fiscal expansion as part of that. Our analysis reinforces our existing view to be long EURi. We may change our mind on evidence of market turmoil broadening substantially.

2. France, risk and correlations. Breakevens are following rates more closely in Europe, but the return of risk-on/risk-off means that the correlation with stocks and energy is less clear. The standout in the cross-asset, cross-currency view is GBPi, however, which has underperformed sharply. That has been our recommendation and we run it into RPI this week.

3. French supply this week. The real test will be the €8bn-€10.5bn medium nominal OATs (alongside Spain) on Thursday. OATi/€i are an afterthought. Higher real rates and long-term end users might be better stories. French iotas have been richening.

4. May CPI round-up. UBS is in line for CPI and RPI week. May was good news for US CPI - can it be carried into H2? European inflation details confirm broad services inflation.

## 1. Will a new French government be inflationary?

Inflation vs risk

The French elections are primarily about risk. What really matters is (i) governability, (ii) policy, and (iii) momentum towards the next presidential election. But the election is also directly (by policy) and indirectly (through risk sentiment) about inflation.

## Governability is probably not yet a key concern

In a hung parliament scenario with a government from a relative majority, the key question would be the ability to win votes of confidence. Assuming that Ensemble (Renaissance-LREM and others) members would at least abstain to preserve a prime minister, a total blockage of parliament seems unlikely.

## Policies look more inflationary

Policy is still being set. From our conversations, we believe the assumption is that both an RN or an NFP government would be more inflationary. That may be correct. The NFP supports wage indexation and pensions to be indexed on wages, retirement at 60, and a 14% rise in the minimum wage. The RN's policies are less clear, but it also seeks incentives for employers to raise pay and markets might assume that its antiimmigration stance might be more inflationary over the longer term. Against that, both parties want energy measures that would be more disinflationary in the short term.

## Fiscal policy is less likely to be more inflationary, however

Markets also seem to be concerned about the possibility of expansionary fiscal policy.
That may be overdone.

Budgets would likely need to be passed using the 49.3 provision, which makes the bill subject to a no-confidence motion. In that case, the parliamentary mathematics would likely allow the President to block the budget by having his party support the noconfidence vote. That would need to be used cautiously to avoid a succession of new elections, but it could be a meaningful guard against a budget that disregarded Commission recommendations, for example.

More importantly, though, market discipline matters. A new finance minister may find (or just fear) that could come sooner than it might have done for Bruno Le Maire. In the case of the RN, particularly, showing fiscal responsibility may also be a key priority as they look to sustain a transition to the mainstream. The UK 49-day Truss government may be a useful cautionary example of what can happen when governments try to do too much too quickly.

## Political momentum and the 2027 Presidential elections

Consolidating momentum towards a presidential run for Le Pen in 2027 is clearly likely to be the key goal for the RN. The NFP's ambitions and durability beyond the legislative elections are less clear.

With the RN leading in the polls, this last point is probably the key one for markets with regard to risk appetite. There is a case, of course, that governing is hard and a spell in government would not favour Le Pen's 2027 campaign. But the counterargument is at least as persuasive: that a prime minister has a very prominent pulpit and that a time in government would be a big step towards normalisation and taking over the right-ofcentre political ground.

Is this a Meloni-style transition? Perhaps, but the differences are also well telegraphed. In addition to questions about market-friendly reforms at home (for example on unemployment insurance), markets might assume that European-level reforms such as capital markets and banking union, and any further debt mutualisation will be pushed further beyond a tradeable horizon.

## Cautious risk, but inflation risks on the upside

Overall, at the moment this is a cautious analysis on risk. The paths to sustained improvement in risk sentiment seem to be (i) polls and second-round simulations supporting no majority and slowing momentum for the RN, (ii) the centre maintaining or growing its share in polls, (iii) comfort with the RN's longer-term transition to the mainstream, or simply market willingness to defer judgement on the matter. When it comes down to sentiment like this, the right strategy is to follow the trend, but for the last of these points the key hygiene factor might be budget discipline, which we think markets might be ready to assume.

On inflation, we see the risks on the upside. The NFP's announced policies are clearer, but we put low probability on the NFP emerging as the largest party. For inflation-linked markets, we think the RN's instincts on labour protection, migration and explicit support for higher wages are likely to dominate optimism on energy price protections.

The French elections strengthen rather than detract from our conviction to be long Euro breakevens. Figure 18 shows breakevens for Euro Area linkers forward-valued from September.

## 2. France, risk and correlations

In recent quarters, inflation sentiment (inflation-on/inflation-off) has dominated risk-on/ risk-off. This is the kind of event that puts risk sentiment back in the driving seat. How is it changing correlations?

## Risk-inflation correlations turned for now, but the EUR inflation beta has been firming.

Six working days isn't enough to update a long-term correlation assessment, but the reintroduction of risk sentiment as a driver of markets in Europe has changed correlations as expected so far: what has been bad for stocks and risk assets has been good for rates and bad for breakevens. Within this, the key relationship for breakevens has been the nominal beta, which has been strengthening in recent weeks. Especially when allowing for the rally in oil, we would have expected a better performance from breakevens.

Trade idea update: Take off short French vs EUR inflation. French inflation has underperformed slightly. We had thought that long EURi against French inflation was more likely to be a consensus trade that might come under pressure, but it has not been volatile. Nonetheless, we close our recommendation to be long EURi in this spread trade now since part of our assessment above is that French inflation risks may be on the upside. We also see a small chance that a new finance minister may restore the inflation linkage in the Livret-A coupon early. We entered the recommendation in January.

## Cross-currency, the UK cheapening is the week's key story. Go with it.

The stand-out in the cross-currency, cross-asset picture (Figure 8) is the UK however. In another week, that would have been the main story. The weak labour market data this month, RPI y/y dropping below breakevens, and possible corporate supply from the government renewable energy CFD auctions this summer have been our reasoning. These are all well known by now, but we see no reason to fade. Short RPI is an existing theme and recommendation for us. This week we run it into the inflation data.

## 3. French supply this week

## Nominal auctions this week are a key test, not linkers

French supply on Thursday this week will be a real test given the volatility and political backdrop. There are €8bn-€10.5bn (medium-term: 3y, 5y, 6y and 8y) and €1.5-€2.25bn linkers. The linkers are longer: €i27s, €i36s, i36s and €i53s.

In French bonds overall, the foreign sector is very important (Figure 4) and may be more defensive. Longer-term linker end users might be a relatively good story and concession in real yield is a small support for the supply: OATei53s are above 1% for the first time this year. The only issue with any relative value worth mentioning is the French CPI 2036, which looks cheap on the curve.
