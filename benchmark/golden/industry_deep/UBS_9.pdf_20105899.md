## Reborn from the ashes stronger in the aftermath of Double Reduction

Since we initiated coverage on Chinese education stocks under the same title as this report in 2017, the sector has been on a rollercoaster ride. After the Double Reduction regulation led to a precipitous sell-off in the sector in 2021, EDU and TAL have delivered a remarkable recovery, forging new markets in non-academic after-school tutoring (AST). Amid concerns the sector is now a 'crowded traded' and future growth priced in, we carry out a deep dive into sector fundamentals and investor positioning, leveraging data from UBS Evidence Lab and UBS Quant Research, as well as evidence gathered from our meetings with 141 buy-side institutions in 2023.

## TAM is smaller, but unit economics have improved

We estimate there is still a substantial Rmb302bn TAM for K12 AST, albeit only a third of our pre-Double Reduction estimate of Rmb1,025bn due to a smaller addressable population, partly offset by higher ASPs. We expect the steady-state learning centre level OPM to improve 6ppts to 22% in 2024E vs 2021 due to: 1) operating leverage from a higher ASP; 2) lower unit rental costs; and 3) less competition. In OP terms, we estimate TAM at Rmb67bn vs EDU/TAL's FY24E adjusted OP of Rmb4.2bn/-49m.

## Our marketing feedback and quant data: EDU not as well-owned as it appears

Our meetings with investors in 2023 suggest hedge funds were generally early to enter the education sector, while long-only interest picked up from mid-year. Long-only funds' positioning in the sector still appears to lag their growing interest. UBS Quant Research data suggests EDU's weight in long-only funds is still at the low end of that for major internet and education stocks. Thus, we expect incremental inflows from long-only funds into the sector, particularly EDU. We maintain our Buy ratings on EDU and TAL.

## Q: How 'crowded' is the education sector in terms of investor positioning?

We believe education stocks are not as much of a 'crowded trade' as they might appear to be at first glance, despite the 110/79% share price gains for EDU/TAL in 2023 and elevated investor interest of late. While UBS Quant Research data shows EDU may have become crowded with hedge funds, longonly funds' positioning in the sector, and EDU in particular, is still not high compared with that for other internet stocks. Based on UBS Quant Research and our analysis, we estimate EDU had only a 0.20% weight in long-only funds vs 0.63% weight in MSCI China and a significant 4.2% overweight among hedge funds. We expect EDU's solid fundamentals and reasonable PE valuation will attract more long-only positioning from here.

## Pivotal Questions

## Q: How big is the TAM for K12 afterschool tutoring post double reduction?

UBS VIEW

In OP terms, we estimate the TAM for K12 after-school tutoring (AST) in China to amount to Rmb67bn, driven mainly by new markets in non-academic AST for elementary school students in Tier-1 and -2 cities. Although this OP pool is only 40% of our pre-Double Reduction estimate of Rmb168bn, it still represents a substantial growth opportunity for industry leaders EDU/TAL, against our Rmb4.2bn/-49m OP forecasts for FY24E. The OP opportunity is driven by: 1) the still-large TAM and market under-penetration; we estimate the TAM for K12 AST at Rmb302bn in 2024E, 12x the aggregated revenue of EDU (K12 AST) and TAL; 2) more benign competition; 3) stable regulatory environment for non-academic AST; and 4) better unit economics.

## EVIDENCE

1) We estimate the total addressable market (TAM) for K12 AST is Rmb302bn in 2024, with elementary school non-academic AST contributing to 67% of this TAM estimate. With Chinese families’ still healthy demand for education and the supplyside shortage post Double Reduction regulation, we view much of this TAM as untapped. Although post-Double Reduction TAM is only 30% of the estimated Rmb1,025bn K12 AST market in 2021, this still represents a substantial market opportunity that EDU and TAL can mine for growth. With EDU and TAL’s K12- related revenue in FY24E makes up only 5% and 3%, respectively, of our TAM estimate, we see ample room for growth in both companies.

2) In pre-Double Reduction era, sector competition was intense. Market leaders such as EDU/TAL faced competition from 1) regional players seeking to grow into national presence and 2) online education funded by organisations with deep pockets, including mega cap internet companies and global private equity / venture capital firms. Post Double Reduction, these two key groups of competitors largely exited K12 AST, leaving behind a more benign sector for the survivors, EDU and TAL.

3) We believe the regulatory framework for non-academic AST is already established, and expect regulatory environment to be stable going forward. While academic AST should remain prohibited for listcos, new licenses for non-academic AST appear relatively easily accessible for compliant education companies, such as EDU and TAL, which should enable them to tap into the healthy underlying demand/supply dynamics.

## WHAT´S PRICED IN?

EDU and TAL are still trading at discounts to pre-Double Reduction levels, depsite their fast-recovering fundamentals. With a smaller but still sizable TAM and better unit economics, we believe EDU and TAL have ample long-term growth potential that the market has yet to fully price in, in our view.

We view the loss of TAM in lower tier cities as mainly because Double Reduction enforcement tends to be less restrict in lower tier cities and much of the tutoring demand there has shifted towards academic AST taught by SMEs and individual teachers, therefore is no longer an addressable market there for compliant companies.

## Valuation Method and Risk Statement

We believe the key risks to China's education sector include: 1) risks from changes in education policies; 2) changes in parent/student studying preferences; 3) rising competition from new entrants; 4) changes in macro economic conditions which could impact consumers' spending power on education; 5) potential decline in overall student population in China; 6) negative impact from pandemic; 7) potential technogical disruptions (incl. AI) to existing education business models.

We derive our price target for New Oriental Education from three-stage DCF. We believe downside risks include: 1) weaker-than-expected demand for overseas test preparation courses due to travel restriction amid COVID, geopolitical risks or tightened domestic regulation on studying abroad; 2) weaker-than-expected acceptance of its non-English courses; 3) weak enrolment growth resulting in slower top-line growth and weaker learning centre utilisation than expected; 4) policy risk (eg, government initiatives to discourage highschool afterschool tutoring services); and 5) rising competition.

We base our price target for TAL on DCF methodology. We believe the downside risks for TAL include: 1) the inability to maintain consistent teaching quality during rapid expansion; 2) weaker enrolment growth, resulting in slower top-line growth and weaker learning centre utilisation; 3) policy risk (eg, government initiatives to discourage K-12 after-school tutoring services); 4) weaker-than-expected student and parental acceptance of its technological initiatives and/or dual-teacher model; and 5) competitive risks. The upside risks include: 1) faster transition progress; 2) higher demand for non-academic AST; and 3) a better-thanexpected unit economy model and cost control.
