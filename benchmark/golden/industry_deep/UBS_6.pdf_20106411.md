# Commodities Copper 101

## Copper 101

Often referred to as "Dr Copper" due to its role as an indicator for global economic health, copper and its alloys possess a variety of properties that make them some of the most versatile materials available. It is a highly ductile semi-precious metal with high thermal & electrical conductivity and excellent corrosion resistance; it is used extensively in construction & appliances. Copper is predominantly extracted from sulphide ore which is typically concentrated, smelted, and then refined to produce cathode, but also from oxide ore (normally found near the surface) which is typically leached with the copper recovered through a solvent extraction & electrowinning (SX-EW) process.

## Suppliers and consumers

Supply: global refined copper production in 2023 was \~26mt consisting of \~22.5mt mine production and \~3.5mt of secondary supply. Mine production is the structural constraint to copper production (rather than smelting/refining). Copper mining is relatively fragmented with >400 operational copper mines; the top 10 miners account for \~40% of the total market. The top copper miners are Chile (24%), Peru (12%), the DRC (11%), China (9%) and the US (6%). The largest copper companies (equity basis) globally in 2023 include Codelco, BHP and Freeport (all \~6%). Consistent with other metals, China dominates copper smelting & refining, the key trade flow being copper concentrate exported from Latam (Chile & Peru) that is smelted/refined in China. Other key refined producers include Chile (smelters & SXEW), DRC (SXEW), Japan (smelters) and Russia (smelters). Looking forward we expect sustained growth in China smelter capacity but also new smelters are also being built in DRC, Indonesia & India.

Demand: China is the largest consumer of copper at \~50% of total demand, followed by Europe (16%) & North America (10%). Key end use sectors include electrical networks (\~25%), construction (\~25%), consumer goods (AC's/heat exchangers), machinery and automotive. Whilst copper has a broad range of end use applications that are geared to global industrial production/GDP, future secular growth drivers that are exposed to energy transition include renewable power generation, automotive electrification (EVs) and a increase in electrical network infrastructure to support the energy transition. Over the past \~30 years, copper demand has grown by \~2.5%pa.

## Copper overview

Copper and its alloys possess a variety of properties that make them some of the most versatile materials available. It is a highly ductile semi-precious metal with high thermal & electrical conductivity and excellent corrosion resistance, used extensively in construction, appliances & electrical transmission. These properties can be enhanced by added other elements to produce alloys, the principal ones being bronze & brass. Chile, Peru, China, DRC and US are the largest copper mining nations with \~60% of supply.

Copper ore undergoes three stages (Fig 3) in the production process to reach copper cathode with a purity of >99%. It is sold around the world with the base price typically based on the LME or SHFE futures contracts plus a local premium (to reflect local logistics, supply & demand dynamics, quality etc).

## Copper geology

Copper deposits come in various forms, each with its own geological footprint; they are located in many parts of the world, being commonly associated with the process of mountain formations. Chile, Peru, China, the USA and DR Congo are the largest copperproducing nations, with ores also found in Australia, Zambia, Russia, Mexico, and Canada. Large quantities of copper are also housed within polymetallic nodules - rocky lumps on the deep sea floor - however high recovery costs have prevented commercial exploitation.

There are two broad categories of ores - sulphides & oxides - which are processed differently. Copper oxide deposits are close to the surface and typically have a larger sulphide deposit underneath.

 Sulphide ores are typically found deep underground and are the primary source of copper. They contain copper in the form of copper sulphide minerals, such as chalcopyrite (CuFeS2), chalcocite (Cu2S), & bornite (Cu5FeS4). As discussed below, the processing of sulphide ores typically involves crushing & grinding, flotation to produce concentrate, smelting to produce matte and then refining to produce blister copper.

Oxide ores are typically found near the surface and are less abundant than sulphide ores. They contain copper in the form of copper carbonate, sulfate, phosphate, and silicate minerals. Examples include malachite (Cu2CO3(OH)2), azurite (Cu3(CO3)2(OH)2), and cuprite (Cu2O). The most common method to process oxide ore is heap leaching, which involves leaching copper out of the rock using sulphuric acid, and then extracting the copper from the pregnant leach solution through a solvent extraction & electrowinning (SX-EW) process.

Types of deposits are classified on the basis of how they form. Porphyry copper deposits (formed by the cooling of magma chambers deep within the earth's crust) are the most important, yielding two-thirds of the world's copper. Sedimentary copper deposits (formed in ancient seabeds), found in the Central African Copperbelt, are also important; there are also vein deposits which are typically smaller scale. Due to copper's high reactivity, copper deposits often host gold, silver and molybdenum, which are recovered as by products. Most copper ores contain only a small percentage of copper with grades varying from 0.3% for open-cut mines to >3.0% for underground mines. Grades in the African Copperbelt can be materially higher.

## Mining to market

The extraction of copper from ore is normally done in three stages: 1) Mining; 2) Beneficiation (crushing/ grinding) for sulphides or leaching for oxides; and 3) Refining. The process varies for oxide and sulphide ores, with oxides typically processed using hydrometallurgy (leaching & SX-EW) and sulphides typically processed using pyrometallurgy (concentrating, smelting & refining).

## Ore beneficiation

Froth flotation is the most widely used method for copper ore beneficiation. Copper ores contain large amounts of waste material known as gangue which must first be separated from the copper mineral via crushing and grinding. Water is then mixed in and further ground by a series of mills to form slurry with the addition of chemical reagents. These chemicals enable copper particles to bind to air bubbles being pumped through the slurry forming a froth which overflows the flotation tank. This is collected and allowed to condense while tailings are discarded. The remaining copper concentrate of 25-40% (depending on the type of ore and mill recovery) is then transported to a smelter for further processing.

## Smelting/ leaching

Once the concentrate is produced, further impurities are removed through smelting, otherwise called pyrometallurgy. Smelting involves heating dry concentrate in a furnace with oxygen-enriched air, producing sufficient heat to smelt the concentrate to slag. Iron and sulphur impurities are oxidised and sulphur dioxide is subsequently formed which can be used in the production of sulphuric acid. At this stage, the copper formed is 98% pure and is called blister copper.

Conversely, the hydrometallurgical method of leaching may also be adopted over pyrometallurgy to reduce sulphur dioxide production. Leach solution is brought in contact with the ore to dissolve the copper which is highly soluble in what is now referred to as pregnant leach solution. Impurities are left behind but must be properly disposed of. The challenge of leaching sulphide ores is poor metal recovery that limits its application.

## Electro refining

The final stage of refining is conducted after both the smelting and leaching methods. The electrolytic cell consists of a copper anode, a copper starter sheet as the cathode and an electrolytic solution of copper sulphate & sulphuric acid. When an electric current is passed through, copper from the anode is deposited onto the cathode. This gradually accumulates, reaching a purity of over 99%.

Refined copper cathode can also be produced through the leaching of oxide ores at the mine site, producing copper cathode (>99% Cu) in one process. Although this hydrometallurgy process can be lower cost and requires less water usage, it is often used for the initial output and is much shorter life and less scalable than pyrometallurgy.

## Copper scrap

Copper can be reused indefinitely without any loss in quality, therefore, scrap is a key component of the copper market, with refined copper demand at \~26mt, but total copper demand at \~32mt including scrap. In terms of total copper units \~10mt or \~30% of copper consumption is derived from scrap.

There are two types of scrap:

Direct use scrap (\~7mt): Direct use scrap is added at the fabrication stage and is a 'demand item' as it is a substitute for refined copper (i.e. changes in direct use scrap impact refined copper demand).

Secondary scrap (\~3.5mt): Secondary scrap is added at the smelter/refinery stage and is a 'supply item' as it is a substitute for mined copper units in the production of refined copper (i.e. changes in scrap impact refined copper supply).

## Copper Demand

Copper is highly efficient in transferring electrons to transport electricity, which makes it highly preferable for wiring and power cables found in buildings and consumer appliances. It also has uses in electrical goods, specifically the wiring for TVs and mobile phones and in industrial machinery as a heat exchanger. Because of its high corrosion resistance, copper and its alloys are also used for the construction of gas and water pipes. Copper also possesses inherent antimicrobial properties and is an effective touch surface in schools and hospitals to minimise the spread of bacteria.

Of all metals, copper ranks second in conductivity, surpassed only by silver. This makes it the most economical conductor over the life of a product, as while the precious metal is more conductive, widespread use is prohibited by higher prices. Aluminium is an important substitute for copper in electricity transmission and distribution, with price ratios above \~3.5:1 incentivising substitution away from copper into primary aluminium.

## Copper's use in the energy transition

Owing to its properties for electrification, copper plays a critical role in the energy transition and is a key material in solar panels, wind turbines, power cables and energy storage systems. In 2023 copper demand from 'traditional' end uses (construction, manufacturing, appliances) was weak in Europe, lacklustre in US and mixed in China (home appliances strong & completion cycle limiting construction sector downside), however, a clear trend across all regions has been strong demand from energy transition applications (renewables, grid & auto) offsetting weakness elsewhere.

We estimate EV (\~1.2mt) and renewables (2.4mt) account for \~11% of global demand in 2023; including an estimate of grid demand linked to energy transition (renewables hook-up & charging infra \~1.5mt), total share of demand is \~15%. By 2025/30 we forecast energy transition share of demand will lift to 18%/23% of demand.

In our view, the key structural debate for copper demand is: When will headwinds from the multi-year contraction in demand from China property/construction be offset by the secular growth from energy transition (EV & renewables). Amidst the euphoria of the Covid reopening rally in 2020/21, energy transition was a key foundation of the supercycle argument. At that stage we did not believe the fast-growing EV/renewables segments (market share \~5%) were large enough to offset moderation in pent-up reopening demand and structural downside risks from China construction (2020 share 10-15%). In our view, 2023/24 will be the inflection point. China construction (which is >70% through a 4-5yr re-basing) is less of a driver as it now accounts for only a 7% (+ shrinking) share of global copper demand vs EV/renewables at 12% (+ growing at >10%). We forecast, energy transition will result in global demand growth slightly higher than historical trend growth of \~2.5% pa (1980-22 CAGR) off a much higher base, and if the growth rates in EV & renewables which we forecast over the next 3-5yrs are sustained over the next 10-20yrs and substitutes for copper are not found, the increasing share of these high growth sectors would result in a higher demand growth rate.

## Copper alloys and their uses

Copper's suitability for a variety of industrial purposes has expanded through the formation of alloys from the addition of other metal(s) for desired properties. Approximately 400 types of copper alloys exist, which are based on the predominant alloying element.

Bronze: Formed from copper alloyed primarily with tin, although varying amounts of arsenic, phosphorus, aluminium and silicon are added to produce different characteristics. Many different bronze alloys exist, but it is traditionally composed of 85% copper and 15% tin. These produce alloys with significantly higher tensile strength and hardness, mainly used in constructing statues and bearings (silicon bronze) and piping (aluminium bronze). The Bronze Age was characterised by the extensive use of bronze, being the first developed alloy over 4,000 years ago.

Brass: Contains varying amounts of zinc ranging from 10-50% and the addition of other metals (usually less than 5%) depending on its use. This improves strength, ductility and is corrosion resistant making it one of copper's most common alloys. It has a strong resemblance to gold and is cheaper than Copper. Cupronickel (Copper Nickel): Mainly used for marine applications due to excellent corrosion resistance from the addition of nickel (20-30%) and small amounts of iron and manganese. Other applications include heat exchange equipment, silver coins and hygienic touch surfaces due to its antimicrobial properties.

Beryllium Copper: Can be heat treated to attain the highest strength and hardness of any copper alloy with a Vickers Hardness (HV) of 100-420. Beryllium Copper has higher corrosion resistance and thermal and electrical conductivity compared to steel while also possessing non-magnetic properties and is used extensively in springs. However, beryllium is an expensive precious metal and poses inhalation hazards due to its toxicity.

## Copper Supply

## Mining:

Global mined copper production in 2023 was \~23mt, with relatively fragmented production with the top 10 producers supplying \~42% of the total market. Chile (24%), Peru (12%), the DRC (12%), China (8%) and the US (6%) are the largest copper mining nations. The rally in the copper price post the GFC (2008/9) incentivised a material increase in new project approvals that contributed to oversupply and low prices in 2014- 16, with mined supply growing at an average of 5% between 2012 and 2016. The last 5 years have seen mined copper supply growth fall sequentially, with average growth of <2%. The period of elevated copper prices in 2020-22 has not stimulated material new project approvals and whilst we acknowledge that smaller/shorter lead time projects may add to supply in 2025-27, the window of opportunity for sufficient new projects being added to prevent a sharp slowdown in mine supply growth is rapidly closing.

Looking we expect mine supply growth in the next 2-3yrs as the latest generation of major projects ramp-up; however the copper industry has a very bad track record of meeting guidance and we do not see strong evidence to support this changing. As a result we have a more cautious view on the supply outlook into 2024/26 forecasting 2- 3% growth in mine supply (post 5% disruption). Beyond 2025 the project pipeline is limited. We expect M&A, acceleration in project development & technological advancements (sulphide leach) to prevent mine supply declining medium-term; however, we think it is unlikely that projects will be developed fast enough to prevent a slowdown in supply growth in the next 2-3yrs.

## Supply disruptions

Copper mine supply has a poor track record of meeting guidance and so a mine disruption allowance of 5% is incorporated into our supply forecasts from 2024 to reflect this. Disruptions have historically included strikes, pit wall failures, lower head grades, technical challenges, adverse weather conditions and delays in project ramp ups, with almost 50% of recent disruptions coming from operations in Latin America.

## Copper projects

## What's in the pipeline?

The number of very large 'Tier 1' copper mines in easy locations is smaller than it was 10 years ago; we also acknowledge that a number of the large mines that have already been in the pipeline for 10-20 years (Tampakan, El Pachon, Taca Taca, Resolution, etc) may not be developed. However, the copper industry is fragmented and a large pipeline of projects exists, many of these are smaller projects with lower barriers to development vs the large multi-billion \$ projects that dominate the supply debate.

In our view, the incentive to invest in a copper mine has never been greater (even more than 10 years ago) and the capital allocation priorities of the large miners have clearly shifted from a focus on cash returns to M&A & project development, and copper at the top of the priority list. The questions are (1) is the copper price high enough to incentive investment; (2) will projects be developed fast enough?

## What is the incentive price?

Although capital allocation priorities from the miners are shifting from cash returns to growth, relative to 10 years ago in our view broader capital discipline from the miners is better. The marked difference vs the last capex upcycle when the miners were investing across the commodity complex, is almost all capex & project development is chasing energy transition commodities (copper, nickel, lithium, etc), with limited appetite to invest in coal and iron ore. In our view, capital discipline, better planning and recognition that there are limits to the number of major projects a company can successfully develop on at one time (BHP \$80bn capex pipeline in 2010) is a significant positive for investors, and pay-out based dividend policies have led to better balance between growth and returns. But the one commodity that every mining company (& China state-backed companies) wants to invest in is copper.

A lot of new projects will be needed to meet demand growth over the next 10 years and an 'incentive price' above the marginal cost in needed. Our methodology for calculating the 'incentive price' is to estimate the supply & demand gap (based on approved projects) in 10yrs time (2022-2032 \~6mt) and take point a at which this intercepts the project IRR curve (based projects with sufficient visibility to analyse).

On this basis we estimate a copper price of \$4.00/lb would be required for \~6mt (UBS estimate of projects required to balance the market in 2032) of potential copper projects to generate a 15% IRR.

We acknowledge that the incentive price is conceptual in nature as it represents a price that mining companies believe they can generate value accretive returns in the future (i.e. when a mine comes into production not now) and assumes a target IRR that may vary between companies, regions & projects.

 Spot vs future prices: In our view for commodities like aluminium, mining companies will need to consistently see spot prices at levels high enough to generate value accretive returns. Given the overwhelmingly consensual bullish long-term copper narrative, long project development timelines (2-5yrs) and a clear desire from almost every miner to increase exposure to copper; in our view mining companies are more likely to approve projects based on the assumption prices will be high enough to generate value accretive returns in the future, rather than spot price today. If enough miners approve projects based on this assumption, the supply & demand tightness that support higher price expectations may never materialise.

 Target IRR: For commodities with structurally challenged demand outlooks (oil, gas, iron ore & coal) companies are likely to require faster payback and higher IRR's. In our view the desire for mining companies to invest in new copper projects may result in miners applying a <15% IRR hurdle rate to copper projects.

Potential downside risk to the incentive price created by a large pool of capital chasing a finite number of advanced projects that might reduce IRR hurdle rates. However, we believe this is offset by upside risks to our forecast supply & demand gap by: (1) persistent issues at operating mines (e.g. Chile/Codelco); (2) stronger than expected energy transition demand (e.g. grid transmission upside). We believe our updated longterm price of \$4.00/lb reflects a balance of these risks.

We expect more M&A and an acceleration in project development to fill the project pipeline, this combined with technological advancements (sulphide leach improvement) is likely prevent mine supply declining medium-term. As a result we have much better visibility and conviction that projects will not be developed fast enough to prevent a slowdown in supply growth in the next 2-3yrs.

## How is copper sold?

There are two ways in which copper is sold by mining companies:

1) Copper concentrate: in the form of powder with 25-40% copper metal. This is sold to smelters/ refiners' who refine the concentrate with selling terms unique to each smelter.

They charge for this service through Treatment Charges (TCs) settled in US\$/t of concentrate and Refining Charges (RCs) settled in USc/lb payable metal as implied charges levied by smelters. TCs and RCs are dynamic although benchmark prices are revised annually and from 2011, there has also been half-year contract negotiations. Previously, these were predominantly set by major Japanese smelters but more recently by major players BHP and Freeport with the Chinese, Japanese and Korean smelters.

Copper concentrate is traded on either a spot or long term contract basis and is sold based on its copper content. Typically, miners are paid the price at the time the smelter/ refinery makes the sale using the London Metal Exchange price, then subtracting TC/RCs and penalties that may be applicable for strict limitations on impurities. A quotational pricing system is used and the price is agreed upon at a fixed date.

2) Copper cathode: consists of 99.99% pure copper made into 1m2 sheets of 1cm thickness. It is traded through warrants, options or swap contracts on the New York (COMEX), London (London Metals Exchange) and Shanghai (Shanghai Futures Exchange) markets.

How do smelters make money?
Figure 31: Smelters income breakdown
![](images/d4673f84a15afcfedcebfe227bd5a1eea4f330d6fe976d245d5488455dd3da4f.jpg)
Source: Boliden 2020 Annual Report

There are three key sources of profitability for smelters:

1) TCs and RCs – Treatment and Refining charges represent the cost of converting a tonne of concentrate into the metal paid by miners. TCs and RCs are negotiated between miners, traders and smelters on the benchmark or spot market, or via fixed contract. Benchmark represents the annual view of the global copper concentrate market typically negotiated between major smelters and miners, whereas spot represents a current negotiation and is more indicative of current market conditions. As a result, TCs are affected by both current and annual views of supply and demand. Impurities in the metal concentrates can also be deducted or compensated. Smelters typically source the majority (>80%) of the concentrate feed on contract (fixed volume & priced at benchmark) and source modest amounts needed to achieve high utilisation rate in the spot market.

2) Free metal – Occurs when the amount of metal recovered exceeds the payable metal content of purchased metal concentrates and secondary materials. Free metal is comprised of four components: the price of the metal on the LME, the metal content of the concentrate, the smelter recovery rate (metal extracted as a % of metal content, which depends on the quality of the process and the material) and the percentage paid by the smelter for the concentrate.

3) Metal premiums – A local adjustment of the LME price to reflect the supply and demand dynamics, quality and shape of the product and its alloys, and the value-added services such as transportation.

## Current pricing

Prices peaked in 2021 amidst the Covid reopening rally, with copper's role in the energy transition being a key foundation of the supercycle argument. Copper prices have since retreated, weighed down by a deterioration in macroeconomic sentiment, weak demand in Europe/US and a structural downturn in the China construction sector. However, weaker than expected production and supply cuts from the mine level, as well as strong renewables demand has prevented a material fundamental surplus limiting price downside in 2022/23.

In our opinion, a material lift in new project approvals and capex into the copper sector is needed for supply to match robust demand growth over the next 10 years; as a result an 'incentive price' above the marginal cost is needed. We believe the combination of cost inflation, more challenging & lower grade projects in the future combined with a structurally positive demand outlook has increased the long-term price for copper. In the next 1-2yrs we forecast a deficit in the copper market and believe the prospect of physical tightness creates upside risk to copper prices.
