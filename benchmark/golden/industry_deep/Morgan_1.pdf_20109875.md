## Introduction

## A quick recap of performance history

China's banks de-rated over the past 10 years with some acceleration between 2021- 2022: This reflects lingering concerns about financial system risks and sacrificing banks profitability to support the real economy. In fact, various forms of window guidance weighed on banks' performance, especially in 2021 and 2022.

However, the de-rating has been more notable than the moderation in banks' profitability – banks on average still reported relatively resilient ROE and posted stable profit growth over the same period, supported by their thick provision buffer, while the macro environment and general corporate earnings showed more volatility in these years.

Banks' outperformance became more notable in 2023 and 2024 YTD, as the policy focus on risk containment has gradually translated into more stable than expected asset quality, helping to alleviate concerns about long-term risks and cost to shareholders.

## Our fundamental outlook in brief

We see ongoing coordinated policy efforts to contain and reduce financial system risks, which should further reduce market concern about bank risks: Though initially meeting market suspicions about sustainability, China's major shift to prioritizing financial risk reduction vs. supporting growth – which started in 2021 – has paid off and proven to be sustainable. In particular, we note heightened focus by policymakers in recent months and dedicated enforcement of the various new risk containment policies.

In addition, we see recent policies to contain vicious competition and irrational growth in industrial investment as another example of prioritizing risks vs. growth. This could contain long-term credit costs for banks.

We expect only a modest increase in bank credit costs if industrial investment growth begins to rationalize.

We also expect continued smooth/moderating NPL formation in LGFV and property loans, particularly as China approaches the end of this cycle of property NPL formation.

New monetary policy framework should support banks' NIM and ROE: Another key market concern regarding China's banks has been declining rates and NIM, particularly as window guidance on both loan volume and yields has effectively led to many asymmetric rate cuts.

We believe reduced focus on loan and TSF volume growth and the new interest rate transmission framework will support more rational NIM for major banks in China.

We expect modest NIM pressure in 2025 and some rebound in 2026, which should give the market more confidence in earnings and dividend sustainability.

Valuation is still low… Average forward P/B is 0.38x and 0.52x for H-share and A-share banks we cover, respectively, compared to around 0.6x and 0.9x in early 2021. Meanwhile, the average ROE of our covered banks dropped just modestly, from 11.45% in 2020 to 11.22% in 2023.

…and dividend yields are high… Our covered H-share banks offer 7-9% dividend yield, with SOE banks at around 8% as of August 2024. A-share banks' yields are slightly lower, largely between 5-7%, but still attractive given the lower rate environment onshore.

## Favored stocks against this backdrop

We move our Top Pick to PSBC-H: We think this bank is best positioned to benefit from stable loan yield, potentially higher bond yield, and lower deposit rates, thanks to its balance sheet structure and larger fixed cost base in agency fees. The negotiation with Postal Group to reduce agency fees could be an upcoming catalyst, expected in 2H24, while market expectations look low compared to the last time it negotiated the agency fee adjustment. In addition, PSBC is less exposed to manufacturing loans than peers, which should lead to more stable credit costs for the bank as the financial system starts to digest industrial credit risks.

## ...and see still good return potential in a number of other banks, such as:

• CCB-H: CCB-H is defensive, with currently still the highest dividend yield at \~8% among H-share SOE banks. CCB has shown more rational balance sheet expansion in recent quarters despite window guidance on loan growth, which could better support margins and control credit risks. Its NIM is also the highest among SOE banks and is above industry average, at 1.57% in 1Q24. We remain EW on CCB-A given lower dividend yield at 5.3% (2024e).

• Ningbo: The bank has been able to maintain its market share in Zhejiang with its client-centric strategy, customized services, and the practice of frequent client visits despite fierce competition from SOE banks in the past several years. More rational loan and deposit pricing, driven by monetary policy change, should help the bank re-accelerate market share gains while keeping pricing power. Despite more exposure to local manufacturers and exporters, we are confident in Ningbo's ability to digest manufacturing risks, as tested by various past cycles.

• Minsheng Bank-H: We continue to believe that Minsheng Bank's ROE will be restored gradually – legacy risks have largely been digested – although it could take more time because the bank changed its strategy to focus on long-term development via the buildup of a strong client base, on which we have seen good progress. We think stabilizing NIM, pickup in balance sheet growth and moderating credit risks should drive ROE gradually higher. We maintain EW on Minsheng-A given dividend yield at 6.4% (2024e) is lower than Minsheng-H's at 8.9%.

• Industrial Bank: Industrial Bank maintains its competitive advantages in loan pricing and risk management, led by departmental cooperation and a strong in house research team. Comprehensive solutions and in-depth industry knowledge could help the bank to quickly identify opportunities and gain market share, especially as overall credit growth should gradually moderate in the coming years.

## Where we could be wrong

## Our scenario of overinvestment and PPI deflation dragging on to 2027 could notably impair bank capital

In a scenario of rising multipolar world challenges, we see notably higher NPL risks for the banks if Chinese manufacturers continue to overinvest to maintain cost competitiveness to defend global market share, sacrificing margin and resulting in PPI deflation. We estimate that NPL ratio could reach \~18% within manufacturing credits by 2027 in this scenario, and it could take 4-5 years for the banks to digest industrial ${ \mathsf { N P L } } { \mathsf { S } } ,$ at Rmb2.8tn per year. Systemic risks could also rise as weaker banks with thin margins and modest provision buffers could be dragged down by the mounting NPLs, weighing on the bank stocks as a whole.

## Overly low interest rates would erode banks' risk digestion capability in the upcoming industrial NPL cycle and weigh on ROE

As the ultimate monetary transmission mechanism is still a work in progress, we think the extent to which banks could restore their risk premium in loan pricing will be key to monitor. It will be especially important for banks to strengthen their capability to digest risk with industrial NPLs gradually surfacing in 2025 and beyond. However, if corporate loan yields continue to fall to \~3% over time, we estimate that it would translate into - 0.3% ROA for banks' industrial loans, which would erode their provision buffers and affect ROE. This could again lead to concerns about the sustainability of dividends and put our investment case at risk.

# Policy priority shift from growth to risk containment has supported banks' outperformance

Despite concerns about China's economic growth trajectory and financial risks, China's banks have in general shown stable earnings and shareholder return, and outperformed the market (both the CSI 300 and HSI) for three consecutive years. Coordinated policy efforts to contain financial system risks have been playing a key role in dissipating market concerns about various bank risks and led to the outperformance.

Looking ahead, we think China's major shift to prioritize financial risk containment vs. supporting growth will continue to pay off and help major banks to generate sustainable shareholder returns in a more prudent development environment in China, in which value and dividends could still outperform.

Notably, we are seeing even more focus in recent months by policymakers and dedicated enforcement of the various new risk containment policies, including reduced window guidance on industrial loan growth, which should help address irrational industrial investment and associated credit risks.

Against this backdrop, we expect banks' ROE on average to stabilize at around 10% in 2025 and 2026 while profit growth can be maintained at a stable pace. Potentially rebounding NIM in 2026 could drive modest recovery in profit growth down the road. At the same time, while we expect banks start to digest industrial credits in 2025, NPL formation in other areas such as property and infrastructure should continue to moderate, leading to a modest rise of NPL formation from 0.87% in 2023 to 0.97% in 2026. ## Comprehensive solutions for LGFV debt to prevent lowefficiency new investments while improving returns from existing asset base

Our LGFV trackers following our in-depth analysis of long-term LGFV solutions have indicated that comprehensive ways are in place to manage local debts, both existing and new. On a high level, we think it's important to note that the cash flow generated from China's infrastructure projects could still cover over half of their interest expense, while the net interest burden borne by the Chinese government as a whole remains at a reasonable level of 2.3% of GDP in 2022.

Partial swaps into local government bonds are being used to replace the local debts with more urgent liquidity needs and higher interest rates, which cushions the tail risks. At the same time, new infrastructure investments and credit growth are being contained by strictly monitoring project quality and a white list system (3899 list). In 2024 there are also provinces (such as Henan) utilizing new local special bond quotas to swap LGFV debt, further slowing infrastructure investments given a lack of efficient projects.

Our field trips to Zhejiang, Henan, Sichuan, and Chongqing provinces also indicated a wellcoordinated process in place, including orderly repayment, LGFV consolidation, and resource sharing coordinated by provincial governments. Notably, the recent Third Plenum requires all revenues backed by administrative power and government credit to be included in budget management.

With more efforts to improve the efficiency of local governments' large – Rmb250tn – asset base, and effective drop in interest rates from swaps and generally lower loan yields, we believe market concerns about LGFV debt risks should continue to abate.

We acknowledge that these efforts have led – and will continue to lead – to some effective fiscal tightening on the local government level. However, we believe this is a needed transition as China deflates the property bubble and transitions from the partly property-driven economic growth model to a high-quality development model focusing on productivity gains. We believe the policy dedication to LGFV risk containment despite the

slowdown is another example of policy priority shift to risk control and digestion vs.
growth.

## Rmb3tn in high-risk property credits have already been digested; we expect loan demand to bottom by mid-2025

As we have analyzed with our property team, the overall housing inventory in major cities should fall to more reasonable levels when excluding the dead inventory – i.e., the housing inventory that has no sale or rental value in view of location and quality, under the new norm of housing demand/supply. Given a slower pace of new construction than sales, we think inventory should improve to a healthier level by mid-2025 and support related credit demand to bottom out. The government-led de-stocking efforts should help facilitate this process without adding new risks to the banks, given a heightened focus on preventing new implicit debt.

We estimate that banks cumulatively digested \~9% of property loans as NPLs from 2021 to 2023, while the total high-risk credits in the property sector digested by the financial sector, including non-bank investors, should reach Rmb3tn. This is equivalent to \~70% of the credit costs associated with what we call dead inventory.

We believe the bulk of property credit cost digestion is already behind. Though some of the housing projects under construction will also become dead inventory over time (20%, based on analysis by our property team), we believe it will be easy for the financial system to digest such losses over the next 2-3 years. We expect the banks to digest Rmb300- 400bn of property NPLs annually, which will be Rmb200-300bn lower than the peak in recent years. Therefore, the rest of the 30% dead inventory will probably result in a further Rmb1tn of credit losses to be digested over the next 2-3 years by various market participants, and mark the end of the property down-cycle.

## Earlier-than-expected industrial capacity rationalization could lower concerns about credit risks

We have expected that banks will start to digest industrial NPLs in 2025, with \~10% of industrial credit at risk by then, which should be manageable because the industrial sector credit risks are at levels more modest compared to the last round of supply-side reform between 2016 and 2020, when banks were digesting manufacturing NPLs at \~5% per year.

In fact, we are seeing quicker actions from the policymakers; capacity rationalization could happen earlier than we have expected. We have seen some key steps taken to contain industrial capacity expansion in China:

1. New rules to ban many local government incentives for industrial investments, such as tax breaks and subsidies;

2. Policies issued by a number of ministries for rational industrial capacity expansion; and

3. The removal of window guidance to maintain high industrial loan growth in the past few years.

These efforts are paying off, as evidenced in May and June data from 400,000 industrial firms – YTD trends in eight key metrics are better than our prior expectation of broad-based deterioration, with some sectors seeing improvement.

## Retail credit quality should be supported by resilient household balance sheets and stable leverage

We believe retail credit risks remain manageable – overall household balance sheets remain resilient despite debates about income, consumption trends, de-risking efforts, and changing dynamics among different income groups.

Contrary to market concerns, our analysis shows still healthy growth in household financial assets at 9.5% yoy in 1Q24, +5.1% YTD, led by bank deposits. We think the trend is largely in line with underlying income growth and consumption growth, based on channel checks on payment data. Though both financial assets and income growth are modestly lower than pre-COVID levels, we think they are healthy given China's de-risking efforts in recent years.

We continue to note a key source of resilient income generation is a large amount (Rmb200tn in 2021) of unlisted equity stakes owned by households, behind >110mn small businesses, and largely unaffected by property market reset. This is also reflected in China's effective income tax rate, which is below 3% despite seemingly high progressive income tax rates.

On the other hand, job application and employment data have shown a notable shift from the Internet, property, and financial services sectors to tech and industrials in recent years. Therefore given the greater concentration of Internet and financial services employment in first-tier cities, and the higher industrial investment in second- to fifth-tier cities in recent years, a notable shift in income growth towards lower-tier cities and rural areas is likely ahead, particularly with increased credit support being allocated to rural areas such as farm land upgrades. This has resulted in consumption downgrade in first-tier cities and upgrade in rural/lower-tier cities, which to some extent raised confusion about overall household balance sheet resilience and credit risk.

However, households have become much more prudent about leverage, as evidenced in 1- 2% annual growth in retail credits since 2022, compared to the low-teens level in 2020 and 2021. This translates into a moderating ratio of credit to household financial assets: to 22.5% in 1Q24, down from 28.6% in 2019, which should reduce risks in retail credits.

In addition, higher-risk and higher-rate retail credit, such as P2P, was cleaned up before 2020. With a more clearly set fintech regulatory framework and lowered rate cap, to 24%, we believe the volatility brought by typical credit cycles should be reduced for both banks and non-bank players.

# Monetary policy framework change to support NIM – a key step back to a market-oriented financial sector

We believe the monetary policy framework is the regulatory effort to set a rule-based, more market-oriented monetary framework following years of rise in administrative measures or direct government interference, which also weighed on the valuation of China's banks. This is in line with the key goal of the Third Plenum, which is more like a rule-setting exercise as China is entering a very different development stage at the moment.

During the period of prioritizing GDP growth (before 2021), many rules were loosened or loosely enforced, which supported growth but also created risks and issues.

Then, during the transition period in the last few years, to contain risks and drive the economic transition, China not only tightened regulatory enforcement in many areas but also notably increased administrative measures or direct government interference in many areas.

With an already notable transition in economic structure in China, the policymakers are trying to establish new rules and framework, which will not be as loose as before but hopefully will provide more stable expectations for the market.

This process is quite notable in the areas of financial services and monetary policy framework. Before 2018, loose enforcement of regulation led to massive development of shadow banking. With the start of financial cleanup in 2016 and further heightened efforts in 2018, policymakers also notably increased policy interference in banks’ normal course of business. That also reversed market-oriented interest rate reform with window guidance on a wide range of banking operations across various types of loan growth and rates. Such window guidance has been the key factor pressuring banks' NIM and long-term earnings expectations.

We believe the new monetary policy framework will not only support banks' NIM over time but also help set more stable long-term earnings expectations. This should support banks' valuation and drive another year of outperformance during China's economic transition period.

## A shift in China's monetary policy framework with reduced window guidance should help support banks' NIM over time

We believe the transition towards a rate-based monetary policy framework is in progress with many actions since PBOC governor Pan laid out the path of monetary framework transition in the Lujiazui forum. This, together with the reduced window guidance on loan growth and certain loan rates, should lead to more risk-based loan pricing, as well as more rational credit growth based on supply & demand and fair market competition. We expect banks to show gradual NIM and ROE recovery with more market-oriented loan supply and pricing.

We expect banks' NIM to remain largely flat in 2025 with modest pressure in 1H25 before a pickup in 2026 with more rational loan and deposit pricing. We look for loan growth to moderate to 6-7% in 2025-2026 on average with reduced window guidance on industrial loan growth. These factors should support banks' profitability and improve the status of their balance sheets.

## 2. De-emphasizing interference in many rates and loan pricing

The PBOC has also started to restructure monetary policy, with reduced interference in many rates. We believe the following efforts are paving the way for more market-oriented loan and deposit pricing:

• Potential breakdown of the relationship between MLF and LPR;

• Setup of the seven-day repo rate as the key policy rate with a narrowing band;

• Some linkage of loan and deposit rates with the key policy rate, but not 100% correlation.

Over time, we expect loan yield to be based on many market factors, such as risk preference, capital usage, competition, etc. This should lead loan yield to rebound on higher-risk loans such as SME and industrial loans; and lower loan yield on mortgages.

Overall, we believe loan pricing will be more stable as banks become more rational about loan supply. This should in turn lead to more rational deposit pricing, benefiting NIM from both the asset and liability side. In addition, we believe the PBOC is also trying to achieve a steeper yield curve for rates with various tenors, which should benefit banks' profits.

## How to position in banks during the expected fourth year of outperformance

In addition to the reduced risk concerns and likely more stable NIM outlook, we believe other factors could also support another year of bank outperformance.

1. While we expect property loan demand to bottom in mid-2025, the property cycle could still weigh on China's overall growth for another year.

2. Despite the earlier-than-expected policy to slow the push for industrial investment expansion, which could reduce overall industrial NPL formation over time, it might still take a year to reach more balanced demand and capacity expansion. Hence, corporate margins might still be under some pressure before that.

Hence, in this still challenging economic transition period in China, combined with still many global uncertainties, we believe stable profits and high dividends from China's banks could be still very attractive to investors.

In particular, the lack of yield from other investment options such as MMF and deposits could drive more fund flows to the bank stocks. Notably:

## One of the banks that will benefit from the new monetary policy the most

As discussed before, we think the shifting monetary policy framework, moderating loan growth window guidance, and PBOC support for long-term bond yields will create a favorable environment for bank NIM to stabilize and rebound. Among all the banks, we think PSBC is one of the best positioned to leverage this trend.

• In general, we think PSBC's larger cost base, with >60% cost-income ratio vs. 30+ % on average for other banks, means that even a similar level of interest income increase or interest expense savings will translate into bigger profit impact. This higher and more fixed cost base comes mainly from the agency branches from Postal Group, which account for 80% of PSBC's total branch network, creating large operating leverage.

• PSBC has relatively larger exposure to bond investments compared to other SOE banks, partly reflecting lower loan deposit ratio. Therefore, it will benefit more if bond yields rebound.

• PSBC has the largest proportion of <1 year term deposits, at 61% vs. \~30% at other banks. Hence, it will enjoy faster repricing of the deposit rate cuts, which happened multiple times in 2023 and YTD 2024.

According to our rates scenario below, PSBC could get the biggest profit lift – 11.8% – among our covered banks:

• Loan rates +5bps;

• Investment yields +10bps;

• Deposit rates -10bps;

• Wholesale funding costs +10bps.

Another benefit from moderating window guidance not shown in the sensitivity analysis is increased room for risk-based loan pricing, which we discussed here. We think reduced volume guidance will ease competition and curb irrational loan pricing, which gives banks more discretion to price loans differently based on risk, demand, capital usage, and competitive environment. This is especially important for loans made in rural areas and lower-tier cities, in view of typically higher costs associated with identifying proper demand and managing credit risks.

We think Industrial Bank maintains its competitive advantages in loan pricing and risk management – driven by cooperation among departments and in-depth industry knowledge. ## Bank of Ningbo – market share gains and pricing recovery in the absence of irrational competition

Despite the aggressive competition from SOE banks, Bank of Ningbo has been able to maintain quick loan growth in the past three years thanks to its roots in its home market, Zhejiang province, where economic recovery is better than in other regions. The bank is able to increase and maintain its market share in Zhejiang with its client-centric strategy, customized services, and the practice of frequent client visits. We believe this should bring back high-quality clients, as well as higher loan yield, in a more market-oriented environment.

In addition, we think SME loan yield could increase in view of higher risks with risk-based pricing, which will benefit Bank of Ningbo more thanks to its SME-focused strategy, especially with the removal of window guidance on SME loan growth for SOE banks. On the liability side, with the suspension of manual deposit interest payments by SOE banks, Bank of Ningbo could also resume absorption of low-cost deposits from the market, leading NIM to rebound.

The bank has adopted stricter NPL recognition given higher risks from retail loans, but is able to keep NPL ratio very low – at 0.76% in 1Q24. As industrial loan growth is gradually rationalized, we believe credit costs at Bank of Ningbo should also stabilize, driving profitability and ROE recovery.

## Where we could be wrong

## Our scenario of overinvestment and PPI deflation dragging on to 2027 could notably impair bank capital

In a scenario of rising multipolar world challenges, continued overinvestment by Chinese manufacturers to maintain cost competitiveness to defend global market share, which sacrifices margin and resulting in PPI deflation, we see notably higher NPL risks for the banks.

Our analysis shows the NPL ratio could reach \~18% within manufacturing credits by 2027 if negative PPI lasts for over four years through 2027 – this would be similar to the overcapacity issue preceding the last round of supply-side reform. Notably, given the length of the industrial investment cycle spanning from 2021 to 2027, total manufacturing-related credit would grow to a much bigger size by 2027: at over Rmb77tn assuming a 10% CAGR from 2023 to 2027, as compared to <Rmb30tn in the last round of supply-side reform.

Given such a large amount of NPLs, we think that it could take 4-5 years for the banks to digest these risks. We estimate annual total industrial NPL digestion at Rmb2.8tn, or Rmb1.4tn above the banks' usual risk digestion level, assuming a five-year digestion process.

We expect major SOE banks would need capital injections (at least Rmb500bn a year) from the government in this scenario to help maintain some reasonable credit supply. However, the financial's system ability to efficiently allocate credits and risk appetite would likely take time to restore. If this happens, we believe systemic risks would also rise as weaker banks with thin margins and modest provision buffers could be dragged down by the mounting NPLs, which would weigh on the bank stocks as a whole.

## Overly low interest rates would erode banks' risk digestion capability in the upcoming industrial NPL cycle and weigh on ROE

While the PBOC is reducing focus on quantitative guidance and trying to sort out a proper link between LPR (which appears to be artificially high) and loan rates, the ultimate monetary transmission mechanism is still a work in progress. In particular, we think the extent to which banks could restore their risk premium in loan pricing will be key to monitor. We think strengthening banks' capability to digest risk will be especially important with industrial NPLs gradually surfacing in 2025 and beyond.

We estimate that manufacturing-related loan rates would probably need to stay no lower than the range between 4.1% and 4.8% to make them economically sustainable for banks through the cycle. This takes into account funding cost, opex, and especially credit cost as through-the-cycle NPL formation of manufacturing credits could range widely between 1.9% and 3.3% annually. Average new corporate loan yield stood at 3.63% as of June 2024, per the PBOC. With help from the reserves built up when interest rates were higher, we believe the banking system can still digest the next industrial NPL cycle. However, if corporate loan yields continue to fall to \~3% over time, it would translate into -0.3% ROA for banks' industrial loans, which would erode their provision buffers and affect ROE. This could again lead to concerns about the sustainability of dividends and put our investment case at risk.
