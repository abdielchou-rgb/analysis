## Increased spread dispersion brings opportunities

The economy is slowing, but consumer demand remains solid. The Beige Book, the recent job market, ISM PMI, and CPI data all indicate that the US is cooling down. However, the Beige Book Sentiment Score is close to the last-two-year average. Furthermore, hard data this week on the consumer and industrial production fronts surprised meaningfully to the upside. Industrial production posted a second consecutive month of robust gains and was strong across the board. Barclays Economics thinks that consumer demand is the main driver (see here). Retail sales also looked solid, with the control group at the high end of the last-18-month range after April's slump. Our economists believe that the spending, hiring, and income virtuous cycle remains intact (see here).

Credit card issuer earnings point to resilient consumers overall. Companies that have reported so far have suggested that the consumer environment is not deteriorating significantly in aggregate, but they continue to see diferences across the income spectrum. There is some weakness at the lower end, which has been more afected by high inflation and interest rates. This has resulted in some rotation out of discretionary into non-discretionary products (see here and here).

Our economists believe that growth and inflation are on a good trajectory, leading the Fed to cut twice in 2024, in September and December, instead of just once. For 2025, they forecast three cuts (instead of four). This change in view was driven by the June inflation data, along with the gradual cooling of the labor market (see here). In this weeks' Focus article, we look at historical credit performance around initial FOMC rate cuts.

Credit indices are moving sideways despite equity and Treasury volatility. Credit indices continue to trade in a narrow range: 6bp in IG and 12bp in HY over the past month. This stability is remarkable compared with the elevated volatility in other asset classes over the same period, including Treasuries (30bp range at the 10y point) and equities (4% range for the S&P500 and 12% for the Russell). We think this indicates that sentiment and positioning are more favorable in credit than in some other markets.

Credit fundamentals continue to look fine, but investors seem to have turned more cautious recently. Conversations with investors indicate that most have adopted a more guarded stance. # Fed cut implications for credit

The Fed looks likely to cut soon. History suggests that spread decompression should be muted into and after the first cut in the absence of recession. But yields typically fall, which could weigh on the technical backdrop if history repeats. Still, the current situation looks most like 1995, and spreads stayed tight then.

Recent inflation and labor data have been softer, pointing to a September rate cut. June core CPI rose 0.06% m/m, the least since January 2021, and the unemployment rate has ticked up to 4.1%. Barclays Economics expects the FOMC's first rate cut to come in September (see updated policy outlook here).

Usually, credit spread premia tend to decompress into the first cut, but this time spreads have compressed significantly. If we assume that the first cut is in September and spreads remain unchanged, the BBB-AAA basis would be 34bp more compressed than last September. This would be the most compression in the 12 months leading up to an initial rate cut going back the 1950s.

Historically, if there is no recession in the next one year, spread decompression should remain muted. Typically, the trend is for credit spreads to compress in the three months after the first rate cut before reversing in the following 3-6 months.

Unsurprisingly, yields typically fall in these periods, which could weigh on the technical backdrop. Yield levels could also pressure credit spreads, since Treasury yields also fell in the years with no recession.

Today looks most like 1989 or 1995. No recession occurred within one year of these periods, spreads are in a similar context, and they also compressed in the year prior to the rate cut. Furthermore, Treasury yields also fell then without a meaningful efect on spreads.

## Data point to a September rate cut

US economic data have surprised to the downside in consecutive months across both hard and soft indicators (Figure 1). Much of this softness has been attributed to relative weakness on the inflation and labor fronts.

Inflation prints show evidence of cooling. Most recently, the June CPI report showed unexpectedly soft readings across most inflation components (Figure 2). Headline CPI fell 0.06% m/m in June (3.0% y/y vs 3.1% consensus), while core CPI rose 0.06% m/m (3.3% y/y vs 3.4% consensus), the lowest monthly print since January 2021. These weaker data have bolstered the view that economic conditions are becoming better aligned with the Fed's parameters for a rate cut.

## What happens to credit when the Fed cuts rates for the first time?

Over the past few years, we have analyzed credit market reactions when the Fed begins hiking cycles, as well as when it pauses rate hikes. With the data, as well as expectations, pointing to the start of a cutting cycle in September (Figure 6), we analyze past cycles for some insight into credit performance.

Since credit OAS data are fairly limited, we use Moody’s data to analyze the spreads between AAA and BBB corporate yields as a proxy for credit risk premia. This allows us to review credit market reactions back to the 1950sand gives us 14 relevant periods to analyze. The economic backdrop matters most for credit performance. As we can see from Figure 7, the growth/economic backdrop when the Fed begins its cutting cycle matters, so we split the starts of the rate cut cycles into three periods, when 1) the Fed begins cutting rates during a recession, 2) a recession occurs within one year of the first cut, and 3) no recession occurs within a year.

Not surprisingly, most of the decompression is during recessions. In the six months leading up to the first rate cut, we find that spread decompression is most prevalent when the Fed is cutting rates during a recession, which makes sense, as the central bank reaction was likely too slow. In cases when there is a recession within one year or no recession, we see marginal decompression. Figure 8 shows the change in basis for BBB-AAA yields in each scenario. Barclays Economics' base case is for growth to remain positive for the foreseeable future.

## High yield compresses when the US avoids a recession

Although we have less spread data for the US high yield index, we do have it for the previous five cutting cycles. Figure 12 shows spread moves in the US high yield market around all Fed cutting cycles going back to 1995. Typically, spreads were much wider than current levels going into the first cut (median of 405bp vs 303bp currently), with 1995 the closest to now in terms of starting spreads.

High yield spreads tighten about 90bp ten months after the initial rate cut when no recession occurs. Similar to the analysis above for investment grade, the periods leading up to the first rate cut look similar whether or not a recession occurs in the next year. Nearly every period (besides 1995) had wider HY spreads in the 12 or six months before the first cut. However, if no recession occurs, spreads tighten about 90bp in the 10 months following a rate cut (Figure 13). On the flip side, when recessions occur in the next twelve months, spreads continue to widen 8-9 months after the first cut.

## Today looks like 1989 or 1995

Looking at the commonalities between cycles is always a challenge. Clearly, there are material diferences between geopolitics and fiscal spending in this cycle compared with others. However, looking strictly at spreads/growth around cutting cycles, those that started in June 1989 and July 1995 are the closest comparables.

In both periods, the BBB-AAA yield basis compressed meaningfully leading up to the first rate cut, and neither had a recession within one year of the cut. For the 1989 period, the economy did eventually enter a recession after 14 months, while Barclays Economics' base case is for GDP growth to stay positive through 2025. In both periods, the BBB-AAA basis decompressed marginally in the 12 months after the first cut, by 7bp and 2bp for 1989 and 1995, respectively. While that doesn't bode well for further compression this time around, carry would clearly help ofset some of this negative spread move.

Spreads are similar to 1989 levels, but slightly wider than in 1995. Average BBB corporate spreads were 116bp in June 1989 and 94bp in July 1995, compared with 110bp today (Figure 14). For US high yield, we have spread data only for the 1995 period, when spreads were also slightly wider, at 323bp in July 1995, compared with 304bp currently.

Any spread compression as a result of rate cuts will likely be reversed in the following months. Clearly, growth is the largest determinate of credit spread performance post-rate cut. While our base case is for no recession in the next twelve months, decompression appears to be fairly consistent in most periods regardless of whether a recession is avoided.

Furthermore, the BBB-AAA yield basis has compressed 34bp leading up to a potential September rate cut. While we don't find a strong relationship between the level of spread compression before a cut and the level of decompression after, we think that spreads near the tights of the range back to 2000, combined with a historically large spread compression prior to a cut, provide enough reason for caution.

But barring a recession, the risk is that yields fall, which could pressure credit spreads. As we have seen for most of this year, the negative correlation between yields and spreads is still prevalent in price action. Barclays Rates Strategy's base case is for 10y Treasury yields to be at 4.5% by year-end 2024 (see More now, less later), implying 35bp of upside from current levels. If that occurs, we think spreads should react favorably, similar to other "non-recessionary" periods analyzed above. However, if yields continue their downward trajectory as more rate cuts are priced in, it might weaken the demand backdrop (and possibly increase supply if growth holds up). Retail inflows and fixed annuity sales have been large contributors to the technical backdrop so far this year, so lower outright yields would likely slow the flows from these sources and pressure credit spreads as a result.

In the periods when no recession occurred within one year, yields still fell but rebounded after 10 months. Figure 15 shows the reaction of 10y UST in the months around first rate cuts when the US economy avoided a recession (1966, 1984, 1989, 1995, and 1998). Yields fell 125bp from the peak but recovered most of that after 10 months, on average. That said, if this pattern were to play out again, demand could be seriously diminished.

# Picking your spots in BBBs

The BBB-A basis has reached its tightest absolute levels since 2007. The spread ratio is also at its most compressed level in years. We favor setting up for decompression at these levels. We highlight swaps from BBBs into As in sectors where ratios are most compressed and where BBBs still ofer compensation vs. As.

For our latest US High Grade company rating changes, click here. For our sector ratings, click here.

The BBB-A industrial basis has reached 39bp, with the ratio at 1.58x. This is the most compressed level since 2007 and the lowest ratio since March 2022. The current ratio is 1.6 standard deviations tight to its historical average.

Tobacco, wireless, tech, and pharma stand out as particularly compressed. In the 10y part of the curve, these sectors' ratios trade tight to broader industrials and to their respective ranges since 2021.

Opportunities for long BBBs exist in cable satellite, construction machinery, consumer products, media, oil field services, and retail. Despite broad compression trends, these sectors have been outliers; as such, there remains meaningful compensation to move lower in quality.

# Time to get negative (basis)

The recent decline in rates and increase in reverse arbitrage activity in CDX.HY have helped drive the CDS-cash basis to the most negative levels of the past year. With cash spreads still historically tight, however, we recommend adding risk through negative basis trades. Investors looking to hedge should consider CDX.HY.

For our latest US high yield company rating changes, click here. For our sector ratings, click here.

Cash spreads have underperformed CDS over the past two months, with the high yield cash index appearing wide versus CDX.HY and the maturity- and constituent-matched CDS-cash basis now the lowest of the past year.

We think the underperformance can largely be attributed to the sizable decline in rates, as the rates-spread correlation tends to be negative, as well as reverse arbitrage trades in CDX,HY, which tend to be a positive technical for CDS tightening.

Despite the underperformance, cash spreads are still tight in a historical context, so we are less inclined to look for outright longs.

For investors looking to hedge, we think CDX.HY still screens as an attractive short.

For those looking to add risk, we recommend taking advantage of recent CDS outperformance to put on negative basis trades.

## Cash has lagged CDS, with rates and CDX.HY technicals the main drivers

Last week, we highlighted how HYG has lagged CDX.HY over the past two months, leading to the most stretched spread relationship of the past year. This week, we take another look at CDX.HY versus cash at a macro level and then turn our attention to single-name relationships. High yield cash and CDX spread performance has diverged over the past twoplus months, with cash excess returns lagging CDX.HY unfunded returns meaningfully since the end of April (Figure 1). From April 30 to July 17, high yield cash index excess returns were 0.70% and spreads were 3bp wider. In contrast, CDX.HY returned 2.40% and spreads were 33bp tighter.

The divergence in performance has driven meaningful compression in the CDX.HY / HY cash spread ratio, with that relationship now close to the lowest levels of the past year (Figure 2). For investors looking to hedge high yield cash portfolios, we continue to view CDX.HY as an attractive hedge (see Caution flag for CDX.HY) and view the recent outperformance as a good entry point for shorts.

That's not to say that high yield cash has performed poorly. In fact, for total return investors, returns during this period were 3.6%. But the bulk of those returnscame from rates, with 5y Treasury yields declining 64bp. We think that the rate move has "crowded out" spread tightening because of the typically negative rates-spread correlation. There have been periods in recent history when spreads and rates rallied together, with November-December 2023 one such example, but spreads were wider and dollar prices were lower then than they are today (Figure 4). The correlation could certainly turn positive again, especially if investors start to believe that yields have peaked, but at least for the past two months, we think the rate move has kept cash spreads in check.

We expect yields to have less of an efect on CDS since it does not have direct rates exposure (beyond the discount curve for calculating points upfront), but we can test that directly. In Figure 5, we compare monthly changes in 5y Treasury yields with changes in the CDX.HY-cash index basis (we use index-level basis for a longer history) and find that the basis has a tendency to increase when rates rise (cash outperforming CDX) and decrease when rates fall (CDX outperforming cash). Since 2021, yields and the basis have moved in the same direction about 75% of the time on a monthly basis, which aligns with our intuition.

On the derivatives side, increased investor positioning in CDX.HY and the subsequent reverse arbitrage activity appear to have contributed to the tightening in many index constituents. Investors have continued to add long risk in CDX.HY over the past two months, with positioning in the on-the-run index nearly doubling and reaching \$14bn, the highest total going back to the beginning of our positioning data in 2011. Anecdotal evidence suggests that the richness of this "skew" has led to reverse arbitrage trades whereby investors attempt to buy protection on the index and sell protection on all 100 of the constituents in equal notional to capture the diference between the traded and intrinsic value.

We think the reverse arb had a negligible efect on the widest trading credits, which trade more on fundamentals and less on technicals, and an outsized efect on the tightest trading credits, whose fundamentals tend to be less of a concern and are therefore more susceptible to technical pressure. In terms of what could cause the basis to compress, we think that a reversal or simply a pause in the trends previously discussed could support future cash outperformance. First, on the rates side, Barclays rates strategists recently recommended shorting 5y Treasuries, as they believe that the market is pricing in too many cuts cumulatively (see More now, less later). At the same time, the skew has compressed (albeit modestly) as the widest trading names have finally joined the rally, which makes reverse arb trades less attractive. # CMBS: Efect of recent events on ARCLO deals

We discuss possible implications of the recent DOJ probe into Arbor Realty on the CRE CLO market.

Excerpted from US CMBS: Spreads and Jams, July 16, 2024.

Several news outlets have reported (and the company confirmed) that Arbor Realty Trust is being investigated by the US Department of Justice (DOJ) in connection with its business practices. The company's subsidiaries, Arbor Realty Collateral Management, LLC (ARCM), and Arbor Multifamily Lending, LLC (AML), are a collateral manager and servicer/special servicer, respectively, for the CRE CLO deals issued under the ARCLO shelf (five deals totaling \$6.9bn, see CRE CLO market update). As such, this news and subsequent events can have implications for ARCLO deals and the broader market, given ARCLO's share of the market.

From an operations perspective, we do not think the investigation in and of itself constitutes an event of default under the indenture for the CRE CLO deals outstanding, which could lead to an acceleration of the underlying notes. However, if there is an indictment for Arbor or any of its oficers, it would likely be construed as a collateral manager event of default, pursuant to the following clause in the ofering documents:

"For purposes of the Collateral Management Agreement, “Collateral Manager Event of Default” will mean......(iv) the occurrence of an act by the Collateral Manager or its afiliates that constitutes fraud or criminal activity in the performance of its obligations under the Collateral Management Agreement or the indictment of the Collateral Manager or any of its oficers or directors for a criminal ofense involving an investment or investment related business, fraud, false statements or omissions, wrongful taking of property, bribery, forgery, counterfeiting or extortion."

If that were to happen, the collateral manager may be removed after at least 30 days. We do not find any similar language for the CLO servicer or special servicer in the deal documents that we examined. However, we recommend that investors carefully examine deal documents for relevant language afecting their holdings.

From a collateral performance perspective, we think one possible efect may be worse performance at maturity, due to a reduction in take-out financing available from the agencies. Arbor Multifamily Lending, LLC, is a sizable originator in the GSE market, for both Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac. One of the exits for the bridge loans in CRE CLO deals is refinancing into agency-eligible loans. However, the GSEs have been getting stricter in their underwriting standards, especially after the issues with brokers and appraisals. Any pullback from Arbor may have a negative efect on the maturity performance for Arbor's portfolio of loans. Also, for the existing agency loans, the GSEs are allowed to pull servicing away from Arbor at any time, with or without cause.
