# China Economic Perspectives 2023 GDP Growth at 5.2%, Lift 2024 Growth Forecast to 4.6%

## Q4 GDP growth edged up to 5.2%y/y on low base

Q4 GDP growth slowed sequentially from Q3 but a low base pushed up the y/y growth to 5.2%. 2023 full-year GDP growth recorded 5.2% in real terms and 4.6% in nominal terms, in line with our expectation. The low base drove up the y/y growth of retail sales, exports, infrastructure FAI and manufacturing FAI, as did industrial production growth. December data suggest that property activities are yet to bottom, with sales and starts declining further from the November level. Retail sales decelerated as low base effect faded, while both manufacturing and infrastructure investment growth picked up. Growth of export volume picked up further in December, leading to a notable rebound in Q4 from Q3.

## Revise up 2024 GDP growth forecast to 4.6%

The slightly stronger Q4 GDP growth outturn (5.2% vs 5.0% of our original forecast) and the revision of q/q growth in the previous quarters push the 2024 annual growth to 4.6% (vs. 4.4% previously envisaged), without any revision of our q/q sequential growth forecasts. In the coming months, we expect property activities and property market to stabilize, helping to underpin household confidence. That should support continued post-Covid consumption recovery along with the economy and labor market, though at a slower pace. Infrastructure investment growth is not expected to accelerate despite more explicit fiscal support. We forecast exports to improve modestly on the upswing of the global tech cycle despite the expected slowdown in US growth.

## More policy support ahead; new PSL in focus

We expect more policy support ahead, though likely modest in scale. We see a headline fiscal deficit of 3.5-3.8% (potentially including issuance of special CBG), a larger special LG bond quota and additional debt-swap LG bonds. We see limited monetary policy actions, potentially a 10-20bps MLF rate cut and 25-50bp RRR cut, and liquidity facilities to help accommodate fiscal expansion. We also expect more property policy easing including more credit support to developers and using the low-cost funding underpinned by PSL to support the purchase of existing housing inventory. We are looking for clarity on the size and use of PSL funding in the months ahead, but expect much smaller impact on the housing market than that of shanty town renovation during 2015-2018.

Q4 and full year GDP grew 5.2%y/y. In Q4, sequential GDP growth softened to 4.1%q/q saar from 6.1% in Q3, but a low base pushed the y/y growth up to 5.2%. Nominal GDP growth is still well below real growth in Q4, with a -1.4% GDP deflator reflecting continued broad-based price decline. Property activities saw smaller y/y contraction, even though the seasonally adjusted property sales dipped down more from Q3. Retail sales growth picked up to 8.3%y/y in Q4 (+4.1ppt from Q3). Export growth turned positive y/y in Nov-Dec, with export volume growth accelerating from Q3. Increased funding support underpinned infrastructure FAI, while recovering exports and improving profits led to slightly faster manufacturing investment growth. Industrial production growth picked up to 6%y/y (+1.8ppt from Q3) on low base. 2023 full-year GDP growth recorded 5.2% in real terms and 4.6% in nominal terms.

Property activities weakened further in December, while the y/y contraction narrowed in Q4. December property sales declined by another 12.7% (vs -10.3%y/y in November), while new starts fell by -10.4%y/y following a short-lived +4.9%y/y growth in November on low base. Property investment contracted more in December by - 12.5%y/y (vs -10.6% previously) partly due to base effect. After seasonal adjustment, property sales and new starts volume both dipped further from November, suggesting property activities are yet to bottom. The seasonally adjusted property new starts volume was largely flat in Q4 from Q3, while property sales dropped further. Developers’ source of funds remained in deep contraction of -14%y/y in December. Due to a lower base last year, Q4 new starts (-9.4%, vs -21.3%y/y in Q3) and property sales (-11.5%, vs - 12.3%y/y previously) both narrowed y/y decline, but real estate investment stayed in deep contraction of -11.4%y/y.

Base effect dragged down December retail sales but boosted the Q4 average growth. Overall retail sales growth softened to 7.4%y/y in December partly due to a fading low base, from 10.1%y/y in November. We estimate that the seasonally adjusted retail sales level (in real terms) edged up by 0.2%m/m in December, following +0.1%m/ m in November. Partly due to fading low base, goods retail sales growth decelerated to 4.8%y/y from 8%y/y. Auto sales growth decelerated notably from 14.7%y/y to 4%y/y and sales of communication appliances softened to 11%y/y from 16.8%y/y. Sales of property related products remained subdued, including construction materials (-7.5%y/ y) , furniture (2.3%y/y), and household appliances (turned negative of -0.1%y/y). On the other hand, catering sales growth picked up further to 30%y/y from 25.8% y/y due to a low base, while retail sales of services picked up further in December. Going-out related products also saw acceleration of sales growth, such as cosmetics and clothing. Despite the slowdown in December, average retail sales growth in Q4 improved to 8.4%y/y from 4.2%y/y in Q3, mainly due to low base. Our seasonally adjusted retail sales level (in real terms) improved in Q4 by 2% q/q (vs -1%q/q in Q3).

Stable labor market with lingering weakness. Overall unemployment rate edged up seasonally to 5.1% in December from 5% previously, same as in 2021 and lower than in 2022. The NBS also introduced a “new” youth unemployment rate (age 16-24) that excludes students in school/university, which could better monitor unemployment situation of youth "actually" in the job market. The new youth unemployment rate recorded 14.9% in December vs the old one that was 21.3% in June. There is no historical data available for now on a comparable basis. While the overall unemployment seems to be largely in line with seasonal patterns, elevated unemployment rate for young people and soft employment index within PMIs suggest that that the labor market remains weak.

Household saving rate rebounded above pre-Covid normal in Q4. Household survey showed real disposable income growth improved to 6.7%y/y in Q4 (vs 6.1% previously), while real consumption per capita decelerated to 9.5%y/y despite low base. The two-year average growth from 2021 decelerated for both real consumption and income in Q4, however. Data suggests that households became more cautious again in Q4 and excess saving (saving compared to pre-Covid level in 2019) rose again after a brief dip in Q3. Excess savings increased by RMB 500bn to an overall accumulated size of RMB 5.6trn (4.4% of GDP) in Q4, after a short-lived small decline in Q3. The 2022-2023 two-year average growth in real household income was 4.5% and real consumption per capita was 4.3%, still well below the pre-Covid average growth of 6-7%. Given lingering labor market weakness, soft income growth and subdued consumer confidence, we expect the release of excess saving in 2024 to be small and gradual.

Overall FAI growth improved in December and Q4. Infrastructure growth picked up more than expected to 10.7%y/y in December from 5.4% in November, partly due to deployment of special CGB issuance. Manufacturing investment growth edged up to 8.2%y/y from 7.1%y/y previously. Together, they offset the weakness from property investment, pushing overall FAI growth up to 4%y/y in December (vs 2.9% previously). The 4-year annual average growth from 2019 picked up pace for overall FAI in December, as well as for manufacturing and infrastructure FAI. Strength in December lifted the Q4 average growth of both manufacturing & infrastructure FAI, and the overall FAI (to 2.7%y/y in Q4 from 1.9% previously).

Exports recorded the 2nd consecutive positive y/y growth in December. Export growth accelerated to 2.3% in December from prior growth of 0.5%, as sequential export expansion offsets a higher statistical base. Real export volume growth accelerated further, sustaining the positive y/y growth since August. Mobile shipments turned to y/y contraction with sequential contraction in both export value and volume, a sign of faded boost from smart phone new product cycle. Auto exports accelerated, while consumer goods exports recorded narrower contraction. Meanwhile, imports returned to a mild y/y expansion by 0.2%, with stronger non-energy imports such as iron ore and copper. On the back of low base and upward tech cycle, overall Q4 export value declined by much less (-1.3%, vs -9.9% in Q3), while growth of export volume accelerated from 3%y/y in Q3 to 13%y/y in Q4, according to our estimate. See our comment.

Industrial production growth edged up in December and Q4 on a low base. December IP growth grew by 6.8%y/y, slightly faster than 6.6% in November. The official seasonally adjusted IP level also increased by 0.5%m/m in December, following 0.9%m/m increase in November. Value-added growth of auto sector held strong at 20%y/y and auto production at 25%y/y, both helped by low base. VA of communication appliance stayed solid at 9.6%y/y, as production of cell phone and electronic IC strengthened further. Coupled with improving trade activities of electronic ICs, these are reflections of tech cycle improvements. On the other hand, VA of ferrous metal slowed while production of crude steel and cement both dipped into y/y contraction, despite low base last year, with subdued property activities to blame. Overall speaking, low base, an upward tech cycle, improving infrastructure investment and retail sales together boosted Q4 average IP growth to 6%y/y from 4.2% in Q3, while the official sequential growth of IP improved to 1.8%q/q in Q4 from 1.1% q/q in Q3.

CPI and PPI both saw narrower y/y deflation. CPI narrowed contraction to -0.3%y/y in December from -0.5%y/y previously. Food price declined less, by -3.7%y/y (vs -4.2%y/ y before), while non-food prices edged up by 0.1ppt to 0.5%y/y. In particular, transportation disruption due to bad weather and seasonal holiday demand have pushed up prices of fresh vegetable and fruit. Travel demand also lifted price of travelling, entertainment and household services ahead of New Year holidays. Core CPI stabilized at 0.6%y/y. On a sequential basis, food price turned positive to +0.9%m/m, while non-food price declined less, by -0.1%m/m. Meanwhile, PPI narrowed deflation to -2.7%y/y in December from -3%y/y before, and continued to drop by -0.3%m/m on a sequential basis. The weakness was primarily from the decline in global oil prices and subdued industrial demand. We expect CPI to recover slightly to 0.8% in 2024 from 0.2% in 2023, while PPI to turn positive only after Q2 but stay in a small full-year deflation in 2024.

Credit growth edged up further. Headline new RMB loans recorded RMB 1.17trn, slightly weaker than market expectation. New M&L corporate loans (at RMB 861bn) was a key drag again, which extended its y/y weakness to -350bn y/y (vs -291bn y/y in November). Meanwhile, new M&L household loans printed RMB 146bn, slightly softer than a year ago, echoing weak property sales in recent months. Government bond issuance recorded a strong reading of RMB 928bn as expected. As a result, new TSF printed RMB 1.94trn in December, slightly lower than expected but still RMB 634bn higher than a year ago. Official and our adjusted TSF credit growth edged up by 0.1- 0.2ppt to 9.5-9.6%y/y. Credit impulse turned to a positive reading of 1.6% of GDP (vs 0% previously) while new credit flow softened to 28% of GDP from 30% on 3mma basis. We expect credit growth to stay robust at 9.5% in 2024. See our comment.

## Policy and Growth outlook

Higher base starts to weigh on y/y readings of high frequency data. Reopening led to a pick-up of mobility and other activities in early 2023 relative to 2022. As a result, higher bases are weighing on the y/y readings of high frequency data, which leads to a weakening of our headline UBS China Activity Index (y/y growth) in early January 2024 (see UBS China Activity Tracker). The overall activity level is somewhat mixed, with some areas showing more persistent improvement. Specifically, property sales in major cities declined more to -39%y/y in the first 15 days of January on a high base, from -11.5%y/y last December. Subway passenger turnover level stayed elevated, while its growth softened to 62%y/y in the first 15 days from 125%y/y in December. FTL traffic index growth held largely stable at 7.1%y/y in the first 15 days (vs 7.2% in December) while port cargo and container throughput improved further to 13.3%/14.5%y/y in the first 14 days from 5.5%/7.1%y/y before. Auto retail sales improved to 21% in the first week (from 9% in December), while auto wholesale decelerated to 14% from 24% before.

We now expect 2024 GDP growth to average 4.6%, up from 4.4% envisage earlier. The slightly stronger Q4 GDP growth outturn (5.2% vs 5.0% of our original forecast) and the revision of q/q growth in the previous few quarters mean that without any revision of our q/q sequential growth forecast, 2024 annual growth would be stronger at 4.6%. In 2024, we continue to expect property activities to stop falling on a seasonally adjusted sequential basis within the next 6 months, with the help of additional property policy easing, more progress in urban village renovation and social housing construction, and improvement of developer financing. We then expect housing starts and sales to improve modestly in H2 2024, leading to a smaller fall in property sales/starts/investment, by 5%/10%/5%, respectively, vs -8.5%/-20.4%/-9.6% in 2023. With property market stabilizing and property prices fall by no more than 10% on average, we expect consumer confidence to stabilize and negative wealth effect to be limited. Based on this, we see post-Covid consumption recovery continuing, though at a slower pace, along with the improvement of labor market and helped by a small release of excess savings. We do not expect infrastructure investment growth to accelerate despite more explicit fiscal support from central government and on-budget spending, and we forecast exports to improve modestly on the upswing of the global tech cycle despite the expected slowdown in US growth.

Modest policy support ahead. CEWC set a clearly supportive policy tone and emphasized policy coordination, while the magnitude of policy support is likely to be modest. While the government may set a growth target around 5% again for 2024 in the March NPC meetings, there has been no clear signs of significant policy stimulus in the immediate future, and the PBC kept MLF rate unchanged in January despite the continued deflationary pressure. We expect the March NPC to set a headline fiscal deficit (3.5-3.8%) potentially including issuance of another RMB 1 trillion in special treasuries. We also expect a larger special LG bond quota (RMB 4trn) and additional special refinancing LG bonds (RMB 2-3trn). We continue to expect the PBC to cut policy rates by 10-20bps and cut RRR cut 25-50bp, together with other liquidity facilities (especially the much focused new PSL). We also expect more property policy easing and some concrete progress in urban village renovation and social housing construction in 2024. See more in 2024 key themes and CEWC takeaways.

PSL in focus but with size and use not yet clear. The government has been emphasizing on “three major projects” (urban village renovation, social housing, and normal-disaster dual-use infrastructure investment), with explicit support from PBC’s various facilities. Recently, PBC announced to grant new PSL quota of RMB 500bn, of which RMB 350bn has been injected in December. It is not clear how much more PSL will be issued and how soon. Our baseline sees another batch of RMB 300-500bn to be granted in late 2024. The size would be larger than RMB 698bn in 2015 but smaller than RMB 971bn in 2016 (vs RMB 749bn per annum during 2015-18). It is also not clear how much of the low-cost funding underpinned by PSL can be deployed by local governments and related entities to purchase existing commodity homes or subsidize households as China did with the shanty-town projects, which would help to lower housing inventory and provide additional cash flow to developers. But such a use faces criticism of high cost and potential moral hazard. Meanwhile some cities are building new social housing projects, which can boost real estate investment but may generate more housing supply, potentially worsening the demand-supply balance. We are looking for clarity on the size and use of PSL funding in the months ahead, but expect much smaller impact on the housing market than that of shanty town renovation in 2015-18.
