# US Inflation Monthly: Was the January CPI signal or noise?

## January inflation has been strong, but it does not change the downward trend

 Last week's CPI and PPI data were quite elevated, but January core CPI and PCE increases appear to be lower than 12 months ago. As a result 12-month inflation continues to decline and we expect further slowing ahead, even if that pace of that slowing now appears more gradual.

 The big surprise in January was in services — particularly core non-rent services. Core non-rent services generally do not surge in January (even on a not-seasonallyadjusted basis) making the January CPI pickup quite different from a usual seasonal acceleration. It also makes the path of inflation over the next couple months more uncertain.

 Owners' equivalent rent (OER) also surprisingly accelerated in January. Looking at new lease rents, the unusually large gap between OER and tenants' rent in January, and 12-month change for OER panels all suggest a notable slowing in OER over the next few months.

## Strong February monthly likely, but gradually slowing inflation continues

 We project core PCE prices to rise 42bp in the January data released at the end of next week. The strong January increase keeps 12-month core PCE inflation at 2.9% (while inflation in December gets revised up from 2.9% to 3.0%). The 6- month annualized change in core PCE prices is projected to move up to 2.55%.

 We currently project the headline CPI to rise 46bp in February (seasonally adjusted; 66bp not seasonally adjusted) with core CPI prices again rising 39bp. Rising gasoline prices and a smaller used car price decline that offsets a slowdown in services are the main drivers for the strength in February. We will update these projections as additional data becomes available.

 A detailed forecast spreadsheet is available on our website.

## Core PCE inflation forecast higher, but still below consensus at 1.9% for 2024

 We continue to expect that moderating demand, easing supply conditions, slowing rents, and a softening labor market will push down inflation over much of the next year.

 However, with the strength of the January price data we have raised our forecast. We project 12-month core PCE inflation to edge down to 2.8% in February and 2.7% in March and continue to fall roughly 10bp per month through June as large base effects from early last year fall out of the 12-month change.

 Our PCE inflation forecast for this year remains below consensus, with our core PCE inflation projection 0.2pp to 0.5pp below other consensus measures. In contrast, our CPI and core CPI inflation projections are a touch above consensus (and swaps) as we expect the wedge between CPI and PCE inflation to remain elevated this year.

## Was the January CPI signal or noise?

The first week or two following the publication of last month's US Inflation Monthly continued to show that inflation was slowing rapidly: PCE prices for December increased moderately, at 17bp — right at a 2% annualized pace — with 12-month core PCE inflation falling to 2.9%. Then the ECI for private sector workers, probably the best measure for labor cost pressures, showed a smaller than expected 3.5% annualized pace over the 3 months between September and December — a pace that if continued is probably close the level consistent with PCE price inflation at 2.0%. Additionally, the CPI seasonal adjustment revisions did not notably alter the downward trajectory of inflation this year, and even revised down owners' equivalent rent a bit in the fourth quarter of last year.

However, the tone changed as the December data ended and the January data rolled in. First, the employment report showed average hourly earnings increasing 55bp in January. Then the January CPI report was the strongest monthly core CPI increase (+39bp) since April (seasonally-adjusted). Finally, adding the PPI and import prices to the CPI suggests core PCE prices rose about 42bp in January.

There have been a lot of questions from clients as to what drove the large upside surprise in the January data and how persistent it will be. Much of this is unclear, but we note that the strength in the January price data may have been caused by residual seasonality

— the tendency for initially released January CPI data to be strong even on a seasonallyadjusted basis — and this was a risk that we noted last month. Nonetheless, the January price data this year was still below the January price data from last year — by 4bp for the core CPI seasonally-adjusted (7bp not-seasonally adjusted) and by 9bp for core PCE prices (assuming our translation proves correct). Carry that monthly difference across 12 months and that is a notable slowing in inflation.

That said, the January data has been above our expectations and as a result we have boosted our February CPI projection and our forecast for the year as a whole. For core PCE inflation our forecast for 2024 Q4/Q4 is now at 1.9%, up 0.1pp from last month. The strength of the data over the past two weeks does not change the overall downward trajectory of inflation, but it does provide some uncertainty on how quick that slowdown will be.

These core non-rent services tend to not have a big January seasonal pickup and generally are among the more persistent components. ## Our view on the slowing in OER is driven by:

a. The OER-rent gap in January: While OER accelerated in the January CPI, tenants' rent has decelerated in recent months from a 47bp increase in October to 44bp in November to 39bp in December and 36bp in January. The 20bp gap between OER and tenants' rent in January is one of the largest gaps in recent years. (Figure 6) Such gaps tend to be reversed quickly. For example, a simple two variable vector error correction model with six lags of OER and tenants' rent suggests OER slows from 56bp in January to 40bp February while the tenants' rent picks up from 36bp in January to 42bp in February.

b. The new lease models: New lease rents have been running at or below their prepandemic pace since the autumn of 2022 (Figure 7 and Figure 8). During this time the gap in levels that had opened up between new lease rents and CPI rents during 2021 and the first 9 months of 2022 has closed considerably. When averaging across new lease rent measures the gap appears to be gone. c. Panel effects: Each monthly change in the CPI OER series is constructed as the sixmonth change from one of six different panels. (For more on OER methodology see the CPI rent factsheet.) This 12-month change is useful since 12-month leases are by far the most common and we find the downward and upward path using this constructed measure has been relatively smooth. (Figure 11) Continuing the downward trajectory at roughly the same pace seen over the past year suggests a much stronger slowing in the monthly OER increase across the next few CPI releases.

3. 2% core inflation does not mean all components need to have inflation close to 2%.

From 1995 until mid-2008 core PCE inflation averaged just below 2% (at 1.9%). However, core services inflation remained well above 2% for most of that time. (Figure 16) Indeed, core non-rent services inflation averaged around 2.8% — just barely below the 2.9% pace of those services in the 6 months through December (though that pace will jump up when the January data is brought in).

There often is a significant gap between core goods inflation and core services inflation. (Figure 13) Since 1959 the gap in 12-month inflation rates between core PCE goods and services has averaged 2.4 percentage points with a median gap 2.6 percentage points.

Recently core goods prices have started to resume their long-run pre-pandemic trend decline.(Figure 14) As a result to have PCE or core PCE inflation near 2% likely means that services inflation needs to be closer to 3% than to 2%.

## Near-term inflation forecast January PCE inflation

January headline PCE prices, released late next week (Thursday, February 29), are projected to have increased 35bp with core PCE prices rising 42bp. Over the past 36 months one standard error around these estimates has been 4bp (with a median absolute error of 2.5bp). With these increases the 12-month change in core PCE prices is projected to hold at 2.9% in January — the smallest since March 2021 and roughly ¾ of the way back to the FOMC's 2.0% inflation targe from its 5.6% peak in February 2022.

## Upcoming CPI prints

We are currently forecasting a 46bp increase in the headline CPI in February (seasonallyadjusted; 66bp not seasonally adjusted) with 12-month inflation rising from 3.1% to 3.2%. Retail gasoline prices appear set for a strong increase after four months of declines and core CPI prices are projected to post another considerable increase. Specifically we forecast the core CPI to once again increase 39bp in January as a slowdown in OER and prices for other services is offset by much more modest decline in used car prices. Even with another strong increase in February 12-month core CPI inflation is forecast to edge down to 3.8% — it slowest pace since May 2021.

We currently forecast that in January core CPI inflation will dip below 4% for the first time in 2 ½ years

We feel the uncertainty surrounding the February projection is high, but skewed solidly to the downside with, at this point, increases anywhere from 20bp to 45bp reasonable. As additional data becomes available over the next few weeks we will refine this projection.

Looking further ahead than next month's print, over the next six months we forecast published seasonally-adjusted core CPI increases will begin to look like more like prepandemic monthly increases as rents slow, used car prices decline, and ebbing demand and easing supply conditions lead to a slackening in the pace monthly increases. ## Drivers of consumer inflation Inflation expectations

Inflation expectations have dropped over the past month, in some cases long-run inflation expectations are down at levels they were when core PCE inflation average 2% (University of Michigan survey of consumer, Atlanta Fed survey of businesses). ## Box: Rules of thumb for impacts of key economic shocks

We usually rely on estimated time-series models to bring in the impact of various factors on our forecast. However, rules of thumb are often useful to give a rough idea of how important a shock is likely to be. Below are a few key rules of thumb that we use:

 1 percentage point increase in the unemployment rate pushes down core inflation by 8bp.

 Permanent \$10 per barrel level shift in the price of Brent crude pushes up the level of CPI by 40bp and core CPI by 4bp.

 A one percent decline in the dollar increases consumer import prices by 0.15 percent.

 A one percent increase in import prices results in a 0.12 percent increase in core consumer prices.

 A 1% increase in CRB spot food index equals about 8bp in prices of food at home with about 2/3rds in the first 6 months and 1/3rd in the six months after that. (CPI food at home is 8% of the CPI basket.)

## Price inflation projection through 2026

We continue to project core PCE inflation will slow over the next year as supply and demand come more into alignment for goods and the considerable easing in market rent increases pass into CPI and PCE rent measures. The rise in unemployment that results from a recession starting in the middle of this year adds additional downward pressure to inflation, particularly for the non-rent core services components.

The cumulative core PCE change in recent months through December looks to be about 20bp higher than forecast in last month's US Inflation Monthly, however, that surprise is split between two calendar years and we have elected to offset a bit of that upward push this year. As a result our Q4/Q4 for 2023 by a few basis points higher than last month ( (Figure 52)) while our projection for this year is up 10bp, to 1.9%.

Given the composition of the surprise in the January CPI we are showing a much bigger upward revision to the core CPI this year (42bp) than we are showing to core PCE prices (10bp).

We project core PCE prices will increase 1.9% this year and 2.1% in 2025E

While the 12-month change in core PCE prices through January is 20bp higher than last month, we have raised our q4/q4 projection for this year by only 10bp.

## Components of core PCE inflation

Figure 55 through Figure 59 show 3- and 12-month changes for the main components of our core PCE projection along with our forecasts and model projections. The models are estimated taking 2023Q4 as data and forecasted starting 2024Q1.

1. Core goods prices (25% of core PCE, Figure 55) have slowed considerably over the past year and are projected to continue to slow over the next handful of months as demand weakens and the effects of easing supply constraints continue to ripple through the production and distribution network (particularly for vehicles). Goods price deflation is projected to hit its lowest point around the middle of the year.

Goods price inflation should slow as demand eases and the effects of looser supply conditions ripple through the system.

Our Phillips curve model for the category has adjusted down considerably over the past few quarters and now the model is roughly in line with our projection next year.

2. We forecast monthly housing services increases (essentially owners' equivalent rent plus tenants' rent, 20% of core PCE, Figure 56) will slow over the next few quarters as the easing in rents for new leases filters into CPI/PCE housing rent inflation.

Our model for housing services inflation based on fundamental rent market factors (the unemployment rate, rental vacancy rate, and housing affordability) shows a roughly similar slowing to our baseline forecast.

The 12-month change in shelter rent inflation is roughly at its peak.

3. Price inflation for PCE health services (19% of core PCE, Figure 57) is projected to run a little above the pace over the past year as the price reductions from the ending of Medicare Covid reimbursement rate increases is past. However, the upward push is only moderate as wage growth in health care has been slowing for the past year and Medicare adjustment for wage increases (which first appeared in the October PCE prices) was estimated by the Center for Medicare and Medicaid Studies to be a touch lower than last year.

4. PCE food services inflation (6% of core PCE, Figure 58Fod servic) has been strong through most of the pandemic. We have seen slowing in recent months and expect further easing of the pace of increases with moderating wage increases.

5. Inflation for other core PCE services (31% of core PCE, Figure 59) has slowed in recent months and we expect the pace will ebb further as wage growth slowly eases.

## Key risks to our modal forecast

We view our projection as the most likely (modal) scenario and see risks on both sides of the inflation forecast with the overall uncertainty surrounding the inflation projection remaining larger than usual over the past 20 to 30 years (though down from a year or two ago). Over the next few months we see the risks as two-sided around our forecast with a downward skew in February.

We think that inflation risks are skewed to the upside through the end of the year.

## Upside risks

1. Stronger-than-expected residual seasonality: Price increases are often strongest early in the year and it is possible that we might not have put enough in for the pick up continuing into February.

2. Continued tight labor market passing into services prices: The labor market remains tight. If the economy does not go into the recession that we have written into our baseline forecast, and wage growth remains elevated, it is likely that nonrent services inflation will be higher than we have forecast over the next year. Our rough estimate based on a fairly steep Phillips curve, is that the labor market continuing at close to its present level of tightness could add 50bp to our core PCE inflation projection in 2024.

3. Rents re-accelerating: While we are not seeing a lot of strength in new tenants rents, the solid housing market and rising existing home prices could start to push up new tenants' rents and, eventually, CPI rents.

## Downside risks

1. A slowing economy: The recession that we have forecast starting this summer could be deeper and have a greater slowing effect on inflation than we have anticipated. In particular, in recessions prices for financial services, nonprofit services, and travel-related services generally fall quite notably.

2. Declining market rents and a faster slowing in CPI rents: Our forecast has CPI rents returning to just above their pre-pandemic pace in the first half of this year, but with market rents running below their pre-pandemic pace late last year and early this year according to some indexes (along with the the huge drop in the BLS new tenant rent index in Q4), it is possible that CPI and PCE rents also will slow to below their pre-pandemic pace. A note from the SF Fed suggests CPI housing rents slowing to a 0% 12-month change by the middle of this year. If this turns out to be correct it would lead to rents contributing about 1.2pp less to headline CPI inflation, and 60bp less to core PCE inflation, this year than in our baseline.

## Comparison to the forecast models

Our forecast models have revised downward notably since peaking in the second half of 2022, though they revised up slightly this month. The models expect core PCE inflation between 2.5% and 2.7% this year (Q4/Q4). Our forecast is lower.

As with most Phillips curve style models they were unable to forecast the inflation as it surged upward and, as expected, they have not been a good guide as inflation has fallen. In particular they do not include an effect for changes in the mix of demand and the supply constraints that hampered efforts to satisfy this changing mix. We believe the combined effects of the changing demand mix and supply issues were far more important in driving the inflation surge over the past 3 years than were the standard Phillips curve drivers of aggregate resource slack.

Our Phillips curve models have performed poorly during thus inflation experience..

## Incoming data and forecast for hourly wages

Average hourly earnings (AHE) surged 55bp in January — its largest monthly increase in almost two years. The reading may have been pushed up by weather that limited weekly hours. (Average hourly earnings is not constructed from individual hourly wages, but instead from aggregate payrolls (in dollars) divided by aggregate hours.) If limited hours did support AHE in January then it is likely that February AHE growth will be weak. Nonetheless, AHE growth has been strong for a few months: the 3-month annualized change has risen to 5.6% (Figure 71), and the 12-month change has also reversed after gradually declining for much of the past two years (Figure 74)

AHE growth has picked up recently

On the other hand, the fourth quarter increases in both the ECI and compensation per hour in the nonfarm business sector were fairly moderate at around 3¾%. (Figure 73) The ECI is probably the best measure of wage costs relevant for businesses and the continued downtrend in that measure is encouraging for lower price inflation. (Figure 75) ECI increases probably need to hold around 3½% to be consistent with the FOMC's 2.0% PCE price inflation target in a sustainable manner.

Overall we expect wage growth to gradually trend down over much of next year as the labor market weakens.

Wage growth will likely continue to slow over the next year
