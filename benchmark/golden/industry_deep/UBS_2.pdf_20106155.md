# European Economic Perspectives Sweden: Outlook for 2024

## A summary of our views on growth, inflation and monetary policy

In this note, we lay out our expectations for growth, the labour market, inflation and monetary policy for Sweden. We also highlight key charts on the topics.

## Growth: Sweden in recession

Sweden has been in a technical recession since Q2 2023, and GDP has declined by a cumulative 1.1% compared with Q1 2023 - a more pronounced slowdown than in the eurozone. The GDP decline has been led by a contraction in the interest rate sensitive parts of the economy, i.e. household consumption and construction investment; while net exports, business investment and government spending have contributed positively to growth. Forward-looking indicators point to weak growth continuing for some time. Hence, we expect the recession to continue until Q1 2024 (with some upside risks in the near term), followed by moderately positive growth rates through the remainder of 2024. In 2024, rising real incomes and a robust labour market should lead to a recovery in household consumption. A large part of the pass-through of rate hikes likely lies behind, and the rate cuts we expect from the Riksbank in 2024 are likely to benefit the economy fairly quickly as of late 2024 and 2025. We expect GDP growth of -0.4% in 2023, -0.3% in 2024, 2.1% in 2025 and 2.8% in 2026.

## Labour market: Resilient, but with some signs of emerging weakness

Like in other advanced economies, the Swedish labour market has been relatively resilient, despite the recession. One of the key reasons is likely to be the growing shortages of labour due to the demographic transition, which is prompting corporates to hoard labour. However, there are signs that the labour market has started to weaken, and we expect the unemployment rate to pick up from 7.9% currently to a peak at 8.5% in Q3 2024.

## Inflation: We expect CPIF inflation to reach close to 2% by H2 2024

CPIF inflation has declined from its peak of 10.2% y/y in December 2022 to 2.3% in December 2023, with energy the biggest driver of the fall. While core goods inflation has declined, the pace of the decline has been slower in Sweden than in the eurozone, probably due to the depreciation of the Swedish krona. Services inflation remains at elevated levels. Barring a temporary rise in January 2024, we expect CPIF inflation to continue to fall and reach close to 2% by H2 2024. Overall we expect average CPIF inflation to decline from 6.0% in 2023 to 2.5% in 2024, 1.8% in 2025 and 2.0% in 2026. We expect CPIF ex energy inflation to decline from 7.5% to 3.3% in 2024 and 2.2% in both 2025 and 2026. The weakness in growth (driven by tight monetary policy) should continue to contribute to the fall in inflation, along with fading supply shocks. According to surveys, companies have adjusted their price plans downwards significantly. Additionally, wage growth in Sweden is at moderate levels (3.8%), inflation expectations remain well anchored (at close to 2%) and the krona has appreciated recently, though it is still c.2.5% weaker than at the start of 2022. The two key uncertainties for the Swedish inflation outlook are the movements in the exchange rate and corporate pricing plans.

## Monetary policy: No further hikes, first cut in May 2024

With rates at 4.0%, we expect no further rate hikes from the Riksbank and anticipate the first rate cut in May 2024 (-25bps) on the back of declining inflation and a weak economy. But the still-weak krona increases the risk of a later cut in June. In our view, the cut in May is likely to be followed by a second cut in June and then a cut every quarter until end-2025, such that the policy rate reaches 2.0% by end-2025; i.e. 100 bps of cuts each in 2024 and 2025.

## Growth outlook

Sweden has experienced a technical recession since Q2 2023, and GDP has declined by a cumulative 1.1% compared with Q1 2023 (Figure 2); a more pronounced slowdown compared to other eurozone economies. The GDP decline has been led by a contraction in the interest rate sensitive parts of the economy, i.e. household consumption and construction investment; both of which have weakened sharply in Sweden compared with the eurozone (Figure 3). At the same time, net exports, business investment and government spending contributed positively to Swedish growth, with exports potentially benefiting from the weak krona.

Transmission of high rates has been rapid and powerful in Sweden

Household consumption (down 4% since end-2021) has been weighed down by the high interest rate sensitivity of the Swedish economy and the weakness in real incomes. Like in other countries, household consumption has been rotating away from goods towards services, and goods consumption has led the decline in household consumption. The transmission of high interest rates has been relatively rapid and powerful, given the high share of variable rate mortgages and mortgages with short fixation periods (Figure 6) along with a high share of mortgage debt (mortgage debt is 65% of GDP). Around 80% of the total mortgage stock has a remaining fixation period of one year or less, and this share has been rising since 2022.

The 400bps rise in interest rates since April 2022 has led to a c.300bps increase in rates on new mortgages and a c.240bps increase in rates on the stock of outstanding mortgages (i.e. 60% of pass-through for mortgage stock). Corporate loans also have short fixation periods (90% of corporate loans have original maturity of one year or less). For corporate loans, there has been a corresponding c.390bps rise in rates on new loans and a c.330bps rise in rates on outstanding loans. Swedish households have accumulated excess savings during the pandemic, though less than other eurozone economies (Figure 8). A part of these savings were used in mid-2022 to support consumption, but overall the stock of excess savings in Sweden is more or less unchanged over the past few quarters, which implies that consumers have been less willing to use savings to beef up spending at a time of a squeeze in real incomes.

Forward-looking indicators point to weak growth continuing for some time

Forward-looking indicators point to weak growth continuing for some time. The Economic Tendency Survey dropped again marginally in December to 84.3 (-0.6 points), the lowest level since mid-2020. Business confidence fell in manufacturing to 95.1 (-4.0 points), and in construction to 89.9 (-1.4 points). Confidence in the services sector remains the most muted (85.3). Confidence in retail trade has been weak since mid-2022, due to the decline in goods consumption, but in recent months there has been an improvement to 92.1. Consumer confidence has also recovered from its lows in October 2022 but remains at low levels (at 74.5). According to the Riksbank's latest business survey, economic activity is expected to continue to be weak over the next six months. PMIs have seen a small improvement to 49.7 in December (+1.1 pts), led by a pickup in services. But the average PMI in Q4 is close to the levels seen in Q3. Having said that, monthly GDP increased in October and November (by 1.0% m/m and 0.2% m/m respectively) thanks to strong exports, which points to upside risks to our Q4 GDP growth forecast of -0.3% q/q. (It should be noted however that the monthly GDP indicator is based on limited information and is subject to revisions.)

We expect the recession in Sweden to continue until Q1 2024 (with some upside risks in the near term), followed by moderately positive growth rates through the remainder of 2024. In 2024, rising real incomes and a robust labour market should lead to a recovery in household consumption. Moreover, given the high rate sensitivity of the Swedish economy, a large part of the pass-through of rate hikes likely lies behind and a large share of households (around two-thirds) have renegotiated their mortgages at higher rates. The impact of rate hikes is likely to be less prolonged in Sweden than in countries with slower pass-through and interest costs for households and companies should peak soon (under our base case that the policy rate has peaked). ## Labour market

Like in other advanced economies, the Swedish labour market has been relatively resilient, despite the recession, though it is showing some signs of slowing down. Employment growth has slowed down to 1.1% y/y in Q3 2023, but is still above the long run average of 0.9%, and the employment rate is close to a record high of 69.5%. The starting point is strong because the employment intensive services sector recovered robustly after the pandemic and demand for labour is high in sectors such as health and social care, due to demographic reasons. Companies are choosing to hoard labour, given the labour shortages during the post-pandemic recovery period, in part also due to the demographic transition. The Economic Tendency Survey has a labour hoarding indicator (the proportion of companies choosing to have more staff than required by output over the short term), which has been at a relatively high level over the past couple of years, though it has started to soften now. The latest Riksbank business survey highlighted that some companies have begun to apply a more flexible approach in terms of managing their staff such as reducing the hours worked (for example, by using hours worked in time banks - a mechanism that allows workers to earn time for each completed working week, which can be taken out in paid leave). Labour hoarding is evident by the fact that while employment is c.4% higher than the pre-pandemic period, hours worked per employee have declined by c.2.5%.

Despite the recession, the labour market has been relatively resilient due to labour hoarding

Some firms are also retaining staff due to the expectation that the economic downturn is unlikely to be particularly deep or long-lasting. The Riksbank has highlighted that the demand for labour is likely high because real wages have fallen and made labour less costly relative to capital. Moreover, companies have been able to raise prices while wage increases have been relatively restrained, which implies firms have managed to maintain profitability and thus are better equipped to retain labour.

However, there are some signs that the labour market has started to weaken. The unemployment rate has picked up by 0.6pp since the beginning of 2023 to 7.9% in November 2023. The number of vacancies has been declining in Sweden since the beginning of 2023. Survey indicators point to a further pickup in the unemployment rate, and employment expectations from the Economic Tendency Survey point to workforce cutbacks over the past three months (-4.0 in November). Recruitment plans are particularly weak in the retail trade and construction sectors. Moreover, evidence from the Riksbank business survey suggests that worker shortages have been easing since last year due to a fall in demand. We expect the unemployment rate to pick up further and peak at 8.5% in Q3 2024. So while we expect the labour market to continue to soften, we don't expect a marked deterioration. This is a key risk to our forecast of a growth recovery in 2024. If companies lay off workers more than we expect, then growth and consumption are likely to be weaker, exacerbated by a potential increase in households' precautionary savings.

CPIF inflation (CPI index with a fixed interest rate<sup>(1)</sup> ) has declined from its peak of 10.2% y/y in December 2022 to 2.3% in December 2023, while CPIF ex energy inflation has declined at a slower pace from its peak of 9.3% in February 2023 to 5.3% in December 2023. In 2022, CPIF inflation averaged 7.7%, within which energy made the largest contribution (2.1pp) followed by services inflation (1.9pp). Core goods and food

inflation contributed 1.7pp each. During 2023, CPIF inflation fell to 6.0% on average, but the contribution of services inflation rose (to 3.2pp), making it the largest driver of inflation. The contribution of food and core goods inflation also rose to 1.9pp while energy made a negative contribution to inflation in 2023 (-1.1pp). Overall energy was the biggest driver of the fall in inflation in 2023 (energy inflation declined from 17.7% in January 2023 to -26% in December 2023) as energy prices fell. Food inflation slowed significantly as well (from 16.6% in January to 5.5% in December 2023). Compared to the eurozone, core goods inflation in Sweden rose to higher levels and the decline (from 8.3% in January 2023 to 4.0% in December 2023) has been slower (Figure 20). This could be explained by the depreciation of the Swedish krona, which fell c.12.4% from late 2021 (in trade-weighted terms) to its trough in August 2023 and has sustained some upward pressure on imported goods prices. The Riksbank has assessed that in the current high inflation environment, the pass-through of the weak krona to prices has likely been stronger and quicker than in past episodes. Services inflation in Sweden remains at elevated levels (6.4% in December 2023, similar to the beginning of the year, though lower than its peak of 8.1% in July 2023).

Barring a temporary rise in January 2024 (when we expect CPIF inflation to rise to 3.2% as energy base effects wear off), we expect CPIF inflation to continue to fall and reach close to 2% by H2 2024. We expect CPIF ex energy inflation to continue to decline and reach close to 2.5% at the end of 2024. The weakness in growth driven by tighter monetary policy should continue to contribute to the fall in inflation, along with fading supply shocks. According to surveys, companies have adjusted their price plans downwards significantly. The Economic

Tendency Survey shows that the share of companies planning to raise prices has declined, though it is still at somewhat high levels for retail trade (Figure 21). Evidence from the Riksbank's business survey shows a more marked decline in pricing plans among the household-related companies (i.e. companies in retail trade and those that mainly sell services to households) over the coming year.

Additionally, wage growth in Sweden has picked up in the past few months from 2.8% at the beginning of 2023 to 3.8% in October 2023, but remains at moderate levels compared to other advanced economies. In spring 2023, an industrial agreement was signed which stretches over two years and encompasses moderate wage increases of 4.1% in the first year and 3.3% in the second year. These wage agreements are likely to apply until the beginning of 2025, which implies wage growth in 2024 and 2025 will be higher than normal for the next couple of years, but remain relatively contained. We expect wage growth to peak in the next few months. Moreover, inflation expectations remain well anchored (at 1.8% at 2-year and 2.1% at 5-year horizon) and the krona has appreciated recently, though it is still c.2.5% weaker than at the start of 2022. The two key uncertainties for the inflation outlook in Sweden are the movements in the exchange rate as well as corporate pricing plans. If the krona were to weaken further and/or if the pass-through of the past weakness of krona to inflation were to be larger than anticipated, then the risk that goods prices remain elevated and inflation is slower to return to target will rise.

## Riksbank

In its November meeting, the Riksbank kept the policy rate unchanged at 4.0%, surprising expectations of a hike to 4.25%. While it acknowledged that inflation is still high, it noted that inflation has fallen (roughly in line with its expectations) and pressures have eased. At the same time, GDP growth and the labour market are weakening. The Riksbank's forward guidance highlighted the need for policy to remain contractionary and even kept the door open for further hikes: "The Executive Board assesses that monetary policy needs to be contractionary and is prepared to raise the policy rate further if inflation prospects deteriorate. The forecast for policy rate is that it may be raised further at the start of next year." But since then, comments by the Executive Board members (see Jansson here and Breman here) have highlighted that rates have now probably peaked. Given we expect inflation to fall further (barring in January) and GDP growth to stay weak in the coming months, we expect no further hikes.

We expect no further rate hikes from the Riksbank

We expect the first cut in May 2024 (-25bps) on the back of declining inflation and a weak economy. We expect CPIF inflation to continue to fall and reach close to 2% by H2 2024. The Riksbank's forecast shows CPIF inflation at 1.7% in 2025 and 2.0% in Q4 2026. But two factors point to the risk of a later cut in June. First, the Riksbank's rate path forecast envisages unchanged rates through 2024 (with a rate forecast of 4.10% in Q4 2024 and 3.87% in Q4 2025). The guidance notes "Monetary policy needs to be contractionary for a relatively long period of time for inflation to fall back and stabilise close to the target of 2%." Second, the still-weak krona increases the risk that goods prices remain elevated and inflation is slower to return to target. If the krona were to weaken further and/or if the pass-through of past krona weakness to inflation were to be larger than anticipated, then the Riksbank could cut rates later. In our view, the cut in May is likely to be followed by a second cut in June and then a cut every quarter until end-2025, such that policy rate reaches 2.0% by end 2025; i.e. 100bps of cuts each in 2024 and 2025. The markets currently price an 11.5bps cut for March, 24.1bps of cuts by May, 55.8bps by June and a total 151.2bps of cuts in 2024.
