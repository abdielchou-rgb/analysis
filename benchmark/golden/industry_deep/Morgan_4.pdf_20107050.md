# China: TSF growth moderated to 9.0% in February

Interpreting the PBOC’s monetary policy operations

• Credit growth slowed in February. TSF growth printed at 9.0% (vs. 9.5% in January) and loan growth was 10.1% (vs. 10.4% in January). The slowdown is mainly due to notable front-loading of credit extension in 1Q23 and back-to-normal pace this year as instructed by the PBOC.

Medium and long-term corporate loans accounted for 89% of new loan increase in February. For non-loan component, government bond rose 601 billion and accounted for 38.5% of TSF increase. By contrast, household loans declined by 591 billion yuan and shadow credit declined by 329 billion yuan.

The PBOC kept 1-year MLF rate unchanged at 2.5%, with net liquidity withdrawal for the first time since November 2022. Today’s rate decision is affected by inflation pick-up in February and uncertainty in Fed rate cut outlook. We maintain our forecast of a next rate cut in 2Q.

PBOC’s monetary policy outlook in 2024: We expect a continuation in interest rate policy and credit policy, but an expansion in structural monetary policy instruments.

China<sup>’</sup>s February TSF flow fell to 1,558 billion yuan (consensus: 2,340bn; JPM: 2,160bn) from a record high of 6,502 billion yuan in January. The growth rate of outstanding TSF moderated to 9.0%oya (vs. 9.5% in January). Meanwhile, new loan increase registered at 1,450 billion yuan in February (consensus: 1,550bn; JPM: 1,380bn), with loan growth moderating to 10.1%oya (vs. 10.4% in January).

In the breakdown, TSF growth in February was led by medium and long-term (MLT) corporate loans and government bond. MLT corporate loans increased by 1,290bn yuan, accounting for 89% of new loan increases in February. By contrast, household loans declined by 591 billion yuan (vs. +980bn in January), and shortterm loans and bill financing to corporates rose modestly by 253bn yuan. Among the non-loan component, government bonds rose 601bn yuan (accounting for 38.5% of TSF increase in February), followed by corporate bond issuance (+176bn), while shadow credit (trust loan, entrust loan and bank acceptance) contracted 329bn yuan (vs. +601bn in January).

Regarding monetary aggregates, M2 money supply growth stayed unchanged at 8.7%, translating to a sequential gain of 1.0%m/m sa (vs. 0.6% in January). Besides, M1 growth fell from 5.9%oya in January to 1.2%oya in February, implying the gap between M2 and M1 growth widened to 7.5%pts (vs. 2.8%pts in January and 8.4%pts in December). RMB deposits growth decelerated further to 8.4%oya (vs. 9.2%oya in January), and the ratio of annual increase in new loans to the increase in new deposits moved up to 99.6% in February (vs. 93.5% in January). Our estimate of credit impulse (the gap between TSF growth and nominal GDP growth) fell to 5.0%pts (vs. 5.4%pts in January).

## What does the February credit report tell us?

The moderation in TSF growth in February is not surprising. The big fall in TSF (flow) between January and February is due to LNY seasonality, as LNY falls in February this year (vs. in January in 2023), leading to a big distortion in the number of working days. The more important reason for credit deceleration in February is due to significant front-loading in credit extension in 1Q23. TSF increase in 1Q23 accounted for 41.9% of full-year TSF increase in 2023, while in normal years 1Q credit extension usually accounts for about one third of the full-year quota. The PBOC has instructed banks to return to a normal pace of credit extension this year. This is what has happened in Jan-Feb: using our full-year forecast of 9.5% TSF growth, combined Jan-Feb TSF increase accounts for 21.2% of the full-year quota (vs. 26.5% share in Jan-Feb 2023).

The breakdown of February credit suggests that credit support is focused on manufacturing and government-supported areas, led by MLT corporate loans and government bond. Contraction in household loans (a decline of 487bn yuan in short-term loans and a decline of 104bn in MLT loan to households) pointed at continuous weakness in the housing market and cautiousness in household borrowing. Interestingly, household deposits increased by 5.73 trillion yuan in the first two months of this year, smaller than the 6.99 trillion increase in the first two months of 2023. The moderation in household deposit increase and less spending in home purchase restriction may be used to support consumption or investment in other financial assets.

## The PBOC kept one-year MLF rate unchanged at 2.5%

The PBOC maintained the one-year MLF rate unchanged at 2.5%, in line with market expectations. The amount of MLF liquidity injection is 387 billion yuan, smaller than expired MLF of 481 billion yuan. This is the first month of net liquidity withdrawal in MLF operation since November 2022, after the cumulative 2,892 billion yuan net liquidity injection via MLF in the past 15 months.

We had penciled in a rate cut of 10bps in March, but have postponed into 2Q for two reasons. First, February CPI inflation rose to 0.7%oya (vs. -0.8%oya in January) on a 0.8%m/m sa increase, and core CPI inflation jumped to 1.2%oya (vs. 0.4%oya in January, the highest reading in the past two years). Although the inflation pick-up is largely driven by LNY seasonality, we think it is comforting for the PBOC as it has continued to downplay the deflation risk concern.

Second, the upside surprise in US inflation reports in February raised uncertainty on the Fed rate cut outlook. Interest rate differentials between 10-year UST and CGB yields have widened again since the beginning of this year, and CNY has continued to face depreciation pressure (USD/CNY exchange rate has remained above daily fixing). Like many other central banks, the PBOC seems to be waiting for clarity on the Fed<sup>’</sup>s rate outlook.

In addition, the pace and magnitude of the PBOC<sup>’</sup>s rate cuts also depend on the ability of banks to lower the liability cost (most importantly the deposit rate), as banks have been facing the pressure of NIM compression. Further rate cuts will intensify the NIM pressure and hurt bank profits. The risk is that banks will not be able to maintain stable loan growth due to limited capacity to increase equity capital (as required under the Basel regulation), which is not consistent with the monetary policy objective.

## Monetary policy operations will rely more on structural instruments

The PBOC has three pillars in its monetary policy operations: the interest rate policy, credit policy, and structural monetary policy instruments. Overall, we expect interest rate policy and credit policy will maintain similar policy stance as observed in 2023, but the PBOC will expand the usage of structural monetary policy instruments this year.

On interest rate policy, we expect the PBOC will continue to cut policy rates, but it will continue to adopt the so-called “attenuation approach<sup>”</sup>, i.e. small-step, incremental change in policy rates. The PBOC conference this week reiterated to lower the funding cost for the real economy, suggesting that rate cuts will continue. Our forecasts expect two 10bp rate cuts in 2024, the same as in 2023.

On credit policy, we expect another RRR cut in 2H24, and full-year TSF growth at 9.5% (the same as in 2023). Our measure of credit impulse, the gap between TSF growth and nominal GDP growth, suggests it will average around 5%pts (similar to 2023). The government work report and the PBOC conference reiterated that “money supply and TSF growth should be in line with growth and inflation targets<sup>”</sup>, and PBOC governor Pan said there is still room to cut RRR further. This is in line with our expectations.

The expansion in structural monetary policy instruments has been emphasized repeatedly in recent months, especially to support five key areas (technology; green finance; inclusive finance; pension finance; digital finance). Our forecast also expects a 1-trillion package (via PBOC<sup>’</sup>s PSL support) to support public housing, urban village renovation and public infrastructure, which is important to partially offset the weakness in commodity housing market. The choice of bolstered structural monetary policy instruments may in part reflect the constraints faced by interest rate policy and credit policy, and likely weaker policy transmission, and in part is driven by the PBOC<sup>’</sup>s confidence on near-term policy effectiveness of these structural instruments (though long-run policy effectiveness is much more debatable, in our view).
