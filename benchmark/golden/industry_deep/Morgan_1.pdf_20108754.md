Introduction

## What's new in this report

We summarize India’s progress on infrastructure and the nation's development plan. Historically, India’s manufacturing competitiveness has suffered from its poor infrastructure. However, things have improved materially in the last few years – and there is significant room for and possibility of further improvement. Recent government initiatives – especially Gati Shakti – can improve efficiencies.

## Investment is not only increasing – it is better targeted and potentially more productive

India's thrust on infrastructure spending is set to continue: India has scaled up her infrastructure strongly over the last decade. Of interest – and contrary to popular perception – India's physical infrastructure scale already compares favorably to China's when viewed in the context of GDP differential. We expect India's infrastructure investment to steadily increase from 5.3% of GDP in F24 to 6.5% of GDP by F29. Indeed, this implies that infrastructure investments are expected to register a strong 15.3% CAGR, resulting in cumulative spending of US\$1.45tn over the next five years. In our view, this will help to lift the investment rate, leading to a sustained period of high productive growth.

The nation's infrastructure is undergoing a transformation: India is now building out efficient infrastructure though implementation of PM Gati Shakti (PMGS) – the national master plan. It deploys geospatial technology, providing real-time information on infra projects. PMGS should achieve the following:

• Facilitating stronger coordination across ministries;

• Significantly improving capital allocation (among competing infra projects);

• Tighter planning and decision-making;

• Faster regulatory approvals; and

• Help in resolving issues during the project implementation phase.

Thus, PMGS should drive faster execution of infra projects while reducing cost overruns.
It should also unleash productivity gains, leading to higher efficiency.

## Key implications

## In our view, the growth of physical infrastructure will have the following major macroeconomic implications:

1. Augmented capital spending levels are likely to usher in a profit boom.

2. Macro stability should improve, driven by higher FDI and export market share.

3. The improved trend in ICOR (incremental capital output ratio) is indicative of greater efficiency and productivity.

4. Growth should be more sustained, with positive social implications.

In this regard, the gains from infrastructure development are likely to percolate to all sectors of the economy. Creating efficient infrastructure via PMGS would potentially drive logistical costs lower, thus improving India's manufacturing competitiveness and boosting exports.

There would also be positive implications for the equity market: These would be driven by dampened inflation and the boost to corporate profitability.

Fundamental Backdrop

This is India's decade: India has conditions in place for an economic boom, in our view, fueled by offshoring, investment in manufacturing, the energy transition, and the country's advanced digital infrastructure. These drivers will make it the world's third-largest economy and stock market before the end of the decade, we estimate. The New India: Why This Is India's Decade (31 Oct 2022)

Rewiring the global economy for a multipolar world: Geopolitical events like US-China trade tensions, Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, and the cost of the sanctions response demonstrated the security benefits of relying on one’s allies rather than rivals for economic needs. The pandemic increased the desirability of "just in case" over "just in time" logistics. These have accelerated the shift towards "slowbalization" and a multipolar world.

The Indian government, for its part, is taking concerted policy steps to address issues that can help improve the competitiveness of domestic industry These include: reduced corporate tax rates, Production-Linked Incentive (PLI) schemes to push local manufacturing, labor reforms, and expansion of infrastructure.

India to ramp up infrastructure spending: The leading Asian economies have invested in physical infrastructure to create an environment conducive to exportled growth. China invested in infrastructure through state funding and often did this countercyclically. Key focus areas of China’s infrastructure spending were transport, electricity, and power infrastructure.

Scaling up infrastructure is one of India's strongest achievements of the past decade, via both better execution and policy actions. We expect India's spending on infrastructure to increase steadily from 5.3% of GDP in F24 to 6.5% of GDP by F29; this implies a 15.3% CAGR in infrastructure spending or cumulative spending of US \$ 1.45tn over the next five years.

We believe this rise in capex will lead to a virtuous cycle of productive growth. We expect broad-based improvement across sectors, led by transport (rails, roads, ports, airports), power (transmission, renewable energy), urban infrastructure (metros, telecom), and logistics infrastructure (warehouses).

## India has scaled up her infrastructure strongly over the last decade

There has been a fair bit of scepticism from investors about India's ability to build infrastructure at scale. Indeed, historically physical infrastructure has lagged the economic needs, with constraints in the form of access and willingness to invest and lack of a coordinated policy approach to execute large projects in a time-bound manner. However, in the last decade the government has focused on building out large-scale infrastructure through the country, which is starting to be reflected in her citizens' lives – yet more needs to be done.

Several ministries have rolled out sector-specific long-term infrastructure plans – e.g., Bharatmala for roads, the national rail plan, Sagarmala for port connectivity, Power for All, waterways development program, etc. – to scale up infrastructure in the country. This is at the margin helping goods move faster and at more affordable cost than in the past.

## PMGS – national master plan: transformation of India's infrastructure

India's growth story is at a strong inflexion point. A well-orchestrated approach towards creating necessary infrastructure is a key to implementation of plans and policies, and to maximize the output from the fast-track growth ahead. Now, India is now building out efficient infrastructure though implementation of PM Gati Shakti (PMGS) – the national master plan. Said differently, PM Gati Shakti is a well-orchestrated approach to radically metamorphose the contours of infrastructure in India. PMGS aims to lend more speed (Gati) and power (Shakti) by connecting all concerned departments on one platform.

We highlight the key features and advantages of this plan below:

• PMGS breaks through silos and brings better coordination between ministries to synchronize planning. PMGS should help improve the government's efficiency in decision making and capital allocation decisions.

• PMGS should help in reducing infrastructure project timelines. With better coordination, the risk of unforeseen circumstances is effectively reduced.

• PMGS is expected to provide seamless multi-modal connectivity infrastructure with last-mile connectivity to the various economic zones, and to bring logistical efficiency through development in key areas of infra development, namely: railways, roads, ports, airports, mass transport, and logistics infrastructure.

• PMGS should help in reducing logistical costs, improve local manufacturing, and boost export competitiveness.

## PMGS: Key risks/impediments in efficient infrastructure creation

#1 – Change in government strategy: India's infrastructure story is on a firm footing with the BJP-led NDA government retuning to power and the BJP's manifesto laying thrust on scale and execution. The budgetary allocation toward physical infrastructure has increased over the last decade – and more importantly, the actual expenditure has also increased. However, we need to monitor if there may be any change in government strategy.

#2 – Dependence on budget for infra financing: Indian infrastructure financing has largely been met through budgetary allocation. There are multiple sectors which need allocation to create appropriate infrastructure. For an emerging economy like India, the key is in managing the balance between growth through infrastructure spending and controlling the fiscal deficit. Indian democracy has given power to states for some items like power, water, metro, land, etc. (concurrent list) and funding for these comes from both central and state governments (also through the Scheme for Special Assistance to States for Capital Investment). Finally, the infra assets are largely owned by the government – Public-Private Partnerships (PPP) have not been successful from the past cycle and revival is awaited despite corporate balance sheets becoming healthier. More recently, the Asian Development Bank (ADB) has committed up to \$25bn for the next five years to fund India's infra under the PM Gati Shakti initiative as well as social development and climate action.

#3 – Execution delays: The success of the national master plan depends upon the proper coordination among various departments of central and state governments, proper planning, and effective implementation. Indian democracy has given power to states for some items like power, water, metro, land etc. (concurrent list); if the ruling party at the central and state level is not the same, implementation of policies could be tougher. Land acquisition process and multiple regulatory approvals have led to delay of infra projects and cost overrun in these projects. Execution of plans holds the key in realizing benefits of lower logistical costs for the economy in the medium term.

## #4 – Potential supply-side challenges:

Given robust targets for building rail infrastructure, there could be stronger demand for underlying commodities such as steel, cement, aluminum, etc. Demand for these commodities is driven by multiple sectors. These sectors should have or build sufficient capacities, or else there could be higher inflation and/or project delays. If certain commodities need to be imported and there are any potential changes in geopolitical conditions, it could lead to inflation in the system.

Availability of adequate amounts of skilled labour for building different types of infrastructure is also a risk factor to monitor.

#5: Global recession or weaker growth: Can influence India's exports and capex trend as well as the availability of global capital flows. We also cite geopolitical events, which can cause supply-side shock to the economy and concomitant risk to policy reform momentum.

## Infrastructure: An Important Pillar of Economic Growth

## Significance of infrastructure to India's economic growth

Infrastructure forms the bedrock of sustained growth in an economy because of its backward and forward linkages to other sectors of the economy and ability to efficiently integrate local and global markets. India's government has recently expedited policies and reforms to promote investment-driven growth and thus eliminate the existing infrastructure deficit to foster ease of living and doing business. The government is directing efforts at synchronizing the confluence of its recently launched National Infrastructure Pipeline (NIP), PM Gati Shakti, and Development Finance Institution (now called National Bank for Infrastructure Financing & Development [NaBFID]) in an effort to lift investment-led growth. Per the Reserve Bank of India and National Institute of Public Finance and Policy, infra spending has an estimated multiplier effect on GDP of 2.5-3.5x.

## Importance of Public-Private Partnerships (PPPs) in infrastructure

1. The private sector is a crucial enabler for infra creation – it brings in investments, innovation, and efficiency.

2. By leveraging Public-Private Partnerships (PPPs), India can accelerate infrastructure development while ensuring sustainability and inclusivity.

3. PPPs foster competition, encourage technological advancements, and promote best practices in project execution.

4. Collaboration between government and the private sector should result in creating resilient, future-ready infrastructure that paves the way for a prosperous and sustainable future.

## Key issues plaguing India's infrastructure development potential

India has also not been able to realize its full potential of economic growth for multiple decades. There is a wide gap between macro planning and micro implementation, thus resulting in subpar infrastructure development. There are several issues, which include:

1. Disjointed planning – various ministries work in silos.

2. Fragmented nature of decision-making – often the scale planned is below potential.

3. Information flow is not structured well.

4. Coordination is lacking during implementation between different authorities like central government ministries, government departments, state governments, and local bodies.

5. Standardization is lacking.

6. Approval process is slower.

7. Speed of execution is slow.

8. Installed capacity is underutilized or lies dormant.

A few illustrations:

1. After a road is constructed, multiple other agencies would dig up the same road again to lay underground cables, gas pipelines, sewage pipes, etc.

2. India has historically has planned many economic zones, industrial parks, logistics hubs, and ports, but they have often not achieved their potential owing to their size and inefficient multi-modal connectivity.

## The net result: delay and cost overruns on infrastructure projects + high logistical costs for manufactured goods

The issues (as discussed above) result in delays in completion and cost overruns while executing infrastructure projects. This also results in higher logistical costs in the system.

1. The delays can arise from several sources: land acquisition, getting forest and environmental clearances, tie-up for project financing, lack of necessary utility support and linkages, finalizing detailed engineering, ordering and equipment supply, and many other factors.

2. The increased cost of infrastructure creation is a wasteful expenditure and is borne by the taxpayer (spending through budgetary resources) and leads to inflation in the system.

3. The private sector's ability and willingness to participate in PPP projects across different sectors has been impaired by past experiences of wasteful expenditure, delays, litigations, etc.

4. Delays and cost overruns lead to higher logistical costs. India's logistical cost (measured as a percentage of GDP) is \~14%, vs. the global average of \~8%. This erodes India's manufacturing competitiveness globally and thus exports of goods suffer.

## Key Macro Implications

From growth hurdle to key driver: Investors remain sceptical with respect to India’s ability to build large-scale domestic infrastructure, which will be the cornerstone of the economy’s ability to achieve high productive growth. Nonetheless, the country’s infrastructure is changing at a rapid pace and is likely to swing from being a hurdle to the growth story to a key driver of the growth trajectory. Indeed, according to Morgan Stanley's economics team, infrastructure investment tracked at US\$195bn/5.3% of GDP in F24e and is likely to reach \~6.5% of GDP in the next five years.

In our view, the growth of physical infrastructure will have the following major implications for the macroeconomy:

1. Augmented capital spending levels are likely to usher in a profit boom.

2. Macro stability should increase, driven by higher FDI and export market share.

3. The improved trend in ICOR (incremental capital output ratio) is indicative of greater efficiency and productivity

4. Growth should be more sustained, with positive social implications.

## 1) Augmented capital spending levels are likely to usher in a profit boom

Investment rate set to rise: The confluence of proactive supply-side reforms, improving capacity utilisation rates, and resilience in corporate and financial sector balance sheets has set the stage for a sustained capex up-cycle in India. The upturn in government capex both at the centre and state levels has allowed private sector investments to be fasttracked; thus, they are exhibiting nascent signs of recovery. As such, we opine that the broad-based rise in capex will engender a virtuous cycle of productive growth and employment generation over the next few years. In our view, the capex rate is likely to rise to 36.2% in F29 from 33.7% in F24, with a broad-based pickup – with private capex driving the highest delta.

A further granular examination of trends by sector within private investments shows three areas – 1) manufacturing capex; 2) capex for energy transition; and 3) core infrastructure capex – contributing the most to incremental capex. Per our projections, manufacturing capex could accelerate from 5.3% of GDP in F21 to 7% by F32, similar to the average manufacturing capex in the decade of the 2000s, while infrastructure capex could rise from about 5.3% of GDP in F24 to \~6.5% by F29.

## 2) Macro stability should increase, driven by higher FDI and export market share

Improving business sentiment, as a result of the progress on the ease of doing business front, has played an instrumental role in attracting foreign capital flows to the country on a sustained basis.

• In the past 24 years (April 2000 to March 2024), India has received gross foreign direct investment (FDI) worth US\$918.2bn, with \~42% of these inflows (US \$383.5bn) received in the past five years (April 2019 to March 2024).

• In 2020, FDI flows accelerated meaningfully, reaching US\$87.5bn, the secondhighest flows in absolute terms after China.

• On a 12-month annualised basis, gross FDI flows reached a record high of US \$93.3bn (3.4% of GDP) in May 2021 and are currently tracking at US\$71bn (2% of GDP) as of March 2024.

• Net FDI flows worth US\$487.6bn were received from January 2000 to December 2023, with \~US\$164.1bn (one-third) of flows in the past five years.

Investor confidence too, as measured by our MNC Sentiment Index, further substantiates the buoyancy in foreign investments. In 1Q24, the index fell 17% sequentially, reversing the 17% rise in the previous quarter. The index now stands close to pre-Covid peak levels, which suggests that overall sentiment remains strong given India's rising position in the world order, a multipolar world, the growing consumer market in India, and government incentives for investments. Relative to China, MNC sentiment gapped higher for the 11th successive quarter while MNC sentiment toward China rose sequentially to approach a two-year high.

Benign trend in current account deficit implies that the investment cycle will not face funding challenges: We believe India's structural transformation will feed into the savinginvestment dynamics, implying gains for its external balance sheet, with a progressively narrower trend in the CAD. In the macro parlance, current account deficit is saving less investment. As such, even though we expect the investment rate to pick up, we believe that improvement in the saving rate is the foundation. The implications of structural reforms will be reflected in a higher saving rate driven by higher export income, improving corporate profitability trend, and higher household saving with more job creation – even as the investment rate picks up. In this context, India stands to gain on its external balance sheet front, as the evolving saving-investment dynamics are likely to reduce the current account deficit on a sustainable basis through improvement in productivity dynamics.

In our view, public sector savings are likely to improve, even as they remain mildly positive, in response to support from the National Asset Monetisation Pipeline and a narrower revenue deficit. The total monetisation target for the NMP stands at INR6tn, over a period of four years (F2022-F2025), out of which a cumulative amount of INR1.14tn has been realised. As such, we expect public sector savings to remain steady and consistently rangebound around 0.5-0.6% of GDP till F2028e, and improve further thereafter.

Furthermore, the household sector is likely to benefit from the virtuous cycle of income growth, a favourable offshoot of which is likely to be in the household savings rate, both physical and financial. We project that the household savings rate will rise to 23% of GDP by F2028e from 17.3% of GDP in F2023. With regard to the private corporate sector, improving investment rates imply higher corporate profits, and subsequently a better savings trend for companies.

In this context, we expect the CAD to narrow to 0.9% of GDP in F28e and further to 0.5% of GDP in F33e from 1.3% of GDP in F25e. A benign current account deficit is likely to lend support to India’s macro stability, in conjunction with the improving terms of trade and improved funding cycle through a more stable source of funding in terms of FDI.

## 3) The improved trend in ICOR (incremental capital output ratio) is indicative of greater efficiency and productivity

The end objective of Gati Shakti encompasses fostering a productive multi-year growth cycle for India, by metamorphosis in the existing infrastructure capacities, which form the building blocks of sustained economic growth.

Improving efficiency and productivity growth: For over two decades, India's total factor productivity (TFP) growth rate has remained weak, averaging sub-1.8% barring 2003-07, when it rose to \~2.5%. However, as the confluence of favourable cyclical and structural drivers lifts India’s investment to GDP ratio and employment, we expect a concomitant boost to productivity growth, currently tracking at 3.8%. In our view, India’s TFP growth is likely to remain strong, rising to an average of 3.2% over the next 10 years vs. 2.4% in the previous decade. The stronger trend in TFP growth will help support a higher real GDP growth rate for India, allowing it to remain the fastest-growing nation, contributing onefifth of the world's real GDP growth. Further, improving productivity is also reflected by improvement in the ICOR (incremental capital output ratio), which is currently tracking at 3.9 vs. 4.9 in the previous decade and similar to the average of 4.1 between 2003-2007.

Increase in manufacturing share in GDP: A notable realisation of the improving infrastructure and consequently capex is likely to be in India’s manufacturing. The government’s supply-side response is visible in the form of policies such as corporate tax rate reduction and production-linked incentive (PLI) scheme. In addition to changing trade relationships and transition to a multipolar world, this further supports our thesis of a pickup in the share of manufacturing in GDP from 14.2% currently to peak at 21% in F31 and then remain steady, which implies that manufacturing revenue will rise from US \$459bn currently to around US\$1.66tn.

## 4) Growth should be more sustained, with positive social implications

More jobs and sustained growth: India’s labour market stands to gain commensurately, as more blue-collar employment opportunities surface, with an upturn in both manufacturing and exports. India’s transition to higher value-added jobs, historically, has been rather slow. Agriculture is still the dominant employer – employing 45.8% of the workforce in F2023 – whilst the share of manufacturing remains lackluster at 11.4%.

Anecdotally, the employment elasticity for agriculture has become more negative, moving from -0.26 in the previous decade (F2000-10) to -0.48 in the current decade (F2011-19). This reflects that a 1ppt increase in GDP growth now would lead to a contraction in employment of 0.5% vs. 0.3% previously. In contrast, the employment elasticity of manufacturing has gained momentum to 0.25 in the current decade from 0.13 in the previous one. This corresponds with the increasing focus on manufacturing activity, which in turn implies greater employment opportunities in non-agriculture sectors. Improved job prospects not only ensure greater social well-being but also promote greater stability of growth.

The government has undertaken a slew of structural supply-side policy reforms to promote greater ease of doing business in the economy, with the objective of propelling a sustained growth cycle in the country. We take a closer look at some of the key initiatives of the government on creating infrastructure, namely:

1. The National Infrastructure Pipeline;

2. PM Gati Shakti – National Master Plan; and

3. The National Logistics Policy.

These initiatives should facilitate building out India's infrastructure – and doing so more efficiently. This would help in reducing logistical costs (bring them to parity with levels in other EMs) and thus make Indian goods more competitive for exports. We believe investments in India's infrastructure over the next decade will create a greater multiplier impact on growth, through forward and backward linkages.

## 3: National Logistics Policy (NLP)

The Indian government, in its resolve to reduce logistical costs, launched the NLP in September 2022. Per the policy

1. It aims to provide comprehensive development of a logistics ecosystem, which is technologically enabled, integrated, cost-efficient, resilient, sustainable, and trusted.

2. It aims to ensure quick last-mile delivery, solve end transport-related challenges, save manufacturers' time and costs, as well as prevent wastage of agricultural products, etc.

3. It is a one-stop platform connecting all stakeholders in the logistics community through IT, aimed at improving efficiency and transparency while reducing costs and time delays.

4. It covers all modes of transport, including waterways, roadways, and airways, and provides a seamless end-to-end logistics service coverage. An integrated network should also end silos between multiple departments.

5. It is a logical step, because it will provide a comprehensive agenda for the development of the entire logistics ecosystem and will support the PMGS initiative.

6. It would support enhancing competitiveness of micro, small, and medium-sized enterprises and sectors like agriculture, consumer goods, and electronics. Inventory levels would decrease as there would be greater predictability and transparency, along with lower wastages in the supply chain.

7. Its vision is to develop a technologically enabled, integrated, cost-efficient, resilient, sustainable, and trusted ecosystem to facilitate accelerated and inclusive growth.

8. The focus would be on enabling adequate development of warehouses with optimal spatial planning, promotion of standards, digitization and automation across the logistics value chain and better tracking and tracing mechanisms.

Railway Segment

Relevance of railway infrastructure: Rail transport is cost-effective vs. roads (average cost per tonne/km for freight to move on rail is Rs1.6, while on road it is Rs3.6 per government estimates. But, despite this, the share of rail in the freight modal mix declined from 86% in the 1950s to 24% in F21 before improving to 29% in F24. This remains one of the key reasons for India's high ratio of logistical costs/GDP at 13-14%, hurting manufacturing competitiveness.

Investment in railways has positive macro implications – viz., it helps in lowering logistical costs, makes Indian manufactured goods competitive, is positive for terms of trade (because it lowers fossil fuel dependence), and helps in moving towards carbon neutrality.

Railway infrastructure – key issues: Underinvestment in railways remains a core issue in the segment. It has led to network stagnation and congestion, affecting speed of trains. Lack of dedicated freight corridors and high freight tariffs hinder freight cargo movements, while lack of capacity (owing to lapse of waiting list tickets) and poor amenities constrain passenger growth.

## 1A: Rail segment-specific challenges

1. Sluggish network expansion: India’s track additions (in route kms) registered a CAGR of barely 0.3% over the last three decades, while for China it was a 3.4% CAGR. Investment in new lines accounted for only \~25% of railways capex in India, which compares to \~80% spent by China for augmenting new lines (F08-F22), per the railway data.

2. High congestion: Stagnant network additions created severe congestion. To give context, \~80% of IR’s high-density and highly utilized network is operating at >70% utilization.

3. Slow speed: Speed has been a function of track availability. Freight trains in India run \~1.5-2x more slowly vs. those in China, the US, and Russia. Freight trains are generally given lower priority over passenger movements owing to shared network and lack of trains on a timetable. This, combined with lack of dedicated freight tracks, has led to slower speed of freight trains in India.

## 1B: Road segment-specific factors

1. Fragmented road segment: India's road freight segment is highly fragmented, intensely competitive on price, has low barriers to entry, and is largely disorganised. Small truck fleet operators (between one and five trucks) make up close to 70% of the market. These small operators are dependent on agents for business and have low pricing power. Roads may not be economical over long distances but are more predictable. A central government focus on investments in the roads segment has also played a strong role. And, there is high price elasticity of demand in road freight, influenced by diesel prices. Diesel price movements directly correlate with freight levels carried by railways.

2. GST implementation and compulsory electronic toll collection: Since Goods & Services Tax was introduced in July 2017, the road segment has become more

competitive at the margin. The waiting times for clearing indirect tax levies at intrastate checkpoints have been reduced substantially, leading to lower operating costs for truckers, which have been passed on to the end consumer – further helping the road segment gain share from the rail segment over the past few years. With the introduction of FASTag, the average waiting time at toll plazas was reduced from 12 minutes in 2014 to 47 seconds in 2023.

3. Increase in payload limit for trucks: An increase in the permissible payload capacity of existing and new trucks, of close to 25% in July 2018, has also led to a preference for road transport over rail in the near term. Because the regulations were changed more than a year ago, and because the road segment had been selectively overloading trucks even before the policy became effective, we do not expect further modal share freight shift towards roads being attributed to this reason.

## 2: Lack of focus on increasing freight share

Freight accounts for 70% of Indian Railways' revenue. But, railways cross-subsidize a passenger tariff, which results in rail freight being less competitive vs. road. Haulage charges are \~85% of operating costs. Negligible non-fare revenues and high freight tariffs contribute to a suboptimal and declining freight share for rail. Low and static prices for the passenger segment have also contributed to low internal generation of resources. The freight tariff-to-passenger tariff ratio doubled from 2.2x, peaking at 4.6x (F13), with the gap narrowing only in recent times to an average of 2.6x (F22).

## The government's plan:

#1 – National Rail Plan: The NRP (draft plan) was released in 2021 with an objective to create a "future-ready" railway system by 2030 by augmenting operational capacities. The core target thus stated includes increasing rail freight modal share to 45% target by 2051e.

#2 – Mission 3,000MT: This was formulated as an intermediate milestone with an objective to identify and prioritize crucial capacity enhancement works for implementation before 2027 driven by: 1) infrastructure enhancement; 2) rolling stock additions; and 3) marketing, pricing, customer service and policy initiatives. To address this, the Ministry of Railways fast-tracked priority railway projects with an aim to increase rail modal to 35% of the mix by F31e or 3,000MT by 2030 (F24: 1,591MT). If railways are able to carry 50% incremental tonnage, it would lead to lower logistical costs of Rs1.8tn or \~0.7% of India's GDP annually. (Assumptions: average lead: 600km; cost savings/ntkm: Rs2 (rail over road) and additional freight: \~1500MT cargo.)

#3 – three new rail corridors: In the Interim Budget 2025, three new rail corridors spanning \~40,900km were announced. These corridors will enable multi-modal connectivity, reduce costs, improve efficiencies, and ease the problem of passenger ticket waiting lists. Once implemented, these tracks will increase freight capacity to 3,000MT (F24: 1,591MT) and passenger capacity to 10bn (F19: 8.4bn, recent years reflect Covid impact). These three corridors will eventually decongest the network substantially; they are expected to be built over the next eight years with initial capex earmarked at Rs11tn.

## Reform initiatives:

1. Merging the railway budget with the Union Budget (2017) – to facilitate multimodal transport planning;

2. Revised Private Freight Terminals policy (2020) – to increase private participation to speed up freight network development with favorable changes with respect to terminal charges, reduction of application fees, etc.

3. Amrit Bharat station scheme for redevelopment (2023) – 1,309 stations identified, work ongoing on 600 stations;

4. Gati Shakti Multi-Modal Cargo Terminal policy (2021) – to increase transportation capacity by developing freight terminal infrastructure; 100 Gati Shakti cargo terminals to be developed between F23-25e;

5. Semi High Speed Train ecosystem (2023) – tTo launch 4,500 Vande Bharat trains by 2047.

## Initiatives under Gati Shakti:

1. Under Gati Shakti, the network planning group (so far) has approved \~46 rail projects worth \~Rs1.14bn, which will improve regional connectivity, connectivity of mines clusters, and help in reducing congestion.

2. The government also launched, the Gati Shakti Multi-Modal Cargo Terminal (GCT) policy in December 2021, which aims to commission 100 GCTs by F25 and increase that number to 300 terminals over the next five years and bring in private sector investment. These terminals are likely to bring cargo traffic in excess of 1MMT each for Indian Railways. GCT involves laying of additional lines, concreting of surfaces, use of electronic in-motion weigh bridges, provision of hamali rest rooms, covered sheds, approach roads, water supply arrangements, and installation of computerized workings.

Relevance of road and National Highway infrastructure: India has the world's second-largest road network in terms of length. In F24, the total length of National Highways in the country reacheds 146,145km. India currently has 44,654km (31% of total) of four-lane highways which is 2.4x F14 levels of 18,371km (20% of the total). National Highways form 2% of the total road network but carry \~40% of traffic.

Key issues – roads and National Highways: Lack of a national master plan (apart from the Golden Quadrilateral and North-South-East-West corridor) remained the core reason for inadequate optimization of the road network. Also, connectivity of major corridors/ports to the hinterland was not planned using an integrated approach. Congestion remained, even on the developed corridors, leading to slower speeds

and higher rate of accidents; also accident response infrastructure was sub-optimal.

## The government's plan:

#1 – Bharatmala Pariyojana: Addressing the historical issue of absence of integrated planning, the government launched the Bharatmala Pariyojana (launched in 2017) with an umbrella approach. The objective is to bridge critical infrastructure gaps integrating national and economic corridors and foster seamless movement of freight and passengers. Under Phase 1, new road length of \~24,800km is prioritized and an additional 8,100km+ is planned under Phase II(A). In addition, measures are outlined to decongest highways and enhance road safety. Total capital outlay earmarked is Rs5.35tn (including 10,000km of residual work of the National Highways Development Project); Phase 1 is expected to be completed by F28e. The Roads Ministry targets an asset base of 185,000km of National Highways by 2030 and 237,000km by 2047 (F23: \~146,000km).

#2 – Multimodal Logistics Parks (MMLPs): A network of 35 MMLPs with an investment of Rs460bn is proposed to be developed as part of the Bharatamala Pariyojana. Of this, 15 locations are prioritized (worth Rs220bn) to be awarded by F25e. MMLPs are formulated to achieve efficient transportation mode, streamlining roads, rail, or coastal shipping connectivity. Once the 35 MMLPs are operational, they can potentially cater to handling \~700MMT of cargo.

Ports Segment

Relevance of port infrastructure: India is globally the seventh-largest country by land area and fifth-largest by economic scale. India has 12 major ports (government-owned) and >200 minor ports (privately owned) along its 7,500km coastline. India's container penetration rate has traditionally remained low, with only 14 TEUs (Twenty-Foot Equivalent Units) per 1,000 population, compared to the global average of 109 TEUs per 1,000 population. Strong port infrastructure and hinterland logistics are enablers of India's manufacturing competitiveness (ability to move goods at speed, with safety, and at an economical cost). Efficient ports infrastructure (well designed and automated) plays a large role in improving turnaround times for goods and thus, reduce logistical costs.

Key issues – ports: India has historically been an underpenetrated country in port services for various reasons. Historically, Indian ports were marked by high capacity utilisation (more than 90%), which translated into extended waiting days of vessels to berth, long vessel turnaround times, absence of supply chain integration and its cascading impact on costs, and cargo visibility/reliability. The result was a high cost of cargo intermediation that translated into a high cost of end-products, enhancing the high-cost nature of the Indian economy. Indian ports also had inadequate last-mile road and rail connectivity. Per the Finance Ministry, ports congestion cost India’s GDP growth 1-2ppt. Inter-port competition was also low, resulting in high tariffs and services not aligned with customer expectations. India also did not have a port-based industrial ecosystem.

## The government's plan:

#1 – Sagarmala program: The program has four key components: port connectivity (52%), port modernization (25%), new port development (22%), and coastal community development (1%). This was launched in April 2016 to enhance performance of the logistics sector by setting up new mega-ports, modernising existing ports, and developing 14 Coastal Employment Zones and Coastal Employment Units. The port-led development focuses on logistics-intensive industries, which would be supported by efficient and modern port infrastructure and seamless multi-modal connectivity. Under Sagarmala, 839 projects worth Rs5.8tn have been identified for implementation by 2035, of which 262 projects (Rs1.4tn) have been completed and the remaining projects are under various stages of implementation and development. The primary objective of Sagarmala is to promote port-led direct and indirect developments, and ensure quick, efficient, and costeffective evacuation of cargo.

#2 – Maritime India Vision 2030 (MIV 2030): This is a 10-year blueprint (launched in November 2020) to catalyse investments of Rs1-1.25tn towards capacity augmentation and development of port infrastructure. The policy covers >150 initiatives across port, shipping, and waterway modes with an objective to transform the country’s logistical effectiveness. The capacity addition target for major ports is 423MTPA for an investment of Rs334bn and PPP of \~95%. To promote inland water transport (lWT), 111 National Waterways (NWs) – five existing and 106 new – were declared under the National Waterways Act, 2016.

#3 – digitisation of ports: In March 2023, the ports minister launched Sagar Manthan, a digital platform containing comprehensive data related to the Ministry and all its organisations.

• Significant efforts have been made to digitise major EXIM processes like Electronic Invoice (e-Invoice), Electronic Payment (e-Payment), and Electronic Delivery Order (e-DO) for the physical release of cargo by custodians.

• The generation of electronic Bills of Lading (e-BL) has also been implemented, along with the digitisation of the Letter of Credit (LC) process.

• The government is working towards achieving complete integration between PCS 1x, a cloud-based new generation technology, and Indian Customs EDI Gateway (ICEGATE) for seamless data exchange.

• RFID solutions have also been implemented at all key ports to facilitate uninterrupted movement of traffic across port gates and reduce the need for extensive documentation checks.

• The government has set up an Enterprise Business System (EBS) at five major ports (Mumbai, Chennai, Deendayal, Paradip, and Kolkata). The EBS aims to provide a digital port ecosystem that adopts leading international practices while remaining aligned with local needs.

#4 – National Multimodal Facilities and Warehousing: To promote intermodal and Multimodal Logistics Parks (MMLPs) as a separate class of infrastructure and to enable fair price discovery, ensure optimal utilisation, and support planned development, a national grid of logistics parks and terminals is being planned. The government has begun to devise certain standards and guidelines that will be implemented for the development of warehousing. The government plans to optimise procedures for obtaining clearances that make the procedure of establishing warehouses efficient, while setting up a system for rating and certifying those warehouses for excellence.

#5 – coastal shipping: The government has estimated that coastal movement of goods can be 340MT by F25.

#6 – Amrit Kaal (AK) Vision 2047: This is a step up from the government's previously outlined Maritime India Vision 2030. This vision document outlines a comprehensive roadmap identifying 11 key themes with 300+ action items to transform India's maritime industry over the next 25 years. The total investment opportunity is worth Rs75-80tn over the next 25 years. Under the AK plan, the government seeks to enhance ports' capacity and promote inland water transport, coastal shipping, and a sustainable maritime sector. AK encompasses aspirations in logistics, infrastructure, and shipping, supporting India's "Blue Economy." The vision, shaped through over 150 consultations with various stakeholders and the analysis of 50 international benchmarks, outlines more than 300 actionable initiatives for enhancing ports, shipping, and waterways by 2047.

Per AK, investments of Rs19tn will be made (through 2047) towards:

• Creating mega ports – developing four port clusters (capacity >=300MTPA) and two port clusters (capacity >=500MTPA);

• Setting up transhipment hubs – Vizhinjam and Galathea Bay (both with 20m deep draft) and targeting India's share of transhipment cargo at 95% by 2047 (current: 32%); and

Airports Segment

Airport infrastructure: India is a large country and hence it is imperative to have a wellconnected transport network that includes road, rail, port (coastal), and air transport infrastructure. Rising disposable incomes, the rising working age population, low penetration of flying (seats per capita), and underinvestment in airport capacity (as reflected in high capacity utilization at key metro airports) imply a strong runway for airport infrastructure growth. The emergence of business hubs like Mumbai (finance), Bengaluru (IT), Chennai (IT), and Delhi (Manufacturing, IT) is likely to boost business travel as well.

Key issues – airports: The upgrading of infrastructure/facilities at airports is a continuous process, which is managed by the Airports Authority of India (AAI) or the airport operators concerned, depending on operational requirements, traffic, demand, commercial feasibility, etc.

1. Capacity: In 2016, India became the world's third-largest aviation market in terms of domestic passenger traffic, reaching 100mn travelers, behind only the US (719mn) and China (436mn), and ahead of Japan (97mn). However, India has fallen behind in terms of its airport infrastructure with capacity augmentation now an urgent requirement. This is testified by the overwhelming capacity utilization seen at major airports across India, where average levels have surpassed 100%.

2. Non-metro airports: Further, while the country has over 147 airports (AAI owns \~125), not all of them are operational. This needs to change at the margin as higher demand for air travel is likely to emerge from non-metro cities, spurred by higher income levels and an increase in middle-income households.

3. Efficiency improvement: Indian airports have some ground to cover versus most global aviation hubs in terms of the number of peak hour movements, utilization of airspace for civil aviation, air cargo dwell times, and so on. Benchmarking Indian airports with global best practices and working on improving efficiencies will help to drive a stronger ecosystem.

The government's plan: Per the Aviation Minister, India’s domestic air passenger traffic is expected to be \~300mn by 2030 (\~2x 2023's level of 153mn). The government's focus is on enhancing regional connectivity and making air travel more affordable for the masses.

#1 – asset-based expansion: The government plans to expand the total number of airports in the country to more than 200 by 2030 (February 2024: 149). There are also plans to add more heliports and water aerodromes, taking the total to 230-240 by 2030.

#2 – UDAN scheme: UDAN (Ude Desh ka Aam Naagrik) is a self-financing scheme with the objective of stimulating regional air connectivity and making air travel affordable to the masses. Promoting the affordability of regional air connectivity is envisioned under UDAN by supporting Selected Airline Operators (SAOs) through concessions by central and state governments and airport operators to reduce the cost of airline operations on regional routes and providing financial support (Viability Gap Funding or VGF) to bridge the gap, if any, between the cost of airline operations and expected revenues on such UDAN routes.

#3 – National Civil Aviation Policy, 2016: This policy envisages the government entering “open sky” agreements on a reciprocal basis with SAARC (South Asian Association for Regional Cooperation, viz., Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Maldives, Nepal, Pakistan and Sri Lanka) countries, as well as countries with territory located entirely beyond a 5,000km radius from New Delhi. Unlimited flights above the existing bilateral rights will be allowed directly to and from major international airports within India. However, the points of call at other airports un­der the existing agreements will continue to be honoured until they are renegotiated. In accordance with this policy, 49 countries beyond 5,000km from Delhi were offered open-sky agreements in 2016. To date, 20 countries beyond 5,000km from Delhi have formalised open sky arrangements with India.

#4 – developing India as an MRO hub: Guidelines were announced in September 2021 with a view to creating an atmosphere in India conducive to the development of the MRO industry for aircraft/helicopters/drones and their engines and other parts. Airlines typically spend 12-15% of their revenues on maintenance, which is the second highest cost component after fuel. The target is to handle \~90% of the MRO needs of Indian operators and \~20% of MRO revenue from foreign-registered aircraft. Key steps to achieve these objectives include: setting up of a high-power task force for promotion of MRO, declaration of MRO and component warehouses as free trade zones with 0% GST, import restrictions etc.

#5 – drones policy: The Ministry of Civil Aviation notified of the New Drone Rules, released in 2021. Several licences, fees, and forms have been eliminated. Per the Drone Airspace Map (September 2021), 90% of India is now in a green zone where no permission is required to operate a drone. The government has also introduced a Production-linked Incentive scheme for local manufacturing of drones. Drones are being used to deliver medicines, newspapers, etc. in different cities while e-commerce platforms are testing drones for delivering their products in different parts of India.

#6 – Vision 2040: In 2019, the Ministry of Civil Aviation released its Vision 2040 document, which estimates a 6x surge in air passenger traffic, to reach 1.1bn pax by 2040 (domestic 821mn, international 202mn). To meet this demand, the report suggests the following:

• 100 new additional airports across the country,

• \$50bn investment in airport modernization,

• 2,359 aircraft to cater to demand,

• Investments in technology for air traffic management systems which can save costs and improve affordability. MCA expects ticket prices to decrease 30%.

• \$15bn allocation towards R&D in green aviation and targeting at least 30% of the aviation fleet running on eco-friendly alternative fuel by 2040.

#7 – work in progress, India to be a transit hub for South Asia: The Indian government is framing a policy to make airport transit hubs for South Asia. The policy, which will need cabinet approval, aims to frame laws for easing security and immigration bottlenecks at airports, allocating international flying rights, and building necessary infrastructure. This would facilitate Delhi's evolution as a transit hub, competing with Dubai Airport and Singapore's Changi Airport. Hubs aggregate passenger demand from the entire area and offer multiple direct flights to major cities across the world. The policy will seek to reform security rules for faster connections between flights, reducing immigration wait times and developing infrastructure linking multiple airport terminals.

## Reform initiatives:

1. The AERA amendment bill was passed by Parliament in March 2021. It proposes to amend the definition of a major airport to include a group of airports. This paved the way for the privatization of small loss-making airports by grouping them together with larger airports.

2. The national air cargo policy (2019) aims to explore potential new markets and the creation of infrastructure for sustained cargo growth over the next 10-15 years at least. It also aims to promote first mile/last mile connectivity.

3. The government has launched Digi Yatra for biometric-based travel using Facial Recognition Technology. This provide a seamless, hassle-free experience for passengers at airports. As part of Phase I, Digi Yatra has been launched at Delhi, Bangalore, Varanasi, Kolkata, Pune, Vijayawada, and Hyderabad airports.

4. Measures taken for improved ease of travel include capacity enhancement through infra changes in existing terminals; installation and commissioning of additional X-ray machines for baggage checking; deployment of additional manpower by CISF (Central Industrial Security Force), airlines and airport operators; better management of slot allocation and better coordination with airlines to avoid bunching of flights etc.

5. 100% Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) allowed in brownfield airports through automatic route.

6. 31 States and Union Territories now levy 1%-5% VAT on ATF (Aviation Turbine Fuel). Only five outliers are left with a higher bracket.

7. Work has been conducted on route rationalization in airspace in coordination with Indian Air Force for efficient airspace management, shorter routes and lower fuel consumption.

## Progress so far:

1. In 2014, only 74 airports were operational in India. By March 2023, another 74 airports/helicopters/water aerodromes were operational. The government intends to take the cumulative number to 220 by 2030.

2. Over the last nine years, 11 more greenfield airports have commenced operations.

3. Since 2014, four airports – Tirupati, Vijayawada, Ku­shinagar and Mopa – have been declared international airports, taking the total number of international airports in to 30.

4. Under the UDAN scheme, 180 RCS airports (including 25 water aerodromes and 40 helipads) have been identified for operation of RCS flights in the country. At present, out of 1,152 valid awarded RCS routes, 475 RCS routes have commenced, connecting 74 airports, including nine heliports and two water aerodromes.

5. Airport capacity is slated to increase from 221 MPPA to over 456 MPPA in the next decade. Capacity expansion is being considered at Delhi, Bengaluru, Hyderabad, and Chennai.

6. In March 2024, the ministry announced the inauguration of 15 airport projects worth \$12.1bn by 2028.

Initiatives under Gati Shakti: The government has announced construction of 16 new airports across five states, namely Rewa in Madhya Pradesh, Ambikapur, Bilaspur and Jagdalpur in Chhattisgarh, nine airports in Uttar Pradesh, one in Rajasthan, and two in Maharashtra.

## Metro Rail Segment

Relevance of metro rail infra: Growing urbanization and rising disposable incomes have led to fast growth in privately owned vehicles in India. However, the road network and its quality have not kept pace with macro trends, resulting in severe road congestion, increased travel times, and rising pollution. Building out metro rail infrastructure would provide an effective mass transport medium and offer cheaper and more environmentally friendly (lower emissions) transport than private cars. The metro route network also brings about commercial real estate development.

Key issues – metro rail: Metro projects involve multiple stakeholders, largely governing authorities. This can cause significant delays in assessing the viability and scope of projects and in gaining approvals and execution. Thus, there is a risk of cost overruns and a potential impact on returns. The slow pace of land acquisition in large congested cities has also been a concern in relation to metro rollout. PPP schemes for metro projects have not been particularly popular, and developers are making large losses, which has not helped the overall situation. These challenges are also amplified by the huge investment requirements and associated lock-in periods. Hence, metro projects have high dependence on state government finances, which in turn leads in many cases to having to tie up funding from multilateral agencies.

## The government's plan:

#1 – metro rollout based on population: The Ministry of Housing and Urban affairs in 2006 set the population benchmark for setting up a metro system at 2 million. In 2011 this was lowered to 1 million. Further, in 2017 the ministry announced a target of setting up 5,000km of metro line in at least 100 cities over the next 25 years (by 2047). This compares to \~900km in operation currently.

#2 – metro policy, 2017: The policy (August 2017) focuses on innovative models of implementation and financing, as well as standardizing and indigenizing hardware and software. Further, the law was rationalized to facilitate greater private participation and investment in construction and operation tasks. The policy also provided options on methods to manage the O&M of metro services, including a cost plus fee contract model, gross cost contracts, and net cost contracts. It also promotes fostering urban transformation and boosting the financial viability of metro rail projects by focusing on last-mile connectivity, transit-oriented development, commercial/property development at stations, and non-fare revenue through advertisements, leasing of space, etc.

## Reform initiatives

#1 – National Urban Transport Policy (NUTP): The government, through the policy, recommends an ASI (Avoid, Shift and Improve) approach towards future metro endeavours:

1. Avoid: This concept aims to reduce the need for travel in urban centers by promoting population concentration, strategic infrastructure development, and any mechanisms that reduce the total number of passenger km traveled.

2. Shift: Shifting choice of transport to one that efficiently manages traffic flow while reducing fuel consumption and pollution.

3. Improve: Improving vehicle technology to promote fuel efficiency and adherence to pollution norms.

#2 – funding support: Government support in terms of funding and tie-ups with multilateral agencies for metro projects is indicative of growing impetus for metro rail development.

Progress so far: Over 55 cities in India have a population of >1mn. India currently has 889km of metro lines in operation with a further 728km of lines under construction and 2,390km currently at the planning stage (including those that have been approved).

## Renewable Power Segment

Significance of renewable infrastructure: Meeting the government's net zero goals will require sizable investment in renewable energy and storage technologies. RE achieved grid parity in India about five years ago. Solar and wind – despite market volatility and various government levies – remain the cheapest ways of generating electricity in India, and we believe further technological improvements will help drive efficiencies and reduce costs further.

Relevance of India's energy transition: Energy transition will help lower India's emissions, reduce imported oil/gas/coal dependence, and improve its current account balance – thus aiding macro stability over the medium to long term, in our view. Incidentally, the power sector accounts for >50% of India's emissions. We expect energy transition investments to have a favorable impact on India’s terms of trade and reduce its headline inflation volatility as the imported energy share of GDP declines. Energy transition would also likely improve living conditions – 14 of the 20 most-polluted cities in the world are in India. We believe sectors such as utilities, industrials, and autos will lead on decarbonization. The country's policy framework is now in step with the rest of the world, with India announcing a National Hydrogen Mission and working on PLI schemes to provide incentives to local manufacturers of solar modules, batteries, and electrolyzers – all necessary ingredients of the energy transition. We believe the energy

transition is a significant theme for the coming decade, with multiple opportunities emerging across the value chain for investors and companies alike.

India has large RE potential and is economically viable: The National Institute of Energy has estimated India's solar potential at 748GW. India is well situated, geographically, for renewables. It has 300 days of sunshine each year, with daily peak electricity use being in the evenings alongside a seasonal peak in the summer. Further, India has around 696GW (120m hub height) and 1,164GW (150m hub height) of wind potential. Wind and solar sources complement each other. By virtue of their inherent characteristics, they generate power during different times of the day as well as seasons. Wind power is at its maximum during the nighttime, whereas solar power is available only during the day.

Key issues – renewables: Solar radiation is not uniform across multiple states and wind speeds over the last few years have been below the long-term average. Further, there is \~60GW of solar assets under construction that are not yet commissioned owing to supply chain-related challenges – land availability, non-availability of evacuation infrastructure (transmission), etc.

## The government's plan:

#1 – net zero by 2070: At the COP26 Summit, India's PM announced five commitments:

1. Increase India’s non-fossil energy capacity to 500GW by 2030;

2. Generate 50% of energy requirements from RE by 2030;

3. Reduce carbon emissions by 1bn tonnes by 2030;

4. Reduce carbon intensity by 45% by 2030;

5. Achieve net zero emissions by 2070.

In F24, renewable energy accounted for 33% of the installed capacity base and 13% of overall generation. The CEA (Central Electricity Authority of India) expects India's proportion of renewable energy capacity and generation to increase to 56% and 34% by F32, respectively.

## Reform initiatives:

1. Must run status: RE plants are must-run plants and shall not be subjected to curtailment or regulation of generation or supply of electricity on account of merit order dispatch or any other commercial consideration. Curtailment would be limited to technical constraints in the electricity grid or for reasons of security of the electricity grid. In case of curtailment for any other reason, the procurer would pay the generation company at the PPA rate.

2. RPO trajectory: The Ministry of Power issued the renewable purchase obligation (RPO) and energy storage application (ESO) trajectory until 2030. RPO will increase from 24.6% in F23 to 43.3% by F30 and ESO will rise from 1% in F24 to 4% by F30.

3. Renewable Generation Obligation (RGO): In February 2023, the Ministry of Power notified of the RGO, which mandates coal and lignite plants getting commissioned on or after April 1, 2023 to establish or procure RE power capacity equivalent to at least 40% of their total capacity. For plants being commissioned between April 1, 2023 and March 31, 2025, 40% compliance with RGO is required by April 1, 2025. Plants being commissioned after April 1, 2025 will need to comply with RGO with effect from the commissioning date. A follow-up draft notification in October 2023 lowered the RGO to 6-10%.

4. ISTS charges waiver: ISTS (Inter State Transmission Charges) charges have been waived for 25 years for RE projects COD to June 30, 2025. For COD every year thereafter, ISTS charges will be levied at 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% of applicable charges respectively for 25 years.

5. Green energy open access rules: The MoP has issued these rules, which include measures for banking settlement cycles, new charges, and exemptions for offshore wind energy projects from additional surcharges. This will enable consumers to purchase green energy based on their consumption. These rules are intended to align with the existing ones. The amendments came into effect from January 27, 2023.

6. National Wind Solar Hybrid Policy: Recognizing the complementarity of wind and solar, the National Wind Solar Hybrid Policy was announced in 2018. The policy provided for flexibility in a share of wind and solar components in a hybrid project, subject to the condition that the rated power capacity of one resource be at least 25% of the rated power capacity of another resource for it to be recognised as a hybrid project.

7. National framework for energy storage: The MoP announced a national framework for promoting energy storage systems. The main objectives of this framework are to encourage development of energy storage systems (ESS) while reducing reliance on fossil fuel plants to ensure a steady supply of round-the-clock RE, cut down emissions, and minimize energy costs. It also aims to promote innovation in energy storage technologies, enable fair access to energy storage for all segments of the population, and improve grid stability and dependability through the deployment of ESS. In addition, the framework intends to use policy and regulatory measures, financial and fiscal incentives and performance-based incentives to assist in the development on installation of ESS. A long-term trajectory for ESO has also been issued by the Power Ministry to ensure that sufficient storage capacity is available with obligated entities. ESO shall gradually increase from 1% in F24 to 4% by F30 (annual increase 0.5%). This obligation shall be treated as fulfilled only when at least 85% of the total energy stored is procured from RE sources on an annual basis.

## Power Transmission Segment

Relevance of power transmission: Timely connectivity is critical for seamless integration of renewable energy and ensuring continued supply of power at the optimal generation mix and cost. Higher-voltage lines and additional substations are also important for a higher quantum of power to be carried longer distances.

## Key issues – power transmission:

1. Transmission delays in the past have stemmed from right of way issues and land acquisition challenges.

2. More recently, supply chain challenges across transformers, gas insulated switchgear (GIS), substations (especially 765kv level), and high-voltage direct current (HVDC) equipment, conductors, reactors, cables, etc. have been posing risks of delays.

3. Grid stability concerns have surfaced with increase in variability from rising share of renewables in the generation mix.

4. Within the renewables realm, wind developers are facing challenges getting substation connectivity because wind requires at least two years of weather data to determine the viability of a wind site.

#1 – draft transmission capex plan: Per the recently released National Electricity Plan for transmission (January 2024), capex of Rs4.75tn would be incurred over F22-27. This includes interstate transmission (ISTS) capex of Rs3.14tn and intrastate capex of Rs1.62tn. Further, the plan envisages similar capacity over F27-32. It considers peak demand of 277by F27 and 366GW by F32, with RE (including hydro) of 537.6GW by 2030.

## Oil & Gas Segment

Relevance of oil & gas infrastructure: Although India has one of the largest populations in the world, energy consumption per capita still lags behind global averages and is \~10% that of the US. We expect India's energy consumption per capita to grow as cheaper energy reaches more of the population via infrastructure growth.

Significance – gas pipeline infrastructure: Pipelines offer the safest and most economical way of transporting gas. The government envisages an interconnected National Gas Grid to ensure adequate availability and equitable distribution of natural gas in all parts of the country. The PNGRB expects a three-fold increase in natural gas consumption – i.e., from 188mmscmd to 500mmscmd by 2030. The government has sought to increase the share of natural gas in India's primary energy mix from 6.5% currently to 15% by 2030, which would help the nation achieve net zero by 2070.

Consumption: India consumed nearly 261.5MT of oil in F24, of which \~89.7% was met through imports. Similarly, on the gas side, consumption grew 19% YoY from 158MMSCMD in F23 to 188MMSCMD in F24, but domestic production has increased from \~70MMSCMD (F16-21 average) to 89MMSCMD in F24. India's dependence on imports for natural gas and LPG stands at \~52% and \~57%, respectively, as of F24.

## The government's plan:

In order to give a fillip to the gas-based economy, the government is focusing on enhancing domestic gas production, fast development of gas infrastructure, as well as development of the gas market by providing open access to gas infrastructure. At present, there is about 17,000km of natural gas pipeline across the network that is operational, and the government envisages developing an additional c. 15,500km of pipeline to complete the National Gas Grid; different elements of this are at various stages of development. The government is also working on multiple areas to increase domestic production, reduce transportation costs, improve energy efficiency and productivity, accelerate the biofuel economy, increase refining capacity and explore areas like coal regasification. It is also looking at expanding the city gas distribution network to provide clean and green fuel.

#1 – oil and gas exploration: The government aims to double the net area being explored for oil and gas to 0.5mn sq. km. by 2025 and 1mn sq. km. by 2030. Favorable government policies revolving around concession for early monetisation, marketing and pricing freedom, as well as a shift in focus from revenue to production maximisation have favored investment in the Exploration and Production (E&P) industry.

#2 – refining capacity: India plans to expand its crude oil refining capacity to 310MMTPA by 2028 and targets a further increase to 450MMTPA by 2030 from 257MMTPA at present (F24).

#3 – coal gasification: The government has set a target of gasifying 100 million tonnes of coal into value-added products by 2030. Indian PSUs (public sector units) have planned an investment of Rs350bn for setting up surface coal gasification projects. In January 2024, the government also announced Rs85bn of assistance for coal gasification projects.

#4 – strategic reserves: Under the Strategic Petroleum Reserve Programme, India has in aggregate 5.3mnt of strategic crude oil reserves in Vishakapatnam (1.33mnt), Mangalore (1.5mnt), and Padur (2.5mnt). Under Phase 2, the government has been targeting additional storage of 6.5mnt since 2021.

#5 – LNG terminals: The ministry has floated a draft LNG policy that aims to increase the country's LNG regasification capacity from 47.7mntpa at present to 70MTPA by 2030 and 100MTPA by 2040.

#6 – gas and pipeline infrastructure: The government also seeks to expand the natural gas grid to 35,500km by adding another 17,000km of pipelines with investment potential of Rs700bn over the next few years.

#7 – city gas distribution: This program envisages the expansion of the CGD networks to 300 geographical areas (GAs), which would provide connectivity to 100% of the population and enable the supply of PNG to households and industrial and commercial units and CNG to vehicles. The government estimates the total capex required for this would be Rs1.2tn over 10 years.

## Telecom Segment

Relevance of telecoms: India's fast-growing digital adoption and data consumption require a commensurate addition of telecom infrastructure. Currently, India has the secondlargest telecom user base globally with \~1.2bn subscribers as of March 2024 and has an overall teledensity of \~84.5% in F24 vs. 84.51% in F23. Of this, \~924mn (March 2024) are data subscribers (wireline + wired broadband data subscribers) vs. 847mn in March 2023.

The average data consumption per subscriber of Reliance Jio is c. 26.9GB per month vs. 10.9GB in F19; Bharti Airtel, the other key operator, recorded average data usage of 22.3GB per wireless subscriber per month in F24 vs. 0.3GB in F14.

The industry has shown exponential growth over the last decade, driven by increasing digitalization, network availability, improving speed, and tariff affordability. The trends are expected to remain strong, given the increasing use of technology, focus on digitalization, and new and upcoming trends like AI adoption.

Per the Indian government (May 2022), 5G will contribute US\$450bn to the Indian economy over the next 15 years. Further, there has been an expected shift towards sixth generation (6G), on which the government has formed a 6G innovation group.

Key issues – telecom: Several telecom infrastructure projects have been languishing for decades owing to a lack of sensible and data-based integrated planning. Concerns have regarded procedural timelines, right of way, financial viability of projects, ease of financing, digital divide, spectrum management, licence tenure, fast-paced technology advancements, and cybersecurity.

Also, given the thrust on localization, the capability and capacity development of the domestic supplier base has been one of the areas to be addressed. Low fiberization of towers in India vs. other countries has also led to slower adoption of 5G in the country.

## The government's plan:

1. Optical fibre cable infrastructure plans: Optical fibre network additions are critical to explore the full potential of the next-generation networks of 5G and 6G. The ministry is leveraging the PMGS for the network planning. In collaboration with the Roads Ministry, it is working towards development of a network of digital highways by installing optic fibre cables (dark fibre) alongside the National Highway Corridors, which would help provide Internet connectivity in remote areas and also be instrumental in providing a direct plug-and-play model or "Fibreon-demand" model for Telecom/Internet Service Providers.

2. 5G subscriber addition targets: India's 5G subscriptions should reach 860mn by 2029, accounting for 68% of all mobile subscriptions by the end of 2029, per Ericsson.

3. Bharatnet: Under the plan, the government seeks to provide on-demand, affordable broadband connectivity of 2 Mbps to 20 Mbps for all households and institutions. Under the National Optical Fibre Network, it aims to provide 100 Mbps connectivity and connect all 250,000 gram panchayats (GPs, or village councils). As of April 2024, 212,229 GPs have been connected through the BharatNet project.

4. Prime Minister Wi-Fi Access Network Interface (PM-WANI): The government seeks to set up Public Data Offices (PDOs) to provide public Wi-Fi services across India.

5. Reducing the digital divide: The government is working on initiatives like broadband for all and connectivity to hilly and remote locations through satellite services to reduce the digital divide within the country. It has also come up with plans like the Telecom Development Plan, Aspirational District Scheme, and initiatives in the North-Eastern Region through the Comprehensive Telecom Development Plan (CTDP) over the past few years.

6. Fiberization: The government has set a target of 70% of fiberization of towers by 2025 and has set quarterly targets for this.

7. Localization of manufacturing: The PLI scheme (Rs122bn incentive allocation) has been launched for manufacturing of telecom and networking products. Of this, Rs40bn of incentives have been allocated towards a Design Led Manufacturing Scheme in order to enhance the local value addition of the products.

8. Skilled labour in the sector: By 2025, we believe India will need \~22 million skilled workers in 5G-centric technologies such as Internet of Things (IoT), Artificial Intelligence (AI), robotics and cloud computing.

## Reform initiatives:

## 1. Reforms supportive of operators and attracting investments:

a. Rationalization of adjusted gross revenue;

b. Rationalization of bank guarantees (BGs);

c. Rationalization of interest rates and removal of penalties;

d. Dispensing with the requirement of BGs (for auctions held henceforth) to secure installment payments;

i. Addressing the liquidity requirements of the Telecom Service Providers by way of moratorium/deferment.

## 2. Licencing-related changes:

a. Increasing spectrum tenure from 20 years to 30 years (in future auctions);

b. Permission for surrender of spectrum after 10 years (in future auctions);

c. Dispensing with the requirement of the Spectrum Usage Charge (SUC) for spectrum acquired in future spectrum auctions;

d. Removal of additional SUC of 0.5% for spectrum sharing;

e. Fixed time for spectrum auctions (normally in the last quarter of every financial year);

f. Requirement of licenses under the 1953 Customs Notification for wireless equipment replaced with self-declaration.

3. Mobile and Satellite Communication (MASC) policy: The policy is aimed at reducing the digital divide in the country and boosting telecoms infrastructure in underserved locations. The policy encompasses plans like satellite services, broadband for all, spectrum management and enhancing cyber security.

4. Some other key reforms include: PLI scheme in 2021, net neutralization rules in 2018, the Digital India program launched in 2015, and infra status granted to telecoms in 2013.

5. R&D focus: The Indian government intends to establish 100 labs for creating applications using 5G services in engineering universities as part of the Union Budget 2023, in order to realize a new range of possibilities, business models, and job potential. To drive the development of sixth generation (6G) technology, the Department of Telecommunications (DoT) has developed a 6G innovation group.

6. Deregulation of Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) norms: The government opened up the sector to 100% FDI under the automatic route in 2021.
