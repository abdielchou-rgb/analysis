## Market commentary

What began as a relatively quiet week culminated in some large price actions on Friday. 2y Treasury yields fell 11bp day-over-day, resulting in a decline of 16bp week-over-week, while the broad curve steepened. Front-end swap spreads also widened significantly. Part of the price action was driven by the weak ISM data, which saw its headline composite declining from 49.1 in January to 47.8, driven by moves down in several key components, including the production index (-2.0pts), the new orders index (-3.3pts), and the employment index (-1.2pts). Away from ISM, the headline for the University of Michigan consumer sentiment index was also revised down from 79.6 to 76.9 between the preliminary and final February reports. This was a pretty large downward revision, and the index now shows some weakening in sentiment relative to the 79.0 reported for January (the preliminary February print showed firming relative to January) (see US: 10:00am data reports show some weakening, D. Silver, 3/1/24).

More notably, Fed Governor Waller<sup>’</sup>s comments about the long-term profile of assets on the Fed<sup>’</sup>s balance sheet seemed to have caught markets off guard. In his speech on Friday, he noted that he “would like to see a shift in Treasury holdings toward a larger share of shorterdated Treasury securities. Prior to the Global Financial Crisis, we held approximately onethird of our portfolio in Treasury bills. Today, bills are less than 5 percent of our Treasury holdings and less than 3 percent of our total securities holdings. Moving toward, more Treasury bills would shift the maturity structure more toward our policy rate—the overnight federal funds rate—and allow our income and expenses to rise and fall together as the FOMC increases and cuts the target range. This approach could also assist a future asset purchase program because we could let the short-term securities roll off the portfolio and not increase the balance sheet.<sup>”</sup>

To be sure, this is not exactly a new way of thinking, as the most recently-released transcripts from the December 2018 meeting revealed a meaningful balance sheet discussion and in particular, around the composition of the Treasury portfolio, with various members, including Powell, supporting a greater allocation to T-bills. As our Treasury strategists commented previously, a shift towards the Fed holding more T-bills makes sense as it not only reduces the volatility of the Fed<sup>’</sup>s remittances but will also more closely match the maturity profile of the Treasury market given the events of the past year. They now see a higher risk that MBS paydowns could be reinvested in T-bills, though the bar to do so is still high given the 2019 playbook. If this comes to fruition, this would support T-bills and other short-dated Treasuries trading richer on a spread to SOFR (see Treasuries).

Where do we go from here? Despite the weak economic data on Friday, our economists note that none of it was particularly alarming. In fact, our US economists have revised up their 1Q24 GDP forecast from 1.75% to 2.25% saar given the recent trajectory for consumption data. On inflation, core PCE rose 0.4% in January, with the oya measure moving down from 2.9% to 2.8%, in line with consensus expectations. The strength in the January data came from super-core (core services, ex. housing), with the PCE price index for this aggregate jumping 0.6%, which was the largest monthly gain since December 2021. But despite a solid reading for the January PCE index, the year-ago inflation rate is still lower than 12 months ago, and we expect the inflation trend to continue to moderate over time (see US: Core PCE inflation is firm in January, D. Silver, 2/29/24). Next week<sup>’</sup>s employment report will shed more light on the state of the economy: we are expecting an increase of 200K in payrolls for the month of February and an unemployment rate at 3.7%.

## T-bills, ON RRP, Logan

Balances at the Fed<sup>’</sup>s ON RRP have been sticky lately. Even with T-bill outstandings increasing by nearly \$235bn in February, usage at the ON RRP remained above \$500bn most of the month, as they were partly supported by MMF inflows (+\$72bn MoM). As we noted previously, the rapid decline in ON RRP balances we<sup>’</sup>ve seen over the past year will likely stall out over the coming weeks in response to net negative T-bill supply in 2Q24 and MMF AUMs remaining elevated. In particular, our Treasury strategists anticipate net T-bill issuance to total -\$180bn in 2Q24, primarily concentrated in April (-\$130bn), in anticipation of tax receipts in April and June. Additionally, they believe there are downside risks to these forecasts (i.e., more negative net issuance) as April tax receipts could exceed their expectations. Indeed, while our Treasury strategists currently project the TGA to rise by \~\$240bn in mid-April, there<sup>’</sup>s a risk it could be significantly higher given the April 2022 episode where TGA balances rose by \~\$400bn amid a strongly-tightening labor market in addition to strong asset performance (see The rainy season for T-bill issuance is coming to a close, 2/29/24, J. Barry). Against this backdrop, MMF AUMs should remain elevated as investors continue to be attracted to an overnight asset that yields above 5%. Indeed, MMFs have already seen \$171bn of inflows YTD.

Speaking of ON RRP, Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan<sup>’</sup>s speech was notable on Friday as she stated that it would be appropriate to slow the pace of QT as the facility drains while also noting that the appropriate long-run size of the ON RRP program should be close to zero. In particular, she dismissed the idea of having a buffer at the ON RRP facility over the long run in order to minimize money market volatility, as doing so would create an uneven liquidity playing field between banks and non-banks.

Stepping back, it<sup>’</sup>s worth noting that the Fed<sup>’</sup>s operating model has always been for monetary policy to go through banks, not non-banks. Maintaining some sort of structural positive balance at ON RRP would imply that available liquidity is not properly being distributed to those that it was meant for (i.e., banks). Furthermore, usage at the ON RRP grew out of necessity when the Fed conducted massive QE at a time when there were also significant leverage constraints in the banking system. Its ideal size remains something close to zero— not an unfathomable outcome considering that was the state of play prior to 2021.

That said, we would also emphasize that while the long-run state might be for ON RRP to be operating near zero balances, over the short-term, the reality is that there is significant uncertainty over where the lowest comfortable level of reserves is. The only thing we know for sure is that reserves have become inelastic at higher levels, particularly following the regional banking crisis last March, and some minimum level of ON RRP balances might be needed to effect smooth functioning of the money markets as reserves increasingly approach “ample.<sup>”</sup> To that end, we continue to think that QT will stop when ON RRP drops below \$300bn as an acknowledgement of this short-term reality. But that<sup>’</sup>s not to say that ON RRP could not decline further depending on future market conditions. Regardless, given our expectation that ON RRP balances will likely become sticky in the near-term, neither a taper nor a halt to QT is a matter of urgency at the moment.

## Corporates are keeping more cash in their portfolios

Based on balance sheet data from S&P 500 non-financial companies, we estimate corporate cash investment portfolios registered \$2.3tn as of 4Q23. This is a year-over-year increase of \$125bn, though still below the peak of \$2.4-2.5tn observed shortly after the March Covid crisis. Even with lower portfolio balances relative to the Covid-era, it is notable that the amount of cash and cash equivalents held as a percentage of aggregate cash portfolios stood at 57% at YE23, surpassing the prior peak in 2020 when there was a dash for cash (Figure 1). We are clearly not in any sort of crisis right now, which only underscores corporates preference for cash in the current interest rate environment. As the \$1tn rise in MMF AUMs last year demonstrated, market participants have a strong desire to park cash in MMFs.

Meanwhile, the amount allocated to investment securities declined to \~43%. Interestingly, the split between short-term and long-term securities continues to hover around 50/50, though there was a slight uptick to long-term investments in the latter part of 2023 (Figure 2). In combination with the increase in exposure to cash and cash equivalents, this might suggest corporates are barbelling their cash investment portfolios, as they try to capture high yields at the very short end of the money markets while also trying to lock in yields further out the curve before the Fed cuts.
