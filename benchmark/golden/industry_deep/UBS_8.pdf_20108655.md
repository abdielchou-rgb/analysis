# EM by the Numbers: Is Goldilocks in transit to EM?

## Stuck in transit

US growth exceptionalism has waned, credit growth has bottomed out in most of EM, and China is installing a housing policy put. Typically this would make for a spell of strong EM asset outperformance. But a few things are different this time. First, global risk premia have already compressed ahead of Fed easing. Second, global growth is being spearheaded by services: spillovers to global trade may remain more muted than previous cycles. Third, China's housing policies look more likely to help the stock market more than growth (and therefore demand from the rest of EM). There are upside risks but UBS is looking more for US growth to catch down than for EM growth to strongly rebound in H2. Goldilocks may be stuck in transit for a while.

## Disinflation is EM's strength. Growth still work in progress

Sequential inflation is running below inflation targets in 43% of EM today. With GDP deflators near record lows in several large economies, nominal growth (which matters more for earnings) is sitting below 10y averages, a period of mediocre growth, in 70% of EM. Over the last two quarters, real domestic demand has sequentially been growing at a sub-3% rate - i.e., below the US - in 11 of 20 major EMs. Real GDP remains below its pre-pandemic trend in 17 of 20 EMs, suggestive of broadly negative output gaps. PMI gains in EM have flattened out since March. EM's real strength is swifter disinflation than DM; this should anchor front end yields. The upturn in growth needs to broaden more, though, for EM equity and FX outperformance to sustain.

## Dollar down-cycle plausible, but isn’t one to be played in EM

Over the next 3-6m, global inflation likely slowing faster than growth should underpin modest upside in EM equities and fixed income. We (still) see FX as the most fragile part of the EM asset spectrum; hold hedges here. Why? Carry is at much lower levels than 12-18m ago, and is unlikely to improve materially. EM export dynamics are highly imbalanced - strong in North Asia (China, Korea and Taiwan), especially semis, but stagnant elsewhere. Seen through the lens of export volumes, EM FX weakness this year should continue. Risk premia has repriced sharply in MXN but not elsewhere in EM. We are defensively positioned, seeing COP, CNH, and CZK as core underperformers ahead.

## China equities with strongest upside risk skew in H2. Modest returns elsewhere

With valuations still depressed relative to RoE and micro tailwinds in play - improving dividends/buybacks, better demand-supply dynamics for property developers in higher tier cities, we see China offering the strongest potential for an upside breakout in EM equities in H2. With 15% earnings growth already priced for 2025 for aggregate EM, the bar for big gains elsewhere looks high. We see Poland, Korea and Brazil as other potential beneficiaries from stronger EM growth / the Fed easing more than is currently priced.

## What it'll take for Goldilocks to arrive in EM

The upswing in global consumer confidence hasn't translated to improvements in business confidence, capex and trade so far. A shift here would be an upside risk for EM assets. China's Third Plenum and Politburo meetings next month will be watched closely – expanding the size / reducing funding costs for local governments digesting housing inventories is needed to improve consumer confidence. Trends in the US election and details of new political administrations in Mexico and South Africa could also provide upside risks for EM assets.

In marked contrast to DM, several large EM economies (China, India, Indonesia, Brazil, and South Africa) are seeing GDP deflators running near 20y lows. Nominal growth in these economies has thus slowed much more than real GDP would indicate. These different inflation dynamics in much of EM and DM point to different pressures on EM monetary policy, debt sustainability, FX carry and earnings growth.

Excluding two outliers - China and Turkey - EM nominal GDP growth is at the 38th percentile / 23rd percentile of its 10y / 20y distribution. It would be weaker still were it not for historically elevated nominal GDP growth in Korea and Taiwan following the robust recovery in export volumes and industrial production since last autumn on the heels of the tech recovery. While we expect DM to catch down in the coming quarters on this metric, which could create a window for tactical EM equity outperformance, ultimately we think EM growth needs to find a much stronger narrative for strong returns. We prefer China within EM equities for H2.

Despite this relatively moderate starting point, EM domestic demand has been growing at a sub 3% growth rate - I.e. below the US - in 11/20 major EM economies in recent quarters. Seen through this lens, there is an even stronger case for EM central banks to be easing than DM. Weak EM currencies may be the key obstacle here for central banks to deliver more easing than is currently being priced.

Unlike the US and Europe, just over half of EM is seeing sequential core inflation running less than 1% above its pre-pandemic (Q4-19) run rate. This dynamic hasn't been rewarded by bond markets - (real) yields are notably higher in EM ex Asia. While term premia could potentially rise into the US election, we do see pockets of opportunity in the front end of selected EM debt markets (e.g. Korea, India, Brazil), FX hedged, assuming the Fed is able to ease in Sept and US CPI slows as UBS expects. FX is an attractive vehicle to hedge these risks.

EM export growth has been heavily imbalanced - strong in North Asia - China, Taiwan and Korea - but stagnant elsewhere. This needs to broaden if EM FX is to be able to find a life outside of Fed easing hopes. The following two charts show Korea and Taiwan export growth today being entirely concentrated in tech products. China's cocktail of strong export volumes, weak export prices and weak credit flow can dilute the gains to exporters in the rest of EM as DM real wages pick up, keeping us very selective in EM FX.

## GDP growth

•Whatthe numbers say: We estimate MSCI- and GBI-weighted EM real GDP growth reached 5.1% y/y and 3.2% y/y in Q1, respectively, equivalent to the 50th and 19th percentiles of their 20y distributions, respectively. Excluding China, which has experienced a well-discussed slowdown with both cyclical and structural traits, MSCI EM growth is at the 67th percentile of its 20y distribution (or 72nd percentile as a spread to DM) and thus appears to have been generally resilient to the drags from weak global trade and tighter global monetary policy. Taiwan, where growth is at the 84th percentile of its 20y distribution, and to a lesser extent India and Brazil (52nd percentile), have been particularly resilient. This resilience, however, is much less clear when looking at nominal GDP growth, however, which matters more for earnings: on an MSCI weighted basis, EM growth is at the 26th percentile of its 20y distribution and its spread over DM at just the 15th percentile. Several EM economies (China, India, Indonesia, Brazil, South Africa) are seeing the GDP deflator close to 20y lows, in stark contrast to DM, e.g., US and Euro area GDP deflators at the 67th and 85th percentiles of their 20y distributions, respectively.

What they mean: EM's post-pandemic growth recovery has seen substantial variation. Brazil, Turkey, and Taiwan in particular have seen real GDP growth levels surpass their pre-pandemic (2015-19) extrapolated trends, while others such as the Philippines, Czechia, Thailand, Hungary, and Malaysia are more than 10% below trend. (The US is essentially at trend and Europe is about 6% below.) Weak GDP deflator inflation implies both scope to ease monetary policy, if sustained, but also poses greater risks to public debt sustainability in markets where rates have been forced higher postpandemic.

12-month outlook: Ahead, UBS economists expect MSCI EM real GDP growth to ease to 4.2% on a full year 2024E basis (from 5.1% y/y in Q1) but GBI EM growth to remain more stable at 3.3%. They expect China, India, Taiwan, Korea, Indonesia and Brazil to slow through the rest of the year, while South Africa, Colombia and the CEE3 should accelerate (from very weak levels in Q1). With DM, namely the US, also seen cooling, MSCI EM and MSCI EM ex China growth gaps to DM are seen falling only moderately.

## PMIs

•What the numbers say: Having bottomed out last July at 48.6, the global manufacturing PMI moved above 50 in February and stood at 50.9 in May. Over the last 3m, this global improvement has been spearheaded by DM economies - particularly the Euro area and the UK - while EM gains have flattened out. On our preferred measures - utilising, for an example, an average of S&P and ISM measures for the US, and NBS and Caixin measures for China - the gap between EM-DM manufacturing PMI gap is positive but has fallen back to levels last seen in Dec 2022. Using z-scores to control for very different levels of volatility in PMI data across economies, PMI levels remain particularly elevated in India, Korea and Brazil, and weak in South Africa, Poland and Czech. North Asia (Taiwan, Korea, and China) have been seeing the strongest improvements in EM (though not as much as the UK or Japan). In China, the gap between the NBS and Caixin PMIs has reached an unusually high 2.2 points in May (Caixin outperforming), meaning a) S&P global/EM PMIs are giving a rosier picture of China growth than the NBS PMI would imply b) exports are dominating more domestic focussed industries in China (though the steel PMI close to 50 does look consistent with less intense drag from the property sector compared to 6-12m ago).

•What the numbers mean: 1) PMI data doesn’t indicate that EM is generally taking the baton of global growth leadership from the US; that’s clearer for Europe than it is for EM. 2) While both global manufacturing and services PMIs have been improving, the relatively wide gap between the two (services stronger by 3.2 points in May; 10y avg 1.2 points) suggests that the trade intensity of today’s recovery may be weaker than in previous recovery phases. 3) Following re-acceleration in Q1, composite PMI output prices in the US and Europe edged back below Q4 levels in May; for EM there wasn't a Q1 bounce in the first place. 4) Import orders in the US and China aren’t signalling an improvement in EM exports ahead.

## Inflation

•Whatthe numbers say: From its 8.1% peak in mid-2022 and 6% a year ago, median EM inflation stood at 3.2% y/y in April. This is 40bp above its pre-pandemic (Q4-19) pace, but exactly in line with its long term (20y) average; in developed markets, by contrast, inflation is \~125bp above Q4-19 run rate and at the 79th percentile of its 20y distribution. As such, the spread of median EM inflation over DM is at just the 2nd percentile of its 20y distribution. EM Asia inflation remains particularly well contained – of 10 major global EM economies where sequential headline inflation (6m saar) is running below central banks’ inflation targets, 7 are in Asia (India and Indonesia are the exceptions). Measures of inflation momentum (3m and 6m 1/3 trimmed mean saar) suggest that the big drop in y/y headline inflation has run out of steam, on aggregate, though there are several economies where y/y inflation looks likely to ease through the summer: Colombia, Turkey, India, Korea and Romania are seeing disinflationary momentum and base effects.

Moderating inflation (through its support for consumption) has been a key driver of EM growth amidst an extended Chinese property downturn, subdued global trade growth and higher policy rates. Many EM central banks have been able to diverge from the Fed and ECB since last summer and ease rates due to disinflation progress.

•12-month outlook: Looking ahead, we think the balance of risks tilts in favour of ongoing EM disinflation: producer prices (running at just 0.1% on a 3m saar basis), international food and fuel price deflators, weak Chinese credit growth and manufacturing capacity utilisation, and PMI output prices do not point to meaningful inflation re-acceleration risks today. We have argued that output gaps, the weight of core goods in CPI baskets, proximity to (weak) China export prices, and generally lower profit margins in EM compared to the US should make EM disinflation a less complex affair than the US. A key signpost we’re watching is how services inflation in much of Latam and CEE behave ahead. Our belief is that stickiness in this component in recent months likely reflects catch-up real wage growth, indexation effects from strong price gains in 2023, and an ongoing demand rotation towards services that does not necessarily provide a lot of insight for the 12m ahead outlook. However, we could be wrong, particularly amid generally tight EM labour markets. We see pockets of value in EM local debt based on our inflation views, favouring India, Korea and Brazil front end rates (FX neutral).

## Industrial Production

•Whatthese numbers say: EM industrial production continues to languish (2.8%YoY is roughly the 19th historical percentile), with three quarters of all economies running below historical average production levels. By region, EMEA looks weakest relative to its own history (13th historical percentile): even though Russia is posting historically strong data, Israel is near all time low levels (3rd historical percentile), and central Europe as well as South Africa look weak. Asia ex-China (37th percentile) looks better, consistent with the trade data also being a bit better for Asia (tech cycle related); Taiwan, Vietnam and the Philippines are posting the strongest production growth (relative to their own history), while Thailand looks particularly weak. Latam (35th percentile) looks similar, but with strength in Brazil (65th percentile) offsetting weakness in Mexico and Colombia (11th and 17th percentile). China’s IP was at very weak levels in March (8th percentile) but then rebounded in April (45th percentile), in part because of two extra working days. Manufacturing and utilities strength is offsetting mining weakness. Note that despite DM production having posted some improvement the last 6 months, EM production has deteriorated, on average, in all 3 regions.

•Whatthey mean: EM IP will go where EM exports go (historical correlation 0.72), and exports are still weak. Although the global exports orders data have improved, it’s mostly been an Asia story, consistent with the upswing in the tech cycle in the region. For instance, in Taiwan data processing equipment exports have tripled and IP growth has swung from -21% a year ago to +7%YoY now, and production levels have improved accordingly. Beyond (pockets of) Asia, however, we have yet to see the consumption recovery and better orders data show up in improved production levels. PMI manufacturing export orders in EM are up 2pts the last 6 month and are back above their historical average (56th percentile), with only EMEA still sitting sub-50, but EM export volume growth is still negative and the weak IP numbers are consistent with that. China’s production has benefited from relative export strength but is also held back by property weakness. And we don’t seem to have reached a critical mass of consumer recovery in DM yet that can sustain positive EM export volume growth.

•12-month outlook: Global import demand weakness should subside in the 2nd half of this year, which should result in better trade and production numbers. Global growth momentum the last 6 months has doubled, and it should be just a matter of time before the trend drifts higher in (export) orders, triggers enough of an inventory drawdown that will require a step up in production levels. Beyond November (US elections), however, there is significant uncertainty over tariffs which may weigh on demand/production optimism (though in 2018 there was a burst of activity as firms attempted to stockpile goods before anticipated tariffs kicked in).

## Money and Credit growth

•What the numbers say: Both money supply and credit growth in EM are running at historically weak levels, though not quite as weak as DM. EM M2 growth (10.1%YoY) is running in the 18th historical percentile, while (valuation adjusted) credit growth (10.4%YoY) is running in the 22nd historical percentile, compared to roughly the 4th percentile in DM. By region, EMEA is a bit above average (64th percentile), with Poland and South Africa showing the weakest and Turkey the strongest credit growth (albeit decelerating now). Latam (19th percentile) is the weakest of the three regions, though note that our aggregate largely reflects Brazil/Mexico, and we exclude Argentina, where base money is currently running north of 180% YoY. Asia ex-China (51st percentile) is at average levels of credit growth, though that hides significant divergence within the region with Taiwan in the 92nd percentile but Thailand in the 5th percentile and South Korea also low (16th percentile). China’s credit growth is near all-time low levels (2nd lowest historical percentile, or 8.4% YoY), and a reflection of both near zero credit flowing to the property sector at present, and the weak nominal growth environment.

•Whatthey mean: Credit growth is being held back by (i) the still weak global demand outlook (our nowcast suggests momentum is improving globally but it is recent; we don’t see it clearly yet in business confidence surveys; and trade/production remain weak), (ii) restrictive real interest rates (the average policy rate is still more than 2 ½ pp above pre-Covid levels); and (iii) in the case of China, the ongoing property slump. New TSF in China printed a contraction in April for the first time since 2005, though that number also reflected weak government issuance (not included for the other EM aggregates discussed here) and may also have been affected by regulation and statistical rule changes. From a credit impulse perspective (the 2nd derivative), credit momentum troughed roughly last September and has been edging back towards more neutral territory since. Among the larger economies, Korea, Brazil, Poland, Mexico and South Africa, have all seen improving credit momentum the last 6m, while Turkey, China, Russia and India have seen momentum deteriorate (though only modestly in the case of the latter two).

•12-monthoutlook: Central banks have started to roll back restrictiveness globally but, at the median level, only a quarter of the EM hikes have so far been removed (a few markets like Chile, Hungary and Brazil are more advanced in their easing cycles). As borrowing costs come down, we should see credit growth pick up, though it will also require an improved demand outlook. We expect to see that materialize in the 2nd half of the year (better consumption lifts import demand and global trade/production). That would potentially also coincide with a stabilization of credit growth in China, as the property market sequentially bottoms, and equipment upgrading demand materializes. though the US election clouds the outlook, given the risk of and potential tariff uncertainty, cloud the outlook beyond December.

## Trade

•Whatthe numbers say: EM export growth has improved from a year ago but remains subdued (1.8%, y/y, 3mma for the median EM, compared to -6% 12m ago). Sequential momentum (3m saar), also at 1.8%, isn’t pointing to quick improvements ahead. Taiwan and Korea exports are currently outperforming – up 9-10% y/y, 3mma – supported by increasing semiconductor prices and flattering base effects (export growth 12m ago for these economies was -16% and -12%, respectively). Outside of tech, export growth in Korea and Taiwan appear weak. Commodity exporters such as Colombia (-9% y/y, 3mma), S Africa (-7%) and Malaysia (-5%) are seeing export growth underperformance, with 3m saar momentum also deeply negative. This isn’t just a commodity story: EM export volumes are barely growing in EM ex China (China export volumes were up 10% y/y in Q1, but EM ex China was down 0.4% y/y according to CPB – weaker still if one excludes Korea and Taiwan where volumes have been holding up well). The good news is that trade balances are generally at healthy levels across EM as import demand has been even weaker: on a GBI and MSCI weighted basis, we estimate 12m rolling trade balances are at the 63rd and 85th percentile of their 20-year distributions: Taiwan, Brazil, and Poland are seeing trade balance/GDP ratios at the 90th percentile or higher of their 20y distributions, albeit with shorter term measures (3m saar) indicating that these surpluses have peaked. By contrast, trade balance/GDP ratios in Thailand, Korea and the Philippines are weak by these economies’ historical standards, 25th percentile of their 20y distributions.

•Whatthey mean: About 3/4 of the variation in y/y EM export growth (values) can be explained by oil prices and the dollar alone. Assuming no change in these variables from today's levels, EM y/y export growth is likely to hover in a -2% to +3% y/y range into year end. More fundamentally, our EM real export lead indicator is pointing to only modest improvement in the outlook ahead. This signal is being held back by persistent weakness in China credit growth, US capex intentions and German new manufacturing orders. We believe trade balances (outside of copper exporters, and probably China where the housing recovery looks weak) are likely to come under pressure in most of EM as lower rates/improving consumer confidence intersect with a weak global industrial cycle.

•12-month outlook: Much hinges on: a) the degree to which China's recent housing stimulus rekindle import demand from the rest of EM; and b) whether the recovery in US and European consumer confidence broadens into capex and global trade. While we expect the Fed to begin cutting rates in September, we are generally not enthusiastic in EM FX partly because we see weak export growth persistent, and a lack of further improvement in trade balances, in general. We remain cautious on the CNH, seeing its large manufacturing trade surplus expansion in recent years creating risks of stronger DM tariffs and CNH weakness (back in 2018/19, the RMB TWI lost 7% whereas it has appreciated by almost 2% ytd). We see equities as a better expression of EM growth resilience than FX, in general, as rates (should) fall and consumption likely outperforms exports.

## Balance of Payments

•What the numbers say: A silver lining of EM’s moderate recovery is that current account imbalances are basically completely absent. The most recent data (mostly to Q1- 24) shows MSCI- and GBI-weighted EM current account balances at +3.1%/GDP and - 0.1%/GDP, respectively, 12m rolling, equivalent to the 58th and 68th percentile of their 10y distributions. Only 3 EM economies have current account deficits exceeding 3%/ GDP (Chile, Egypt and Romania), compared to 9 in mid-2022 or 8 at the time of the 2013 taper tantrum. However, because net FDI has cooled in several large economies (China, India, Indonesia, Mexico, Brazil, Turkey, etc), basic balances in MSCI- and GBIweighted EM are less impressive, at the 15th and 43rd percentiles of their 10y distributions, respectively. (This improves to the 35th and 48th percentiles if China is excluded). Generally speaking, smaller economies – Hungary, Poland, Chile, Colombia and Taiwan – have seen the strongest improvements in basic balances (80th percentile or higher of their 10y distributions) – while parts of Asia (China, Malaysia, the Philippines and Thailand) are seeing weaker positions (20th percentile or lower) relative to their historical norms.

•Whatthey mean: Basic balance surpluses in most of EM imply that a potential ‘higher for longer’ US rates environment, while it would very likely induce FX depreciation, should not fundamentally destabilise EM currencies. Also, portfolio inflows into EM appear to have been moderate over the last 12m, though some economies such as Hungary, Poland and Korea have seen strong portfolio inflows relative to GDP by their own historical standards, particularly into debt (though equity flows are recovering). FX reserves have been broadly stable in EM in recent quarters, though Turkey more recently is seeing strong improvements (net-net FX reserves are back to positive territory), while Indonesia, Korea and Malaysia have been seeing moderate outflows.

•12-month outlook: Improving credit impulses and real wage growth in much of EM, at a time when US capex seems likely to slow and China housing-related demand remains weak, are likely to make for a lack of big improvements in EM current accounts ahead. EM FX reserves could come under renewed pressure if the Fed doesn’t ease policy rates in H2 (37bps of easing priced at the time of writing), particularly in economies such as Malaysia, Chile, and even China where FX reserves adequacy is, according to the IMF, below adequate levels.

## Exchange rates

•What the numbers say: The median EM FX has depreciated 3.3% ytd in spot terms against the USD. For reference, the EUR has dropped 2.1% vs USD and the JPY is down 10%. While a handful of carry currencies have performed well this year - e.g. the Turkish lira has posted impressive 20% total returns ytd – carry strategies in general have been far less successful than they were last year - BRL, MXN IDR, HUF have posted negative total returns ytd thus far. Positive factors for EM FX; growth has been broadly improving in EM Ex China as real wages pick up; trade balances are generally at healthy levels; and Chinese authorities appear to be resisting CNY depreciation via strong countercyclical fixes. Negative factors: weak export growth, especially outside N Asia; carry available is generally low (we estimate that the average carry available on 12m FX forwards is at just the 8th percentile of historic ranges); for CNH – the most important EM currency – tariff risks continue to pose risks ahead; generally tight levels of EM FX implied vol risk premia. Our tactical short-term trading models don’t indicate that EM FX weakness has ‘overshot’ the move in US real rates, credit spreads, earnings estimates etc. By contrast, EM trade-weighted FX performance is screening strong relative to (weak) export volume growth.

•Whatthey mean: We are not confident that the carry available in most EM currencies will improve ahead, unless and until EM FX weakens more and potentially generates higher forward implied yields. Generally weaker output gaps, lower profit margins; higher exposures to global food inflation, and greater proximity to weak export prices from China arguably suggest even greater pressure on EM central banks to ease than the Fed, barring strong FX depreciation. We are also sceptical that China’s housing market stabilisation bring will bring EM FX relief. China's subdued credit flows, capacity utilisation and export prices pose challenges to trade growth in the rest of EM, while the risk of additional US tariffs on Chinese imports looks under-priced for the CNH, in our view.

•12-month outlook: We remain cautious on EM FX as a whole, preferring: TRY, EGP, PLN, INR. Preferred shorts: CNH, THB, COP, CZK, ZAR. Key catalysts for moving to a more positive view include a clear broadening of improving global consumer confidence into capex and global trade growth; and a more aggressive housing stimulus from China next month at the Third Plenum/Politburo meeting.

## Interest rates and spreads

•Whatthe numbers say: EM local bonds index lost 4.5% YTD, led by large FX losses vs the dollar, although bonds' 50bp surge in yields (GBI-EM weighted average) didn't contribute positively either. EM local bonds' surge in yields, albeit similar to USTs, sits in contrast to the progress in EM disinflation or/and elevated real yields (as discussed in the inflation section of this report). Likewise, this is at odds with about 70bps of rate cuts in EM on an equal weighted basis (or \~40bps on GBI-EM basis). Even more striking has been the texture of YTD performance of intra-EM bonds, where in the region with most rate cuts (Latam, Ceemea) underperformed the ones with none (Asia). For instance, average 10y yield in five economies where central banks eased the policy rates by >100bps YTD (i.e. HU, CL, CZ, CO and BR) recorded on average a 80bp surge in bond yields. Vice versa, average EM Asia bond yields were fairly insulated to the external conditions amidst ongoing muted CPI inflation pressures – thanks to China’s sub par growth and its possible disinflationary effect on the region. Indeed, only two major bond markets with YTD decline in yields sit in Asia i.e. CGBs (China) and IGBs (India), with 10y yields down 16bps and 25bps respectively. On the sovereign credit side, the asset class saw a modest positive YTD total return of +1.8% (EMBI GD) as carry income more than offset losses due to the rise in UST yields (10s up 55bps) and a slight pickup in EM spreads. The YTD positive return has been spearheaded by the HY segment – particularly Egypt on the back of large FDI inflows and an enhanced IMF deal (contributing alone 0.7ppt contribution to the index return YTD), Ecuador (+0.6ppt) and Argentina (+0.6ppt). By contrast, in the IG space, A and BBB buckets YTD returns were close to zero, presenting slight outperformance vs. US IG (down 1.3% YTD).

•What they mean: The above mentioned divergence between disinflation/rate cuts and intra-EM local bond yields leave us with three lessons for 2H. 1) As EM and US disinflationary narrative gathers pace in 2H, part of the above mentioned surge in yields should reverse. This leaves EM local debt in a sweet spot. 2) FX is EM’s weak link, and its spillover on EM local debt performance should not be completely discounted. We’d recommend investors to position for a long select EM duration strategy, overlaid with hedges in EM FX. 3) Front end bonds (2-3y sector) might be the best tenor to position for EM disinflationary theme, and not the long end. Put other ways, lesson from the performance of bond markets in rate cutting CBs is that the long end yields’ could stay elevated despite rate cuts as curves steepen in the context of depressed term premia in DM bonds, depressed EM credit spreads, UBS expectations of a surge in JGB yields, and a crowded schedule for elections including in US. In the sovereign credit space, we continue to argue that valuation is too demanding particularly at the IG space (currently 1.5-1.6 STD below 10y avg. across AA/A/BBB) as markets largely priced in for a US softlanding outcome. We estimate that EM spreads could potentially widen by 75bps/165bps by year-end on alternative no-landing/mild recession scenarios (based on UBS DM credit team analysis). On the supply side, a pickup in issuance is possible later this year while most BB-rated sovereigns and a few single-B names yield comfortably below 10%. We calculate that EMBI GD economies net supply fell to \$106bn in 1Q24 (4Q sum) from a peak of \$223bn in 1Q21.

•12-monthoutlook: With median EM inflation only 40bp above its pre-pandemic (Q4- 19) pace vs. 125bp for DM, we continue to see pockets of value in EM local debt. Specifically, UBS expects the central banks in Latam, and CEEE3 to ease much faster than the Fed and EU, and faster than the market pricing over the next twelve months. As such, we expect EM local debt - amidst elevated real yields vis-à-vis DM - to record about 40bps decline in 10y yields (on a GBI-EM weighted average). This should translate to ex-FX returns of 5-7% in H2. We like front end local debt (FX hedged) in India, Korea and Brazil and steepeners in Mexico and Chile (some of these economies are only priced for a rate cut in 12m). By contrast we're cautious on US duration and the front end in Poland and S Africa. In credit, valuations look expensive (EMBI spreads screen \~40bps / 1.5 s.d. tight), led by IG. Await better levels to re-engage here. Although EM rating outlooks is titled favorably (26 positive outlooks vs. 15 negatives using S&P/Moody’s/ Fitch), the negative convexity in spreads due to the above-mentioned US growth scenarios should keep one selective. In the B space, we continue to like Turkey (+\$67bn in net reserves since late Mar amid de-dollarization and inflows) and Egypt (where we expect excess of FX inflows of \$7bn-\$8bn in fiscal 2024/25); but would engage only on dips as valuation in these two markets does not screen as cheap. In the BB space we are more cautious on Brazil (recent downward adjustments to fiscal targets, coming BCB gov appointment). We also stay overweight Hungary in the BBB space.

## Stock markets

EM equities are up 5.9% YTD, but have lagged DM equities which are up 11%. EM Asia had the strongest performance, up 9% YTD, while LatAm and EMEA declined by -11% and -1%, respectively. In EM Asia, Taiwan is the best performer, up 22.0%, followed by India (+11.0%), China (+9.3%) and Malaysia (+9.2%) (Figure 142EM markets' pfonc dvlui () . Meanwhile, Thailand and Indonesia are the biggest laggards, down 10.5% and 9.7%, respectively. Korea is also amongst the biggest underperformers, down 4%, in contrast to its other cyclical counterpart, Taiwan. LatAm’s performance was mainly dragged by Brazil (-15.1%) and Mexico (-9.0%). In EMEA, Turkey is the best performing market (+36.5%), while Egypt is the worst performer overall (-33.6%). By sectors, Semis, Consumer Services and Media & Entertainment outperformed in EM, with the biggest drags were REITs, Pharma and consumer staples & retails.

EM total returns so far have been driven by PE re-rating as well as earnings growth, largely offsetting the impact of currency (Figure 3). Consensus forecasts EPS growth of 21.2% and 15.5% for EM in 2024 and 2025, faster than the 8.2% and 12.7% for DM, respectively (Figure 142 . However, EM continues to see EPS downgrades, while DM is seeing upgrades. EM valuations remain undemanding, with the valuation discount to DM at 33%, higher than the 10-year average of 28%. This is in part due to China’s inexpensive valuations. MSCI China is a key overweight for UBS, given resilience in internet and consumer earnings, increasing focus on policy reforms and capital returns and favourable risk/reward stemming from ‘national team’ support. Investor positioning also remains low, with EM funds reducing their overall China holdings by 2.9% in Q124 (report), but we think this may be bottoming. Meanwhile, the sustained performance of the Asia tech sector on the back of the cyclical recovery and AI optimism meant valuations are now at decade-high premiums relative to EM (Figure 135 ). India’s valuations remain extraordinarily expensive while companies’ fundamentals are ordinary.

Our year-end 2024 MSCI EM index target is 1,020, which implies - 5% downside from the current levels. Strength in DM economies relative to EM, delayed Fed rate cuts and strong USD make it a tough setup for EM equities to outperform DM, in our view. However, relative EM valuations are supportive, coupled with a stronger earnings outlook. Our preference is for markets that have attractive fundamentals and being favourably exposed to these themes: 1) strong domestic economic support to index EPS, 2) higher for longer oil prices, 3) higher for longer US rates and 4) exposure to the Asia tech cycle. We are overweight China on better earnings expectations on consumer-related sectors (55% of MSCI China), underpinned by inexpensive valuations. Against this, we have turned neutral Korea and Taiwan as relative valuations are now at decade high premiums on the tech upcycle.
