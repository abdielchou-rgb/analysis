# APAC Economic Perspectives MAS: Not even contemplating easing

## MAS kept the S\$NEER policy unchanged, less dovish overall

On 29 January, MAS kept the S\$NEER policy band rate of appreciation unchanged, with no change to the width of the band or level at which it is centred, in line with UBS and consensus. We had expected a hawkish monetary policy statement (MPS). Parsing the MPS (link), we think this conveys a clearly less dovish tone. MAS sounds more optimistic on growth, highlighting it expects the negative output gap to "narrow in the second half of 2024", and that it expects the economy to "strengthen in 2024, with growth becoming more broad-based". (See Figure 1). Core inflation had surprised MAS to the upside, and is now expected to "rise in the current quarter" vs. "on a broad moderating trend" in its October MPS. Even so, MAS kept its full year core inflation forecast unchanged at 1.5–2.5%, and downplayed the travel inflation upside to "conditions in tourism markets abroad". MAS also revised down the headline CPI inflation forecast range by 50bp from 3–4% to 2.5–3.5% due to lower COE premiums. MAS still sees its policy as "appropriate" (appropriately restrictive), although removing the reference to "tight" policy that it had in the October statement.

## Administered prices and travel costs the main driver of recent price increases

By our estimates, administered prices would add 33bp to core CPI. (+3bp for polyclinic services and +17bp for bus & train fares, +13bp for point-to-point transport i.e. taxi fares) The bigger contributor is travel costs. Holiday expenses (package tours & hotels) and airfares have risen by \~7% over the past three months i.e. an annualized rate of almost 30%, adding about 62bp to core CPI. Our on the ground checks suggest that these are likely to remain elevated through March due to a series of large-scale concerts, but unwind rapidly thereafter. Factoring in the sharp increase in December core CPI and upside to travel costs, we raise our MAS Core Inflation forecasts from 2.8% to 3.0% for 2024, and expect core inflation to ease to 2.7% by Q4. While we expect GST passthrough this year to be smaller than last year due to a less tight labour market, upside risks remain due to the uncertainty from the pass-through and government budget.

## MAS can still afford to be patient with labour demand cooling

We think that the MAS can afford to be patient for now. We see tangible signs that wage growth might cool further (given falling vacancy rates), which will cap demand pressures. Our view is that travel related cost increases are transitory. Furthermore, unless growth is towards the top end of the government's 1-3% growth forecast, the output gap doesn't close fully. The REER remains restrictive from a demand perspective, in the sense that it has risen by more than productivity gains have warranted. However, if upside risks to inflation materialize, such that m/m price increases do not progress in a way that allows core inflation to fall comfortably below 3% by end 2023, we do not rule out slope steepening to ensure that core inflation returns to 2% by 2025.

## Strategy: Neutral SNEER for Q1; Look to receive rates on upticks

With MAS expected to stay put for the foreseeable future, S\$NEER is merely left as a carry trade. Admittedly, this is quite a lucrative carry (1.0x on vol adjusted terms, at 80th %ile of 15+ year history), and should continue to buoy S\$NEER 1-2% above the midpoint of the band. However, we find the risk-reward in OIS receivers as more attractive, and would fade the upticks in 2-3y sector. Three reasons: 1) easing sequential core inflation amidst slowings exports volume growth suggests that the fundamentals pushing up the REER in '22-23 (+15% since Dec-21) are no longer favourable. 2) SG’s real overnight rate has already risen 1.3% above 15y average (to 85th %ile); with this restrictiveness poised to increase further as core CPI edges lower to about 2% by H2 (ex GST effect). 3) Amidst a solid BoP position and MAS's FX appreciation stance, we’d expect any signs of policy easing to be visible first in rates.
