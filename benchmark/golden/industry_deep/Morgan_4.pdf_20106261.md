## China’s policy easing: A comparison with 2015-16

The challenges faced by the economy share a lot of similarities to 2015-16

• Policy responses have some overlap but are also different, overall more modest this time

• Here we compare fiscal, monetary, housing and exchange rate policy responses

The government can step up policy easing, more importantly rebalance policy support

Since the Politburo meeting last July, China has rolled out a series of easing measures to stabilize growth. The pace of policy easing has been modest and piecemeal, covering a range of areas including fiscal, monetary, housing, exchange rate and capital market policies. Many in the markets express disappointment at the pace of policy easing, rasing questions around the scope for policy easing, and its effectiveness and magnitude compared to prior episodes. In our view, the policy easing in 2015-16 (link) serves as a good benchmark in that the economy is facing similar challenges and policy responses are also similar.

## Macro backdrop: similar to 2015-16

In both the 2015-16 and 2022-23 episodes, the economy faced similar challenges of a housing market downturn, weak domestic demand, deflation pressure and an economic slowdown, and by extension currency devaluation and capital outflow pressure. In addition, local government hidden debt posed a significant fiscal challenge in both episodes.

On the macro front, both episodes saw growth struggle amid deflation pressure. China<sup>’</sup>s real GDP growth fell below 7%oya in 3Q15, for the first time since 1Q09. The change in the GDP deflator was negative in 3Q15 and 4Q15, dragged by prolonged PPI deflation (54 consecutive months from March 2012 to August 2016). This time, average real GDP growth in 2022-23 registered at 4.1% per annum, and the GDP deflator has been negative for three consecutive quarters since 2Q23.

Housing market corrections play an important role in both episodes. Measured by home price (Figure 1) and housing activity declines (Figure 2), the current cycle and 2014-15 represent the two most severe downturns in China<sup>’</sup>s housing market history. In our view, what is different is that, the 2014- 15 housing market correction was mainly cyclical, while this time it is not only cyclical but also structural (link).

The local government hidden debt problem stands out in both episodes. In 2015, the government started a large-scale, multiyear debt swap program (2015-18) to deal with the massive local government hidden debt accumulated since the 4-trillion yuan stimulus in 2008, with 16 out of 24 trillion yuan debt swapped into official local government debt. A new Budgetary Law took effect in January 2015, resulting in a local gov ernment funding shortage and creating a significant fiscal drag in 1H15. This is similar to the current situation, as the collapse in land sales and large COVID-spending have led to fiscal difficulties for local governments and a significant fiscal drag in 1H23. Meanwhile ballooning local government hidden debt results in a new round of local government hidden debt resolution (here and here).

CNY weakened in both episodes. The PBOC surprisingly reformed the currency regime on August 11, 2015, and allowed a 2% one-off CNY depreciation. The market reacted with panic and the USD/CNY moved from 6.40 in mid-August 2015 to 6.63 by June 2016, and the trade-weighted-index (TWI) also depreciated by 5.1%. Implied capital outflows averaged US\$82.3bn per month between August 2015 and April 2016. This time, USD/CNY moved from 6.35 to 7.31 between April 2022 and September 2023, and monthly capital outflows averaged US\$39.4bn (Figure 3).

## Policy responses: Similar but different

Facing similar challenges, the government has adopted simi lar approaches. First, policy responses include fiscal, monetary, housing and currency stabilization measures (we leave out capital market policy discussion in this note). Second, in both cases, the government reiterates an intent to avoid any massive policy stimulus. Third, there is a gradual shift from conventional to unconventional approaches in the growth stabilization efforts.

But the policy responses are not exactly the same. Policy easing this time has so far been modest compared to the 2015-16 episode, reflecting a shift in focus to high-quality growth since the 19th Party Congress in 2017 (e.g., a different attitude toward the housing market) and more limited room for fiscal and monetary easing. Hence, detailed policy measures exhibit important differences in the two episodes.

## Fiscal policy support more limited

There is a similar turnaround in fiscal policy between the two episodes. In 2015, the government announced increasing the budgetary fiscal deficit to 2.4% of GDP (vs. 2.1% in 2014). However, because of fiscal contraction in off-budgetary items (tightened LGFV borrowing and decline in land sales), the augmented fiscal deficit was lower than in 2014 (from 9.4% of GDP in 2014 to 8.9% in 2015). The fiscal drag was notable in 1H15, then the July 2015 Politburo meeting reversed course, followed by a rebound in local government bond issuance in 2H15-1H16 and quasi-fiscal support by three policy banks (after an 800bn yuan capital injection in 2H15). The augmented fiscal deficit rose to 10.1% of GDP in 2016, mainly via expansion of budgetary items (budget deficit and special local government bond, see Figure 5).

The fiscal dynamics are similar in 2023. The government announced a modest increase in the budgetary fiscal deficit to 3.0% of GDP (vs. 2.8% in 2022), but the contraction in actual utilization of special local government bonds and decline in land sales jointly led to a significant fiscal contraction in 1H23. Similarly, the July Politburo meeting reversed course, followed by 1 trillion yuan additional fiscal deficit in late October. Still, the augmented fiscal deficit in 2023 was lower at 12.1% of GDP (vs. 12.9% in 2022). Looking into 2024, we expect the fiscal drag will discontinue, replaced by a neutral fiscal policy stance. We forecast the augmented fiscal deficit to increase marginally to 12.2% of GDP in 2024, also mainly via expansion in budgetary items (link).

Clearly, the magnitude of fiscal support this time is more modest than in 2015-16. This perhaps reflects the more limited room for fiscal stimulus: the augmented fiscal deficit is higher in 2022-23 than in 2014-15, hence it needs a higher fiscal deficit to achieve the same fiscal thrust (Figure 5). In addition, the aggregate level of public debt, including central and local government debts and local government hidden debt, has increased from 50.5% of GDP in 2014 to 98.8% in 2022.

Market investors have been disappointed at the absence of consumption stimulus measures in this round of easing. Such measures were also absent in 2015-16. However, the fiscal support in 2015-16 focused on tax and fee reduction (from 22.1% of GDP in 2015 to 21.4% in 2016). This time, a tax cut is no longer a policy priority. Fiscal support has mainly been used to support investment and mitigate funding shortages for local governments.

## Monetary policy goes structural

Monetary easing in both episodes follows a similar path, starting from conventional aggregate monetary policy instruments (rate cuts and RRR cuts) and then shifting to structural monetary policy instruments via the PBOC<sup>’</sup>s balance sheet expansion.

The magnitude of rate cuts and RRR cuts is smaller this time. In the 2015-16 episode, the PBOC cut benchmark deposit rates six times between November 2014 and October 2015 (totalling 165bps), and the 7-day reverse repo rate was lowered from 4.1% to 2.35% during the same period. Meanwhile, the PBOC cut RRR four times, totalling 250bps (from 19.5% to 17%). This time, the PBOC cut the 7-day reverse repo rate twice in 2023, totalling 20bps (to a record low of 1.8%) and cut RRR three times between March 2023 and February 2024 (totalling 100bps), with average RRR lowered to 7% (Figure 6). TSF growth picked up from 12.3% in May 2015 to 16.1% in April 2016 in the previous cycle, but has stayed relatively stable this time (Figure 7). We have elaborated on the various reasons for smaller rate cuts this time (link), such as more limited policy room and the constraint from banks<sup>’</sup> NIM pressure.

In both episodes, structural monetary policy operations play an important role. The PBOC introduced pledged supplemental lending (PSL) in April 2014 and medium-term lending facility (MLF) in September 2014. The notable expansion of MLF/PSL operation in 2015-17 (cumulative net increase of 2.4 trillion yuan for PSL and 3.9 trillion yuan for MLF), especially in 2016 (net increase of 971bn yuan for PSL and 2.8 trillion yuan for MLF). In particular, the PSL operation played a critical role in driving the rebound in the housing market, as the money was allocated to local governments via policy banks, then to beneficiary households in the shanty town renovation program in the form of monetary subsidy. This quickly drove up housing demand and home prices, and reversed the housing market sentiment.

This time around, the PBOC<sup>’</sup>s balance sheet in OMO/PSL/ MLF/SLF increased by 4.66 trillion yuan in the five months after the late July Politburo meeting, comparable to the expansion in 2016 (Figure 8). Moreover, our forecast includes a 1-trilliion yuan package funded by PSL in 2024 to support public housing, urban village and public infrastructure development. Though the scale is less than in 2015-17, it will play an important role in mitigating the decline in real estate investment (link).

However, this time is different in that: (i) the housing market correction was mainly cyclical in 2014-15, but is both cyclical and structural this time; (ii) the government<sup>’</sup>s attitude towards the housing market is also different. ## Cushioning CNY weakness

In both episodes, the economic challenge has been reflected in the currency market. CNY not only depreciated against the USD but also in basket exchange rate terms. The one-sided CNY depreciation expectations triggered policy responses in both episodes, especially when USD/CNY approached sensitive thresholds (7.00 in 2016 and 7.30 in 2023).

However, the approaches to defend the currency are different. In 2015, the PBOC committed to keep daily fixing in line with the closing spots on the previous day, hence it had to sell USD to mitigate CNY depreciation pressure. In the two years of 2015-16, China<sup>’</sup>s FX reserve fell by US\$832 billion (from US\$3.84 trillion at end-2014 to \$3.01 trillion at end-2016). This time, the PBOC<sup>’</sup>s strategy is different. From August to October, the PBOC kept daily fixing almost unchanged and allowed it to deviate from spot price (link). As a result, the FX reserve has stayed almost unchanged. As the financial market started to price in the Fed<sup>’</sup>s policy pivot, the CNY has reversed the weak trend since November and stabilized around 7.15-7.20 recently. Still, the reversal of the US-China interest rate differential since 2022 is a new phenomenon, and it will continue to stay throughout 2024, limiting the CNY strength against the USD for the foreseeable future.

## Bigger, rebalanced policy support needed

The challenges faced by the Chinese economy are similar to the 2014-15 situation, and the pressure comes from both cyclical and structural slowdowns. Policy responses in 2015- 16 did not repeat the massive stimulus as observed in 2008- 09, but overall the magnitude of policy easing then was larger than in the current cycle. In the 2015-16 episode, economic growth stabilized at around 7% in 2016-18. In our view, in order to achieve the growth stabilization objective in 2024 and the coming years, the government needs to step up policy efforts. This could mean bolstered fiscal and monetary support and more forceful measures to stabilize the housing market. More importantly, the government needs to change the policy direction, by rebalancing policy support between investment and consumption, between manufacturing and service upgrade, and between SOEs and non-SOE sectors (link).
