## US Fixed Income Overview

## From the last mile to the road ahead

● Yields drifted higher over the week, supported by hawkish Fedspeak and better-than-expected data, while corporate spreads remained rangebound as well-received 4Q23 earnings were balanced with continued robust issuance

Despite concerns around later and slower Fed cuts in the face of resilient economic data, we maintain our forecast that the Fed is set to cut rates by the June meeting, as growth and inflation moderates into mid-year

Economics: We project headline CPI rose 0.1% m/m (2.9% oya) and core CPI increase 0.22% (3.7% oya) in January. We look for a 0.9% decline in January retail sales while the important control group remains flat

● Treasuries: We recommend adding 5-year duration longs: yields are near the top of their 2-month ranges, with less Fed easing priced in, even as our fundamental outlook is broadly unchanged, and we expect softer-than-expected data in the week ahead. Remain neutral on breakevens, but position for lower 5-year real yields for better risk/reward

● Interest Rate Derivatives: Remain bullish on volatility on the back of heightened policy uncertainty and lingering tail risk. Carry trades remain attractive but we prefer to implement them conditionally in a selloff due to tail risks: underweight 2s vs. a weighted blend of 3s/5s. Initiate asymmetric exposure to a rally by buying Reds versus near-fronts and 10s

● Securitized Products: Remain neutral on mortgages. Bank demand for Ginnies could become more focused on discounts, structured CMOs, or customs after a fast prepayment report. HPA came in \~5.7% in 2023, close to our expectations, and we project growth to remain flat in 2024

● Corporates: HG bond spreads remained rangebound despite equity markets moving higher, an active pace of supply with February likely shaping to be another above average issuance month and outperformance of Financials vs Non-Financials. We continue to see spreads heading higher in February

● Near-term catalysts: Jan CPI (2/13), Jan retail sales (2/15), Jan FOMC minutes (2/21)

Unlike many giddy fans this weekend, fixed income markets have already moved past their <sup>‘</sup>Super Bowl<sup>’</sup> of monetary, fiscal, economic, and banking surprises last week. Indeed the data calendar was light while Treasury auctions and 4Q23 corporate earnings were well received, whereas Fedspeak skewed hawkish. As Figure 1 illustrates several other FOMC participants expressed support for a “patient<sup>”</sup> stance, suggesting that the first ease will come either “at some point<sup>”</sup> or “later this year.<sup>”</sup> On net 10-year Treasury yields steadily drifted 16bp higher over the week as the broad 2s/30s curve steepened 4bp. Mortgage spreads likewise are roughly 2-3bp higher across the stack as OAS continues to trade directionally with rates while high grade corporate credit spreads were able to remain rangebound despite heavy issuance. Where do we go from here? We continue to prefer a short bias in longer-dated corporate spreads and remain neutral on mortgages. Meanwhile we turn bullish on duration given the backup in yields, supportive positioning, and disappointing economic data flow.

Indeed near-term risks from the data skew to the downside, with inflation and consumer spending reports next week likely to disappoint market expectations. We project headline CPI rose 0.1% in January (consensus: 0.2%), leading the oya rate to decline from 3.4% to 2.9%, with this moderation led in part by a large 1.8% decline in energy prices. Meanwhile we forecast core CPI increased 0.22% (consensus: 0.3%) last month, as the oya rate to drifted lower from 3.9% to 3.7%. Leading indicators suggest we should see a step down in the monthly pace of OER growth from 0.42% to 0.31% in January and we look for a substantial 1.9% reduction in used vehicle prices last month (see Economics). Turning to retail sales, we expect a 0.9% decline in the headline rate in January (consensus: -0.2%) alongside a flat reading for the important control group (consensus: +0.2%). However a jump in the seasonal adjustment factor should make the upcoming January data look unusually <sup>‘</sup>harsh<sup>’</sup>, whereas declining December seasonal factors made the previous report look unusually <sup>‘</sup>generous (see Focus: Expect noisy weak retail sales data for January, Daniel Silver, 2/9/24).

The recent backup in yields together with our expectations for weakness in the near-term data is supportive of lower yields, and we recommend longs in 5-year Treasuries. After the recent moves, intermediate yields are now back to their highest levels since the December FOMC meeting and the OIS forward market has also pared back Fed easing expectations for 2024 to only 112bp of easing. Furthermore, while positioning is not totally clean, the technical backdrop has become more supportive: out of our five preferred positioning indicators, only the macro hedge fund beta remains meaningfully long relative to its 1-year average, while all others are now within 1 standard deviation of their respective 1-year averages (Figure 2Our positng dcaely  hv b wk). Furthermore, the share of neutrals in our Treasury Client Survey rose to 74% this week, the highest share since April of last year and the 98th percentile over the last 10 years. Given these factors, we turn bullish and recommend adding duration in 5-year Treasuries (see Treasuries).

In inflation space, front-end real yields are also trading near their highest levels since just prior to the FOMC meeting, despite the shift in policy guidance over the last two months, and real yields should also be headed lower over coming months if our forecasts for growth and inflation prove correct (Figure 3). We recognise the risk that progress on the inflation front could stall over time, as supply side supports fade and if growth remains robust, which could keep the Fed from cutting as much as we forecast. However, breakevens are likely to widen in such an environment, limiting the upside risk to real yields. Thus, we think longs in 5-year TIPS offer attractive risk/reward in the current environment (see TIPS).

In derivatives, we highlight trades that are likely to outperform asymmetrically in a rally. One such example involves buying Reds versus near-fronts and 10s. For instance, as Figure 4 shows, the yield spread corresponding to buying the 4th and 7th SOFR futures contracts (30:100 weighted) versus the 2nd SOFR futures contract has been well correlated to 6M forward 10Y yields with a beta of 0.55, and currently appears too steep. Thus, buying the 4th and 7th SOFR futures contracts (30% and 100% risk weights) versus selling the 2nd SOFR futures contract (100% risk weight) and paying fixed in a 6M forward 10Y swap (50% risk weight) is a way to construct a trade that is likely to outperform in a rally, while also offering over 20bp of positive slide over 3 months. Our views on swap spreads remain unchanged - we continue to find value in swap spread wideners in the belly as they flag too flat to fair value, while front end swap spreads look too wide and we remain biased to narrowers in this sector (see Interest Rate Derivatives).

Since late last year, SOFR has been on a steady drift higher. The combination of QT and the continued increases in Treasury coupon collateral, exacerbated by dealer balance sheet constraints, has not only pushed daily settings higher in the fed funds corridor but also prompted spikes around recent month-ends/quarter-ends. SOFR now regularly sets 1-2bp above RRP (or 1-2bp below EFFR), versus 0-1bp above RRP (or 2-3bp below EFFR) for most of 2023 (Figure 5). During the January FOMC press conference Chair Powell noted some discussion on QT, with the details to be gleaned in the minutes released in two weeks<sup>’</sup> time. We expect a QT taper gameplan will be formally announced in March and implemented in April, and will involve the monthly Treasury caps being halved and the Fed looking to target about \$300bn of balances at the Fed<sup>’</sup>s ON RRP to effect smooth functioning of the money markets (see It<sup>’</sup>s all over now, QT, Economics, Rates, and Securitized Products Research, 1/12/24). If right, we could see QT end as soon as September of this year when there are still \$3.2tn of reserves in the system (see Interest Rate Derivatives: All Eyes on Washington, Srini Ramaswamy, 1/26/24). If we are right on the timeline and using an empirical model for estimating the spread, this would suggest the SOFR/FF basis settles closer to around -1bp to -1.5bp at this time. On the margin, given ongoing Treasury coupon issuance and continued balance sheet constraints, this might push it closer to -1.5bp to -2bp, or RRP + 4-5bp. For comparison, markets are pricing SOFR to trade 3bp above FF by the end of this year, which, all things considered, seems too narrow at the moment (see Short-Term Fixed Income).

High grade bond spreads are 1bp tighter on the week with our JULI at 110bp. In a relatively quiet week, focus was on the tail end of 4Q23 earnings which were well received. While this translated into equity market strength, the same could not be said for credit markets. This is to be expected given equities are trending markets while credit is rangebound. We also note some of this divergence has been driven by compositional changes, by a renewed focus on shareholders over creditors, a pickup in M&A activity, and given that HG companies are focusing on issuing credit. Indeed January was a record supply month across HG + HY + loan markets for a combined \$361bn as corporate treasurers see current spreads as attractive and this trend is likely to continue (Figure 6Combined crpat suly w J, v gh). With \$40bn issued in HG markets in the first five days of February, there is a risk that issuance may exceed the recent \$107bn February seasonal average. While markets have been able to easily digest this issuance, we think there are risks for spreads to widen and the curve to steepen. Over the medium term, increased M&A activity and fading recession fears are likely to negatively impact the credit profiles of Non-Financials. Meanwhile MTD 30yr issuance has represented 30% of supply, compared to 7.6% in January (see Corporates).

High-yield bond spreads tightened 21bp over the week to 364bp, the lowest level since April 2022, as investors absorbed better-than-expected earnings, an active primary market, and a limited macro calendar. Indeed a notable 3.9x as many HY companies have beaten (43%) EBITDA expectations as have missed (11%), whereas 2.1x as many HY companies have provided positive guidance positive (23%) than negative (11%). With HY spreads now 110bp tighter since the beginning of November, primary markets are producing the heaviest refinancing activity since 2021, after companies executed patience throughout 2022-23. This is particularly the case in leveraged loan space, where spreads continue to hover at lows since May 2022 and issuance has been robust with \$164.5bn YTD, of which \~50% is repricing, \~42% refinancing, and \~8% other. MTD CCCs continue to outperform Single Bs and BBs in both HY and LL (see High Yield).

Given heavy leveraged loan issuance, our analysts revisit CLO bond redemptions. CLO bonds are increasingly priced above par, with negatively-convex profiles not only diminishing upside but also tempting equity holders to call. While CLO growth is in focus following the busiest January ever, we think future de-leveraging as likely given amortization becomes too difficult to avoid for seasoned CLOs failing WAL/other tests. Our analysts review some optionality for managers and equity holders to partly negate this <sup>‘</sup>Great De-Leveraging<sup>’</sup> (see CLOs). Meanwhile we note that CRE CLO loan delinquencies are on the rise — reaching an all-time high of 3% during the January remit period — alongside a decline in prepayment speeds to their lowest levels since 2018, with 12m CPR at 10.8%. The elevated levels of delinquencies currently in the CRE CLO market have made managers more wary of forced deleveraging of relatively inexpensive CRE CLO vehicles. This has resulted in a record level of delinquent buyouts, for \$235mn in December 2023, in order to ensure exiting vehicles can pass their note protection tests. At the same time reinvestments and cash levels are on the decline (see CMBS).

While mortgage OAS generally moved higher alongside rates, the major highlight for MBS was a prepayment report which showed a sharp divergence between conventionals and Ginnies. While GSE paper was mostly in line with expectations, Ginnie premiums paid far faster than models implied across most pools issued last year. It seems the suddenness of the rally, combined with the lack of previous exposure to low rates for many high coupon Ginnie pools distorted normal speed ramps. How could this impact demand? Large banks and Japanese investors did not go on a buyer<sup>’</sup>s strike the last period marked by fast and difficult to predict Ginnie speeds in 2016/17 (Figure 7). However if durations into a rally are shorter than expected, the 0% risk weight and LCR treatment could become secondary considerations for investors who may want to add G2s in AFS to capture AOCI gains into a potential rally (as businesses hedge against recession). Instead they may look to add structure, prefer

YoY HPA across the top 20 largest MSAs, sorted by 2023 HPA; % customs, or buy lower coupons for more durable duration. More yield focused investors may still prefer higher coupons, as the market will necessarily be baking in higher option cost. At these levels we remain neutral on mortgages and underweight 15yrs (see Agency MBS).

Despite the NYCB headlines, active Fedspeak, and speedy prepayment prints, spreads were generally calm in the private-label mortgage credit space. A pickup in prepayments for inthe-money borrowers may shift some investor interest back into lower coupons but, broadly speaking, mortgage spreads are likely to remain rangebound (see RMBS). CoreLogic released its December<sup>’</sup>23 home price data this week, giving us a complete picture of how housing fared in 2023. Home prices were up 5.7% yoy (vs our forecast of 5% HPA) as low housing supply trumped declining demand. HPA remained highly regional, with Sunbelt MSAs seeing some of the larger changes versus 2021, while Miami and New York held up considerably well (Figure 8). While home buyers seem responsive to marginal rate rallies, we think rates are likely to hover in this range and do little to create any lasting demand or supply. We may see some fluctuations in home prices, but we keep our home price forecast for 2024 flat. In the meantime, we<sup>’</sup>re keeping an eye on any upticks in DQs from distressed borrowers. The high DTI FHA segment has seen continual increases in DQs, while other segments of the market remain in line with historicals (see Home Price Monitor: Ending the year soft, John Sim, 2/6/24).
