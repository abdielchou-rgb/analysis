## The Sahm Rule: A user's manual

Jonathan Millar, BCI, US | Marc Giannoni, BCI, US | Pooja Sriram, BCI, US | Colin Johanson, BCI, US

Excerpted from US Economics: The Sahm Rule: A user's manual, published on August 29, 2024

July's unemployment rate hit levels that either trigger — or are very close to triggering — the Sahm Rule, an indicator with a good (but hardly perfect) record of identifying when a recession is underway. This "manual" discusses the rule's strengths and weaknesses, looking behind the hype to deliver much needed perspective.

## Quick Start Guide

• Congratulations on your decision to upgrade to the Sahm Rule, the gold standard for indicating that a recession is underway. It performs better than other indicators, such as inversions in the yield curve.

• The rule says that a downturn is likely already underway if the 3mma of the unemployment rate surpasses its low over the preceding 12 months by at least 0.5pp. We walk through the latest monthly data, showing that the rule may or may not have been breached in July.

• This 0.5pp threshold works from the intuition that, in recessions, the unemployment rate increases sharply alongside declines in overall activity. Given this, logic implies that there might be some "point of no return" when such sharp increases become inevitable. The threshold is determined by back-testing using available historical estimates from 1970-2018. By construction, it has a perfect record identifying recessions over that timespan.

• Although the rule is accurate, optimal use requires careful interpretation and application. Multiple conditions will "void the warranty":

° <sup>One</sup> <sup>is</sup> <sup>that</sup> <sup>conditions</sup> <sup>must</sup> <sup>resemble</sup> <sup>those</sup> <sup>seen</sup> <sup>during</sup> <sup>this</sup> <sup>historical</sup> <sup>sample</sup> <sup>over</sup> <sup>which</sup> the Sahm Rule was calculated. Our tests show that it is less accurate when applied to a longer history of available data, and when it is applied to estimates as they evolved in real time rather than revised estimates. It also may not work well if the unemployment rate is being boosted by noncyclical influences, such as labor supply shocks (especially if these boost the NAIRU) or measurement error.

° <sup>Aftermarket</sup> <sup>modifications</sup> <sup>will</sup> <sup>also</sup> <sup>void</sup> <sup>the</sup> <sup>warranty.</sup> <sup>Risks</sup> <sup>of</sup> <sup>falsely</sup> <sup>identifying</sup> <sup>an</sup> <sup>active</sup> recession intensify when one uses a lower threshold than the recommended 0.5pp: Being "close" to this threshold is not the same as surpassing it. Our historical analysis identifies a number of close calls and false positives, demonstrating that a recession is not inevitable following significant increases in the unemployment rate.

° <sup>The</sup> <sup>warranty</sup> <sup>is</sup> <sup>also</sup> <sup>voided</sup> <sup>if</sup> <sup>one</sup> <sup>applies</sup> <sup>the</sup> <sup>Sahm</sup> <sup>Rule</sup> <sup>in</sup> <sup>ways</sup> <sup>that</sup> <sup>go</sup> <sup>beyond</sup> <sup>its</sup> intended design. It should neither be mistaken as a leading indicator of a recession (it performs poorly at that) nor should it be applied as an indicator of the recession's duration (overruns are the norm).

## Europe

What's the story? Our H2 views

Søren Willemann, Barclays, UK

Excerpted from European Credit Alpha, published on August 30, 2024

In spite of lower all-in yields and lingering growth risk, we remain broadly optimistic for H2, aided by starting levels and the initiation of rate cuts. Short-term cash bonds could face supply-driven headwinds.

Little by little, the summer holidays are coming to an end, and markets are gearing up for H2.
We revisit our views across major parts of European credit for the rest of the year.

Don't look back at inflation uncertainty. With the Fed appearing to start on its rate cutting path, the scope for inflation numbers driving volatility appears much reduced, with the focus turning to how much the Fed (and the ECB) could cut, and for how long. Just like over the summer, focus will stay on economic downside, with the US remaining the more uncertain market, in our view. Yields dropped over the summer, partly due to expectations of the Fed entering its rate-cutting cycle, but also due to concerns around US growth. Looking at the data, however, we – and more importantly our economists – have a hard time seeing signs of a US hard landing, with available indicators suggesting that the US economy has remained resilient in Q3. In turn, our rates strategists see EUR rates as too low.

No longer standing on the shoulders of giant yields. A very popular chart in the last two years has been that of €IG yields vs SXXP dividend yields (Figure 7). With the recent drop in yields,  €IG credit is now only "out-yielding" SXXP by 15bp. The general notion that much credit demand recently has been yield-driven has led to concerns that demand could now drop meaningfully. While this remains to be seen, anecdotally, more recently we have seen demand shifting away from "yield buyers" to a broader set of investors, which gives us some comfort in this regard.

Some might say the backdrop outlined above is bearish. However, given valuation levels, we still see tighter spreads in IG and HY cash bonds over the rest of the year, with IG outperforming HY. This does not extend to covered bonds, where we expect slightly wider spreads in H2 as a result of rising NICs. Nor does it extend to CDS over H2, when we see Main and SenFin only marginally tighter and Xover wider. In general, our RV tilts cautious – specifically on cyclical risk and autos, across IG and HY – and we also tilt towards up-in-rating in general. In a similar vein, surveying the European credit universe for compensation for taking beta risk vs upside/ downside trade-of (Figure 8), hybrid capital generally screens worse than straight senior credit, or even HY.

Supply supernova. In the short term, we expect supply to pick up across the board, posing a technical headwind to cash bonds. In IG, we expect c.€70bn of issuance in September, the third highest for a September post-GFC, and HY has a healthy forward public pipeline (Barclays-led public transactions alone total just under €7bn for September/October across bonds and loans). Covered bond supply has picked up in earnest in recent sessions, but YTD supply is lagging 2023, with only €35bn of net supply YTD vs €77.5bn for the same period in 2023.

## The calm before the payrolls

Christian Keller, Barclays, UK | Mark Cus Babic, BCI,US | Akash Utsav, Barclays, UK

Excerpted from Global Economics Weekly, published on August 30, 2024

After market volatility in response to NVIDIA earnings, the US employment report will be the next crucial milestone. ## Crunchtime: from NVIDIA to NFPR to FOMC

Data point to resilient demand and solid employment report...

The volatility following this week's NVIDIA earnings showed how nervous markets remain and reconfirmed how 'macro-relevant' the fate of a single company has become. Next week's US nonfarm payroll report will be the next, even more important, milestone for markets globally, as it will be crucial for determining the Fed's policy path.

In principle, one data point does not make a trend. Nor do two. But if next Friday's (6 Sep) employment report surprises to the downside for the second consecutive time, any such caution is likely to fall on deaf ears. This is why the risks going into this crucial release seem highly asymmetric: a solid report is very unlikely to derail the September cut, or gradual further cuts thereafter, not least as inflation data remain soft (evidenced by July core PCE printing at 0.16% m/m this week). In contrast, a weak report would likely validate the popular narrative that the US economy and labour market are on the precipice, necessitating a fast and deep cutting cycle, leading to another sharp repricing.

But that is not our baseline. After July's 114k gain, we expect nonfarm employment to have risen by 175k (current consensus: 165k) in August, alongside a decline in the unemployment rate to 4.2% (-0.1pp). We also forecast a rebound in the workweek to 34.3 hours and an acceleration in average hourly earnings to 0.3% m/m, boosting incomes. Ahead of the employment release, we think the July JOLTS survey will show job openings remaining resilient and rising slightly to 8.3mn from 8.184mn in June.

This forecast rests on the premise that the July employment report was skewed by Hurricane Beryl and underlying demand remains healthy. On the former, there are plenty of signs that weather left an imprint on previous data. For example, the number of workers who were absent from work due to weather and the number of workers who were temporarily laid of surged in July. Similarly, initial claims spiked as Hurricane Beryl made landfall but they have since normalised to pre-pandemic norms. Moreover, a number of other indicators, such as housing starts and industrial production, were also afected by the hurricane.

On aggregate demand, the incoming data continue to point to an economy that may be slowing from above-trend growth but remains robust. This week, the BEA revised up Q2 GDP growth from 2.8% q/q saar to 3.0% q/q, driven by stronger consumption. Similarly, July estimates of real personal spending surprised to the upside and accelerated to 0.4% m/m from an upwardlyrevised 0.3% m/m in the prior month. Incorporating the latest data, our GDP tracker points to another solid increase in Q3 (2.2% q/q saar), slightly above our oficial forecast (2.0% q/q saar). In addition, this week's resumption of the Atlanta Fed wage tracker showed continued deceleration in wage growth, but it remains elevated relative to pre-pandemic levels.

## ...but a further rise in the unemployment rate could make the Fed go 50bp

That said, should our baseline forecast prove incorrect and the unemployment rate were to increase to a solid 4.4% (ie, increase by at least 15bp), along with a weak payroll print of 100k or lower, a 50bp cut would likely be on the table at the upcoming FOMC meeting on 18 September. Namely, at last week's Jackson Hole symposium, Powell made clear that the Fed's reaction function provides a path to more aggressive cuts if warranted by labour market data, saying that the committee does not "seek or welcome further cooling in labor market conditions" and that the Fed has ample room to react.

Aside from payrolls, next week also brings ISM PMIs, where we expect a small firming in the services reading to 52.0 (+0.6pt) as well as an improvement in manufacturing (47.8, +1.0pt), although these indicators are likely to be overshadowed by the subsequent employment data.

## Europe in economic and political limbo

Euro area data lack clear direction...

The US labour report's importance for the Fed will make it consequential globally. This includes Europe, where our rates strategists have shown that global factors have been the dominant driver of European yields so far this year, with Bunds and Gilts largely in the passenger seat (see Breaking Bunds (below 2%)).

This may be particularly true because domestic data still lack a clear direction. On activity, the expenditure breakdown in this week's German final GDP adds weight to our suspicion that higher euro area GDP growth in H1 was driven by government spending and the volatile components, while underlying domestic demand remains soft. The released credit data for July remained lacklustre, showing only a minor pick-up in mortgage lending and essentially no lending to non-financial corporates. On the other hand, the composite economic and employment sentiment indicators in the European Commission survey edged slightly higher, French monthly household consumption ticked up, and the euro area unemployment rate fell back to its all-time low of 6.4%. Overall, we read the data as pointing to positive but soft growth in Q3.

Similarly, August flash HICP did not give a strong directional signal. Headline and core both printed about in line with expectations as the former dropped to 2.2% y/y (-0.4pp) on energy base efects and the latter was roughly unchanged at 2.8% y/y (vs. 2.9% y/y last month, but this reflects rounding). Compositionally, the release pointed to stickier services ofset by softer core goods, but both dynamics were possibly overstated by volatile components.

With such uncertainty about the outlook for growth and inflation, in the euro area as well as globally, and divergent views on the Governing Council, we think the ECB will stick to its meeting-by-meeting communication, which keeps the October meeting live, in addition to our baseline for cuts at the forecast meetings in September and December. Next week, we will look for further signals from euro area retail sales, German industrial data, and euro area final Q2 GDP.

## ... and so do politics

In France, there still has been no progress made in forming a new government following July's snap elections. President Macron this week rejected the candidate proposed by the alliance of left-wing parties that now forms the largest bloc in Parliament. The President has not given any indication about the timetable for naming a prime minister, but only a month remains now until 1 October, the legal deadline by which a government must present a draft budget law for 2025.

Macron's colleague, German Chancellor Scholz, is also in a precarious situation. Against the background of his very unpopular  on the national level three-party coalition, two of the German states (Saxony and Thuringia) will hold elections this Sunday (1 Sep). Opinion polls point to strongly increased support for the far-left and far-right parties, which could alter the German political landscape<sup>2</sup> . Depending on the ruling coalition's parties' magnitude of defeat in the regional elections, more infighting and a reignited debate about snap elections could follow.

In the UK, the national elections now lay two months behind, but politics remain in the limelight. With limited dataflow this and next week, the focus is firmly on expectations for the upcoming Budget in October (the Prime Minister this week laid down the groundwork for tax increases) and the prospect of re-alignment with the EU in some areas.

## China still in muddle-through mode

## Fiscal data point to significant domestic demand weakness...

In China, this week brought little new data, but our China economists took a deep dive into the released fiscal data, now available until July. Using fiscal data to gauge activity could be particularly fruitful in China, where many are skeptical about the oficial growth statistics.

Indeed, the fiscal results paint a much bleaker picture than the released activity data as fiscal revenue has fallen into y/y contraction since the beginning of the year, reflecting a broad-based drop in tax revenue. This includes individual income tax revenue, which has been falling for 17 consecutive months, pointing to challenges in the labour market not evident in the oficial quarterly household survey and the unemployment rate.

....while the PBoC wants to prop up bond yields but also support mortgage finance Meanwhile, the PBoC continues its eforts in trying to prevent domestic bond yields from falling further. Following the MoF’s announcement of a roll-over plan for special treasuries last week, the PBoC this week conducted the relevant open-market operation, purchasing CNY400bn of long-dated special treasuries from primary dealers/banks. In principle, the PBoC would of course have to sell rather than buy bonds in order to support higher yields. However, this week's operation did not afect the liquidity of the banking system (the MoF issues bonds to the banks and the banks pass them to the central bank).

Hence, the PBoC's move may rather be interpreted as building a war chest to eventually start actively trading sovereign debt. It is worth highlighting that the PBoC's newly purchased special treasury with 10-15y maturity replaced a previous note in the same amount but only carrying 7y duration. We think these transactions are likely meant to give the PBoC flexibility to sell bonds at a later date to prop up longer-term CGB yields.

A policy to set a floor on rates in a deflationary environment implies keeping real yields higher than where market forces would take them. The cited financial stability reasons (preventing Silicon Valley Bank-type balance sheet collapses) may be better addressed by direct regulatory measures, rather than by preventing the yield curve from adjusting to the macroeconomic reality.

At the same time, recent news reports<sup>3</sup> suggest that the PBoC is also considering allowing homeowners to refinance existing mortgages with new loans, in order to lower borrowing costs for households and boost consumption. Hence, there is obviously an awareness of the need for lower financing costs to support the housing market. The question remains whether this combination of in part seemingly contradictory interventions will in aggregate make for an efective policy response.

Ahead, we expect the NBS manufacturing PMI to remain in contractionary territory for the fourth straight month while the Caixin indicator could improve amid better regional trade performance.
