## Market commentary

July is shaping up to be an Olympic month, whether related to sports or not. Since we last published two weeks ago, there was the assassination attempt on former President Trump, President Biden’s withdrawal from the 2024 presidential race, followed by Vice President Kamala Harris becoming the presumptive nominee for the Democratic party. And while the world will be focused on the Paris Olympics next week, market participants will also be following the August Treasury refunding process, which begins on Monday, followed by the FOMC meeting on Wednesday, and further releases of Tier 1 economic data, namely JOLTS, ECI, and the July payrolls report...is that enough?

Recent price action in rates generally resulted in a steeper Treasury yield curve: 2-year yields closed lower by 7bp to 4.39% and ranged between 4.39% to 4.52% peak to trough since 7/12, while the 10-year increased by 1bp to 4.20%. Economic data continued to highlight the possibility of a soft landing as inflation continued to cool. Indeed, retail sales were stronger than expected, with control category up 0.9% in June, along with upward revisions to May and April (see US: June retail sales point to 2Q consumer spending near 2%, A. Reinhart, 7/16/24). The US economy also demonstrated solid momentum in the second quarter, with real GDP increasing by 2.8% q/q saar, surpassing expectations. Consumer spending also showed improvement, rising by 2.3%q/q saar, compared to a 1.5% increase in the first quarter (see US: The US economy kept chugging along last quarter, A. Reinhart, 7/25/24). Meanwhile, June core PCE deflator rose 0.18%m/m, matching expectations, with the year-ago rate at 2.6%, unchanged from the prior month. Over the last three months, core PCE decelerated to 2.3% saar, fully reversing the spike in the early part of the year. Additionally, real personal spending rose 0.2%m/m , and there were upward revisions to the prior two months, resulting in decent 2.2% saar growth in the last three months. Personal income did disappoint, rising just 0.1%m/m and 1.0%oya, though nominal employee compensation continues to grow at a decent pace, up 4.7%oya, (see US: No surprises on core PCE; solid consumption trajectory, A. Reinhart, 7/25/24). By week’s end, OIS markets have priced in 68bp of rate cuts this year, and 174bp of easing by December 2025 (Figure 1).

On refunding, Treasury will release its quarterly financing estimates, which our Treasury strategists forecast a \$1.915tn deficit in FY24, but this estimate overstates the increase in projected outlays, as it’s partially driven by subsidy costs related to student loans. Furthermore, our strategists expect Treasury to announce \$809bn in net marketable borrowing for July-September, with an end-of-quarter cash balance of \$850bn. This is slightly below last quarter’s forecast due to a slower QT pace and a higher June cash balance. For October-December, they forecast \$496bn in net borrowing needs, assuming a \$775bn year-end cash balance and Fed continues to reduce Treasury holdings by \$25bn per month through yearend. Overall, they forecast \$2.309tn in net privately-held borrowing for 2024, with T-bills accounting for 17% of this, down from 65% last year.

Speaking of T-bills, issuance has been positive since the start of July, consistent with guidance Treasury provided in May. Our strategists forecast positive net T-bill issuance throughout the month of August, before cutting shorter-maturity auction sizes in early-September against a surge in the TGA around the corporate tax date. Additionally, it’s likely Treasury and TBAC will revisit discussions surrounding the T-bill market. Notably, in November 2020, TBAC and Treasury agreed that T-bills should represent a 15-20% share of marketable Treasury debt outstanding. As of the end of June, T-bills represented a 21% share of debt outstanding, down from the peak near 23% earlier in the year, and above the top end of the optimal range for nearly a year. However, we believe the 20% upper end is a soft ceiling and suspect that the optimal share of T-bills may migrate higher than the current recommended range over time (see Treasuries).

Lastly, at the conclusion of next week’s FOMC meeting, our economist anticipates that the Fed will leave policy rates unchanged. More importantly, the interesting developments will be any tweaks to the post-meeting statement that might indicate an increasing likelihood of a September cut. The current statement reads: “The Committee does not expect it will be appropriate to reduce the target range until it has gained greater confidence that inflation is moving sustainably toward 2 percent.” This could be modified to say: “until it has gained further confidence,” or something similar. During the press conference, Chair Powell will likely refrain from providing specific guidance on future rate decisions, instead emphasizing that decisions are made on a meeting-by-meeting basis. However, it is possible that Powell could suggest that recent data is giving the committee greater confidence that inflation is on the right track. To be sure, Fed rhetoric over the past two weeks leaned dovish as committee members continue to gain more confidence of inflation returning back to their 2% goal (Figure 2). To that end, our economist continues to expect the first rate cut in September, followed by quarterly reductions thereafter (see FOMC preview, M Feroli, 7/26/24).

## Are we there yet?

Price action in the repo markets has once again brought into question whether we are on the brink of reserve scarcity that would prompt the Fed to end QT prematurely. For the better part of this month, SOFR has been persistently setting at or above EFFR despite ON RRP balances and bank reserves registering around \$400bn and \$3.3tn, respectively. In and of itself, the shift higher in SOFR relative to EFFR is not a new phenomenon, as two years of QT alongside a substantial increase in Treasury issuance at a time when the Fed and other price-insensitive real money buyers have stepped back from buying Treasuries are fueling significant collateral financing needs (Figure 3 Thesift grnSOFR lav oE wpm).

We continue to believe reserves are currently abundant and that the recent volatility/elevated levels with SOFR is a product of market frictions and inefficiencies in the repo markets.

As we highlighted last week, given the rapid growth of the repo markets, MMFs might not have been as effective in policing repo rates due to counterparty concentration limits (see What’s going on with MMFs and their dealer repo/RRP exposures?, T.Ho, 7/18/24). This has in turn prompted them to rely on the ON RRP facility as a source of backstop supply to meet their structural liquidity needs, thus keeping ON RRP balances elevated.

However, that’s not to say the recent stickiness with ON RRP is here to stay. We think balances can decline more as net T-bill issuance turns positive in 2H (our Treasury strategists are forecasting +\$300bn of net T-bill issuance) and as we get further clarity around the Fed’s easing cycle. These factors should not only provide more liquidity supply to MMFs, but also on the margin cheapen T-bill valuations and allow MMFs to extend. To be sure, WAMs of government MMFs have meaningfully shortened this year, registering 31-32 days currently from local peak of 38 days at the start of the year. During past easing cycles, they can go as high as 40-50 days (Figure 4Governmt MFWAsha igfuly d  31-2 c pk8  ,  405   ). Net of the liquidity drain coming from QT (estimated at \~\$40bn/mo or \$200bn for the remainder of this year) and holding TGA balances stable, a decline in ON RRP balances should result in an increase in bank reserves and overall liquidity.

More importantly, we would emphasize that MMFs are not the only net providers of liquidi ty in the repo markets. Besides MMFs, GSEs and banks are also significant providers of liquidity that can police repo rates. Dealers are too with the use of SRF. Of course, each cares at different clearing levels, with MMFs often providing the cheapest form of financing in the form of tri-party, followed by the GSEs, banks, then dealers (Figure 5). Based on the most recent Senior Financial Survey, banks have noted that they would care when repo trades 5-15bps above IORB. GSEs would care at any rate above RRP since they do not earn IORB. Dealers would care when repo trades above the upper bound of the fed funds target range. Also, in terms of size, MMFs’ exposure to repo dominate (\$1.8tn), while the GSEs and banks currently only make up 14% (\~\$250bn) and 33% (\~\$610bn) of what MMFs lend out in repo, respectively (Figure 6MFs’ expourt dmina (\$1.8), whlGSE bkcy  4%\~250 36 f  v ). Admittedly, GSEs are less likely going to be as effective going forward in policing repo rates as their pool of liquidity is unlikely going to grow further, though it’s possible they might reallocate from other liquidity asset classes into repo. Even so, the presence of these players should help police repo rates on the upside, limiting the likelihood that SOFR persistently trades outside of the policy rate corridor.

What does this all mean for the Fed? The odds that the Fed will have to end QT prematurely are low. We are not at reserve scarcity yet and the recent shift higher in SOFR and TGCR is not indicative of burgeoning funding pressures. We acknowledge SOFR volatility outside of month-ends and quarter-ends is worth monitoring going forward. Even so, we suspect a lot has to happen before the Fed decides to step in and end QT prematurely, particularly given the set of repo buyers that could naturally police repo rates, not to mention the availability of the SRF for dealers. It’s perhaps also worth mentioning that the recent rise in SOFR has had no impact on EFFR, in terms of both rate and volumes. We still think QT can run through the end of this year with ON RRP balances ending the year slightly below \$300bn and reserves at \$3.1tn. Though with year-end only five months away, we wouldn’t be surprised if we hear more from the Fed on QT and the conditions they think will necessitate an end as we increasingly approach reserve scarcity.
