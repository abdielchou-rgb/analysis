## US: Future core inflation to bolster Fed confidence

• Core CPI inflation should slow in 1H24 following stillfirm readings late in 2023

• Core PCE inflation should pick up a bit after recent run near 2%

These outcomes should boost Fed confidence in the near term

• Sustained inflation near 2% should require softer wage inflation over time

The trend for core consumer price inflation moderated in 2023 but the CPI and PCE measures sent differing signals about how close inflation was to target late last year. The core PCE price index—one of the Fed<sup>’</sup>s preferred inflation metrics—basically came in at the FOMC<sup>’</sup>s 2% target during the second half of 2023. But the core CPI measure was significantly above target (even accounting for the normal gap between CPI and PCE inflation), running 3.3% over the last three months of 2023 (Figure 1).

We think the broad inflation trends will keep moderating over the first half of 2024, but we expect shorter-term run rates on inflation to continue to behave differently between the CPI and PCE price measures—core CPI run rates should slow significantly in 1H24 relative to 4Q23, while we expect some modest firming in core PCE inflation. For the core CPI, we expect slowing from a 3.3%3m, saar as of December to 2.7% as of June (Table 1); for quarterly run rates, we forecast 3.1% growth in 1Q and 2.6% in 2Q following a 3.4%saar jump in 4Q23. Meanwhile, we expect core PCE to reaccelerate modestly, with the quarterly run rate rising from 2.0% in the last two quarters to 2.2% in the first two quarters of 2024.

We believe that the unwinding of supply chain shocks has been an important driver of the “immaculate disinflation<sup>”</sup> observed in recent quarters. But these tailwinds appear to be fading, suggesting we will likely need a softer labor market in order for inflation to remain close to target on a sustainable basis. Nonetheless, a few factors should support further disinflation in the near term. Though the pace of core goods deflation from recent months is unlikely to be sustained over the medium term, the core goods basket is likely to be held down in the near term by a further leg down in used car prices, and rent inflation should continue moderating through the summer. With these factors weighing more on CPI inflation than PCE inflation given differences in relative weighting, we expect these broader measures to behave differently in the short run.

While there are a lot of moving pieces in the CPI-PCE wedge, the Fed sees its inflation objective solely in PCE terms. At his most recent post-FOMC press conference, Chair Powell mentioned the PCE measure several times but didn<sup>’</sup>t discuss the CPI measure. If our forecast is correct, year-ago core PCE inflation could dip below 2.5% as soon as the February report (released in late March). This should bolster Fed confidence that inflation is sustainably moving toward 2%. We continue to see the first cut at the June FOMC meeting, but there is a clear chance it could happen as soon as the May meeting.

## Core goods deflation won’t last forever

Declining core goods prices have been a noteworthy part of the recent downshift in inflation, with the core goods CPI falling 1.6% saar over the last three months of 2023 after increasing at a 4.6% annualized rate over the preceding 36 months. Easing supply chain constraints have been a big part of this recent trend. Additionally, as our colleagues note, China<sup>’</sup>s export deflation likely played an important role in driving global core goods prices lower over the second half of last year, though this impulse appears to now be fading.

## Rent inflation declining...slowly

In our note last July, we estimated that primary rent and OER inflation would slow to roughly a \~4.5%3m, saar by the end of the year, based on the lagged passthrough of softening inflation in asking rents, as estimated by the Zillow Observed Rent Index (ZORI). While the run rate of CPI rent inflation has continued to soften, the pace has been slower than we anticipated, with primary rent and OER still running at a 5.7%3m, saar in December. To some extent, we think differences in the pace of inflation moderation across apartments and detached houses could be contributing—as has been welldocumented by BLS economists, rent inflation for different types of housing units sometimes diverges, even in the same neighborhoods, with large apartment complexes tending to have more flexible rents than detached units, for example, and supply deliveries to the multifamily market more of a headwind to prices relative to the single family market.

While apartments represent the vast majority of the rental market, OER largely tracks single-family rents—even more so since January 2023, when BLS adjusted the weighting methodology for OER to give a higher weight to detached houses in the index sample. In Figure 5, the Zillow Single Family Residence Index shows a much slower pace of disinflation relative to the ZORI and to other industry rent measures. By relying more heavily on the Single Family Residence Index, we estimate a somewhat slower path of disinflation in OER CPI. Of course primary rent CPI has also remained more elevated than we expected, even though this component should be more representative of the multifamily market. We acknowledge that the model is fitted based on a time series with only eight years of data, and may have simply overestimated the speed of the passthrough to the CPI measures. Nonetheless, given the significant softening observed across a range of industry rent measures, we feel confident that CPI rent inflation will continue to soften closer to under a 4% annualized pace by midyear (Figure 5).

The New Tenant Repeat Rent (NTRR) Index is a relatively new addition to the suite of alternative rent inflation measures, that has the advantage of focusing on those units in the BLS housing survey that appear in repeated transactions. However, the sample size is a very small relative universe of all housing units tracked by CPI, especially for the latest data point, which looked very weak and is subject to large revisions.

It's also worth noting that easier Fed policy could temporarily push down rent inflation—as we have shown in the past, higher mortgage rates tend to increase the relative demand for rental units by lowering home-buying demand. Similarly, falling mortgage rates and an easing in financial conditions could reduce the relative demand for rental units. There also were about 1.7mn housing units under construction at the end of last year and future increases in available supply look set to weigh on shelter inflation.

## Super core to stay noisy near term

Away from rent, a few core services components have had significant moves over the past year that are worth highlighting. The health insurance CPI fell roughly 3.8% per month (37% saar) over the year through September before resetting higher in October alongside the annual change in the industry source data. Going forward, we look for this component to rise roughly 1% per month through March. Beginning in

April, the BLS will use semiannual source data to calculate price changes for this component, which should result in a somewhat softer but still positive pace of health insurance CPI inflation over the subsequent six months (see TIPS Strategy, 8/4/23). Separately, airfares and hotel prices may remain volatile in coming months but we think they are unlikely to continue falling at the pace observed through December. In fact industry source data point to a bounce in hotel prices more recently, and rising jet fuel prices should add upward pressure to airfares in the near term. Auto insurance inflation remains elevated compared to other parts of the services basket but has moderated recently and should continue to soften alongside falling vehicle repair cost inflation. If we strip out some of these idiosyncratic components, other core services inflation has continued to run at a 3.3%saar in the last three months, and it<sup>’</sup>s likely we<sup>’</sup>ll need to see further softening in the labor market to generate sustained disinflation in this basket.

As much as we think the aforementioned idiosyncratic reasons could determine the near-term inflation dynamics for various individual CPI categories, the larger macro backdrop remains important for the broader inflation trajectory. In the past we have highlighted the labor market<sup>’</sup>s importance in influencing inflation, with the most meaningful effects generally coming for core services or the “super core<sup>”</sup> subaggregate (Figure 6). We therefore think that additional softening in the labor market (like we anticipate in our forecast) could be an important drag on inflation moving forward. Of course, the past year has demonstrated that we can get meaningful softening in inflation even without significant deterioration in the labor market (and a flat Phillips curve according to several estimations suggests that even significant weakening in the labor market may not necessarily push inflation meaningfully lower). Recent declines in consumer inflation expectations also may have helped bring realized inflation down lately, and while the outlook for inflation expectations is highly uncertain, we think it is unlikely that they move sharply higher in the near term absent some unforeseen shock.

## Upcoming CPI revisions

Upcoming annual adjustments to the CPI data add some uncertainty to the outlook for inflation. But we don<sup>’</sup>t see reasons to think that an update to the seasonal factors will be especially meaningful for recent inflation rates on net and we also don<sup>’</sup>t anticipate larger-than-normal relative importance adjustments across the main CPI components. The release of the related figures on February 9 likely will remove an uncertainty tied to the inflation outlook, but it probably won<sup>’</sup>t change the broad story much.

## What does this mean for PCE prices?

The net of the factors discussed above should be a sharp slowing in core CPI inflation rates early this year. But we don<sup>’</sup>t look for the same thing to occur in the PCE price data. In fact, we look for some modest acceleration in core PCE inflation early this year. Part of this divergence is expected to be due to weighting differences. While the CPI and PCE price data should show declines in used vehicle prices and moderating rental inflation, smaller weights for these components in the PCE data imply that these factors should weigh less on PCE inflation than on CPI inflation (Figure 7).

There are also differences in scope and measurement across the two indexes, driving notable divergences in medical care services and insurance price inflation. The sharp reset higher in health insurance CPI inflation since October is one reason for the widening differential in core CPI and PCE run rates in 4Q23 (Figure 7). The gradual moderation expected in both health insurance and auto insurance over coming months should contribute on the margin to a narrowing in the wedge. More generally, the scope of medical care in core PCE is significantly broader than in core CPI, which also results in medical care services having roughly twice the weight in core PCE. We think it could be hard to see material disinflation in this component, given health care ECI continues to rise at close to a 5% pace.

Lastly, financial services have a materially larger weight in core PCE than in core CPI, and tend to be a significant source of volatility in the index. Within this component, portfolio management fees in particular have come in weaker than other core services components. Sourced from the PPI data, management fees are most often based on a percentage of assets under management, which is directly tied to market performance. After falling sharply throughout the fall, portfolio management fees rebounded in December and are likely to continue rising in the near term, further contributing to a narrowing in the core CPI/PCE wedge.

## Near-term confidence, longer-run risks

The discussion above outlines our expectations for inflation for a variety of specific goods and services as well as the broader core inflation aggregates over 1H24. Through all of the moving parts, we think that the incoming inflation data in the near term will continue to boost the Fed<sup>’</sup>s confidence around moderating inflation. By the middle of this year, we think the core PCE price index will be running fairly close to the FOMC<sup>’</sup>s 2% inflation target while the core CPI will be stepping down. The outlook for the longer-run inflation trend is beyond the scope of this note, but we think sustained inflation near 2% will likely require softer labor markets over time.
