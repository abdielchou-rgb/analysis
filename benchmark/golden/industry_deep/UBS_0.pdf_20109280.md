## Core inflation continues to slow

 As noted yesterday, we project an 19bp increase in core PCE prices in June, which would see 12-month core PCE inflation going to (2.58%) — roughly the same as last month and 0.2pp below the FOMC's current year-end projection.

 We forecast the headline CPI to increase 20bp in July (seasonally-adjusted; 18bp not-seasonally-adjusted) with core CPI prices rising 18bp. This would keep 12- month headline CPI inflation at 3.0% while core CPI inflation would edge down to 3.2%. We will review these projections as additional data becomes available over the next few weeks.

 We expect monthly core price increases to pick up through October in part due to the effect of seasonal factors that have been overly-influenced by the pandemic.

 A detailed forecast spreadsheet is available on our website.

## Key questions include rents, tariffs, & shipping costs

 The slowing in rents in June was mostly expected by our models. As a result we have revised down our OER forecast only a few basis points per month and still expect rent increases to pick up a bit over the next few months.

 A back-of-envelope calculation suggest tariffs on imports from China under a new Trump administration could push up the price level as much as 1.3%. But we go into a number of reasons that calculation probably overstates the impact. In the end the effect will depend on details of the tariffs that are not yet known.

 Shipping costs have surged since December and pose an upside risk to the forecast, but a simple calculation suggests the impact will likely be only 0.1pp, or maybe even less, given some of the impact should have already passed into the CPI.

## Core PCE inflation forecast on the low edge of consensus at 2.6% for 2024

 We continue to expect that moderating demand, easing supply conditions, slowing rents, and a softening labor market will hold down monthly price changes over much of the next year. But base effects will likely keep 12-month core PCE inflation from declining notably until to the end of this year.

 Our core PCE projection for this year as a whole (Q4/Q4) remains at 2.6%. Our inflation forecast continues to be at the low edge of current measures of consensus inflation expectations.

## Key US inflation questions

The June CPI was a very good inflation report with the core CPI rising only 6bps — the smallest increase since January 2021, which was before the surge in inflation began. Even if core PCE prices are unlikely to be as weak as the core CPI, the 19bp core PCE price increase from our translation is lower than 4 of the 5 months so far this year. Along with the easing in owners' equivalent rent (OER), one of the most persistent components in the CPI, this was an very good report.

In the month since our prior US Inflation Monthly, and particularly in the days since the June CPI and PPI reports, various issues and questions have arisen in US inflation space. The main inflation questions we have heard are on rents, tariffs, and shipping costs.

## 1. How much signal should be taken from the rent slowing in June?

Most of the step down in the June OER increase to 28bps from 43bps in May was already expected by our models (which projected a step down to 34bp). As a result our OER models based on new tenant rent increases have revised down only a few basis points per month on the June CPI data (as well as updated data from our various source of rents). The median and average of the models continue to suggest OER is likely to accelerate into the autumn, but monthly increases generally should stay below 40bp. (Figure 2) The pick up over the next few months likely from some of the models expecting stronger OER increases in order to catch up with the level of new tenant lease rents since the start of the pandemic.

We have taken only a little signal from the OER slowing in June — largely because most of that slowing was expected

In our forecast we have moved similarly to the models and we have lowered our projection by roughly 3bps per month through November, but again, that suggests some rise in OER increases from June's 28bp pace.

## 2. How could tariffs impact US inflation next year?

We have not yet done a full analysis of the potential tariff impacts as the timing and the exact specification of the tariffs will matter significantly, However, a simple back-ofenvelope calculation can be done by:

Effect of tariff on price level =

(Value of goods with additional tariff / nominal personal consumption expenditure)

Since imports from China are roughly 2¼% of US consumption expenditures additional tariffs of 60pp on all imported goods from China would work out to a roughly 1.3pp boost to the US price level assuming 100% pass-through.

However, this 1.3pp increase in US consumer prices is probably a considerable overstatement for a few reasons:

1. It is likely that not all items will be covered. During the prior round of Trump tariffs on imports from China tariffs were proposed, but never implemented on the items which we thought would having the largest impact on inflation (tranche 4B, which included laptops, celll phones, toys, and large amount of consumer products). It is quite possible something similar will happen this time with the biggest-impact items carved out.

2. A 60pp increase in tariffs may be too high. Rather than a 60pp increase, it may be a total tariff level of 60%. Since tariffs on imports from China are roughly 20 to 25% already that would be a much smaller effective increase. Or it could be that 60% is just a directional marker and the actual tariffs will be much smaller.

3. 100% pass-through of tariffs to end consumers is probably an overstatement. The pass-through rate in our back-of-envelope calculation encompasses a large number of factors that a more complete model would take into account including: margin compression, trade diversion, changes in demand, responses of domestic manufacturers, retaliation from other countries, exchange rate changes, et cetera. In the 2018/2019 rounds of tariffs we assumed a pass-through rate that varied with the degree of market share China in each of the specific import codes. In aggregate this led to a pass-through rate of something well below 100% and roughly in the neighbourhood of 50%. If anything, this assumption may have been a bit too high in hindsight. Something similar would likely be appropriate this time.

Overall, the impact of a new round of tariffs on China could be as large as 1.3pp, but in all likelihood it will be notably smaller. It will depend considerably on the (largely unknown) details.

## 3. How might shipping costs impact inflation?

An upside risk to our core goods inflation projection is a surge in shipping costs. Since mid-December spot shipping costs have quadrupled and are now roughly half way back to their pandemic peak. (Figure 7) As a result a rough approximation of the inflationary impact would be to assume that shipping costs as share of the value of imported goods (i.e. the CIF/FOB ratio) will rise by half of the 1.6pp that they did during the pandemic. Taking that resulting 0.8pp and multiplying by imports share of US PCE (roughly 16%) would give an effect of 13bp on headline PCE prices assuming complete pass-through. Complete pass-through of the increase in shipping costs is probably too large of an assumption, Further, some of the rise in shipping costs that occurred in Q1 and earlier should already have passed into final consumer prices at this point. As a result this 13bp should be viewed as an upper bound.

The impact of the rise in shipping cost is probably 0.1pp or less.

## Upcoming CPI prints

We are currently forecasting a 20bp increase in the headline CPI in July (seasonallyadjusted; +18bp not-seasonally-adjusted) with 12-month inflation holding at 3.0% (2.96%). For the core CPI we forecast a 18bp increase in July with 12-month core CPI inflation edging down from 3.3% (3.27%) to 3.2% (3.21%) As additional data becomes available over the next few weeks, we will refine this projection.

We currently forecast a core CPI increase of 0.18% in July.

Looking further ahead than the July print, we forecast seasonally-adjusted core CPI increases will gradually edge up through the third quarter as seasonal factors become less of a downward push and the impact of the rise in shipping costs pass through to core goods prices. Nonetheless, increases are projected to remain below the pace that prevailed early this year. (Figure 22)

## Drivers of consumer inflation Inflation expectations

Long-run inflation expectations edged down in the preliminary University of Michigan survey for June. More importantly, those long-run inflation expectations are, and have been for the past couple of years, close to the levels they were at when core PCE inflation averaged 2%. In contrast, CPI inflation expectations from Philly Fed Survey of Professional Forecasters are below levels from when PCE inflation was, on average, at the FOMC's 2.0% inflation target. Either way, there is no sign from survey measures that inflation expectations are likely to keep inflation notably elevated.

Over the past 25 years inflation expectations in the Michigan survey are positively correlated with past inflation, but negatively correlated with future inflation.

10-year breakeven CPI inflation rates from swap markets are currently near 2.5%, slightly closer to their pre-pandemic level (2.1%) than their 2022 peak (3.1%) . 5-year breakeven inflation 5-years ahead derived from TIPS are at 2.3% — roughly in line with the level consistent with the FOMC's 2.0% PCE inflation target.

## Exchange rates and import prices

After a significant jump in import prices for consumer goods in January, they have returned to a more normal pace in the past four months. Except for that January surge, consumer import prices have been roughly flat — a pace that is much closer to the prepandemic price trend (when CPI goods prices tended to decline) than the strong upward trend during much of the pandemic. (Figure 35) We are inclined to discount much of the January surge as it was related to medical goods where there is not a high correlation with the CPI. That said, medical commodities increased solidly for the few months prior to June and that could have been be a result of the pass-through from the January import price jump.

Except for the huge January increase, import prices have been trending slightly downward.

Additionally, as noted in the front section of this report, upward pressure on goods prices could come from freight costs, which have increased sharply since mid-December. These costs are generally not included in US import price statistics and are an upside risk to goods inflation over the next handful of months.

## Resource utilization and the labor market

The unemployment rate rose to 4.1% in June — a low level, but up 0.7pp from its 3.4% trough in early last year. We project labor slack to rise only about another 0.1pp or 0.2pp over the next 12 months.

The overall employment-population ratio remains below its pre-pandemic level but the prime-age ratio is roughly in line with the February 2020 peak. We expect a weakening economy will lead to no further rise in the employment-population ratio.

The unemployment rate remains quite low

A normal Phillips curve coefficient where 1pp in unemployment leads to anywhere from 0.1pp to 0.3pp of inflation would suggest that the low level of unemployment can explain little of either the pick up or the slowing in inflation — particularly as the current unemployment rate is only 50bp above the rate in the year prior to the pandemic. However, alternative labor market indicators, particularly the job opening rate (shown later in the "Wages" section) suggest a somewhat larger role for labor market tightness.

## Box: Rules of thumb for impacts of key economic shocks

We usually rely on estimated time-series models to bring in the impact of various factors on our forecast. However, rules of thumb are often useful to give a rough idea of how important a shock is likely to be. Below are a few key rules of thumb that we use:

 1 percentage point increase in the unemployment rate pushes down core inflation by 8bp.

 Permanent \$10 per barrel level shift in the price of Brent crude pushes up the level of CPI by 40bp and core CPI by 4bp.

 A one percent decline in the dollar increases consumer import prices by 0.15 percent.

 A one percent increase in import prices results in a 0.12 percent increase in core consumer prices.

A one percent increase in CRB spot food index equals about 8bp in prices of food at home with about 2/3rds in the first six months and 1/3rd in the six months after that. (CPI food at home is 8% of the CPI basket.)

## Components of core PCE inflation

Figure 49 through Figure 53 show 3- and 12-month changes for the main components of our core PCE projection along with our forecasts and model projections. The models are estimated taking 2024Q2 as data and forecasted starting 2024Q3.

1. Core goods prices (25% of core PCE, Figure 49) have slowed considerably over the past year. We expect essentially no further disinflation, but for core goods inflation to move up only slightly temporarily before settling close to its pre-pandemic pace.

Goods price inflation is projected to remain not too far from its current level.

Our Phillips curve model for the category has adjusted down considerably over the past year and a half and now the model is roughly in line with our projection though the middle of this year and below it further out.

2. We forecast monthly housing services increases (essentially owners' equivalent rent plus tenants' rent, 20% of core PCE, Figure 50) will continue to slow over the next few quarters as the easing in rents for new leases filters into CPI/PCE housing rent inflation.

Our model for housing services inflation based on fundamental rent market factors (the unemployment rate, rental vacancy rate, and housing affordability) also shows slowing, but flattens out in the middle of next year more than our forecast.

3. Price inflation for PCE health services (19% of core PCE, Figure 51) is projected to run a little above the pace of the past year as the price reductions from the ending of Medicare Covid reimbursement rate increases is past. However, the upward push is only moderate as wage growth in health care has been slowing for the past year and Medicare adjustment for wage increases (which appear in October for hospitals and January for physicians) are estimated by the Center for Medicare and Medicaid Studies to be a similar to recent years.

The 12-month change in shelter rent inflation is past its peak, but still very elevated.

4. PCE food services inflation (6% of core PCE, Figure 52Fod servic) has been strong through most of the pandemic. We have seen slowing in recent months and expect further easing of the pace of increases with moderating wage increases.

5. Inflation for other core PCE services (31% of core PCE, Figure 53) has slowed in recent months and we expect the pace will ebb further as wage growth slowly eases. This is a small contrast to the model that has taken more signal from the strength earlier this year in these core services prices.

## Key risks to our modal forecast

We view our projection as the most likely (modal) scenario and see risks on both sides of the inflation forecast with the overall uncertainty surrounding the inflation projection remaining larger than usual over the past 20 to 30 years (though down from a year or two ago). Over the next few months we see the risks as two-sided around our forecast though skewed somewhat to the downside now that the early-year surge is likely past.

We think that near-term inflation risks have shifted and are now skewed to the downside over the next few months

## Upside risks

1. Shipping costs pushing up core non-transportation goods prices: As noted earlier we think shipping costs will only have a modest effect of overall core prices, but if those shipping costs continue upward or the pass-through is greater than expected it is possible that there could be a much larger increase in core prices than we have penciled in.

2. Rents remaining strong: While June was a very positive step in slowing rents, it is only one month and we have seen large one-month changes in rents before (for example, see the spike in January). It remains possible that Zillow's estimate of the amount of catch-up that CPI rents still need (around 6%) is more appropriate than taking the average across various measures, which suggest only a little additional catch-up. If Zillow is correct, CPI rents could continue to increase at roughly 45 to 50bp per month for the next 18 to 24 months.

3. Continued tight labor market passing into services prices: While we no longer forecast a recession, we do expect some easing in labor market pressure. If, instead, the economy does not gradually ease and wage growth remains elevated, it is likely that non-rent services inflation will be higher than we have forecast over the next year.

## Downside risks

1. A slowing economy: The recession that we had previously forecast starting this summer could still come to pass and have a greater slowing effect on inflation than we have anticipated. In particular, in recessions prices for PCE financial services, non-profit services, and travel-related services generally fall quite notably.

2. Rent weakness: Instead of the Zillow index being correct, it is quite possible that either the BLS NTR or Apartment List's index are right. If so, then OER and tenants' rents in the CPI/PCE prices could be slowing sharply in coming months and be negative in CPI reports by next summer.

3. Stronger productivity growth: The past couple quarters have seen a pickup in productivity growth. While we see this pickup as a return to the to the long-run productivity trend after some time below that trend, it could be the start of a longer-run productivity boom. If so, such a move could lead to lower price inflation for the same level of wage growth. In the late-1990s, as productivity growth picked up, price inflation fell.

4. Lower natural rate of unemployment: Additionally, it is possible that the ability to work remotely has led to better employment opportunities pushing down the natural rate of unemployment. If so, the natural rate of unemployment could be closer to 3.7% than the 4.0% we assume. If so, this would subtract close to a tenth percentage point off our or inflation forecast.

## Comparison to the forecast models

The models forecasts for core PCE inflation this year (Q4/Q4) range from 2.9% to 3.1% as they have taken a greater signal from the strength in Q1 than we have.

As with most Phillips curve style models, they were unable to forecast the inflation as it surged upward and, as expected, they have not been a good guide as inflation has fallen. (Note that in the early part of 2022 the three higher models below in Figure 54 projected core PCE inflation remaining above 4% throughout the forecast horizon.) In particular, the models do not include an effect for changes in the mix of demand and the supply constraints that hampered efforts to satisfy this changing mix. We believe the combined effects of the changing demand mix and supply issues were far more important in driving the inflation surge over the past 3 years than were the standard Phillips curve drivers of aggregate resource slack.

Our Phillips curve models have performed poorly during thus inflation experience.

Nonetheless, over the next couple of years we believe those unusual factors in this inflation surge will continue to diminish and and the models will continue to revise down. Our forecast is only slightly below the lower range of models by 2026.
