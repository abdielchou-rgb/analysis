## Equity Market Review

MSCI India (\$ index) gained 2.6%, underperforming the region and its peers (MSCI APxJ/EM: +4.3%/4.6%). Large-caps (+2.7%) rose during the month, outperforming both mid-caps (+1.3%) and small-caps (-1.1%). NIFTY gained (+1.2% MoM), having slipped from its then life-highs of c. 22,220 levels seen in the second half, and ended the month at 21,983. The index however hit a fresh high crossing 22,400 on 2<sup>nd</sup> Mar after a robust Q3FY24 GDP number of +8.4% YoY (GVA at +6.5% YoY). INR appreciated 0.2% over the month and ended the month at 82.91/USD. DXY gained +0.9% over the month, ending at 104.2.

Global equities gained across regions (MSCI World +4.2% MoM /+4.7% YTD), driven by China and US. Among major regions, MSCI China was the best performer in the month (+8.4%), followed by US (+5.2%) and EM (+4.6%) while MSCI South Africa and MSCI Brazil were the weakest (-5.6% and -0.3% respectively). In DM, we are OW Japan and UK while staying UW Eurozone. In EM, we have a structural OW on India. China and Brazil are our other major OWs with an UW stance on Colombia, Malaysia, South Africa.

We believe that there is a risk of the narrative turning back from goldilocks towards something like 1970s stagflation, with significant implications for asset allocation. In the 1970s, high inflation came in 3 separate waves, all in some ways related to geopolitical developments, equity markets were essentially flat in nominal terms, and bonds outperformed significantly. We see upside risks to inflation given loose financial conditions, tight labor markets, high government spending, and geopolitics (as the 1980s-2000s <sup>‘</sup>peace dividend<sup>’</sup> unwinds). Another macro risk, related to risks for global trade, geopolitical developments and inflation, is the US elections this year. Our view is that there is likely no market upside related to the US election, as the outcome is either status quo or increased uncertainty related to global trade and geopolitical/domestic tensions. Investor positioning has increased significantly and presents an increasing headwind for the market.

Japan stays our OW in regional allocation, on the back of TSE reform and increased buyback announcements. In the European context, we prefer UK which has the advantage of valuation, low beta, and highest dividend yield. As for the record stock concentration, while the weight of the top 50 stocks within S&P 500 remains roughly unchanged, the largest 10 stocks have continued to increase at the expense of the next 40 and the broader index. We reiterate that long duration trade is to have a tactical respite, Fed cuts might still be over-discounted, USD matters and could be bottoming out. In Eurozone, we stay OW Growth vs. Value as it screens cheap vs. the US, at a 15% greater discount than typical on a sector-neutral P/E metric. SMid-Caps look attractive already relative to Large-Caps in DMs ex-J, as they could underperform by another 200- 300 bps tops, but stand to deliver 34-59% of alpha over the coming 2-3 years. Chinese equities are trading below our 2024 bearish target, and are within the buying zone despite near-term volatility, as market sentiment had stabilized heading into CNY.

MSCI India (in local currency) gained 2.5% over the month. Real Estate (+8.2%) and Consumer Discretionary (+6.9%) were the strongest sector in the month, while Communication Services (-2.1%) was the weakest. Materials and Financials (-1.1% and -0.2% respectively) were the other sectors which ended in red and underperformed the broader market in the month. The top performing stocks in MSCI India in February

were: Trent, Jio Financial Services, BPCL, Cummins India and IHC. Top laggards were: One 97 Communications, Bandhan Bank, Hindalco, UPL and REC.

## Economic Review

India<sup>’</sup>s 3Q GDP surges but needs careful interpretation (link): India<sup>’</sup>s 3Q GDP (October-December) growth surged past expectations, coming in at 8.4% oya (J.P. Morgan: 6.5%; Consensus: 6.6%, RBI: 7%). While stronger growth is very encouraging, the print needs careful interpretation because it contains both signal and noise. This is because GDP is computed as the sum of Gross Value Add (GVA) on the production side – the sum of value added across agriculture, industry and services – as well as a more volatile Net Indirect Taxes (NIT) component, which comprises indirect taxes net of subsidies. As it turns out, GVA growth slowed sharply last quarter to 6.5% oya – exactly as we had forecast – from an upwardly revised 8% oya in the first half of the fiscal year. Furthermore, implicit in the NSO<sup>’</sup>s full-year estimate is that GVA is pegged to slow further in the current quarter to 5.4% oya. The GVA trajectory is therefore reflecting dynamics that we have long expected (see, here and here): that growth in 2023-24

would be a tale of two halves – strong in the first half of the fiscal year followed by a discernible slowing in the second half of the year.

India: CPI softens; IP picks-up after recent slump (link): India<sup>’</sup>s CPI predictably softened to 5.1% oya (J.P. Morgan and Consensus: 5.0%) from 5.7% in December, though largely on favorable base effects. In sequential terms, consumer prices rose 0.5% m/m, sa in January close to the 0.6% increase in December. As in recent months, the soft run of core-core price momentum continued, with core prices increasing by just 0.27% m/m, sa in January. Since September, core prices have printed in a benign 0.2- 0.3% range. Consequently, year-on-year, core-core inflation softened further, to 3.7% oya, the lowest in four years. This likely reflects existing slack in the economy (output is still below the pre-pandemic path), lower input prices and softening inflation expectations. After a string of disappointing prints, IP finally printed above expectations, coming in at 3.8% oya (J.P. Morgan: 3.3%, Consensus: 2.5% oya) in December. On a sequential basis, IP grew a solid 1.2% m/m, sa in January but this was after three consecutive contractions that cumulated to a 4.4% sequential contraction over the last three months.

RBI stays pat, but liquidity helps ease overnight rates (link): The February MPC meeting held policy rates at 6.5%, reiterated its “withdrawal of accommodation stance<sup>”</sup> and retained a cautious tone, bucking expectations of a slightly softer tone. That said, in recent days, the RBI – through a series of fine-tuning liquidity operations – has helped push down overnight money-market rates from about 6.8% closer to the policy rate of 6.5%.

India<sup>’</sup>s Interim Budget Doubles Down on Fiscal Consolidation (link): India<sup>’</sup>s Interim Budget prioritized fiscal consolidation and shunned any big-bang announcements. In fact, the projected outturns were even more conservative than we had forecast. For starters, authorities now project this year<sup>’</sup>s (FY24) fiscal deficit at 5.8% of GDP, a tad less than was budgeted (5.9% of GDP). On the back of that, the budget for next year (FY25) targets a fiscal deficit of 5.1% of GDP – a non-trivial consolidation of 0.7% of GDP. The leit-motif of central budgets in recent years has been pushing on capital expenditure. In FY24, for example, despite the deficit narrowing from 6.4% of GDP to 5.8%, central capex rose sharply from 2.7% of GDP in FY23 to 3.2% of GDP in FY24 – though this was 0.2% of GDP less than budgeted. The pace of capex is expected to slow in FY25 with the budget pegging capex to increase by another 0.2% of GDP to 3.4% in FY25.

## Consumer Staples

NSSO’s (National Sample Survey Office) recent report highlights changing trends in household consumption of various goods and services, based on consumer surveys during 2022-23, covering \~262K households in 8,723 villages and 6,115 urban blocks across the country. Overall monthly per-capita expenditure in urban/rural India households stood at Rs6,459/Rs3,773 growing at a CAGR of 8.5%/9.2% since 2011-12. At a broad level, consumer wallet share across urban and rural is moving towards non-food items, with rising spends on durables, home & personal care products, conveyance, tobacco & liquor, rents , consumer services and medical care. Within the foods basket, we see a pertinent shift away from basic staples (cereals, pulses, sugar/salt) towards packaged foods & beverages and animal protein (dairy, eggs, meat).
