## Tarifs: Counting the costs

Christian Keller, Barclays, UK | Marc Giannoni, BCI, US | Jian Chang, Barclays Bank, Hong Kong | Silvia Ardagna, Barclays, UK | Mark Cus Babic, BCI, US | Pooja Sriram, BCI, US | Yingke Zhou, Barclays Bank, Hong Kong

Excerpted from Global Trade: Tarifs: Counting the costs, published on September 12, 2024

Tarif increases by the US on China and the RoW would lead to output losses for all. Their size and distribution depend on US trade exposure, retaliation measures and confidence efects. China would be hardest hit, knocking \~2pp of growth, followed by the US and EA. The downturn would lead the Fed and ECB to ease more.

US tarif increases to 60% and 10% on goods imports from China and the RoW, respectively, as proposed by former President Trump, would be a significant escalation of trade restrictions. They would reverse decades of trade liberalisation, with consequences far beyond the 2018-19 US-China trade war.

Assuming symmetric retaliation from all US trading partners, but accounting for diferent confidence efects, we estimate efect on the level of real GDP to be -2.0%, -1.4% and -0.7% for China, the US and the euro area (EA), respectively, in the first 12 months. The US and China are mostly hit by the direct impact through the trade channel, while for the EA, adverse confidence efects play a significant role.

Inflation would rise in the short run, especially in the US, by around 0.9pp, as the negative supply shock from higher tarifs increases prices. It would then revert down, after the tarif increases have passed through prices and as demand weakens over time. In the EA, the inflationary efects would be much more limited (0.1%), while in China the efect of higher import prices would be more than ofset by much weaker demand (-0.1%).

US monetary policy would initially likely remain slightly tighter than in our baseline of no tarif increases, as inflation rises. But as activity starts weakening, amid trade policy uncertainty and tighter financial conditions, we would expect the Fed to ease policy rates more aggressively, possibly as much as 100bp. We think the ECB would initially keep rates in line with the no-tarif baseline, but would then lower policy rates by 50bp more than in the baseline as activity weakens.

For the larger and more closed US economy, the negative short-term efect would likely more easily dissipate in the following years. It could be more persistent for the more exportdependent China, EA and small open EM economies, especially if global trade tension were to escalate beyond the initial US tarif increases: for example, the RoW could impose tarifs as protection against diverted Chinese goods flows due to the very high US tarifs.

## What to expect when you are expecting (Fed cuts)

Themistoklis Fiotakis, Barclays, UK | Lefteris Farmakis, Barclays, UK | Andrea Kiguel, BCI, US | Mitul Kotecha, Barclays Bank, Singapore | Marek Raczko, Barclays, UK | Shinichiro Kadota, BSJL, Japan

Excerpted from FX & EM Macro Strategy Quarterly Outlook: What to expect when you are expecting (Fed cuts), published on September 10, 2024

We revise our views to allow for a little more dollar weakness in the near term, which however, we expect to be limited and reversible 12 months out.

You are not early here: The dollar typically weakens ahead of Fed cycles. The move has already been chunky by historical standards. And if the past is any guide, the market tends to overestimate Fed cuts during soft landings (an insurance premium). We are still at a stage when bouts of further dollar weakness are likely, but a large part of the dollar move is probably behind us.

A recession would be no reason to sell dollars: Our analysis shows that LatAm, CEEMEA and G10 cyclical currencies can weaken substantially, while low-yielders are, on average, stable against the USD. We also find vol premia low versus historical episodes of elevated recession risks.

Lifting the Fed constraint for EMs: EM fixed income can benefit further, in contrast to EM FX. EM terminal rates expectations remain at or above neutral. With pressures from higher-forlonger neutral US rates reversing, there is room for central banks to soothe their reaction functions. However, we expect carry erosion to weigh on EM FX.

Weak China credit impulse: This will have a significant global efect, through weaker Chinese imports, declining export prices and the commodity price channel. The USD is a beneficiary and commodity currencies will face varying degrees of pressure while the outlook for the CNY is structurally negative.

China sliding into a liquidity trap? Excess focus on supply, easing monetary conditions, insuficient demand and limited appetite for investment risk are leading to an oversupply of cash and boosting safer fixed income assets. A look back at other liquidity trap occurrences points to a clear trade-of between FX and fixed income.

Will Trump debase the dollar? We argue that the bar for an intentional debasement of the dollar is quite high. This is because low-cost policies – fiscal consolidation and multilateral devaluation – face significant coordination challenges, while high-cost policies – eroding the Fed's independence and unilateral intervention – are simply too costly.

## Election 2024: Trade policy implications

Venu Krishna, CFA, BCI, US | Japinder Chawla, CFA, BCI, US | Rex Feng, BCI, US | Madhuresh Rai, CFA, BCI, US | Young Chen, BCI, US

Excerpted from U.S. Equity Strategy: Election 2024: Trade policy implications, published on September 12, 2024

Should Trump's latest proposed tarifs be enacted in a potential second term scenario, we see a 3.2% drag on 2025 SPX EPS, with an added 1.5% hit from retaliatory actions. Materials, Discretionary, Industrials, Technology, & Healthcare appear most at risk. We revisit our tarif stock screen, integrating bottom-up analysis.

While national polling averages indicate that Kamala Harris is leading Donald Trump in the race for the White House (see Figures 1 and 2), the race is much tighter in key battleground states. In addition, we believe that in the most likely outcome of a divided Congress, the 2025 White House may resort to executive and regulatory actions to advance policies that do not require legislation. For example, the president has wide authority to set tarifs.

Trump has been vocal about his perception of unfair global trade, with a keen focus on countries with which the US runs large bilateral trade deficits (such as China and the EU). If he wins a second term and he enacts policies that reflect this view, then we could see a significant escalation in global trade tensions as potentially expanded tarifs are met with retaliatory action from other countries.

In our recent report Election 2024: Impact on US Equities (June 18, 2024), we screened for exposed stocks to this theme using a top-down approach, leveraging trade and gross output data to gauge the potential tarif impact on individual companies. In reality, US companies can deploy a variety of tactics to mitigate the impact of tarifs.

In this note, we revisit our top-down approach, this time using a bottom-up element by crosschecking our estimates for imports/exports versus sales ratios with fundamental factual input from Barclays’ sector analysts. Our analysts’ feedback was based on a mix of guidance provided by company managements and discussions of tarif efects either on the company earnings calls or in their financial reporting. Our screen consists of 64 names.

Using these assumptions for imports/exports versus sales ratios of S&P 500 stocks, we estimate that the direct impact of Trump's latest proposed tarifs on 2025 SPX earnings amounts to a 3.2% drag if enacted, with an additional 1.5% hit if those countries retaliate with similar measures. While the direct impact seems modest, second-order efects from a combination of higher prices and lower-growth shocks that tarifs imply could act as an incremental headwind to corporate earnings. The Materials, Discretionary, Industrials, Technology, and Healthcare sectors appear to be most at risk given their strong dependency on global supply chains.

## Cyclicals' undershoot

Emmanuel Cau, CFA, Barclays, UK | Matthew Joyce, CFA, BBI, Paris | Magesh Kumar Chandrasekaran, CFA, Barclays, UK | Emmanuel Makonga, Barclays, UK | Arihanth Bohra Jain, Barclays, UK

Excerpted from Equity Market Review: Cyclicals' undershoot, published on September 13, 2024

Oversold Cyclicals bounced this week and reversed some of their recent undershooting vs. fundamentals. With outsized rate cuts of the table for now and data not falling of a clif, Cyclicals may find a floor. China plays continue to face a bleak outlook, but sentiment/ positioning on the space is arguably very bearish now.

Cyclicals' undershoot. Erratic price action in equities suggests confusion amid ever changing market narratives. This is understandably frustrating for investors, but is reminiscent of typical September seasonality and US election cycle. On top of that, stocks usually struggle around the start of Fed easing cycles, and this time is no diferent, with a first cut expected next week. As we have discussed already (see Recession, or no recession?, 6 Sep), the answers to whether a recession occurred or not won't be known until later (ie. ex-post). Therefore, equity price action may stay volatile and data dependant, until we get more clarity on the economic outlook. Still, we remain of the view that the global easing cycle will enable the economy to achieve a soft landing, as per our Global Outlook published this week (Global Outlook “As goes America, so goes the world”, 12 Sep). Market internals don't give it the benefit of the doubt, however, as Cyclicals have underperformed Defensives for months now and given back all their ytd gains. Although a fundamental catalyst for a sustained beta rebound seems missing, which is why we stick to a broadly neutral Cyclicals vs. Defensives stance, technicals like RSIs turned oversold for most cyclical sectors, while defensives looked overbought. Even compared vs. activity data like PMIs, recent Cyclicals' underperformance appeared overdone. So this week Cyclicals' tentative rebound from oversold level is a logical catch-up, in our view.

Rates expectations matter to the Cyclicals/Defensives trade. After the ECB cut rates this week, next week will be busy again on the policy front, with Fed, BOE and BOJ meetings. Progress on disinflation amid slowing growth has led to a sharp repricing lower in US rates expectations. This in turn has helped the rotation in equity market leadership from Cyclicals to Defensives. Although we expect the Fed to start cutting rates next week, we don't see it

endorsing current market pricing given the labour market isn't collapsing, while the latest CPI/PPI releases (see Not ideal, but not concerning yet, 11 Sep) surprised a bit to the upside. We believe that some stabilisation in rates expectations is needed for Cyclicals to find a floor, and would reassure on the state of the economy. BOJ meeting will likely get a lot of attention too, given the spillover efects of the recent JPY appreciation. ## Rates Strategy

## Tarifs: Cross-asset implications

Anshul Pradhan, BCI, US | Rohan Khanna, Barclays, UK | Moyeen Islam, Barclays, UK | Joseph Abate, BCI, US | Shinichiro Kadota, BSJL, Japan | Michael Pond, CFA, BCI, US

Excerpted from Global Trade: Tarifs: Cross-asset implications, published on September 12, 2024

## The "stag" in "stagflation" would win out

Across-the-board tarifs are likely to result in a significantly adverse global growth shock, with heightened trade uncertainty. Absent a material ofset from easier fiscal policy, we believe longterm yields would move lower as a result. The US would likely face a significant inflationary impulse at the same time, which would lead the very front end to sell of as Fed cuts are delayed. This would result in a flatter 2s10s yield curve, posing a threat to popular curvesteepener trades, in our view. In the euro area, the negative growth impulse would dominate across the curve.

## US Rates: Reshaping the curve

As noted in the earlier chapter "A new trade war: Counting the costs", across-the-board tarifs imposed by the US on trading partners would create a material stagflationary impulse. In the scenario of 60% and 10% tarifs on goods imports from China and the RoW, respectively, US inflation would be 0.9pp higher, and assuming heightened trade uncertainty amid full retaliation from trading partners, US real GDP would be 1.4pp lower (both versus the baseline). This poses a conundrum for rates investors; should they respond to higher inflation by pushing yields higher or to lower growth by pushing yields lower?

We believe the very front end would sell of, as the Fed would slow the easing campaign, which, in light of aggressive cuts priced in over 2025, would result in higher front-end rates. However, further out the curve, we believe the "stag" in "stagflation" would win out, with the markets ultimately pricing in more cuts cumulatively, resulting in lower long-term rates. This would be exacerbated by a bullish impulse from rest of the world, which would primarily face an adverse growth shock. A potential ofset would be the fiscal response in the US, and the make up of the Congress would be key to watch.

## A couple of things of note:

First, markets appear to be pricing in a very low likelihood of an aggressive price shock. Figure 17 shows that 1yf 1y inflation CPI swaps are trading at just 2.2%, implying PCE inflation slightly below the Fed's 2% target. Further out the curve, 5y5y inflation swaps are also trading at the recent lows. One could argue that these are being weighed down by the tail risk of a recession and the resulting very low inflation in that scenario; a read across asset classes suggests that recession risks are still muted (for example, credit spreads are quite tight). In our view, low inflation swap rates reflect a benign baseline view and reduced worries about upside inflation risks, which is also why 5y5y inflation swap rates are low. Our economists estimate that across-the-board tarifs would raise 1y-ahead CPI inflation 0.85pp, pushing it well above market pricing.

Second, given low worries about rising inflation, markets are putting a very low weight on the Fed's keeping the policy rate elevated; doing so would come as a meaningful surprise. Figure 18 shows the option market-implied distribution of the policy rate by YE 25. As can be seen, the modal outlook is for the Fed to cut to about 3%. The likelihood of very aggressive cuts is not unusually high (15% probability of the policy rate <1.5%), which is consistent with recession risks not being seen as unusually elevated. On the flip side, there is about a 20% probability of the policy rate being >4% by YE 25. Hence, even if the Fed does not hike in

response to higher inflation, pausing the easing campaign (which, in our view, is very plausible in a trade war) is also seen as a low likelihood scenario.

Hence, the market is of the view that high inflation is not a concern any more and the Fed can ease aggressively over the coming year. A trade war would likely change that picture.

Some have argued that conduct of monetary policy might change under President Trump and the Fed would set policy on a dovish path, mitigating its efect on the policy rate. For instance, during his administration, President Trump repeatedly called for the Fed to lower rates. Still, he has also noted that he would simply not reappoint (rather than try to fire) Powell as chair of the Federal Reserve Board after his term expires (in May 2026). Further, we are skeptical that the next Fed chair would deviate from setting monetary policy with respect to its dual mandate. For instance, in 2020, the Senate voted not to confirm the nomination of Judy Shelton, who was widely seen as an unorthodox candidate, to the Federal Reserve Board.

Further out the curve, the dynamics would be diferent. The key drivers for long-term rates would be a reassessment of the the trough of the easing cycle and term premium. Both would argue for lower long-term rates.

Ultimately, the price shock should prove temporary and below trend growth create slack in the labor market and pull inflation forecasts lower (versus the prior baseline). In a typical policy rule, that would argue for the Fed eventually to bring the policy rate below the prior baseline. At the end of the day, global trade restrictions would likely be detrimental to potential growth; as a result, all else equal, one should also expect the neutral rate to be lower. Figure 19 shows that as the markets have priced in a lower trough of the easing cycle, long-term rates have also come down.

Further, in the rest of the word, the impulse is tilted even more towards an adverse growth shock. For instance, in the euro area, our economists expect inflation to rise a tenth, but growth to fall 0.7pp (China growth would also be marked down substantially). This would be bullish for global bonds (see below for the efect on European long-term rates), which would reduce the term premium in the US as well. For instance, if Bunds meaningfully break below 2%, that would pull US long-term yields lower as well.

Hence, across-the-board tarifs would be bullish for long-term rates. Overall, the 2s10s yield curve is likely to flatten, as the front end would need to reprice to higher inflation and a delayed easing cycle and the long end to lower growth prospects.

A key question for rates investors would be the overall stance of fiscal policy amid an ongoing trade war. A budget deficit-neutral trade policy, ie, tarif revenues being used to fund tax cuts and provide government subsidies, would mitigate the growth efect; however it would still be growth negative, as heightened uncertainty would weigh on investments. However, a material expansion of deficits could potentially more than ofset the adverse growth efect and tilt the impulse to higher inflation, rather than lower growth. Hence, the makeup of Congress would be key. For instance, in a unified Republican government, rates could move higher across the curve. Figure 20 shows that the election markets have put a relatively low likelihood of R sweep; such an outcome would come as a surprise to the markets.

## EUR Rates: Confidence hit to rally Bunds

For the past year and a half, our central thesis on Bunds has been that they are in the passenger seat of global fixed income. Specifically, caught in a tug-of-war between sizable foreign shocks and domestic fundamentals, Bunds have been trading in at 2-3% yields. If term premium shifts were the primary driver of Bunds in 2023, the evolution of rate expectations has been the primary driver this year (Figure 21).

In recent months, pessimism about Europe's growth prospects has grown, given that the manufacturing sector remains in doldrums and slack is building in EA labour markets. Even though headline growth numbers are better than last year's, the underlying picture is weak, with a consumption bounce from positive real wage growth not coming through yet. In this already weak setting, the imposition of tarifs could turn out to be a body blow to already fragile market sentiment on Europe (Figure 22).

Indeed, as our economists have shown in the previous chapter, the trade war headwind for Europe is likely to manifest more powerfully through the confidence channel, on account of higher policy uncertainty, rather than the actual trade channel. They see negative and longlasting efects of these policies on euro area real GDP, but relatively muted efects on inflation. Calibrating the shock to the scale of the 2017-18 trump tarifs, our economists pencil in a potential 1% decline in euro area real GDP and only a 0.2% increase in the headline price level. Naturally, relative to their baseline view, which is for the ECB’s terminal rate reaching 2.5%, they see more easing of 50bp, on account of this negative growth outcome.

All else equal, these dynamics would create a bullish backdrop for European rates and may finally be able to move them into the driving seat. In Breaking Bunds (below 2%), we had argued that to generate a sustained rally, market pricing efectively needs to shift towards a hard-landing. From a domestic perspective, the necessary conditions include a downside revision in the underlying inflation profile or a significant surprise to the ECB's growth and inflation projections (Figure 23). Alternatively, Bunds will need to rely on the kindness of strangers—ie, external spillovers—to help them glide below 2% (Figure 24).

What the tarif shock, as calibrated by our economics team, does is generate bullish impulses from domestic and external spillovers for EUR rates. With the hit to growth, the ECB would likely look through the small uptick in inflation and instead argue that the weakened demand outlook and uncertainty shock would ensure that any increase in inflation is not persistent; hence, monetary policy can respond to ofset the weakness in demand. In the US, as argued above, the bulk of the pressure higher would be in the front end of the curve, so the long end would be unlikely to generate strong bearish spillovers. In summary, this outcome should allow for Bunds to break well below 2%.

## Economic Outlook

## Calibrating the cycle

Christian Keller, Barclays, UK | Mark Cus Babic, BCI,US | Akash Utsav, Barclays, UK

Excerpted from Global Economics Weekly, published on September 13, 2024

The ECB's 25bp cut this week was expected, and we believe the Fed will opt to start with 25bp next week. Yet 50bp Fed cuts or an ECB cut in October remain live as banks evaluate economic data, markets and the political economy. This will also play a role when BoE and BoJ stand pat next week and Brazil's BCB likely hikes.

## An asynchronous easing cycle...

It is easing season for monetary policy, with the Fed highly likely to join other central banks in reversing some of the steep post-pandemic hiking cycle. However, a closer look across regions shows the global cycle to be far from fully synchronised. Japan’s unique macro mechanics make the BoJ adjust its policy rates higher when its peers lower theirs; China’s occupation with exchange rate and domestic financial stability keeps it from lowering rates as much as the acute deflationary dynamics would otherwise dictate; and Brazil, to take one prominent EM example, is about to start hiking rates again next week, just as the Fed is only starting its cutting cycle (and having originally hiked much ahead of the Fed). Thus, the notion of a synchronous global easing cycle misses many nuances.

## ... against uncertain data and politics

The reason for these asynchronous central banks moves is not only diferent macro data, but also politics. The likely hike in Brazil has much to do with a deterioration in inflation expectations as a consequence of fiscal decisions and BCB nominations (and more recently also increased actual inflation pressures). Similarly, when and how much Turkey’s CBT will cut is widely perceived to depend also on how long President Erdogan can stomach the orthodoxy of fighting high inflation with high interest rates.

But political factors also play a role for institutionally more independent central banks in advanced economies, even if more indirectly. The path to policy normalisation by the BoJ will not be independent from the economic convictions of Japan’s next political leader. Her or his attitudes towards 'Abenomics' and 'reflation' could define how fast and how far the BoJ will dare to go. This is why the election campaign starting this week will be followed by those focused on Japan’s interest rate path (see LDP presidential election update: A fierce battle with low visibility).

The ECB is fiercely independent, but the GC analysis cannot ignore the euro area’s political situation, not least because of its fiscal consequences. France finally has a new prime minister, but the government has already requested a delay for submitting its budget after the oficial EU deadline. So has Italy this week. Germany's fiscal sustainability is Iess of a concern, but the government in Berlin faces another important test with upcoming elections in Brandenburg (22 September), which could further weaken the central government coalition and dampen the already gloomy sentiment among the country's businesses and households.

The UK's BoE is all but certain not to change interest rates next week. This is mainly to gather more economic data before a decision to cut next (in November, we expect), but in part also to have the uncertainty of the new government's first budget out of the way. The latter has become a focal point, following warnings by the new Labor government about having inherited larger-than-expected fiscal shortfalls from its predecessors and, thus, needing to raise taxes and/or issue more debt.<sup>2</sup>

Last but by far not least, the Fed cannot escape from the approaching presidential elections (5 November). Trump has warned the Fed against any cuts so shortly before the election, while Powell explicitly stated that the Fed 'would never use our tools to support or oppose a political party, a politician or any political outcome'.<sup>3</sup> Abstracting from these tensions, the Fed must observe that the two candidates are running on almost diametrically opposed policy platforms (eg, higher versus lower corporate taxes). A likely divided Congress means that many of these policy proposals would probably not achieve approval. Some of them, however, such as sharp tarif increases in the event of a Trump administration, would stand a realistic chance of being implemented (via presidential decree). The economic consequences for the US and the rest of the world would be far reaching. The Fed is aware of these looming uncertainties. This week's Trump-Harris debate may have further established Harris as a stronger candidate against Trump than incumbent President Biden was, but the election outcome remains highly uncertain.

Thus, the Fed and central bankers around the world will typically refrain from referencing politics, but the truth is that they cannot ignore them as they calibrate the easing cycle. Data dependence is not enough.

Our views and analysis in more detail:

## FOMC to begin the easing cycle with a 25bp move next week

In the US, August CPI surprised to the upside, with core CPI rising 0.28% m/m (+12bp vs. July; August CPI: OER surprises to the upside). This was due to services, led by an uptick in OER inflation and strong increases in airline fares and lodging away from home, while core goods remained in deflation. However, certain key components of the PPI, such as airfares, were softer, implying that core PCE is likely to be weaker than the CPI counterpart, printing at 0.14% m/m, a touch lower than in July. Next week, we expect control group retail sales in August to post another solid month of growth (0.4% m/m) and see industrial production jumping 0.8% m/m, rebounding from the previous month's decline.

Next week, we expect the FOMC to begin its easing cycle with a 25bp cut, based on commentary by Williams and Waller on the eve of the blackout period. Recent news reporting has intensified speculation that the FOMC will opt for a 50bp cut, which we recognise as possible, but we do not think those reports were meant to tip of to markets about the Fed’s likely decision. Aside from the rate decision, the meeting also brings the updated SEP, where we expect Q4/Q4 GDP growth in the vicinity of 2% through 2026 (and in 2027), the unemployment rate peaking at 4.3% at end-2024, and core PCE price inflation descending to a downwardly revised 2.7% in 2024 and 2.2% in 2025, then to 2% from 2026 onward. For the funds rate, we expect the SEP to show a steady stream of gradual, meeting-by-meeting cuts over the medium term, including three in 2024, five in 2025, and one each in 2026 and 2027. This implies a terminal funds rate target of 2.75-3.00%, in line with the median long-term dot (2.8%). We expect Powell to be noncommittal about the pace of cuts in the post-meeting presser, continuing to emphasize that the FOMC would be responsive to further evidence of deterioration in the upcoming data flow.

Besides the economic developments, this week had the first presidential debate between Vice President Harris and former President Trump, and our Washington Research analyst writes that post-debate poll and prediction markets suggest Harris won it. The next catalysts to watch will be the vice presidential debate on 1 October and the possibility of a government shutdown at the end of September.

## ECB to cut again in December, but October remains live

In the euro area, the highlight of the week was the ECB meeting, where the Governing Council trimmed the deposit rate 25bp (ECB Watching: A guarded cut), but avoided committing to any future decisions and ofered no guidance. Besides the rate decision, the ECB also revised down its growth forecasts, taking a more pessimistic view on consumption. We expect the next cut in December, but think the October meeting is 'live', and our confidence in back-to-back cuts at the beginning of 2025 has increased.

Other than the ECB, this week brought July industrial production, which fell 0.3% m/m, while next week, the focus will be on final August HICP and flash September consumer confidence.

## BoE likely on hold next week

In the UK, the data this week included the labour market report, which showed continued easing (UK Labour Market: Under the hood, easing continues), as regular private sector wage growth softened to 4.9% 3m/y in July from 5.3% 3m/y in June. As we had expected, singlemonth unemployment in August snapped back after an idiosyncratic drop in July, though the headline 3m rate ticked down as a result of that print. Meanwhile, GDP started the third quarter somewhat softer than we had thought, showing no growth in July (we and the consensus expected 0.2% m/m; July GDP: flat start to Q3).

That said, we do not think these data are suficiently weak to push the MPC to cut Bank Rate next week, and the totality of data since the August forecast update has evolved broadly as expected (Bank of England Preview: Time to take a breath). The Bank also signaled a cautious easing cycle after the previous decision. Thus, we expect an 8-1 vote in favour of a hold next week, along with unchanged guidance. Instead, the focus of the meeting will be on the balance sheet announcement, where we expect the MPC to vote for a continued £100bn of QT. After next week, we expect Bank Rate to be cut sequentially in November, December and February, and then in May and August, by 25bp each time, to reach 3.75% in August 2025.

In China, August inflation data surprised the consensus to the downside. In the same vein, credit expansion as measured by total social financing growth slowed a little further, to 8.1% y/y from 8.2% y/y in July (China: No signs of improvement in credit demand). Elsewhere, the trade data showed a large divergence between exports, which were stronger than expected and partly ofset

Next week, we will get further indications on activity in China with the release of August retail sales (to stay below 3% for the third consecutive month), industrial production (to moderate slightly), and investment (to ease further).

For the PBoC, we think that an RRR cut of 50bp is due in the coming weeks, but do not expect a change to the 1-year medium-term LFR or the loan prime rate next week.

## BoJ to stand pat amid the LDP presidential race

In Japan, this week marks the oficial beginning of the LDP presidential election, which will efectively determine who will be the next prime minister (LDP presidential election update: A fierce battle with low visibility). At this stage, the race is crowded and unpredictable. On monetary policy, most of the candidates' views are unclear or they favour a hands-of approach, while three are relatively hawkish (but unlikely to nudge the BoJ beyond its own normalization initiatives) and one is notably dovish. The candidates will hold press conferences and debates on 13-24 September, ahead of the voting on the 27th.

However, with a change to the face of the party, there is a growing expectation that a general election will be brought forward to this autumn. Combined with the US election 5 November, we think the BoJ will be in a wait-and-see approach while political uncertainty clears. This includes next week, when the BoJ should stand pat, and October. Instead, it will likely be confirming a stable recovery in consumption, investment, and services price markups into the second half of the fiscal year, setting the stage for the next hike in January, but with the possibility of an earlier move in December. That said, market volatility and adverse economic developments in the US could present headwinds in policy normalisation. Next week also brings August nationwide CPI, where we expect the core measure to move sideways at 2.7% y/y.
