# Gen Z: Soft living, but financially prudent

Now that Gen Z has just begun to outnumber the Baby Boomers in the US, a key question for corporates and asset managers is how the younger generation's changing financial habits will affect spending and investment outcomes. We see changes in both - just not in ways most people expect.

In this piece, we note the 'soft living' phenomenon that is contributing to Gen Z's financial choices. This involves the greater prioritisation of life over work. Yet at the same time, our surveys show Gen Z wants to save and invest to ensure financial security. They tend to balance their books each month, and they buy homes and make investments quicker than did Millennials.

We believe Gen Z's more traditional values of financial prudence will likely stick. They are more insecure about their finances and yet they are financially literate, have money to invest, and appreciate risk. This is likely to make them more proactive at building wealth to finance a balanced life.

Gen Z's changing impact extends to politics. We look at what their different views mean ahead of the US presidential election and UK general election.

The big takeaway is that Gen Z appears to be more conservative with both their money and political choices compared with the previous generation. In some ways, that resembles older generations. It seems some traits come full circle.

## Gen Z has already overtaken Baby Boomers in the US

Coming out of covid, the wealthiest generation in the US has been outnumbered by a younger generation. Generation Z, born between 1997 and 2012, is generally defined as those aged 12-27. As their number reached 69.1m in 2022, they surpassed the 68.6m of Baby Boomers, born between 1946 and 1964.

This changing of the guard moment may reveal changes in behaviour that could reshape economies, financial markets and politics. This piece explains how Gen Z differ from other generations in their attitudes towards investment and finance. As Gen Z's entry into the workforce accelerates, and they ramp up their spending and investment, these differences will soon be felt by corporates and asset managers

Maybe most critically, Gen Z's political desires are different to those of Millennials and they may soon make their voice heard in elections.

## 'Soft living' combined with financial prudence

'Soft living' means to choose wellbeing over stress, it is about prioritising happiness and personal growth, even if it involves splurging. It is about rejecting the concept of 'doing it all' and embracing boundaries at work. 'Soft living' does not mean being lazy and does not necessarily mean scaling back a successful career. But, for some, it means saying "no" to working beyond normal business hours.

The charts below show part of the 'soft living' trend for Gen Z. They place more value on family and friends than their job relative to Millennials, something that stems from being more burned out at work. Later in the piece we examine these behaviours in more detail - Financial prudence stickiness may be amplified by Gen Z's preference for 'soft living'.

## 'Soft living' does not necessarily mean 'soft saving

Gen Z appear to understand that 'soft living' requires hard finances. They are not 'soft saving', which means spending today instead of focusing on long-term finances.

In this regard, the evidence shows that Gen Z are likely to differ from Millennials somewhat, even though both are 'young' generations. While the two share some common traits such as stronger preference for experiences versus material things, Gen Z appear to be more financially prudent. In fact, their conservative approach to personal finances resembles more older generations.

One way to observe Gen Z's financial prudence is in their living costs. The following chart shows that they are more likely than other generations to have money left over after paying for their normal spending.

Gen Z's feelings of financial insecurity aligns with the evidence showing they have a higher risk of poverty. In fact, people younger than 25 years old are ten percentage points more likely to be poor than are people aged over 64. Before the 2008 financial crisis, the difference was minimal. <sup>3</sup>

One potential key reason behind young people's heightened financial stress and worry is that many entered adulthood or the workforce during covid and have experienced the social dislocation, as well as high inflation.

## Financial prudence drives precautionary savings

As Gen Z lives within their means, they can save quicker (from a lower base) than can some other generations. Indeed, our dbDIG survey found that 70.4 per cent of Gen Zers in Europe save into either a savings, cash, or deposit account every month.

This savings rate is similar to that of Millennials, however, when Millennials were as young as Gen Z are today, they were not as frugal. Indeed, a separate survey showed that 40 per cent of Millennials at the age of 23 said they had some savings, versus 48 per cent of Gen Zers at 23.<sup>4</sup>

Most importantly, Gen Zers said they save 4.2 per cent of their income during an average month. The share is the same as that for Millennials (who are generally more financially stable), and much higher than that for Gen X and Baby Boomers. This is unusual because generally younger generations save less than the older ones due to their smaller incomes.

At the start of the 1980s, when Baby Boomers were in their 20s and early 30s, their savings rate of around 12 per cent was much smaller than that of older people then, who saved between 20-30 per cent of their income.<sup>5</sup>

Gen Z's desire for financial security is reflected in the fact that they are more likely than were other young people to save for 'general' purposes rather than for something specific, like a car or a house. Indeed, a smaller share of 44 per cent of Gen Zers aged 25 and under said they saved for a specific purpose, versus 57 per cent of Millennials when they were of the same age.

## Precautionary savings translate into investments, which then translate into savings..

The second most important reason for why Gen Z invests is for "unexpected expenses".<sup>6</sup> The first reason is to have "enough money to travel". The combination of the two further suggests the trend towards 'soft living' with willingness to fund it appropriately.

Gen Z's 'rainy day' savings contrast with how Millennials invested at a similar age. A survey<sup>7</sup> found that the main factor behind Millennials' decision to start investing was "curiosity".

## Gen Z investors are financially literate, have money to invest and appreciate risk

Growing financial literacy in young people in Western countries is driving investing behaviour. In the US, close to six in ten Gen Zers have at least some investments. In Canada, nearly three quarters do, the highest proportion in the Western world.

The high participation rate of Gen Z investors is because, unlike older cohorts when they were young, Gen Z learn about finance from young age. Indeed, 38 per cent of those who invest said they learned about financial topics in school, while 42 per cent said they learned in university. These figures are much higher compared to

Millennial and Gen X investors.

Covid amplified younger people's desire to know about investing. In the UK, 34 per cent of Gen Zers said they learned how to invest after the pandemic. It comes with no surprise that about a quarter of US, UK, and Canadian Gen Z investors said they started investing before they were 18 years old.

Gen Z investment preferences differ by region. A full 55 per cent of American Gen Z investors older than 18 say they own crypto, while 41 per cent own individual stocks. This is not the same in Asia, where Chinese Gen Zers appear to be more risk-averse. Only seven per cent of them invest before they turn 18 and their favourite investments are mutual funds and wealth management products.

The common trait between Western and Eastern Gen Z is that both groups like risk. Both in the US and in China, 46 per cent of Gen Zers said they are willing to take "substantial or above-average" risks to reach financial goals.

Even though Gen Zers are risk-takers, they do explore more traditional assets, such as gold, when NFTs, meme stocks and crypto do not show positive results.<sup>8</sup>

Gen Z investment attitudes come as a stark contrast to those of Millennials at the same age. One survey<sup>9</sup> found that when Millennials were between 18-25 years old, only 18 per cent of them were investing.

## The growth of investing apps with small amounts has helped

A key reason why Gen Z invests is because they can do so with small amounts of money. Indeed, 67 per cent of Gen Z investors say this is a major factor in their investment decisions. Hence the popularity of investing apps among Gen Z with 65 per cent of them reporting usage, a higher share than Millennials or Gen X.

Gen Z find their financial information on social media … but don't really trust it

Social media is Gen Z's main source of financial information. It is where nearly half of Gen Z investors learn about financial topics. Yet when it comes to trust, social media is way down at the bottom of the list. Gen Z investors first trust their family and then, financial professionals. The latter, however, is least likely to be a source of information.

However, one implication that is possible is that it may encourage more savings in the short-tomedium term. The chart shows that Gen Z is much more inclined than Millennials to think their savings will increase, rather than decrease or stay the same, in the next 12 months. ## Homeownership as a cornerstone of financial security

About a quarter of Americans under 25 years old own a home.<sup>10</sup> This is not too different from when the Silent Generation and the Baby Boomers were that young.<sup>11</sup> The fact that younger people are considering homeownership is a growing phenomenon. Homeownership growth rates were some of the highest among people younger than 29 years old between 2016 and 2022.<sup>12</sup> Interestingly, last year

Gen Zers bought the smallest properties.<sup>13</sup>

One big difference in Gen Zers' attitudes towards real estate is that they are less likely to delay buying a house until they are married - compared to Millennials and other generations. The largest decline in marriage rates among 20-something-year-olds happened at the interchange of the Silent Generation and Baby Boomers during the late 1960s.<sup>14</sup> Since then, young people have been postponing such major life events.

One reason why Gen Z buyers can afford a house is because they are more likely to live with their parents until they do. Indeed, 30 per cent moved from their parents' home into their own.

Homeownership among younger people could follow the trajectory of older generations if we are to take into account that wealth accumulation follows the same trajectory over the lifetime of a person regardless of generation. For example, Millennials earned a similar amount to Gen Xers at the age of 30. Furthermore, Gen X earned a similar amount to Baby Boomers at the age of 40, as well as 50.<sup>15</sup>

## Gen Z's conservative behaviour may stick

One theory for why there is a notable difference between Gen Z and Millennials relates to the timing of when they entered the workforce. Millennials began to look for jobs around the financial crisis of 2008 and 2009. Then, unemployment was high and policy stimulus less of a buffer. In contrast, Gen Z has entered the workforce during times of quantitative easing and with the backdrop of policymakers doing whatever it takes to provide a buffer to economic problems, particularly during covid.

The timing of the '08-'09 financial crisis may give weight to various studies that show very different attitudes in the groups of people who graduate into recessions or into good times.

Financial prudence developed at early age tends to define a person's life. The Silent Generation stayed frugal because of the Great Depression and war when they were young. Gen Z now face the aftermath of a great event in modern history and that has made them financially aware. Indeed, in the UK, a survey<sup>16</sup> found that 40 per cent of the younger generation believe the pandemic has opened their eyes about the importance of secure finances.

Today there is a broad range of financial tools and investment products. And so the younger generation has a lot more choices with which to keep financially prudent. Also, they are surrounded by peers on social media that share advice on how to manage finances. Advice such as how to combine a 9-5 life with travel while managing a budget, which should bode well with the trend of 'soft living'.

The performance of bonds and equities may contribute to Gen Z's view of savings and investments

Gen Z's willingness to take on equity market risk means they have the potential to earn higher returns than did some prior generations. This may be the case if equity markets continue their long-term appreciation trend. Note that equity investing is easier today than it was 20 years ago and more. That is due to the proliferation of apps and financial products that make it easy to invest with small amounts of money.

This outlook contrasts with attitudes of other generations when they were young.
In particular, some older generations preferred more of a mix of equities and bonds.

More broadly, decisions around savings and investments are often dictated by economic cycles and financial booms and busts. At the start of the 1970s, when the youngest of the Silent Generation were in their mid 20s, yields on ten-year US treasuries embarked on a steep trajectory and continued to increase until 1981, when they reached nearly 16 per cent. That made savings accounts, bonds and certificate of deposits a popular choice.

## Financial prudence stickiness may be amplified by Gen Z's preference for 'soft living

Because Gen Z prefer 'soft living', we think they will continue to be financially prudent. In some ways, this is different to how Millennials approached work, life, and finances. Figure 2 at the start shows that globally for Gen Zers, family and friends are more important to their sense of identity, than work. That comes as more Gen Zers than Millennials in 44 countries say they are exhausted at work. Gen Z appear to be more mentally distracted from their job and struggle to perform to the best of their ability at work more so than Millennials.

Gen Z thinks burnout at work is too high of a price to pay. Nearly three in four Gen Zers say they would rather have a better quality of life than more money in the bank. Meanwhile, 23 per cent of Gen Zers said they would respond to work emails and/or messages outside of normal working hours for the entire working week. In contrast, 30 per cent of Millennials said they would answer.<sup>17</sup>

By not placing work at the centre of their identity, Gen Z is more confident to make demands from their employers. On top of this, they are entering adulthood as mental health discussions are more common and that appears to have added to their desire to prioritise a work-life balance.

More Gen Zers than Millennials are prioritising mental health. For example, 25 per cent of Gen Zers who were offered access to mental health apps used this offer versus 22 per cent of Millennials. On top of this, 23 per cent of Gen Zers used paid-for counselling and therapy, compared to 19 per cent of Millennials.

Gen Z prioritise family over work when considering where to buy a home Gen Z is more likely to make important life decisions, such as buying a home, in consideration with family and friends rather than work.

This is different from how older generations approach home buying. Millennials, as well as Gen X, are much more inclined to choose a home that is more convenient for work.

## Conservative leanings of Gen Z is reflected in political choices

Gen Z may be crucial in the upcoming US presidential election. People often assume that younger generations lean to the left. But, Gen Z appears to be leaning more Republican than did Millennials. That comes as Republicans have organised conferences for aspiring Gen Z politicians for the fourth year in a row.

One poll<sup>18</sup> showed that if they had to made a choice right now, more Gen Z would vote for Trump than for Biden. Indeed, in answering the question "If the 2024 presidential election were held today, who would you vote for?", of those aged 18- 29, 43 percent said Biden, while 49 per cent said Trump. Interestingly, more Millennials aged 30-44 preferred Biden over Trump in December, as seen in the chart below.

Figure 13: The Times/Siena Poll of Registered Voters Nationwide shows that in December more Gen Zers would vote for Trump if elections was held today, while more Millennials said they would prefer Biden

If the 2024 presidential election were held today, who would you vote?
![](images/476ba1ea85d2ce75ec8371891b7f9ec50addc00f59dd26fb81a0d031633237d0.jpg)
Source : The New York Times, Deutsche Bank

This swing of Gen Z preferring Trump over Biden happened for the first time. More than that, the swing was enough to give Trump a narrow lead over Biden overall (46 v 44 per cent). Other polls show different results for Gen Z. But the fact that the polls disagree highlights that Gen Z politics is far more balanced between 'left' and 'right' than most people usually assume about younger generations.

The preference of Gen Z voters in this year's election could be crucial as Trump did not do well with this cohort in 2020. Indeed, Trump performed worse among voters aged 18-29 in the 2020 election when compared to 2016 across almost every Midwestern state that was important to his re-election campaign. <sup>19</sup>

Such polling results are not evident just in the US. In Europe too, young people appear to be trending towards the political right. Several reports show young voters choose far-right populist parties such as Party for Freedom in the Netherlands, National Rally in France and Brothers of Italy. In fact, Georgia Meloni's party at some point was the most popular among people under 35 years old.<sup>20</sup>

The reported trend of younger people leaning more to the political right is important because of traditional assumptions that young voters generally lean left. It was not always like that. In the 1992 UK election, the Conservatives did fairly well with 18- 24-year-olds as there was only an eight percentage point difference between the share of this group's votes and that of the general population.<sup>21</sup>

Then things changed. In the 2019 election in the UK, fewer younger people voted right. Only 19 percent of those aged 18 - 24 voted for the Conservatives, compared with 45 percent of the entire population.<sup>22</sup> But things are shifting again now. The return to the 'older' behaviour of considering conservative parties could have a significant impact on the next 10-20 years of elections in the Western world.

Macroeconomic fluctuations often account for most of the risks associated with exposures to instruments that promise to pay fixed or variable interest rates. For an investor who is long fixed-rate instruments (thus receiving these cash flows), increases in interest rates naturally lift the discount factors applied to the expected cash flows and thus cause a loss. The longer the maturity of a certain cash flow and the higher the move in the discount factor, the higher will be the loss. Upside surprises in inflation, fiscal funding needs, and FX depreciation rates are among the most common adverse macroeconomic shocks to receivers. But counterparty exposure, issuer creditworthiness, client segmentation, regulation (including changes in assets holding limits for different types of investors), changes in tax policies, currency convertibility (which may constrain currency conversion, repatriation of profits and/or liquidation of positions), and settlement issues related to local clearing houses are also important risk factors. The sensitivity of fixed-income instruments to macroeconomic shocks may be mitigated by indexing the contracted cash flows to inflation, to FX depreciation, or to specified interest rates - these are common in emerging markets. The index fixings may - by construction - lag or mis-measure the actual move in the underlying variables they are intended to track. The choice of the proper fixing (or metric) is particularly important in swaps markets, where floating coupon rates (i.e., coupons indexed to a typically short-dated interest rate reference index) are exchanged for fixed coupons. Funding in a currency that differs from the currency in which coupons are denominated carries FX risk.
