# China: FX reserves largely in line with expectation

A glance at BoP and customs’ trade balance difference

China’s June FX reserves fell US\$9.7bn to \$3,222.4bn, largely in line with our expectation of \$3,225.8bn. It follows the US\$31.2bn rise in May. For 1H as a whole, FX reserves fell US\$15.6bn. Same as May, gold reserves stayed unchanged, after solid increases in the prior 18 consecutive months.

Our baseline forecast looks for 0.2%m/m sa uptick in exports, and 1.2% gain in imports, leaving trade surplus elevated at US\$84.3bn in June. Services account has roughly recovered to the pre-pandemic level, averaging at a US \$21.2bn deficit in Jan-May. Hence, we expect current account surplus for goods and services may have moved up to \$39.3bn (vs. \$33.6bn in May).

Meanwhile, the US dollar index ticked up from 104.7 to 105.9 in June, leading to an estimated currency valuation loss of US\$15.6bn. Taken together, we tentatively estimate a US\$33.4bn implied capital outflow in June (vs. -US \$22.0bn in May).

Note that, foreign investors added another US\$24.0bn holding of Chinese bonds in May, led by \$12.2bn inflows for NCDs and \$7.1bn for policy bank bonds. June foreign bond flows data is not published yet. Our strategists tracking suggests a return of retail bond outflows in June after May’s inflows, though at a moderate pace. We expect foreign bond inflows may have continued in June, but at a slower pace than May. For equity flows, Stock Connects recorded another US\$17.3bn net outflows in June (vs. US\$7.7bn in May), the largest net outflow in the past 9 months. In further breakdown, we had \$6.1bn outflows via Northbound Connect, and \$11.1bn outflows via Southbound Connect.

The gap between goods balance under the SAFE’s BoP current account and China Customs’ merchandise trade balance has gained more market attention recently. It happened after the US Treasury’s recent comments on the numbers difference. It is not a surprise to us and we have flagged the difference between the two measures nearly two years ago. The two measures of goods balance usually co-move. In 12-month rolling average terms, the gap tends to fall within the range of ±20%. However, goods balance under the SAFE’s BoP current account has come in consistently lower than China Customs’ merchandise trade balance since early 2021, and widened to 20-50% of BoP’s goods balance since early 2022. We first lay out some accounting standard differences between the two measures.

First, according to the IMF’s 6<sup>th</sup> edition of the BoP manual, SAFE’s goods account covers residents’ exports to or imports from non-residents. Change of ownership (actual or imputed) is the principle determining the coverage and timing of recording the goods international transactions. Instead, China Customs comprises all the merchandise passing through China’s Customs territory, including: goods from abroad entering or leaving customs warehouses, bonded areas or special economic zones, goods for inward or outward processing or assembling, etc. In general, by method of trade, ordinary trade and processing trade with imported materials are booked in BoP’s goods balance, but not processing and assembling trade or outward processing trade.

However, even if we look at China Customs’ adjusted trade balance for ordinary and processing with imported materials trade only, the gap stays largely in place in the past four years.

Second, under China Customs, imports are valued on a CIF basis (cost, insurance and freight) while exports are valued on a FOB basis (free on board). However, both import and export of goods under SAFE’s BoP are valued on an FOB basis and domestic importers’ shipping costs are booked in BoP’s services account. The container freight costs for exports spiked much more than that for imports in 2021 and 2022. Also, valuation effect of imports tends to bring BoP’s imports lower than the customs’, hence enlarge BoP’s goods balance. However, in China’s case, the customs’ trade balance has been larger the BoP’s.

Third, deductions from exports and imports. For example, goods later returned to the original owner are supposed to be reflected in the BoP’s goods balance. We think this part should be far from sufficient to explain the continuing gap in recent years.

The above accounting differences could at best explain only part of the discrepancy between the two measures. We offer two alternative explanations. First, part of the exports were domestic exporters’ shipping to their foreign entities, which may have been booked in customs’ exports but not SAFE’s due to no change in ownership. Looking at regional bilateral trade data, we did find that China’s reported exports to major regional destinations in EM Asia have been consistently higher than the counterparties. The gap can’t be fully explained by the re-exports via Hong Kong SAR. Two forces are generally responsible for the discrepancy between bilateral trade figures: (1) China exporters reporting higher exports, or (2) counterparty importers under-reporting imports from China. For example, the consistently large discrepancy between China and Philippines trade can be partly attributable to under-reporting incentive of Philippines importers to avoid import duties; Chinese exporters may have included exports to their foreign entities or warehouses in Singapore in their report, but that was not covered by Singapore importers; and one likely reason for the Malaysia and China discrepancy is oil trans-shipments through Malaysia. It happened on the backdrop of ongoing supply chain relocation amid tariff and friend-shoring concerns and ASEAN’s rising role of linking China with the traditional major destination markets like G3.

Second, it is likely related to the exporters’ retaining USD exports revenue overseas. The Compulsory Foreign Exchange Settlement and Sales System, an administrative arrangement whereby the foreign exchange income obtained by residents shall be sold to financial institutions designated by the State and foreign exchange to be utilized shall be purchased from financial institutions designated by the State, has been gradually phased out since early 2010s. However, the unfavorable US-China rate differential, Russia-Ukraine war related geopolitical concerns, and ongoing domestic growth headwinds and recent growing concerns of renewed US-China trade war have been weighing on CNY FX. This has raised the exporters’ incentive to hold USD instead of CNY, and retain exports revenue overseas instead of transferring them back to China. This also partly explains the relatively muted FX reserves despite elevated trade surplus. If we look at China’s trade surplus and net FX settlement, the two measures were co-moving quite well before 2014, but net FX settlement has been consistently below trade surplus (in three-year rolling terms) since 2015. We think this factor may have contributed to the lower trade balance under BoP.
