## SOEs in the spotlight again

On 23 January 2024, Yi revisited the concept of VCC in the 2024 CSRC work conference. Just a day later, the State-owned Assets Supervision and Administration Commission of the State Council (SASAC) announced that it would include the market cap management results in the assessment of SOE executives. In addition, a flurry of key announcements and guidance issued in the following days relevant to VCC raised market awareness of the theme. These coordinated actions have put VCC in the spotlight again.

# What is the "valuation system with Chinese characteristics"?

The "valuation system with Chinese characteristics" in a nutshell

In APAC Focus: Your guide to the "valuation system with Chinese characteristics", we discussed in depth how China's ageing population is leading to a pension shortfall. This is partly due to some lingering historical issues: residents who retired before 1997 received pensions even though they had not paid into their personal accounts. The gap has been further exacerbated by China's early retirement age. According to the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences, the accumulative balance for the basic pension fund will peak in 2027F and be depleted in 2035F. As employers in China already bear a heavy burden of social benefit contributions, it would be difficult for them to further increase pension contributions to fill the shortfall. Apart from fiscal support, we think that postponing the retirement age and diversifying pension fund investments could also help to alleviate the burden.

In our view, the ongoing transfer of capital from the government related entities to the National Council for Social Security Fund (NCSSF), or "state-owned capital transfer", could partially help fill China's looming pension shortfall. Cash dividends from the transferred state-owned capital could help supplement pension payments before 2030E, while "capital operation" may become mandatory over 2030E to 2040E. The acceleration of SOE reform aims to boost SOEs' profitability and dividend payouts, improve corporate governance, and narrow the valuation gap between SOEs and non-SOEs. A "valuation system with Chinese characteristics" has been proposed by regulators to support an upward revaluation of state-owned assets/capital and to create resources for capital operation over the long run.

## Why are SOEs undervalued by investors?

SOEs are substantially undervalued by investors in both the A-share and Hong Kong equity markets. Currently, the valuation of SOEs is only half of the non-SOEs in both PE and PB terms, and the gap has widened in recent years. In our view, the vast valuation difference between the SOEs and their non-SOE peers are due to three major reasons, namely sector distribution, operating efficiency and investors' negative perception.

Figure 5: Trailing PE for A-share non-financials (SOEs vs non-SOEs)
![](images/af0a9b95a0e33537942cc1e408662c5416a0676a49a231140c026e9d27e4a0ea.jpg)
Source: Wind, UBS-S

Source: Wind, UBS-S

First, sector distribution differences contribute to the valuation discrepancies as a majority of SOEs are in upstream commodity-related sectors. Mining is the most extreme case as 94% of the total market capitalisation in this sector belongs to the SOEs. In fact, commodity-related sectors are usually monopolistic industries with high entry barriers. As a result, we think their low valuations are partly due to earnings volatility and investors' negative perception.

Figure 7: Sector breakdown by ownership in terms of market capitalisation
![](images/3c72b9d218008d3c79047e07fc4c37147423530902fc7242e69313767aa54583.jpg)
Source: Wind, UBS-S. Note: Data as of January 2024

Second, SOEs have long been perceived by the investing community of suffering from low operating efficiency. Since SOEs shoulder more social responsibilities and do not necessarily always put the shareholders' interest first, it is only natural that their earnings capacity is constrained to certain extent. Here we use ROE as a proxy for companies operating efficiency. Calculating the ROE gap as the ROE of the non-SOEs minus that of the SOEs, we find that, out of 25 non-financial industries, the ROE gap is positive for 14 industries, while it is in negative territory for the rest. On average, the positive ROE gaps are much wider than the negative ones.

Third, a few non-operational issues have led to negative perceptions over the SOEs. For example, unsatisfactory corporate governance and the duties of "national services" may keep many investors away from the SOEs.

## Mind the gap #1: PE - Earnings growth perspective

From a PE multiple point of view, non-financials SOEs have been trading at an increasingly steep discount to their non-SOE peers to the extent that the non-SOEs' PE multiple has been more than double that of the SOEs since 2021. Can the large valuation gap be explained by differences in earnings growth?

Figure 11: Non-financials non-SOEs' valuation premium over SOEs
![](images/be75db64888c672a49fbf61d4edf5a670401bc7dd7cf1837a1f478a48532abe1.jpg)
Source: Wind, UBS-S

To some extent, the SOEs' earnings growth is indeed slower. When we compare the non-financials A-share SOEs and non-SOEs earnings results, we can clearly see that non-SOEs' annual profit growth outstripped that of the SOEs' in seven of the last ten years. However, if we make an aggregate comparison, the lead is not as alarmingly wide, with profit growing at an average rate of 13% for non-SOEs compared with 10% for SOEs over a span of 10 years (2013-22).

In recent years, non-SOEs have found it more difficult to sustain their lead in earnings growth. Although non-SOEs greatly outperformed the SOEs in 2020, the latter grew more than four times as fast as the former in the following year during a much more challenging business environment. However, if we take valuation into consideration, the picture starts to look muddled. SOEs, whose profit have grown much faster since 2021, are trading at a steeper discount to non-SOEs. The discount has only narrowed in 2023 as VCC gathered popularity.

## Mind the gap #2: PB - ROE perspective

PE valuation aside, we note that a big chunk of the difference in PB valuation between SOEs and non-SOEs cannot be easily explained by differences in ROE. Hence, we have coined the term "non-ROE factors" to try to capture the portion going unexplained by ROE differences. "Non-ROE factors" are the difference between non-SOEs' PB premium over SOEs and non-SOEs' ROE advantage over SOEs.

Figure 13: Non-SOEs have not had a clear advantage in ROE since 2018
![](images/6fe79a63ad6c7beea0ce4f5e1c6592fc3176ab9be32add5ec3d0968b967bdb32.jpg)
Source: Wind, UBS-S. Note: ROE advantage is calculated as non-SOEs' ROE over SOEs' ROE minus one.

In order to gauge how much of the valuation multiple, PB in this case, is determined by sector ROEs , we compare the PB premium of the non-financials non-SOEs over SOEs along with the non-financials non-SOEs' ROE advantages over SOEs.

We found that, out of 25 non-financial sectors, only 14 sectors' ROE display explanatory power for the PB ratios. Although there are some sectors, such as non-ferrous metals and real estate, whose almost entire valuation gap can be explained by the difference in ROE, other sectors, such as textiles and agriculture, suggest operating efficiency is next to irrelevant in explaining the valuation differences. In other words, "non-ROE factors" for 11 out of 25 sectors are simply too large to ignore.

## How can SOEs bridge the gap from a sector perspective? Dividend payout and share buyback easily followed

As we have already covered in Part I of this series of reports, there are multiple ways to increase SOE valuation. In general, this includes: 1) improving corporate governance and transparency through SOE reform; 2) improving operating efficiency and ROE; and 3) increase dividend payout to shareholders or buyback shares.

We note that many fundamental investors are unlikely to react without first seeing concrete evidence of improved operating efficiency and profitability at SOEs. Two easily followed metrics are dividend payout and buyback volume and we have been seeing promising developments in both in recent years. The dividend yield of A-share nonfinancials SOEs picked up materially from 1.75% in 2020 to 2.94% in 2022, while total buyback volume by listed SOEs rose to Rmb33bn in 2023, at a CAGR of over 17% since 2013. We think a continued improvement in dividend yield, coupled with a growing buyback volume, would convince fundamental investors to consider VCC as a long-term theme and attract investors to explore the investment opportunities among high-quality SOEs.

## Category A: Enhance the SOEs' ROE should be sufficient

Looking deeper into the sector level, we can see that SOEs from different sectors face different obstacles. We grouped A-share level 1 industries (excluding conglomerates, banks and non-bank financials) into three categories based on the different measures that are available to the SOEs to bridge the valuation gap with their non-SOE peers. Some of the valuation gap is the result of SOEs' low operating efficiency, which could be fixed to some extent through more thorough SOE reform. Some of the valuation gap cannot be resolved because SOEs in these sectors already appear overpriced by investors as the "non-ROE factors" are in their favour. The rest are mainly due to investors' negative perceptions, which require further investor education. In the following section, we will have a closer look and offer solutions that SOEs might find helpful to catch up with their non-SOE peers in terms of valuation.

In category A, we identify sectors for which enhancing ROE should be sufficient for SOEs to catch up with non-SOEs in terms of valuation. The five sectors in this first category are non-ferrous metals, real estate, machinery, steel, and electrical equipment. They share the trait that most of their non-SOE peers' valuation premium can be explained to some extent by their greater operating efficiency. In other words, we believe share prices in these five industries are dictated by ROE. We also believe that if the SOEs in these five industries are able to gradually catch up with their non-SOE peers in terms of operating efficiency, the valuation gap may narrow.

Figure 17: Enhancing the SOEs' ROE should be sufficient to bridge the valuation gap in these sectors
![](images/4f369854dd48114bb06cfd06f8500a866a72f826bacca4f6bfd329f1f5e6d524.jpg)
Source: Wind, UBS-S estimates. Note: PB premium and ROE advantage are calculated using average numbers from 2010 to 2022.

Two weaknesses of SOEs stood out to us when we talked to UBS-S sector analysts. 1) SOEs usually operate at lower efficiency, which typically comes from a bloated workforce and insufficient incentives. 2) SOEs are usually unequivocal leaders in their particular sectors, but the business itself inherently carries a thin margin. We acknowledge the fact that SOEs shoulder more social responsibilities, which means neither of the two weaknesses we have mentioned can be overcome overnight, but any improvements or changes can be interpreted as a friendly gesture to the investing community.

Let's use the steel sector as an example. The non-SOEs here have commanded a valuation premium of around 135% over SOEs since 2010. This valuation lead is backed by a similar lead of 153% in ROE over its SOE peers. If we break down the ROEs, we can see that the weaker ROE of the SOEs is the result of their much lower profitability and asset turnover. Based on our labour cost calculation and feedback from UBS-S sector analysts, steel SOEs generally keep a much larger workforce and offer an overall more generous compensation package to employees. The two combined have resulted in a bloated labour budget.

Another reason is that the steel SOEs and the non-SOEs are in different businesses. The steel SOEs are concentrated in the low margin carbon steel business, while the non-SOEs have specialised in the more lucrative special steel and pipeline business. It also helps explain why 11 out of 23 steel SOEs registered net losses in 2022, while all of the 13 non-SOEs posted positive earnings.

We believe with higher output per capita and a gradual transformation in its business mix, the SOEs in the steel sector should be able to partially offset some of their disadvantages and narrow their valuation gap with their non-SOE peers.

## Category B: Non-SOEs' higher efficiency not even fully priced in

In category B, we identify sectors for which enhancing the ROE would only be able to lift SOEs' valuation modestly. The five sectors in this second category are textile, home appliances, electronics, agriculture, and light manufacturing. Like the SOEs in category A, SOEs in the second category lag the non-SOEs in terms of ROE. However, the valuation premium the latter enjoys does not fully reflect its advantages in business operation. Although the SOEs in these five sectors in this category can enhance their ROEs, their valuation can only catch up to a certain extent as they are already overpriced.

Figure 21: Enhancing the SOEs' ROE can only lift SOEs' valuation modestly in these sectors
![](images/0dc6d20839f40171f51910e21c8e086b00597c4d10cb63eb7a2a69161decc92f.jpg)
Source: Wind, UBS-S estimates. Note: PB premium and ROE advantage are calculated using average numbers from 2010 to 2022.

The home appliances sector offers some insight into this phenomenon. Looking at how they traded, we see that the valuations of SOEs and non-SOEs in the home appliances sector converged from 2010 to 2016 but widened from 2017 onward. The SOEs were once traded at a low of only half of the non-SOEs in 2019 when the industry leaders were in the consolidation process.

We believe the premium is due to non-SOEs superior net margin and market leadership. The margin differences between non-SOEs and the SOEs in the Dupont analysis in the home appliances sector are by far the largest of all 25 sectors. Although these stark profitability differences are partially offset by the latter's higher asset turnover and leverage, the SOEs' ROE still comes at around roughly half of the non-SOEs. Based on our conversations with the UBS-S home appliances analyst, non-SOEs are usually the market leaders in their niche market in which they enjoy economies of scale, thus commanding outsized pricing power. We believe the pricing heft of the SOEs may naturally come with market share gains.

However, we do not think SOEs' share prices can see as much improvement as the current ROE differences suggest. Even if the SOEs can catch up with the non-SOEs in terms of profitability, we think their share prices are unlikely to rise by as much, as they are already "overpriced".

## Category C: New businesses & investor education to lift SOEs' valuation

Most sectors fall into category C. SOEs in this category can use a combination of new business development and focus on growth in order to boost their valuation. In addition, the valuation woes of SOEs in this category are also partly attributable to investors' deeply-rooted negative perception of SOEs, as explained by the non-ROE factors in our calculations. In this case, our view is that more investor education could be helping the investing community form an unbiased, if not more justifiably positive perception, of the SOEs.

Figure 25: New business lines and investor education are key to SOEs' re-rating in this category
![](images/9be701e1ea65ef08b76cca0face37b0f1ad95d2c1ca8f3d4c430b797dd0de520.jpg)
Source: Wind, UBS-S estimates. Note: PB premium and ROE advantage are calculated using average numbers from 2010.to 2022

Differences in margins between non-SOEs and SOEs in the chemicals sector are the third largest among the 25 sectors, suggesting that the SOEs and non-SOEs in this space may not actually be in the same line of business. If we dig deeper into the list of SOEs and non-SOEs in the sector, we can see that the SOEs are mainly focused on the traditional chemicals and petrochemicals business, while their non-SOE peers have a much more diverse reach. In addition to traditional chemicals, the non-SOEs are spread across subsectors including new materials, national defence related materials, building materials and cosmetics, all of which enjoy higher profit margin and much better growth prospects.

From a valuation point of view, the share prices of non-SOEs as a whole in the chemicals sector have recorded multiple spikes since 2010 while the SOEs' share price performance is much more subdued. We believe this phenomenon is the result of non-SOE composites being heavily skewed to small and mid-cap SFPI (specialised, fine, peculiar and innovative) companies that are "little giants" in one particular niche market. As such, these non-SOE companies are typically high-beta firms that will be major beneficiaries of a stock rally and abundant market liquidity, with valuation multiples that naturally show stronger elasticity.

In sum, the SOEs' weaker operating efficiencies and growth prospects are structural. However, a gradual shift in the SOEs' business mix away from traditional business segments to more innovative areas may boost their ROE, improve their growth prospects and, in the process, investors' perceptions, in our view.

## Appendix: What has Japan done to manage a turnaround?

In 2023, a combination of 1) reform championed by the Tokyo Stock Exchange (TSE), 2) corporate actions, such as M&A taken by the TSE listcos, 3) imported inflation that translated into the subsequent nominal GDP and wage growth, and 4) net foreign inflows in addition to the rollout of the Nippon Individual Savings Account (NISA), has propelled the Japanese economy out of the "three lost decades", drew capital into its equity market and reshaped investors' perception of the nation. We will discuss each factor in detail in the following sections.

## Japanese listcos' low valuation conundrum

For a long time, many listcos on the TSE have share prices below book value (P/BV less than 1x) and low operating efficiency (ROE less than 8%). This phenomenon is so widespread that among the TOPIX 500 constituents, 37% had a P/BV below 1x, whereas for S&P 500 and STOXX 600, the percentage was only 4% and 23%. In terms of operating efficiency, among the TOPIX 500 constituents, 40% had an ROE of below 8%, whereas for S&P 500 and STOXX 600, the percentage was only 14% and 16%.

## What actions have the Japanese listcos taken?

To tackle this issue, the TSE established the Council of Experts Concerning the Follow-up of Market Restructuring, and required listcos to classify themselves into 'Prime' (industry leaders facing global investors), 'Standard' (companies with sufficient liquidity and decent corporate governance) or 'Growth' (companies with strong growth potential). Firms in each category need to meet the corresponding criteria for that category to stay listed. The TSE subsequently published its Action on Cost of Capital-Conscious Management and Other Initiative, in which it explicitly laid out the rules and requirements for the TSE listcos to follow in order to improve their corporate governance and valuation.

In the Action, the TSE guided listcos to conduct capital market operations in the medium- to long-term (such as, M&A) to maximise shareholders' value. The Japanese listcos also adopted a series of measures to improve transparency and corporate governance in the near term, such as setting up a board of directors, providing English language financial statements to foreign investors, and selling strategically held shares to improve liquidity. The key focuses for investors at present include stock buybacks, dividend payouts and new targets if P/BV reaches 1x.

In the Action, the TSE stressed the importance of promoting the "industrial metabolism and innovation in business and society" through "exchanging factors of production" and the implementation of "M&A with other companies". In the past, a lack of transition of mobility in personnel and capital resources limited Japanese companies' growth potential, which led to the prolonged slump of the economy in the first place. Therefore, the TSE highly encourages the Japanese companies to increase productivity through M&A activity, which has led to ongoing corporate actions.

Following the collapse of the asset bubble in the 1990s, the first decade of the new millennium was marked by slow M&A activity in corporate Japan, averaging USD124bn a year. It quickly picked up to around USD227bn per year in the following decade as the Japanese business environment gradually recovered from the previous recession. Entering into the third decade, although the deal volume came down from the peak in 2018, the deal count remain elevated above 4,300 a year, more than double the average deal closed in the first decade of the new millennium. The total volume of closed M&A deals stood at USD213bn in 2023, representing an increase of 13% from the previous year.

## Net foreign inflows and the rollout NISA boost Japan's stock market

The Japanese stock market has experienced a tailwind from a combination of net foreign inflows and the rollout of the NISA programme.

The main reason behind global investors' change of perception over the Japanese economy is high global inflation. Stagnant economic growth following the collapse of the asset bubble in the 1990s essentially led to the so called "three lost decades", which was marked by low inflation, sometimes even deflation. However, with the pandemic and Russia-Ukraine war disrupting the global supply chain and causing energy prices to surge, inflation is once again being imported into Japan. The "deflation equilibrium"(no inflation with no wage growth) has gradually shifted to "mild inflation equilibrium"(some inflation with decent wage growth), which has changed corporates' price setting behaviour as well. The mild inflation created a self-reinforcing cycle of wage growth and goods prices increase. As a result, corporate Japan as well as the equity markets are once again on the rise.

Consequently, foreign investors are returning to Japan in droves. Warren Buffett announced that he would be investing in the five largest trading companies in Japan in April 2023. Offshore investors aggressively bought Japanese stocks in the following five trading days, driving up the country's stock market. According to UBS Japan Macro team's conversations with clients and the latest market data, the major driver of this rally is foreign investors, rather than their more conservative domestic peers who has been selling.

The re-rating of Japanese listcos is also connected to the rollout of NISA. NISA was originally established by the government in a low interest rate environment in an effort to channel personal savings into the capital market. According to documents from the TSE, the re-rating of Japanese stocks could help supplement the pensions of Japanese households and drive the development of NISA.
