# Picking Our Spots

The rally in December limits directional upside from here. In leveraged credit, we see room to catch up in CCCs and Loans. In IG, the long end looks unattractive, while the front end should see inflows from cash.

## Key Takeaways

■ Our soft landing view for 2024 remains intact. But rich valuations, bumpy macro data, and delayed policy easing make for a tougher short-term set-up.

■ Valuations favor IG over HY, but we think quality compression WITHIN both assets is likely short term.

In leveraged credit, we see a window for CCCs to outperform. Our preference for Loans overs Bonds is more strategic.

■ In IG, we stick to the front end where carry and income remain reasonable. Longend technicals should weaken on both the demand and supply fronts.

In synthetics, we take off the CDX decompression trade and prefer alternatives with less exposure to CCCs (short HY 15-100% vs. Long CDX IG).

Patience Is a Virtue

The rally at the end of 2023 in credit markets has abated somewhat at the turn of the calendar. Following a sharp decline in December, bond yields have risen and risk assets have weakened in sympathy. IG index is 6bp wider from the tights, while HY spreads have moved by 35bp. Total returns are at -1% across IG and HY, after an impressive 2023.

We see this repricing as a healthy correction that takes valuations from excessively rich levels to more reasonable ones. The Morgan Stanley house view for 2024 remains benign (see 2024 US Inflation Outlook), with our economists expecting inflation to decline, while the economy sees a soft landing. FY2024 should also see the start of policy-easing cycles not just in the US, but in Europe as well.

But a year is a long time and sticking the soft landing requires patience. Through the last quarter of 2023, credit markets were supported by a combination of 1) attractive yields and reasonable spreads, 2) strong data on both growth and inflation, and 3) the lack of opportunistic supply given the level of funding costs. In the near term, we see all three factors being much less supportive. All-in yields – while well above pre-COVID norms – are at levels where investor "FOMO" (Fear of Missing Out) should be lower. Credit spreads are priced for a smoother soft landing (mid 1990s style) than our economists are pencilling in. Additionally, our economists expect to see bumpiness in the trajectory of inflation in the first few months of the year. This path also informs their view that the Fed will wait until June to start policy easing, in contrast with market pricing that currently has odds of a March rate cut at \~70%.

Overall, we see a tricky setup f near term given this combination of fairly rich valuations, likelihood of higher supply, volatility in macro data, and potential disappointment from realized policy easing vs. what is priced in. This caution is reflected in our cross-asset strategists' tactical indicator being risk-negative. At the same time, seasonality for credit tends to be weaker in the first quarter, especially for IG, in part because supply is strong.

In short, we think it makes sense for investors to be patient to kick off the year. Not only should levels get cheaper as macro volatility increases, but in our view technicals should also gradually gain momentum at those cheaper levels.

In addition, the rally in November and December was as much about what was happening under the surface as it was about the headline indices, with rotation being a key theme. In equities, this has shown in the outperformance of small caps, cyclicals, and growth stocks since mid-December. In credit, the initial leg of the rally from November to mid-December was led by high quality, but HY caught up during the back half of December.

At current levels, the "beta" opportunity in credit is limited, and investors need to pick their spots. Specifically, we look for pockets that have lagged in the rally and could catch up, and others that have tightened too much and could potentially give up gains. In the former camp, we place CCCs and leveraged loans.

After the December Fed meeting, we called for a window of CCC outperformance. Based on headline index levels, this has played out so far. But lack of liquidity into year-end has meant that day-to-day moves are very choppy. We continue to see room for CCCs to perform in the short term, and irrespective of market direction. That said, we emphasize that this is a tactical call. Long term, risk-reward for CCCs is mixed as growth slows, and large capital structures face secular challenges.

We like loans over HY bonds tactically and strategically. As we cover later in this report, valuations in the Loan market have rarely been this cheap compared to Bonds. For now, the market is placing more emphasis on the technical support for HY as rates decline and inflows recover. Over time though, the fundamental implications of policy easing are stronger for floating rate borrowers that have seen interest coverage and free cash flow decline sharply through this hiking cycle. In contrast, even with the fall in rates, HY issuers will see their coupons reset higher over time as 2025/26 refinancing walls come in view.

Within IG, risk-reward is weakest for the long end, given how flat spread curves are. Over the autumn months, flat curves were supported by a lack of long-end supply and strong investor appetite with all-in yields above 5.5%. We see this sector being most directly impacted by the fall in rates through a technical lens. Further, early indications for the year show strong momentum in new issuance. In contrast, we think the front end of IG remains a good source of income as well as spread carry. We discuss this later, in the investment grade sub-section

## US Leveraged Credit | Room for CCCs and Loans to Catch Up

Within leveraged credit, HY performed impressively in the year-end rally. Spreads tightened 67bp in November and another \~50bp in December. The Bloomberg index hit tights of 312bp in late December, in response to growing optimism about upcoming rate cuts. The impact was amplified by thin trading volume during the holiday season. Since the turn of the new year, the rally has lost some steam. Already, HY spreads have risen back to 353bp, as of Friday, giving back over 60% of the tightening in December.

Meanwhile, after lagging meaningfully in December, Loans are showing signs of catching up. At a touch below 97pt, the LSTA Lev Loan Index is trading through 2023 highs and now has reached levels last seen in May 2022. Despite the solid rally, the extent of the spread tightening pales in comparison to HY. The spread differential between Loans and HY widened to 130bp, the highest in LSTA Lev Loan Index's history. That's not solely driven by the divergence of index quality and the large universe of loan-only names. Even after controlling issuers, investors can still clip a notable spread pickup when switching from secured bonds to pari-passu loans. For the median issuer, loans screened \~90bp wider to like-rated secured bonds within BBs and Bs, and the spread basis was even more pronounced for CCCs.

A confluence of factors may be anchoring loan valuations, preventing further material tightening, including ongoing downgrade concerns and the cliff effect around CCC demand from CLOs. While it is not uncommon to see loans underperforming amid leveraged credit market rallies (e.g., in 4Q22 and May-August in 2023), the gap has rarely been this wide

historically, with HY spreads at 300-350bp range, which then raises the question – will loans catch up with HY in this rally?

In the near term, we think the rally, especially in Loans, may still have some legs. While HY bond valuations can be choppier around fast moving fund flows, loan technicals are less volatile. But fundamentally, more aggressive rate cuts should favor loan borrowers more, just as how the intensive rate hikes weighed more on the floating-rate-heavy names.

Seasonally, January has historically been a good month for leveraged credit, with 1.0-1.5% total returns on average within HY and Loans. Notably, Loans tend to do quite well in January after some mediocre returns in December, potentially as CLO machines start to print again. Anecdotally, we have heard that CLO issuance has started the year strong with existing warehouses not as highly ramped, which further adds to potential loan demand. Meanwhile, supply continue to be driven by refinancing and the M&A/LBO pipelines remain muted at the moment, another positive technical.

Over the medium term, we continue to expect more fundamental weakening for HY names given that they face more of a "sticker shock" at the time of refinancing. And yet, HY borrowers have not seen the same momentum of downgrades as in Loans. At a minimum, we reiterate our call on Loans over HY.

CCC outperformance may still have room to run short-term. After approaching 310bp in late December, the HY index spread quickly reverted and along with it, we see a modest spread decompression with CCC vs. BB widening by 47bp YTD. Typically, we see spread compression in market rallies and vice versa. But the window for CCC outperformance may still exist. In other words, we could see less spread decompression or even a bearish compression with the HY market in the near term.

Several considerations inform our view. First, in terms of valuation, we still see a sizeable dislocation of CCC spreads vs. higher-rated buckets. CCC bonds are trading at a 622bp spread premium vs. BBs, the 80th percentile over the past 20-year range. This implies room for further normalization. Furthermore, with BB prices rising \~5.5pt from end of October, the convexity profile got worse. We estimate that \~\$1bn of BB HY bonds are now economical to be called, and the number will reach over \$30bn (or 5% of BBs) if spreads were to tighten by another 100bp. By contrast, while CCCs valuation also richened, the distressed cohorts remained cheap at the bottom 10% of the 2010-23 range, suggesting a better convexity even in downside scenarios.

On the technical front, with richened valuation for higher-quality papers, HY active funds may rotate down to ${ \mathsf { C C C s } } ,$ which was the unloved part of the market for most of 2023. As of late December, the largest HY mutual funds remained under-allocated to CCCs, although the CCC weights rose slightly vs. the end of 3Q (anecdotally). Meanwhile, with rates still at restrictive levels, we see HY supply continue to skew to higher ratings in 2024. Combined, these paint a less supportive technical backdrop for BBs and Bs.

Third, as we alluded to in All Eyes on Beta, the CCC universe in HY is less cyclical now compared to history. Communications and Healthcare make up approximately one-third of the HY CCC bucket, and account for some of the widest names in this cohort. In fact, there are fewer high-beta names in the CCC bucket now. Measured by the correlation coefficient between CCC bond spreads and HY index spreads, the spread betas of CCC names skew more toward lower betas compared to 2019. The lower sensitivity argues for a more moderate decompression of the CCC spread when HY spreads widen.

But our preference for CCCs is tactical. The risk-reward of CCCs over the long term is not so clear-cut. On a risk-adjusted basis, CCCs tend to underperform higher-quality cohorts over longer time frames, as seen in lower Sharpe ratios (measured by total returns over return volatility). The underperformance becomes more pronounced as we extend the time horizon.

Looking ahead, despite the optimism about a soft landing, CCC names are not yet out of the woods. Defaults are rising and the public funding market remained shut for most CCCs. While private credit provides a release valve to some names (potentially the ones with a more sustainable business model), it is not a solution for all. Even for the ones with the private credit backstop, it remains to be seen how the names navigate the higher refinancing cost. Therefore, investors should stay nimble when playing for the "beta" trade.

What would make us structurally bullish on CCCs? A few things worth mentioning here. If the growth backdrop turns out to be much stronger than our economists pencil in, CCCs can outperform over a longer window in keeping with the "mid-cycle" expansion playbook. Alternatively, if growth troughs this year and rebounds next year with the Fed easing aggressively, one could frame CCC credit as an early-cycle beneficiary in that environment (loosely analogous to small cap stocks). Finally, our Sector Credit Research colleagues cover some of the largest CCC names in the HY index, and remain fundamentally cautious about the outlook. We will reassess the risk-reward of the generic CCC index if their view changes around the micro-challenges.

## US Investment Grade | Curves to Steepen Further

In our Year End Outlook, we discussed the flatness in long-end spread curves. Following the Fed meeting in December, and the big decline in government bonds, we turned cautious on the long-end as discussed in All Eyes on Beta. So far, our views are playing out per our expectation. Since the December Fed meeting, the IG index is 2bp wider as of January 5. But the credit curve has "twist-steepened" over this period. The 1-5Y index spread is roughly unchanged from pre-Fed levels, while long-end spreads have widened 8- 10bp ( Exhibit 21 ).

Despite this widening, the 30Y IG index spread remains rich both across the curve and in absolute terms ( Exhibit 22 ). The squeeze in long-end spreads during the autumn months was driven by a perfect storm of 1) yield-buyer demand that was aggressive and "grabby" for fear of missing out on attractive yields, and 2) very limited long-end supply as corporates refrained from doing longer-dated deals at 6% coupons.

Broadly speaking, the demand-supply dynamics are much less supportive for the long end, and we recommend reducing exposure here. The base-line demand from yield buyers should remain in place, but at a slower pace. On the other hand, for index-based investors that can look across the curve, we see very little value in the sector, with spreads at multiyear tights. Also worth emphasizing is that long-end IG tends to have vol-adjusted excess returns, further denting the case for a big allocation there within a fixed income portfolio that looks across the curve. Any duration-driven rally in weaker growth can be captured simply via the government bond market.

In contrast to the long end, we still see carry opportunities in the front end. The front end has outperformed sharply post the November Fed meeting as rates have fallen and regional bank stresses have eased. As markets have embraced the likelihood of rate cuts, the competition for asset dollars – especially from money market funds – will likely reduce.

Our economists still see four Fed cuts next year and our rates team reiterates a view for bull-steepening in the Treasury curves, which all bode well for front-end IG. Furthermore, while front-end spreads have also rallied, spreads remain at fair levels by historical standards, with 1-5Y spreads in the 50th percentile while the index and higher tenors are at much richer levels. In contrast to the inverted Treasury yield curve, there remains an upward-sloping credit term structure in the front end that provides roll-down opportunities in IG credit.

# US Credit Derivatives | Better Forms of HY vs. IG Decompression, Long Equity Tranches to Capture CCC Outperformance

Within synthetic credit, we have recommended CDX decompression as a liquid up-inquality trade. That said, with the bifurcation between tail credits in CDX HY vs. the rest of the portfolio, we don't see compelling risk-reward in the index-based trade anymore. Given our call for tactical CCC outperformance, the index decompression trade has micro headwinds.

Instead, where decompression still makes sense is within the high-/mid-quality part of the HY market vs. IG. BBs and Bs trade very rich relative to history (as discussed above), but are likely to see credit quality deteriorate as in-place funding costs reset higher. On the other hand, we see limited credit quality concerns in the IG universe where liabilities are termed out and balance sheet cash is ample.
