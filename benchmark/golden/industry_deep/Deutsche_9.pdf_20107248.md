## Policy diversion

The PBoC held its policy rate unchanged again in March amid an upward surprise in CPI inflation and sustained pressure on the CNY. While we have maintained our forecast of further MLF rate cuts, their timing would be data-dependent. China’s economic activity indicators in the first two months of the year fared better than expected, despite a still-weak property market and softening credit growth.

India’s GDP growth surprised to the upside, prompting a significant upward revision to our forecasts, while inflation came in slightly higher than consensus. If it intends to start cutting rates at mid-year, a change from the current monetary policy stance of "withdrawal of accommodation" to neutral is likely to come ahead of such a move.

While the Korean government offered investors a silver lining, promoting the Corporate Value-up Programme, we expect inflationary pressure to ease, led by fresh food prices, giving the BoK room to contemplate the timing of its rate cuts amid sustained weak domestic demand.

Despite its dovish comments, suggesting steady policy rates ahead, we think that there is a non-negligible risk of further rate hikes by the CBC. It has yet to conclude its assessment of a "structural change in inflation".

ASEAN economies are facing either a cyclical turn or a structural challenge, prompting them to reassess their policy stances and/or the level of their neutral rates. That said, for many, their concerns over FX stability stand in the way – although a recovery in exports could provide better support – while "bumps" in inflation may add to their concerns ahead, albeit briefly. Although FDI inflows to ASEAN economies faced headwinds last year, the region has remained a favoured investment destination for manufacturing MNCs.

In this report, we also provide a fresh look at consumer sentiment in China and how it may be affecting consumption, savings and investment decisions.

## DB Survey: Chinese consumers are becoming less worried

China's consumer spending has unexpectedly strengthened lately; DB's latest survey can help understand what has changed. China witnessed an uptick in travel and tourism spending during this year's Lunar New Year holidays. Both retail sales and consumer prices improved in the first two months of 2024. Our dbDIG team surveys a sample of Chinese consumers in large and medium-sized cities on a biannual basis. Consumers were asked a range of questions focusing on their income, spending preferences, prices, and forward expectations. The results can help us better understand the direction of change in consumer sentiment and main drivers.

Chinese consumers' income expectations have deteriorated under a stillchallenging economic environment, according to our survey results... According to our survey, the proportion of consumers considering themselves financially better off initially increased in 2023 after reopening, but has since dropped, from 58% in Q2 2023 to 36% in Q1 2024. Similarly, the percentage of consumers anticipating an increase in income has also dropped, from 66% in Q2 2023 to 39% in Q1 2024. This decline can be attributed to the decrease in property and stock prices. Notably, the deterioration in both current situations and expectations was more significant among the younger population and individuals residing in tier-1 cities, as they are more exposed to the property and stock markets.

...but they have become less anxious about economic uncertainty, and in turn have reduced their tendency to save. Average scoring of future uncertainty has fallen to 4.1 in Q2-24 from 5.2 in Q3-22 (on a scale of 1 to 10, with 10 indicating the highest uncertainty). Concerns regarding inflation volatility, property market volatility, and debt repayment have all decreased. Consequently, the percentage of consumers intending to increase savings in the future has also decreased. from 47% in Q2 2023 to 42% in Q1 2024. However, when examining a sub-sample of consumers below the age of 35, there is an increasing worry about uncertainty, possibly due to the softening labor market conditions for youth employment.

The property sector's negative impact on consumer spending appears to have faded somewhat. When asked about changes in their spending patterns in response to property market volatility, the proportion of consumers answering that they will reduce expenditure dropped visibly in Q1 2024 compared to Q3 2023. We think this is likely in part owing to lower mortgage rates, in particular the one-off adjustment on existing loans in September 2023, which reduced interest payments by \~15% for most borrowers. In addition, the younger generation (age 34 and below) has answered this question much more favorably compared to the older generation. The younger generation likely benefited from the mortgage rate cut, or, if they have not bought a home yet, from the drop in home prices which reduced the need for them to save for future home purchases.

Regarding inflation, Chinese consumers generally observed a lower increase in prices compared to a year ago. Only 28% of consumers reported an increase in prices compared to a year before in Q2 2024, down from 38% in Q2 2023 and 31% in Q3 2023. Meanwhile, 47% of consumers observed no change in prices, increasing from around 40% in Q2 and Q3 2023. About 18% of consumers observed a drop in prices in the latest survey, which is still smaller than the proportion of those observing price increases. This seems to be broadly consistent with China's official CPI readings, which reported a small negative yoy headline inflation owing to food and energy prices, but most other price categories stayed positive.

## China

n China's economic activity indicators in January and February 2024 came out stronger than expected, despite a still weak property market and softening credit growth. We see upside risks to our current 2023 Q1 growth forecast of 4.4% and annual growth forecast of 4.7%.

Yi Xiong, Chief Economist, China

n The government set a 5% growth target for 2024 at the National People's Congress, higher than the market's consensus growth forecast of 4.6% this year. An acceleration in fiscal spending in the upcoming months, underpinned by special government bond issuances and financing from the PBOC's PSL facility, will be crucial for achieving the growth target.

n PBOC held its policy rate unchanged again in March amid an upward surprise in CPI inflation and its efforts to resist depreciation pressures on the CNY. We still expect it will cut the MLF rate by 45bps this year, but the first rate cut will likely be delayed to April or June, depending on data in the next few months.

## Growth momentum improving

China's economic activity indicators in January and February 2024 came out stronger than expected. Retail sales improved sequentially thanks to higher consumer spending during the LNY holidays. Unexpectedly, industrial production also improved owing to higher output in a number of upstream industries, but the drop in sales-to-production ratio raises the question whether the improvement can be sustained, absent a pick-up in downstream demand. Investment was also stronger than expected, which may reflect an increase in government-led housing projects. However, property sector remains a weak spot, with sales growth staying deeply contractionary. All in all, our model estimates a 5.3% YoY growth in Jan-Feb 2024, which is much higher than our current Q1 2024 growth forecast of 4.4% (1.3% QoQ) and market expectations of 4.3%.

The property market still appears weak, and a stabilization/rebound has not yet been confirmed. Assessment of property sector performance was complicated by seasonal effects as well as last year's high base. On a seasonally-adjusted basis, housing new starts were stable and sales and investment improved slightly from late-2023. But in growth rate terms, the property sector remained deeply contractionary as housing sales and new starts were both down more than 30% YoY in Jan-Feb, in part owing to a high base in Q1 last year. Housing prices have also continued to fall on both new-home and secondary-home markets.

Can China's growth rebound without a meaningful recovery in the property sector? A strong property sector is commonly viewed a precondition for a strong Chinese economy. However, recent activity data has surprised on the upside despite a still weak property sector. We think it might be time to consider the probability of China reaching 5% or higher growth this year without a meaningful property sector recovery. This could happen if the substitution effect – households spending more on other items as they spend or save less for property purchases – starts to play a more important role than the negative wealth effect from property price drops. Upcoming government policy measures, including public investment projects and an action plan to promote replacement of equipment, cars and home appliances, will also help domestic demand in the near term. Such a "property-less recovery" may be the best explanation for the seemingly conflicting signals between activity and credit data lately. We see upside risks to our current 2023 Q1 growth forecast of 4.4% and annual growth forecast of 4.7%. The upcoming monthly data release in mid-April will be a key test for our growth assumptions.

PBOC held the policy rate unchanged for another month. PBOC decided to hold its 1-year MLF policy rate unchanged at 2.5% for another month, against our expectation of a 15bp rate cut. The PBOC's decision is likely based on a number of factors, including a positive surprise in inflation, its recent easing measures for RRR and LPR cuts, and exchange rate and bank profitability considerations; the latter suggest PBOC may need to allow banks to further cut deposit rates to preserve their interest margins if the PBOC wants to reduce lending rates further.

We continue to expect the PBOC will cut by 45bps this year, but the timing will be data-dependent. Money and credit growth remained subdued, and a rate cut is necessary for both boosting credit demand and managing liquidity. Indeed, PBOC is finding it difficult to inject more liquidity through the MLF given market rates have dropped far below the 1y MLF rate of 2.5%. The timing for the first rate cut will likely be in April, but if growth and inflation data come out sufficiently strong in March and RMB depreciation pressure remains, the first rate cut could be delayed to June, after the Fed's expected first rate cut.

## Highlights from the National People's Congress

The government set its 2024 growth target at 5% at the NPC. The target is higher than the market's consensus growth forecast of 4.6% at the moment. Premier Li recognized in his speech that “achieving this year's expected goals will not be easy” and will require strong efforts. An important fiscal announcement is the government will, in addition to the budget deficit (3% of GDP), issue CNY1tr of ultralong term special CGBs in 2024 and more in the coming years for important investment projects. This means the fiscal stance will likely stay expansionary in the medium term, and the supply of long-term government bonds (20yr or above) will likely be substantially increased, from around RMB 300-400bn per year in the past few years to more than RMB 1tr per year going forward.

More measures to boost domestic consumption and investment will be a policy priority in 2024. The government will promote consumption through a new round of "trade-in" programs, targeting household purchases of new-energy vehicles and electronic products. The government also aims to accelerate the key investment projects under the 14<sup>th</sup> Five-Year Plan. To do that, it will allocate local government bond quotas more efficiently, and improve the participation of private capital in public investment projects.

The government's work plan also highlighted a few other key areas. 1) The government emphasized there are simultaneous aggregate and structural issues with employment. It has maintained its annual target of creating 12 million new jobs, unchanged from last year. 2) The work plan has maintained a supportive tone on the property sector, but did not provide much detail on additional measures. 3) Efforts will be made to improve the policy environment for private and foreign companies. 4) The government is refocusing on climate goals by reinstating the target of reducing energy consumption per unit of GDP by 2.5%.

## India

n GDP growth surprised to the upside in Oct-Dec'23, leading us to revise up our FY24 and FY25 full year forecast to 7.6%yoy and 6.5% respectively.

Kaushik Das, Chief Economist, India

Gross fixed capital formation grew by 10.6%yoy in Oct-Dec’23 (vs. 11.6%yoy in July-Sep’23), while private final consumption expenditure (58% of GDP) growth remained soft at 3.5%yoy, though improving somewhat from previous quarter’s 2.4%yoy growth.

1 Feb CPI inflation came at 5.09%yoy, closer to our own forecast of 5.07%, but slightly higher than Bloomberg consensus of 5.04%. The good news is that CPI ex vegetables inflation moderated to 3.7%yoy in Feb (vs. 3.9%yoy in Jan).

n Core inflation (CPI ex food and fuel) also eased further to a multi-year low of 3.37%yoy (vs. 3.53%yoy in Jan). Currently, core CPI is 140bps lower than the pre-pandemic average, while real rates calculated in terms of core CPI is in excess of 300bps.

## Growth continues to surprise to the upside

Oct-Dec’23 real GDP growth came at 8.4%yoy, significantly higher than our forecast of 7.0% and Bloomberg consensus estimate of 6.6%yoy. Meanwhile, real GVA growth came lower at 6.5%yoy, broadly in line with consensus estimate, but the gap widened significantly between GDP and GVA growth in Oct-Dec’23. Net taxes explain the difference between the two, though we think real GVA trend is a better reflection of the underlying growth rate in the economy. The current fiscal year growth numbers also got a boost as the previous year (FY23) growth was revised lower to 7.0%yoy, from 7.2%yoy reported earlier. So, we think the headline GDP number should be analyzed and viewed with a lot of caution.

Gross fixed capital formation grew by 10.6%yoy in Oct-Dec’23 (vs. 11.6%yoy in July-Sep’23), while private final consumption expenditure (58% of GDP) growth remained soft at 3.5%yoy, though improving somewhat from previous quarter’s 2.4%yoy growth.

Post the Oct-Dec'23 GDP data, we have revised up our FY24 real GDP growth forecast rate to 7.6%yoy, in line with the National Statistics Office’s latest forecast (earlier NSO had projected FY24 real GDP growth at 7.3%yoy), given the latest data release and revisions to the past quarters’ data. However, we think a better reflection of the underlying growth rate in the economy is captured by the GVA figure, where the Statistics office’s latest forecast for FY24 has remained unchanged at 6.9%yoy relative to its provisional forecast. Even 6.9%yoy real GVA growth in FY24 vs. a downward revised growth rate of 6.7%yoy in FY23, is a considerable achievement, in our view, given the repeated global shocks that the Indian economy has encountered in the last few years. But GDP growth figures are more confusing to interpret, given the large contribution of discrepancies to growth at this stage; hence, we think it makes more sense to look at India’s real GVA growth trend to assess the underlying growth rate of the economy.

For FY25, we revised up our real GDP growth forecast only marginally higher to 6.5%yoy, from 6.3%yoy earlier, but maintained our real GVA forecast at 6.3%yoy for the next fiscal year. With a GDP growth of 7.6%yoy in FY24, the base will become more challenging for next year’s growth, and hence we keep our growth estimate below 7%yoy at this stage. The data revisions of the past year and the large discrepancies in the GDP component, which will need to be adjusted at a later stage, make the analysis of trend growth rate difficult. Consequently, we will rely more on the GVA growth trend as well as the momentum of high-frequency growth indicators to inform our view on India’s growth outlook.

## Core inflation surprises to the downside

Feb CPI inflation came at 5.09%yoy, closer to our own forecast of 5.07%, but slightly higher than Bloomberg consensus of 5.04%. In Jan, CPI inflation was 5.10%. This resulted in a small increase in CPI index by+0.16%mom in Feb, in contrast to the fall of -0.11%mom in Jan. Despite the increase in month-on-month momentum, the year-on-year CPI inflation rate remained steady around 5.1%, led by a favorable base and continued softness in vegetable prices owing to positive seasonality. With the base effect having become more favorable in Jan-March'24 and vegetable prices remaining soft during the winter months aided by the arrival of new crops, this is likely to help maintain a CPI average of 5.1% in the current quarter, close to RBI's forecast of 5.0%.

The good news is that CPI ex vegetables inflation moderated to 3.7%yoy in Feb (vs. 3.9%yoy in Jan), while core inflation (CPI ex food and fuel) also eased further to a multi-year low of 3.37%yoy (vs. 3.53%yoy in Jan). Note that core CPI averaged 4.8%yoy in the pre-pandemic period between FY18-FY20, when headline CPI averaged around 3.9% during the same period. Currently, core CPI is 140bps lower than the pre-pandemic average, while real rates calculated in terms of core CPI is in excess of 300bps.

## Key observations:

1. Headline inflation and real rates: RBI's own forecast of FY25 headline CPI inflation is 4.5%. This implies more than 200bps positive real rates on a forward looking basis. In our view, there is no need to maintain 200bps positive real rates like during the FY18-FY20 period, as it would entail a greater growth sacrifice. If the central bank thinks that maintaining 100bps of positive real rates is sufficient to maintain macro financial stability in this cycle, then room could open up for 75- 100bps of rate cuts in this cycle, as per our assessment.

2. Core inflation and real rates: Core inflation is currently at 3.4% and is likely to average 3.5% in FY25, as per our estimates. The forward looking real interest rates work out to be in excess of 300bps at this stage. In the pre-pandemic period (FY18- FY20), core inflation average was higher at 4.8% and real rates were about 100bps with respect to core inflation.

3. Core inflation and growth: Why is core inflation easing, if real GDP growth is surprising to the upside? Does it imply that the potential growth rate is higher than the underlying growth rate in the economy? As per our assessment, India's potential growth rate is at 7.25% and has not been affected materially by the pandemic, as supply-side reforms have likely helped offset the medium-term impact of Covid-19. While India's growth momentum has surprised to the upside, relative to expectations, it may still be below the potential growth rate of the economy, once adjusted for outsized base effects and seasonality. This could be the reason why core inflation has drifted lower in FY24, responding to the impact of rate hikes and liquidity tightening, despite GDP growth surprising to the upside.

4. Taylor Rule implied policy repo rate: We use the RBI's forecast of inflation and growth for FY25 to calculate the implied policy rate using a simple Taylor Rule formula. We take RBI's latest real GDP growth forecast for FY25, which is at 7.0%yoy and CPI forecast of 4.5% average. We assume that a 100bps positive real rate should be sufficient to maintain macro-financial stability in this business cycle. We further assume that India's potential growth rate is at 7.25%.Giving equal weight to CPI drift from the target and output gap, we arrive at a terminal repo rate close to 5.65% using RBI's growth-inflation forecast for FY25.

We repeat the same exercise using Deutsche Bank's forecast for FY25 real GDP growth and CPI inflation. We assume that FY25 real GDP growth will average around 6.5%yoy, while CPI inflation will average about 4.6%. In this scenario, the Taylor Rule formula indicates a terminal repo rate close to 5.50%, in line with our current forecast.

Overtightening risks and forward looking monetary policy response: RBI has kept liquidity tight in the system - delivering almost an effective rate hike since Oct'23 and increased risk weights for personal loans and NBFCs to curb credit growth and bring the credit/deposit ratio to more sustainable levels of 70-80%, which we believe will start showing the desired impact over the next few quarters. 6. Appropriate timing for changing the current monetary policy stance: If the easing cycle indeed commences from June'24, considering the 1-year ahead outlook, then it is likely that the central bank will change the existing monetary policy stance of "withdrawal of accommodation" to neutral in the preceding April monetary policy meeting. But if the RBI considers postponing the rate cuts to August, then the stance change could also be pushed back to the June policy. In our view, the timing and the quantum of India's rate cut cycle will be linked closely to the Fed rate cut cycle; if the Fed delays its rate cut cycle or/and reduces rates lower than what is currently priced in by the markets, then the extent and timing of India's rate cuts will also be affected, irrespective of the inflation trajectory, in our view.

## South Korea and Taiwan: Diverging domestic factors

While the Korean government offered investors a silver lining, promoting the Corporate Value-up Programme, we expect inflationary pressure to ease, led by fresh food prices, giving the BoK room to contemplate the timing of its rate cuts amid sustained weak domestic demand.

n Despite its dovish comments, suggesting steady policy rates ahead, we think that there is a non-negligible risk of further rate hikes by the CBC. It has yet to conclude its assessment of a "structural change in inflation".

## South Korea's silver lining

While the Bank of Korea may not discount the rebound in inflation readings as "bumps", as the Fed (chair) recently has, it is likely to reiterate its expectation of moderation towards the 2% target in the medium term. We expect inflationary pressure to ease, even in the near term, as LNY and seasonal factors drop out of the data, while the impact of weak domestic demand will likely drag on inflation over a longer term.

South Korea's CPI inflation surprised to the upside in February, largely due to surging fresh food prices, led by fruit: mandarin oranges, pears and strawberries. That pressure should ease as the economy leaves LNY and seasonal demand effects behind. Moreover, the government has opted to keep both electricity prices and fuel tax cuts in place, at least through 2H, although the impact of sharp increases in health care services and insurance costs at the outset of the year will remain through end-2024.

Meanwhile, retail sales continued to contract, on a real basis, by 2.8% in January. Auto sales (units) fell 8%yoy in January/February, led by a 22.1% fall in imported car sales. Equipment investment fell 4.1% in January and is likely to remain in negative territory for some time.

Weak domestic demand calls for a mid-cycle rate adjustment (cuts), in our view, to avoid overtightening – and a hard landing. Our baseline view remains that the BoK delivers three (25bps) rate cuts, starting in May. An alternative to our baseline forecast is that the BoK's rate cuts are curtailed, in the event of an alternative scenario in which the Fed funds rate bottoms out earlier, above 4%, vs. our US team's baseline forecast of 3.625%.

While a weaker yuan and policy shocks (from the US and/or China), amid the US election cycle, pose a significant threat to the Korean won, there are also longterm tailwinds for the currency. Putting the impact of notable improvement in South Korea's trade balance aside, South Korea's WGBI inclusion and Corporate Value-up Program offer structural tailwinds for the Korean won, turning our attention also to the benefits of silver/longevity dividend.

Taiwan: CBC charting its own path

![](images/c478358c7f28b0cefc8ffcf1fa7f136c7e6490570655dd333e67eb7ed36dc441.jpg)
Source : CEIC, Deutsche Bank Research

While we were expecting it to strike a hawkish tone, as warned in our report, we thought it would conclude its study on "structural change in inflation" before resuming rate hikes.

Governor Yang underlined "limited upside for rates in future", rendering the CBC's latest rate hike as a dovish one. More importantly, he noted that the policy rates are "now at an appropriate level" and that the CBC "won't keep hiking rates whenever CPI is over 2%", especially as it expects inflation to "gradually fall". Although the CBC raised its 2024 headline and core inflation forecasts by 0.27ppts and 0.2ppts to 2.16% and 2.03%, respectively, as it assumed a 10% hike in electricity prices, the figures would fall below last year's headline and core inflation of 2.49% and 2.59%, respectively.

That said, the risk of further rate hikes remains non-negligible, given ongoing concerns about a "structural change in inflation". By February this year, Taiwan's five-year headline inflation had risen to 1.57% from 0.77% in February 2020. In fact, both headline and core inflation stood relatively high, at 2.24% yoy and 2.28% yoy, respectively, in the first two months of this year, with services inflation (2.6%) leading that of goods (2.2%) (Figure 19). As in advanced economies, Taiwan's labour-market conditions have tightened, with the employment rate rising to its highest level since 2000, to 96.7%. Taiwan's working-age population peaked in November 2015, although the impact was mitigated by the rising participation rate (by 0.5ppts to 59.3%) and increasing foreign (migrant) workers (constituting 4.4% of the workforce now). Data (through January) pointed to a notable acceleration in wage pressure, which could result in more persistent, higher inflation. (Figure 4) The Yuan passed a minimum wage act that allows for more regular predictable increases, taking into account CPI, among other factors. This year, Taiwan's minimum wage was hiked by 4.1%. Together with electricity price hikes, the matter of removing the hospital registration fee cap has also added to inflation concerns.

The central bank also remains concerned about the recovery in housing-market activity, as "growth in housing loans and loans to the real estate sector has picked up...and all banks' ratios of real estate lending to total lending remain elevated" (Figure 23 and Figure 24). The January election results highlighted the public's call for, among others, quality jobs, higher wages and affordable housing. Affordable housing – pitted against rising housing prices – has certainly reinforced our expectation of no rate cuts by the CBC through 2025, despite our US team's forecast of 175bps of rate cuts by the Fed, starting in June.

On growth, the CBC's latest policy statement reflected the board's growing confidence in Taiwan's growth prospects – also raising its 2024 growth forecast by 0.1ppts to 3.22% – noting that private consumption "gained momentum on stronger consumer confidence amid an economic recovery" amid sustained gains in payrolls, while exports recorded "continuous growth, underpinned by stabilizing final demand and booming business for AI and other emerging technology applications" (Figure 9), although investment remained an area of weakness (Figure 3). Note that consumer sentiment has continued to recover this year, led by a sharp rebound in the consumer outlook for the stock market, to levels not seen after the pandemic-induced plunge (Figure 5).

Our baseline view has the CBC holding its rates steady for the foreseeable future, with average inflation likely moderating to slightly below 2% in 2025 as growth moderates. In connection, our US team's forecast of a total of 175bps rate cuts by the Fed (starting in June) points to a rather high bar for further rate hikes by the CBC. That said, the risk of further rate hikes by the CBC looks non-negligible. First, the CBC has yet to conclude its assessment of "structural change in inflation", suggesting that its view on the neutral rate is subject to change - ie higher than the current level. Second, our US team has raised the risk of an alternative scenario that could see the Fed funds rate bottoming well above 4%, with the Fed opting for a prolonged pause after mid-cycle (limited) rate cuts, leaving the TW dollar notably weaker than expected against the US dollar. Such a scenario could prompt the CBC to hike further, albeit after the Fed's pause. If nothing else, the CBC's latest decision is a reminder that both cyclical and structural factors are in play. Moreover, its reaction function prior to the GFC is far more informative than the post-GFC period. Recall that the CBC hiked after a prolonged pause in early 1981 and it also continued to hike through June 2008, despite the Fed's rate cuts. (Figure 22).

## ASEAN: Cyclical and structural policy considerations

ASEAN economies are facing either a cyclical turn or a structural challenge, prompting them to reassess their policy stances and/or the level of their neutral rates. That said, for many, their concerns over FX stability stand in the way – although a recovery in exports could provide better support – while "bumps" in inflation may add to their concerns ahead, albeit briefly. Although FDI inflows to ASEAN economies faced headwinds last year, the region has remained a favoured investment destination for manufacturing MNCs.

Juliana Lee, Chief Economist, APAC

Although its exports have benefited significantly from the commodity price boom in recent years – from coal, iron and steel to organic chemicals – Indonesia has also enjoyed a notable rise in the export of electrical machinery and apparatus and vehicles. (Figure 21) Indonesia's success in attracting FDI (in down-streaming) in EVs (batteries) has continued to broaden. Hyundai LG Industry Green Power will begin battery manufacture for EVs next month, with the capacity to produce enough battery cells for 150k EVs. CATL is building EV-battery factories, while BYD expects to break ground for its own later this year. Data suggest that a cyclical peak in ASEAN's passenger-vehicle sales may have passed, while those in advanced Asia are in clear decline. In contrast, sales in India have continued to increase and rebounded in China. (Figure 19)

Macroeconomic fluctuations often account for most of the risks associated with exposures to instruments that promise to pay fixed or variable interest rates. For an investor who is long fixed-rate instruments (thus receiving these cash flows), increases in interest rates naturally lift the discount factors applied to the expected cash flows and thus cause a loss. The longer the maturity of a certain cash flow and the higher the move in the discount factor, the higher will be the loss. Upside surprises in inflation, fiscal funding needs, and FX depreciation rates are among the most common adverse macroeconomic shocks to receivers. But counterparty exposure, issuer creditworthiness, client segmentation, regulation (including changes in assets holding limits for different types of investors), changes in tax policies, currency convertibility (which may constrain currency conversion, repatriation of profits and/or liquidation of positions), and settlement issues related to local clearing houses are also important risk factors. The sensitivity of fixed-income instruments to macroeconomic shocks may be mitigated by indexing the contracted cash flows to inflation, to FX depreciation, or to specified interest rates - these are common in emerging markets. The index fixings may - by construction - lag or mis-measure the actual move in the underlying variables they are intended to track. The choice of the proper fixing (or metric) is particularly important in swaps markets, where floating coupon rates (i.e., coupons indexed to a typically short-dated interest rate reference index) are exchanged for fixed coupons. Funding in a currency that differs from the currency in which coupons are denominated carries FX risk.
