# China: December trade activity beat expectations

2024 Institutional Investor All-Asia Equity Research Survey VOTE Voting Open Jan 9 - Feb 2 Please vote for J.P. Morgan (5 stars)

![](images/5cb311a5cac60aa12d095744355fc0c09328d94c58547fb38cb78686443edab8.jpg)

China<sup>’</sup>s merchandise exports (in USD terms) rose 2.3%oya in December, partly on a base effect, or up 1.0%m/m sa. Nominal imports also rose 0.2%oya or 3.0%m/m sa, with the trade surplus widening to US\$75.3bn.

By product breakdown, exports of low-end consumer goods rose a solid 2.3%m/m sa on holiday consumer goods related demand, while mechanical & electrical product and high-tech exports only inched up by 0.6%m/m sa and 0.1%, respectively.

By major markets, exports to the EU (+5.4%m/m sa), ASEAN (+4.2%), Korea (3.7%) and Japan (3.4%) led the external demand, while exports to the US, Russia, and Latam declined modestly.

Major commodity import strength resumed, with solid gains in imports of crude oil, iron ore and steel products, along with a modest recovery in imports of mechanical and electrical products and high-tech products.

China<sup>’</sup>s exports have generally held up better than expected in 2023, benefiting from its diversification efforts across major markets (rise in exports to EM Asia, LatAm, Russia and Africa partially offsets weaker demand from G3 economies) and by products with rapid expansion in exports in new energy (solar cell and lithium battery) and motor vehicles.

We hold a relatively steady outlook on 2024 export activity. Exports growth (in USD terms) may be flat or register low-single-digit growth, with a fading negative price effect on a stable CNY outlook. One concern is that, if demandsupply imbalance continues and exporters continue to aggressively push for price competition, how the trading partners will react.

China<sup>’</sup>s trade activity beat market expectations in December. Merchandise exports (in USD terms) rose 2.3%oya (J.P. Morgan: -0.9%; consensus: 1.5%), compared to the modest rise of 0.5%oya in November. The positive %year-on-year growth was partly supported by a low base, due to a COVID-related drag in 4Q22. In sequential terms, we estimate exports gained 1.0% m/m sa in December, after two months of contraction.

Imports also came in stronger than expected, rising 0.2%oya (J.P. Morgan: -0.6%; consensus: -0.5%). Seasonally adjusted, we estimate nominal imports increased 3.0%m/m sa, partially paying back the 5.9% drop in November. As a result,the trade surplus widened to US\$75.3bn in December (vs. \$68.4bn in November). For 2023 as a whole, China<sup>’</sup>s merchandise trade surplus amounted to \$858.6 billion, almost unchanged from \$858.8 billion in 2022.

## Stronger-than-expected exports partly lifted by holiday consumer goods demand

In recent months, China<sup>’</sup>s trade reports have been volatile. The sequential trend growth of exports turned negative again in December despite the modest gain on the month, falling modestly by 2.5% 3m/3m saar (vs. +2.0% in November). The price effect has been weighing on this year<sup>’</sup>s nominal exports, due to domestic deflation, insufficient domestic demand and CNY depreciation against the dollar. Exporters have cut export prices more aggressively than that implied by the PPI. Excluding the price effect, export volumes have held up well. In November, we estimate export prices (in USD terms) declined 11.5%oya, while export volumes jumped 12.0%. With a continuing low base in December last year, we think export volume growth in %oya terms may have stayed elevated.

In further breakdown, low-end consumer goods export picked up further while exports of mechanical and electrical products as well as high-end products ticked up modestly. Specifically, exports of low-end consumer goods (including garments, clothing, footwear and toys) rose by a solid 2.3%m/m sa in December, extending the 1.6% gain in November. It was likely supported by year-end holiday related consumer goods demand. Exports of hightech products inched up 0.1% m/m sa in December, after the 5.0% and 2.3% declines in October and November. Exports of mechanical and electrical products ticked up 0.6% m/m sa, partially paying back the 0.9% fall in November. In particular, exports of mobile phones fell 12.0%m/m sa, following the 9.1% drop in November. The two months<sup>’</sup> declines have largely paid back most of the gains in the prior two months (September and October), suggesting the demand boosted by launch of new iPhone models has largely faded. Meanwhile, exports of integrated circuits gained another 5.2% m/m sa in December, the same pace as November. Exports of data processing machines also grew 5.2% m/m sa (vs. -3.0%m/ m sa in November). Besides, China<sup>’</sup>s auto exports increased 6.3% m/m sa in December, after the 1.5% modest fall in November.

By major market, exports to the US eased 1.6% m/m sa in December, staying in contractionary territory for the third consecutive month. Exports to the EU jumped 5.4% m/m sa (vs. -3.8% m/m sa in November), and exports to Japan rose 3.4% m/m sa (vs. -3.8% m/m sa in November). Meanwhile, exports to EM Asia inched up by 0.7% m/m sa(vs. -0.3% m/m sa in November), with a 4.2% rise in exports to ASEAN, a 4.5% drop in exports to Taiwan, and a 3.7% rise in exports to Korea. Regarding exports to other key EM economies, exports to Russia, Africa and Latin America all declined modestly, by 2.8%m/m sa, 0.6% and 1.7% respectively.

## Import strength resumed, likely on domestic demand amid infrastructure and public housing policy support

Regarding the broad import categories, process imports, which accounted for 15.1% of total imports in 2023 (vs. 16.9% in 2022), dropped another 5.7% m/m sa in December (vs. -4.7% in November). Meanwhile, ordinary imports (which accounted for 64.9% of total imports in 2023 or 63.7% in 2022) rose 6.6% m/m sa (vs. -7.4% m/m sa in November).

A breakdown by trading partner suggests imports from the US gained 5.4% m/m sa in December (vs. -7.8% m/m sa in November), imports from the EU rose 3.1% m/m sa, imports from Japan increased 4.5%, and imports from EM Asia ticked up 1.5%. In addition, regarding imports from major commodity exporting countries, imports from Brazil gained another 14.3%m/m sa in December, following the 8.3% and 9.5% solid gains in October and November. Imports from Australia jumped 11.7%m/m sa and imports from Russia increased 3.1%.

Regarding energy commodity imports, in volume terms, imports of crude oil jumped 13.9% m/m sa in December (vs. -13.3% m/m sa in November), while coal imports inched down by 0.2% m/m sa (vs. +10.8% m/m sa in November). Regarding imports of key industrial metals, import volumes of iron ore gained 5.6% m/m sa (vs. +0.2% m/m sa in November) while copper imports dropped 14.0% m/m sa (vs. -4.7% m/m sa in November). Besides, imports of mechanical and electrical products rose a modest 2.4% m/m sa in December (vs . -4.7% in November), and imports of high-tech products ticked up 1.2%m/m sa (vs. -4.0% in November).

## A summary of trade activity in 2023 and outlook for 2024

Monthly trade data could be affected by temporary factors. For instance, the strong exports to EU and Japan or low-end consumer goods could reflect seasonal holiday demand. Hence, a full-year review of 2023 trade activity should be more interesting to gauge the trend.

Overall, trade performance in 2023 came in stronger than expected. On the export front, although full-year exports fell 4.2% (the weakest since 2017), it was largely driven by the price effect (CNY depreciation and export price cuts). In volume terms, China<sup>’</sup>s merchandise exports may grow at around 3%, outperforming the sluggish trend in global activity. China<sup>’</sup>s share of the global exports market remained relatively stable at 14.4% in the first three quarters of 2023 (vs. 14.2% in 2022). On the imports front, total merchandize goods imports fell 5.5% in 2023, but commodity imports were much stronger than expected.

Along with the resilience in China<sup>’</sup>s export sector performance, we have noticed several important changes.

First is diversification in export markets. In 2023, China<sup>’</sup>s exports to G3 had the poorest performance (US: -13.0%y/y; EU: -10.2%; Japan: -8.7%), exports to EM Asia also lost steam (-7.3%), while China<sup>’</sup>s exports to Russia (46.1%) and Africa (6.9%) became the bright spots. G3 share in China<sup>’</sup>s exports fell from 40.6% in 2018-19 to 34.1% in 2023. By contrast, China<sup>’</sup>s exports to EM economies (including EM Asia, LatAm, Africa and Russia) rose from 32.5% in 2018-19 to 37.7% in 2023.

Second, China<sup>’</sup>s export structure has faced two major challenges. Exports of low-end consumer goods fell 8.6% in 2023, due to increase in production costs and fading comparative advantages in labor-intensive sectors. Meanwhile, exports of high-tech products fell 11.1% in 2023, amid the trend of technology de-coupling between China and the US and as a consequence of technology restrictions on China. By contrast, exports of middle- and upper-middle value-added products have outperformed. Exports of mechanical and electrical products fell more modestly by 3.1% in 2023. Remarkably, exports of auto, solar cell and lithium battery were very strong, and their share in China<sup>’</sup>s exports jumped from 0.6% in 2018-19 to 6.1% in 2023.

In addition to trade diversification, Chinese companies have also accelerated the pace of overseas investment, e.g., opening factories in ASEAN, Mexico and other countries. The increase in overseas investment is driven by multiple factors: lower production cost; bypassing the higher tariff from the US; support from the government<sup>’</sup>s Belt-and-Road Initiatives; and risk diversification (both business risks and policy risks). If this trend continues, the interpretation of bilateral trade balance could become more complicated.

Entering 2024, we have a relatively steady outlook on China<sup>’</sup>s export activity. Total export growth (in USD terms) may be flat or register low-single-digit growth in 2024. The negative price effect as observed in 2023 should gradually fade, as CNY will likely be relatively stable with a year-end forecast of USD/CNY at 7.10 (link). One concern is that, if domestic demandsupply imbalance continues and exporters continue to aggressively push for price competition, how the trading partners will react. In the near term, China<sup>’</sup>s export of disinflation is probably desirable, but the aggressive price competition and China<sup>’</sup>s large manufacturing capacity may lead to a disruptive impact and trigger countermeasures by competing trading partners. For instance, in the auto sector, the EU and the US have raised concerns about unfair competition related to government subsidy and low-price dumping. These could backfire on China<sup>’</sup>s export sector.
