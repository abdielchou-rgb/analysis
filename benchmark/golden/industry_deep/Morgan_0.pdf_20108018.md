## R2-P2

In the week before Star Wars day on May 4<sup>th</sup>, market participants could be forgiven for feeling that the Force was with them - after all, the week<sup>’</sup>s highlights are best summarized as R2-P2 (Refinancing estimates, the Refunding announcement, Powell<sup>’</sup>s press conference and Payrolls). Beginning with the Treasury, refinancing needs for the current and the next quarter came in about \~\$100bn larger than our estimates after accounting for differences in QT and TGA assumptions (see Financing estimates come in larger than expectations, J. Barry et al., 4/29/2024). The refunding announcement was more in line with our expectations, with coupon sizes being left unchanged. Treasury also indicated that it does not anticipate needing to increase coupon auction sizes for at least several quarters. Thus, the higher financing needs, coupled with slightly higher indicated TGA levels, implies higher levels of T-bill issuance relative to our earlier estimates. With respect to the FOMC meeting, the Fed left rates unchanged and Chair Powell's comments echoed his recent tone, signaling that more time will be needed for the committee to gain the necessary confidence in disinflation to begin rate cuts.

Seen in the light of the post-meeting remarks and Friday's softer employment report, the recent strength in inflation data is likely more an argument in favor of patience on the part of the Fed than a reason for the resumption of rate hikes (see Hikes aren<sup>’</sup>t in the gameplan, M. Feroli, 5/1/2024). But the bigger picture is that the market backdrop remains one of data dependency and policy uncertainty for the next few months, until clarity emerges as and when new data convincingly corroborates progress on disinflation. This message was echoed by numerous Fed speakers again this week (Figure 1)

This view is also reflected in options market pricing. As regular readers may recall, we have often looked at the prices of calls and puts at various strikes on December 2024 SOFR futures, and then (i) backed out the implied probability density function, which we then (ii) further decompose into a weighted combination of pre-specified "prior" Normal density functions that correspond to various specific Fed outcomes (such as a distribution with a 50bp lower-than-spot mean, which would correspond to 2 cuts by year end). By doing this, we can use the resulting weights on the individual "prior" distributions as gauges of the relative significance of each different policy rate outcome. As seen in Figure 2, policy uncertainty remains significant, even though there is a rebalancing of sorts underway. Although deep-cut scenarios are waning in relevance, implied distributions from SFRZ4 options are telling us that there remains a wide cone of possibilities that span scenarios ranging from shallow cuts (up to 3 cuts by year end) to no cuts and even hiking scenarios.

The only cure for such policy uncertainty is time - more time must pass, during which new data materializes to clarify the macroeconomic outlook that in turns produces more clarity around the likely path of policy rates. This means that rates are likely to remain rangebound in the near future until such clarity emerges. This has bearish implications for volatility, as we have noted in recent weeks and as we discuss later (see Options).

But this also argues in support of positive carry trades on the yield curve, since an environment of rangebound rates makes it likely that forwards will roll up towards unchanged spot rates. But thanks to the dramatic repricing in Fed easing expectations in recent weeks, carry has become greatly diminished and good yield curve carry trades that offer attractive risk-reward have been hard to find, causing us to favor relative value trades as the main yield curve trading theme. This remains the case still, but this week's rally in the front end has brought back some carry in front end shorts, creating a window of opportunity to add some exposure to carry.

One attractive trade that offers reasonably attractive carry as well as relative value is to initiate 3M forward 1s/3s swap curve flatteners, hedged with a 65% risk weighted long in the 3Mx3M sector and a 25% risk weighted short in the 15Mx3M sector. As seen in Figure 3, the weighted spread corresponding to this trade has been reasonably mean reverting, but has steepened recently and has now begun to retrace. Moreover, carry and slide on this trade is attractive, at \~5bp over a 3 month horizon. Given the combination of carry and relative value, we recommend this trade (see Trade recommendations).

Another trade that offers modestly better carry as well as attractive relative value is initiating 15Mx3M / 1YX1Y forward swap curve flatteners, paired with 20% of the risk in a long in 18Mx3M and a 24% risk weighted short in 3Mx5Y forward swaps. As seen in Figure 4, the weighted spread corresponding to this trade has been generally mean reverting, but has become mispriced recently and appears to be normalizing. In addition, after this week's moves, carry on this trade is modestly attractive at 2bp per quarter. Therefore, we recommend this trade (see Trade recommendations).

## Swap spreads and Term Funding Premium

Maturity matched swap spreads narrowed slightly across much of the curve, except for the 2Y and 7Y sector where it is a touch wider (Figure 5). Spread curves are generally flatter - the 2s/5s, 5s/10s and 5s/30s spread curves are all flatter on the week, with the 10s/30s spread curve being a minor exception. These flattening moves in spread curves are consistent with a rise in Term Funding Premium - as we noted recently (see Term Funding Premium and the Term Structure of SOFR Swap Spreads), the term structure of swap spreads is intrinsically linked to term funding premium (i.e., the additional return demanded by the market for committing principal to term rather than rolling overnight commitments of principal, all else equal). It is worth noting that term funding premium is now at a historical high, at over 4.9bp per year of modified duration (Figure 6).

This week's rise in term funding premium (or TFP, for short) is not a surprise, and risks remain skewed towards further increases. We can see this by examining the outlook for the various supply and demand proxies that drive TFP. As we have described in the note referenced earlier, TFP is well explained by the size of the Fed balance sheet, the aggregate AUM at the top 20 core bond funds, monthly UST duration supply and the total RRP balance at the Fed. The first two are demand-side factors, while the third factor represents supply and the last factor reflects a liquidity draining effect from higher RRP balances (controlling for the overall size of the Fed B/S). As seen in Figure 7, our outlook for the Fed's balance sheet (see discussion below) looks for a modest increase in the RRP balance and continuing declines in overall balance sheet size - both of which point to a rise in TFP in the near term. The other factors are expected to remain flat - duration supply will likely remain constant given that Treasury indicated that coupon UST auction size changes will not be needed for at least several quarters, and our UST strategists expect AUM growth to remain weak given an inverted yield curve and given that Fed easing does not appear imminent. Thus, TFP appears biased higher in coming months, which should - all else equal - pressure swap spreads narrower by amounts ranging from \~1bp at the front end to \~6bp at the long end. Given this view, and given swap spread valuations that are cheap in the 5Y sector (Figure 8), we recommend adding exposure to 5s/30s swap spread curve flatteners (see Trade recommendations).

## Fed balance sheet update

As was widely expected, the FOMC announced changes to its balance sheet policy on Wednesday. The FOMC decided to reduce the cap on the monthly decline in Treasury holdings from \$60bn/month to \$25bn/month, starting in June. This was \$5bn/month of additional taper relative to our prior expectations, but also begins a month later than we had thought. We update our Fed balance sheet forecast to reflect these changes to the QT timeline (Figure 9), as well as minor updates to our TGA forecast post May refunding estimates. As a reminder to our readers, we continue to assume that RRP balances are likely to be stickier in the near-term, and we expect QT to halt when O/N RRP falls to \~\$300bn. Putting it all together, our forecast continues to indicate that QT will likely continue for the remainder of the year, at which point Reserves and O/N RRP balances will likely fall to \~\$3tn and \~\$370bn, respectively.

## Options

Swaption implied volatility followed yields lower this week, with implieds lower across the surface as both the quarterly refunding announcement and the FOMC meeting were mostly in line with expectations. Friday's softer employment data caused implied volatility in the upper-left sector of the vol grid to rise sharply, but all in all, 6Mx2Y finished the week slightly lower, while 6Mx10Y is lower by about 0.35 bp/day (Figure 10).

Looking ahead, we maintain our bearish stance on gamma. As much as the soft Payrolls number has helped bring Fed cuts back in play for this year, it does little to erase policy uncertainty. As we discussed earlier in this piece, the implied distribution from SFRZ4 options is telling us that there remain a wide cone of possibilities that span scenarios ranging from shallow cuts (up to 3 cuts by year end) to no cuts and even hiking scenarios, with relatively similar weights on each (see Figure 2). Moreover, this is unlikely to change until more data arrives in coming months and corroborates either path - indeed the few Fed governors who have spoken since the meeting on Wednesday have focused on data dependency and have not explicitly ruled out hikes nor have they committed to cuts (Figure 1).

For the vol markets, this represents a continuation of the recent status quo - i.e., yields are likely to be volatile on a day-to-day basis given the high degree of data dependence, but they will also likely be ultimately mean-reverting and rangebound, until a clear path for policy rates emerges. As we have noted in the past, rangebound yield environments are quite favorable for short gamma positions, even if yields are choppy and volatile on a day to day basis. Therefore, as carry-seeking investors look to short gamma strategies to earn carry in a rangebound market, implieds could experience downward pressure and even trade cheap to fair value. Indeed, such is the case, and implieds continue to appear cheap to fair value, although not as cheap as was the case in March (Figure 11).

Interestingly, even if we acknowledge the cheapness of implied volatility relative to fair value, our empirical projections suggest that they aren't cheap enough to make long gamma returns profitable going forward. To see this, we return to our empirical model for returns on long straddle positions (delta hedged daily). Our empirical model incorporates the ex-ante residual from our implied volatility fair value model as a relative value measure, in addition to other factors (short rates and real rates). Even accounting for the currently cheap valuations, this model is pointing to only very modestly positive returns in short and intermediate tails, and negative returns on long gamma returns on 30Y tails (Figure 12). Thus, when we factor in the additional benefit to short gamma positions from delta hedging less frequently, and also considering that implieds could cheapen even further if yields stay rangebound like we expect, we maintain our bearish stance on gamma and continue to recommend delta hedging less frequently (or delta hedging to wider tolerances).

## Callable UST bonds - back to the future?

In the refunding documents, the second charge question reprised a presentation from 2019, asking TBAC to “comment on the likely costs and benefits of potential new Treasury products that might assist Treasury in … ways to minimize borrowing costs, better manage its liability profile, enhance market liquidity, and expand the investor base in Treasury securities.<sup>”</sup> Of potentially new products, some stood out from the presentation, one of which was callable bonds. To be clear, the posing of the charge question is to be seen as exploratory. In addition, given the pros and cons of various potential products in the TBAC presentation, it would appear that callable bonds are not near the top of the list - indeed, our UST strategists have flagged 1Y FRNs and 3Y TIPS as more likely candidates. Nevertheless, the prospect of callable issuance by the Treasury, however remote, is worth noting here given how significant the impact would likely be for vol market structure.

The TBAC member argued that callable bonds might be attractive to investors who seek yield enhancement from selling optionality by investing in callable bonds or residential MBS. If issued unswapped (i.e., if Treasury were to issue callable bonds and retain the optionality), callable bonds would potentially allow Treasury to lower its interest costs during recessionary environments, when Treasury could benefit from calling the bonds and refinancing at potentially lower rates. However, the presenter noted that market liquidity implications were unclear, and that pursuing callables would require significant operational advancements and developmental work. Although it does not appear to higher up on the priority list of new products, we would note that callable issuance by the US Treasury could significantly impact the structure of the US interest rate swaptions market. We think it<sup>’</sup>s very unlikely Treasury would swap these callables, meaning that callable bond investors<sup>’</sup> yield enhancement needs would be met without translating into vol supply to the street. This is because callable bond issuers currently do not typically seek to warehouse the embedded call, but rather sell it to a dealer (in the form of a callable swap) to benefit from lower financing costs. Thus, the callable bond market in its current form serves as a mechanism to supply vol from yield-seeking investors to dealers, while offering a benefit to issuers in the process. A potentially large and regular Treasury callable issuance schedule could impair vol supply to the street, and could structurally bias implied volatility higher as a result.
