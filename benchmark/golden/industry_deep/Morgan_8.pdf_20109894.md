## Did Someone Say 'Sahm'-thing About a Recession?

What probability do we put on a US recession? We get asked this question often, but it's a question better directed to our economists. It's also a question that requires specificity. What is the probability we enter recession again at any point in the future? What is the probability we will enter a recession in the next year? What is the probability the recession has already begun?

The Philadelphia Fed surveys professional forecasters every quarter about the probability they assign to negative real GDP growth in the quarter of the survey, the following quarter (the “Anxious Index”), and the few quarters thereafter.

For the 3Q24 survey published August 9, professional forecasters – including our Chief Global Economist Seth Carpenter – assigned a 21% probability, on average, to real GDP contracting in 4Q24 (see Exhibit 1 ). They assigned a 25% probability, on average, to real GDP contracting in 4Q25 (see Exhibit 2 ).

Exhibit 1: Philly Fed Survey of Professional Forecasters (SPF): Anxious Index
![](images/5c66c7f8b24489600047ae9bbddbb0859a25b3089b2b3be701c022db09295504.jpg)
Source: Morgan Stanley Research, FRB Philadelphia, NBER, Bloomberg

Exhibit 2: Philly Fed SPF: Probability that real GDP declines in 4 quarters
![](images/f3a7f653d9b3b6c9004998ffc80ee4c2ea8b1d44c100a3445aa6489ac905e40c.jpg)
Source: Morgan Stanley Research, FRB Philadelphia, NBER, Bloomberg

Forecasting involves art and science, and remains as difficult an endeavor as ever. Forecasting recessions, given the infrequency, seems to be one of the most difficult aspects of the profession. Data from the Survey of Professional Forecasters (SPF) make that clear.

• In the quarter before NBER recessions, professional forecasters almost always assigned "usual" probabilities that a recession will begin in the following quarter, where we define "usual" as within 1 standard deviation of the non-NBER recession average assigned probability (see Exhibit 3 ).

• In the quarter an NBER recession begins, professional forecasters placed higherthan-usual probabilities that the recession will begin that quarter, but still "low" probabilities on an absolute basis, where we define "low" as around a 1-in-3 chance or lower (see Exhibit 4 ).

Exhibit 3: Anxious Index in quarter before NBER recession begins and non-recession average
![](images/a8d1d1ac9f39e146985561ac345184fc377a8026ff6ebec15b9c6dcbbd844b2d.jpg)
Source: Morgan Stanley Research, FRB Philadelphia, NBER, Bloomberg

Exhibit 4: Probability real GDP growth negative in quarter recession begins and non-recession average
![](images/f94bb2936f9be82ede62b3bc53a528f0fb83b1f743370f83dd74db870ead69a1.jpg)
Source: Morgan Stanley Research, FRB Philadelphia, NBER, Bloomberg

Investors, recognizing the challenge forecasters face in assessing the probability of a recession, turn to other approaches. The yield curve remains a popular indicator, but has fallen short – so far – in the current cycle. Investors also look to the Sahm Rule, which compares the 3-month average unemployment rate to the minimum of that 3-month average over the past 12 months.

The Sahm Rule indicator reached its publicized recession threshold of 0.5pp in July. But our economists think the Sahm Rule misleads more than it informs in this cycle, given the recent increase in the labor force. Another "rule" making the rounds comes from Michaillat and Saez (2024), which also reached – and then surpassed – its recession threshold of 0.3 (see Exhibit 5 and Exhibit 6 ).

Exhibit 5: Michaillat and Saez (2024) recession indicator and NBER recessions: 1952 to present
![](images/02083f4a575c3479cf5f802e804127621f44cfe6acc287323fce994cadea49f6.jpg)
Source: Morgan Stanley Research, Michaillat and Saez (2024), Bloomberg

Exhibit 6: Michaillat and Saez (2024) recession indicator and NBER recessions: past 2 decades
![](images/0694895aa641555ce9dc579c04733e3d50dc20481a632d45b9ce7b9ecc72fecf.jpg)
Source: Morgan Stanley Research, Michaillat and Saez (2024), Bloomberg

Instead of the Sahm Rule or Michaillat and Saez, our economists prefer an indication that comes from the employment-to-population ratio (EPR, see A New Rule). They proposed a 0.5pp recession threshold for the EPR indicator, similar to the Sahm Rule. As shown in Exhibit 7 and Exhibit 8 , the EPR indicator does not hint at a recession.

Exhibit 7: Employment-to-population indicator and NBER recessions: 1952 to present
![](images/7f93418102c645b26942096e769e1d0eb9c3155b783d8ca117408fa2ec67ac99.jpg)
Source: Morgan Stanley Research, Bloomberg

Exhibit 8: Employment-to-population indicator and NBER recessions: past 2 decades
![](images/8a9a33b4620c7b70f5e1f9fa1ebb19a77e915a978b136341646213c13a898c0c.jpg)
We think two features of the Michaillat and Saez approach deserve attention:

First, the approach combines the Sahm Rule with an indicator of labor demand: the vacancy rate. This aligns with our economist's preference to focus on labor demand more than labor supply.

The Michaillat and Saez approach takes the minimum of either the Sahm Rule or the vacancy rate indicator to calculate a minimum indicator. This approach produces a better track record than the Sahm Rule and one that detects recessions a few months earlier.

Second, Michaillat and Saez use two thresholds – a lower and higher threshold – in order to calculate the probability of a recession.

1. A lower threshold, which is the lowest threshold such that the indicator does not trigger false positives, and

2. A higher threshold, which is the highest threshold such that the indicator does not produce false negatives.

If the indicator rises to the lowest threshold, 0.5pp in the case of the Sahm Rule and EPR indicator, history would suggest a recession began. But history rhymes instead of repeats. And given the small sample set, we cannot be 100% sure a recession began.

If the indicator subsequently rises to the lowest indicator peak associated with past recessions, then confidence in a recession call should grow. Michaillat and Saez use the distance between the lower and higher thresholds to calculate the probability, from 0 to 100%, that a recession began.

In recognition of our economist's preference for the EPR indicator and our interest in the Michaillat and Saez approach, we calculate and name another indicator that combines the two: the Triumvirate Rule.

The Triumvirate Rule takes the minimum of the Sahm Rule, the vacancy rate indicator, and the EPR indicator. Using the approach in Michaillat and Saez, we assign a lower threshold of 0.2pp and an upper threshold of 0.8pp (see Exhibit 9 and Exhibit 10 ).

Exhibit 9: The Triumvirate Rule and NBER recessions: 1952 to present
![](images/0c2dd5cf33ecabe32a5ed1d39e58f35d13d48b89cea14fc1e6891fbd71a99d9d.jpg)
Source: Morgan Stanley Research, Michaillat and Saez (2004), Bloomberg
Exhibit 10: The Triumvirate Rule and NBER recessions: past 2 decades

Source: Morgan Stanley Research, Michaillat and Saez (2004), Bloomberg

According to the Triumvirate Rule and the 0.2-0.8 thresholds, we calculate a 22% probability that a recession began (see Exhibit 11 and Exhibit 12 ). This compares to the 16% probability professional forecasters assigned to negative real GDP growth in 3Q24.

In the end, these indicators continue to support our economist's baseline for a soft landing. At the same time, the Triumvirate Rule has moved to 100% probability of recession within 2 to 6 months after rising above 20% historically, with an average of 3.7 months. This suggests investors should continue to exercise caution.

However, non-banks include both non-bank financial institutions (NFBI) and the nonfinancial sector. We separate the total non-banks credit into the two categories using the NFBI vs non-financial sector cross-border claims distribution of Japanese banks as a proxy.

We estimate that the JPY credit to NFBIs outside of Japan is currently \~100bn USD. Before the Fed started increasing its policy rate in 2022, making long USD/JPY positions increasingly attractive from a carry perspective, these cross-border claims on NFBIs stood at \~80bn USD ( Exhibit 126 ).

However, there are two caveats to keep in mind with the BIS data: 1) These estimates likely understate the true size of the carry trade, and 2) The purpose of the JPY borrowing is undisclosed.

1) The BIS data only include on-balance sheet data. Off-balance sheet transactions including more complex derivative structures are not included in these data. In other words, the above-mentioned data may capture the JPY carry trend but not the full size, as asset managers and hedge funds typically fund their JPY carry trades in a different way.

Our understanding is that HF/AM's treasury desk typically fund JPY via FX swap/repo from their prime brokers if their portfolio managers want to position for long CCY/JPY. Meanwhile, CTA might prefer FX futures to leverage their exposure (which will be captured by CFTC data). All in all, the total JPY carry positioning would be bigger than that of direct JPY funding from banks.

2) We think it is reasonable to assume that some non-bank financial institutions have borrowed in JPY to buy a higher-yielding currency or a higher-yielding asset denominated in a different currency. However, the data do not allow us to track the exact purpose of the JPY borrowing, implying that we cannot know for sure which amount of JPY borrowing was used for carry trades.

As a result of elevated levels of UST supply, higher-for-longer rates, and ongoing Fed QT, demand for repo financing has increased. This has been reflected in a steady increase in SOFR volumes, which peaked at \$2.211tr on August 1 ( Exhibit 104 ). SOFR volumes have remained elevated at \$2.12tr on average, since then.

Concerns around dealer intermediation capacity and a flatline in RRP balances as a consequence has provoked discussion about a drying-up of liquidity in money markets. This has been reflected in a tighter SOFR/FF basis over recent months as market pricing implies SOFR to be above fed funds, on average, over the next three months, six months, and one year ( Exhibit 105 ).

We think either the delivery of Fed rate cuts in September – our economists' base case – or a technical adjustment by the Fed in lowering the RRP rate will be the catalyst to put the RRP on a track to fully deplete, allowing that liability item on the Fed's balance sheet to shrink along with QT (see Whither QT and the RRP?).

## Trading Strategy 1 – "Trade Longs/Fade Shorts"

When the 5-day moving average crosses above the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract (long duration) and hold for a 25-business-day period. When the 5-day moving average crosses below the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract and hold for a 25-business-day period. In short, this strategy buys futures when the Simple Moving Average Crossover (SMAX) generates both a long and a short signal, given the historical outperformance of long signals traded long and underperformance of short signals traded short. Given that the SMAX could generate both a long and a short signal within the predefined holding period, an investor may have a 200% long position since each of the two signals would be traded in separate portfolio sleeves.

## Definition of terms
