# Venture Vision: Speed to \$100bn?

Only two private VC-backed companies are officially valued at > \$100bn: SpaceX & Bytedance. However, OpenAI - currently \$29bn - is reportedly on its way to join the club. A valuation of \$100bn by end '24 would rank it as the 8th fastest company in history to bridge from \$10-\$100bn, albeit behind 4 of the Magnificent 7.

Despite much discussion in the media (here and here) and relatively liquid secondary market transactions, OpenAI's last known primary funding round equity valuation was \$29bn. Clearly though, much has changed since this funding round. Nonetheless, in spite of annualised revenue reportedly heading towards \$2bn there is rising pressure to stay ahead of a field of well capitalised foundational model builders (discussed here)...whilst also providing liquidity to insiders. We have written about private AI companies and their interplay with (and funding from) incumbents in the "Land of the Giants" (here and here).

Tech stocks have averaged c60 months from \$10-\$100bn of market cap: If OpenAI were to raise before the end of 2024 and its valuation were to expand to \$100bn, we want to put into context how exceptional this would be relative to history. Technology stocks - many of which were helped by the timing of the dot com bubble - bridged from \$10bn to \$100bn of market cap in a c60 months window on average. By comparison, consumer companies took >200 months to achieve the same feat. Clearly, therefore, it would not be entirely unexpected for OpenAI and other AI enablers to be at the quicker end of the spectrum. We note though that we are comparing public liquid market valuation change versus private illiquid valuation change.

We have written at length about the challenges facing many unicorns which raised capital at the peak of the market exuberance in 2021 and have not raised (nor repriced their equity) since. However, at the upper end of the spectrum of valuations - and particularly as it relates to foundational AI model builders - things have proven to be different, more resilient. Valuation step-ups continue to defy the rest of the venture-backed industry. More broadly, the AI/ML vertical now represents >20% of total Venture/Growth funding as other verticals have either lost growth in a higher rate environment or lost key lead investors.

OpenAI has become the flagbearer to lead an otherwise soggy venture/growth market out of its malaise. But how quickly could OpenAI's valuation rise from \$10bn to \$100bn relative to companies that preceded it? In short, if the next funding round were to happen in or before December 2024 and the valuation were to breach the \$100bn mark, the company would have spanned this \$10-\$100bn valuation gap in fewer than 45 months. By contrast it took SpaceX c80 months to achieve the same feat and Bytedance 50 months.

It is also worth caveating OpenAI's more complex than usual non-profit structure, which transitioned to a "capped profit" structure in 2019, has played a meaningful factor on its valuation trajectory since its founding in late 2015. Moreover, without detail on a prospective tender offer for internal liquidity nor the complexity of any structure in future term sheets, it is impossible to give a likelihood of timing or valuation outcome at this point beyond what has been reported in unconfirmed press reports.

Perhaps surprisingly though, given the amount of newsflow and hype around the company and the AI theme in 2023, this in fact would be only a middle-of-the-pack performance relative to the leading technology companies of the past 30 years. If we take the Magnificent 7 as proxies, for example, OpenAI would achieve this \$10- \$100bn move slower than four of those stocks.

Many of the more household names that achieved this rally in valuation did so during the dot-com bubble. One could argue, therefore, that this is an unfair comparison to OpenAI today in a period of higher interest rates post-ZIRP. In more normalised market conditions, such as the 2011-2020 decade, the speed to achieving this \$10-\$100bn rally took 50% longer on average than the bubble years leading up to the dot com era. In any case, OpenAI would represent a remarkable scale up in valuation in almost all historical contexts.

Amara's Law & Valuation: Like other major technologies, AI likely follows Amara's Law, which states that the market overestimates the potential of a technology in the near term but underestimates it in the long term. This is a pattern which analysts demonstrated during the PC, Internet, Mobile and Cloud adoption eras ( Exhibit 6 ). The combination of accelerating AI technology development and the widespread productivity challenge underscores our view that AI is the most important investment theme of this decade. We view 2024 as an investment year that will be followed by measurable productivity improvements beginning in 2025. History suggests, those firms in the private markets which are enabling this wave of revenue opportunity and productivity enhancements for Adopters could see similar exponential increases in their primary and secondary valuations.

Where we could be wrong - Open vs Closed models & commoditisation: As a broad rule of thumb, closed (proprietary) models will generally be more expensive for Adopters to build on top of, but with generally higher quality/accuracy output than Open Source models, and vice versa. However, this dynamic is converging quickly ( Exhibit 7 ), bringing cheaper democratisation and quality to all Adopters in 2024. This Closed vs Open Source model quality and inference cost is one we will be tracking closely in 2024 to determine the speed and affordability with which Adopters can build on top of these models. It also holds the key to understanding how the spend by Adopters will flow back to Enablers. This is in fact something we are seeing in live time in the Venture capital-backed AI market as Adopter start-ups build on top of foundational models but are at risk of those very foundations changing to the detriment of their business model.

As greater competition from Open Source gathers in the foundational model layer, the valuations of those companies building the hitherto leading closed models could come under greater scrutiny by investors.

## The Fintech Radar: Digital euro - what's the impact to banks and payments?

• #1 Visa finalises \$1bn Pismo acquisition 7 months post deal announcement. On 16th Jan, Visa completed its acquisition of global cloud-native issuer processing and core banking platform Pismo, which James Faucette and team believe can improve Visa's optionality to gain exposure to consumer payments growth in international markets. Pismo is based in Brazil but has operations in LatAm, APAC and Europe, and while they have consistently said that they don't incorporate a meaningful contribution from card network share gain in international/emerging markets given that these governments may favor their own local payment schemes (e.g. UPI in India and Pix in Brazil), they think Pismo's global exposure could help address this by enabling Visa to be a back-end infrastructure provider for new and existing payment systems in the geographies it operates.

• #2 Trustly finalises €70m SlimPay acquisition. Last week (11th Jan) Swedish Open Banking solutions provider Trustly announced that it has completed the SlimPay acquisition, which was announced back in Aug-23. SlimPay is a Paris-based recurring payments platform that processes €5bn in payment volumes p.a. and 300k transactions per day. Trustly is aiming to create a pan-European recurring payments offering, by combining its A2A technology with SlimPay's SEPA Direct Debit capabilities. It also expects the deal will drive further roll-out of its payments engine Azura, which Trustly claims can boost conversion for merchants by up to 95%.

• #3 Cloud native core banking platform 10x Banking announces new £35m funding round. The funding round, announced 15th Jan, was led by existing investors BlackRock and JP Morgan. 10x Banking works to modernise core banking by supporting banks' replatform to the cloud, and it provided the technology which powered JP Morgan's launch of Chase in the UK. 10x recently expanded into Australia and New Zealand, and the company highlights in the press release that it has "more strategic expansions on the horizon".

• #4 Netherlands-based BNPL fintech Billink raises €30m. This week (18th Jan) Billink announced it has raised the funding from the German Varengold Bank, €4.5m of which is a direct commitment. It aims to deploy the capital to expand its offering in the Benelux region and to expand into the German market. Billink has 3m users and more than 3k online retailers offer its payment service.

## Real-time Valuation Monitor:

A simple top-down metric to look at is the Refinitiv Venture Capital Index, which provides an aggregated and rebased index of US venture capital-funded firms. The index:

• Leverages Refinitiv private company data of over 22,000 US firms;

• Estimates the value of each company over time by compiling known firm values, estimating missing values and interpolating values between events; and

• Aggregates month-to-month returns to compute a market cap-weighted index tracking the broad US venture capital universe since 1995.

While the log-scale sees a more modest pull-back in valuations of US VC-backed companies, the index as a whole is nonetheless nearly 18% down YoY and 40% off its highs.
