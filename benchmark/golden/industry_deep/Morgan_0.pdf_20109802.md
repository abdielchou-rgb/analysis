# Macro Balance Sheet: Analyzing Debt Dynamics

We see a swing in indebtedness levels as private sector leverage continues to pick up while public sector leverage declines. This should improve the productivity of incremental debt while also ensuring that overall debt rises at a moderate pace.

How do India's debt levels stack up? Post the pandemic-led rise in overall indebtedness to 166.1% of GDP in F2021, the overall debt ratio has declined to 154.5% in F2024, driven by moderation in public & corporate debt. However, overall debt still remains elevated vs pre-pandemic (2019) due to higher government debt followed by household debt, while corporate debt levels are lower.

How does the trend compare to other EMs? Post pandemic, EMs witnessed a rise in overall indebtedness levels, however the rise in India's debt has been modest. As the pandemic-related disruptions are behind us, economies have de-levered, however the pace remains heterogenous. For instance while India's debt levels are only modestly above pre-pandemic levels, debt in China is at peak levels.

Is private sector leverage picking up? With credit growth at 15%Y vs. the prepandemic trend of 11% there has been concern over the pace of increase in private sector leverage. Within the private sector, while household sector leverage has been picking up, corporates have been reducing debt driven by improving fundamentals. Having said that, corporate debt to GDP has risen a tad in F2024 from the cyclical trough in F2023, while household debt to GDP continued its upward move.

Is the pace of public sector deleveraging slow? India's public sector debt to GDP remains high both at an absolute level and relative to other countries, the trend being further accentuated post pandemic. However, the government has been proactive in reducing its debt, such that it is down 6%pts from the peak level in F21. As compared to other countries in the region as well, the pace of public sector debt consolidation in India remains faster.

Do we see financial stability risks due to increases in household or private sector leverage? The pickup in household leverage, especially unsecured loans, has led to rising concern over financial stability risks. However, the RBI has tightened macro prudential norms for unsecured lending and data on NPAs/stressed assets shows that the same is the least for personal loan segments. Further, other measures by the RBI such as tighter regulations for project financing and proposed draft regulations on higher LCR, indicate that the central bank remains vigilant.

What are the risks? Risks can emanate from a deterioration in macro stability which may warrant tighter financial conditions & higher real rates, deteriorating credit quality, and/or slower growth.

## Overall Debt – Evolution and the Way Forward

Debt trend has moderated post pandemic-induced spike: India's debt to GDP is tracking at 154.5% in F2024 vs. the peak of 166.1% of GDP in F2021 (pandemic) and remains higher compared to pre-pandemic trend of approx. 147% of GDP. While public and household leverage remain above pre-pandemic levels corporate debt has undershot pre-pandemic levels. Even though public sector leverage has picked up post pandemic, the same has been redirected towards capex and thus productivity of incremental debt was manageable.

Incremental debt picking up in private sector: On an incremental basis the pace of leverage has risen in the private sector with both corporate and household leverage rising in the last 12 months, however incremental debt continues to decline in the public sector.

Debt levels likely to see modest increase over the next 2 years: In our view overall debt will continue to rise driven by a pickup in corporate and household leverage, which will be partly offset by reductions in debt by the public sector. A key driver of this view is the expectation of a broad-based private capex recovery cycle which will reflect in higher leverage in corporate balance sheets over the next two years. Having said that, we expect capex to GDP to peak at a lower level than the previous cycle and similarly the pace of increase in leverage to be rather modest. Indeed, corporate debt to GDP rose from 36.5% in F2004 to 51.2% in F2008, delta of 14.7%pt, we expect the current cycle to see a more modest increase in leverage given the starting point of strong corporate balance sheets.

Debt burden expected to remain sustainable: We expect the debt burden to be sustainable as we expect real GDP growth to remain above real interest rates. Indeed, we expect that overall debt ratios will see modest expansion in the next two years mainly driven by an increase in private sector debt levels.

What are the key risks? Risks emanate from macro stability conditions worsening, which may warrant tightening monetary policy to restrictive levels. Apart from implications from higher real / nominal rates on indebtedness levels, tight financial conditions would also weigh on overall private sector risk sentiment. A slower growth trend may impact the dynamics between public and private sector leverage which will be more complex given that public debt levels are elevated compared to history.

## Corporate Sector Balance Sheet – Nearing an Inflection Point

Corporate sector leverage decelerated to a 16-year low of 49.1% in F2023, triggered by a cleanup of corporate balance sheets on the back of improving fundamentals. From F2024, however, we expect the leverage buildup to gain pace, driven by an improving trend in private capex which will push growth in corporate debt higher relative to nominal GDP.

# Household Sector Balance Sheet – Leverage Build Up Expected to Continue

The growth rate in household leverage <sup>1</sup> slowed to 16% between F2014 and F2023 from 20.7% between F2004 and F2013. In F2021, household debt leapfrogged to a then all-time high of 19.2% of GDP, an increment of 280bps from the previous year. We expect household leverage to remain on an accelerating trend, as household debt growth surpasses nominal GDP growth. The central bank remains vigilant on increased leverage by households and has tightened macro prudential norms in the last 12 months.

## Public Sector Balance Sheet – Slow Paced Consolidation

Government debt is tracking around 83% of GDP currently, after having moderated by about 6pp from its peak of 89.3% of GDP in F2021 due to the pandemic. It, however, continued to remain elevated relative to the pre-pandemic average when it remained rangebound between 67-71% of GDP from F2011-19.

The government remains steadfast on its fiscal glide path to align the fiscal deficit with the medium term target of 4.5% of GDP by F2026, which in turn is likely to aid lower government debt further. In addition, in the F2025 Budget speech, FM Nirmal Sitharaman mentioned, “From 2026-27 onwards, our endeavour will be to keep the fiscal deficit each year such that the Central government debt will be on a declining path as a percentage of GDP.”

## External Sector Balance Sheet – Maintained its Resilience

India's external debt reflects a moderating trend, as it softened to 18.6% of GDP in F2023, below its 10-year average of 21% of GDP. Sector-wise trends show that corporates constituted the highest share in overall external debt followed by financial institutions. We expect external debt to decelerate further, and a relatively strong external balance sheet position (as suggested by a robust trend in FX reserves and import cover) to help offset concerns of capital market risk aversion. Further, foreign inflows from India's inclusion in JPMorgan's GBI-EM index are likely to provide additional buffers to fortify the external landscape.

# Financial Sector Balance Sheet – NPAs at Multi-Year Lows

The share of credit in GDP has edged upwards to touch 53.8% in F2024, relative to a range-bound trend at 51-53% between F2011-20. Deposits, on the other hand, track at 69% of GDP in F2024, within their pre-pandemic range of 67-71%. The decline in nominal GDP in F2021 due to the pandemic led the share of both credit and deposit in GDP to accelerate to record highs, both of which have moderated from those levels.

Furthermore, the banking sector's impaired loans have decelerated to a 16-year low of 3.5% in F2024, in response to the improved banking sector performance following macro-prudential reforms undertaken to overhaul the banking and financial sector. Of late, the RBI has once again stepped up macro-prudential and regulatory reforms in the form of increased risk weights for unsecured lending, tighter regulations for project financing (guidelines on minimum exposure and standing asset provisioning under the construction phase) and proposed draft regulations on higher LCR (additional 5% runoff factor for retail deposits that are enabled with Internet and mobile banking).
