## Rate cuts are coming

The series of central bank meetings all had the same tone this week: rate cuts are coming. With the exception of the Bank of Japan, which hiked and finally moved away from NIRP after 17 years, the Federal Reserve, the Bank of England, and the Swiss National Bank all had a dovish tilt. In fact, the Swiss National Bank is now the first central bank to deliver a rate cut this cycle. With respect to the Fed, there was mixed news on the economic and interest rate projections, but the overall message from the meeting was dovish. The Treasury curve responded in kind. By week<sup>’</sup>s end, 2y yields are lower by 12bp and the broad curve steepened by 9bp week over week. OIS markets are now pricing in an 80% probability of a rate cut in June and 83bp of easing in 2024, versus 64% and 72bp, respectively, a week ago.

As expected, the FOMC left rates unchanged and made very few changes to the FOMC statement. However, the median <sup>’</sup>24 dot was unchanged from December, still showing three

25bp cuts this year, in spite of the upward revisions to core PCE and GDP. Meanwhile, the median <sup>’</sup>25 and <sup>’</sup>26 dots were revised up by 25bp in both years, while the economic forecasts were little changed for those time periods. Finally, the median longer-run dot edged up ever so slightly from 2.5% to 2.563%. Importantly, during the press conference, Chair Powell downplayed the upward revisions to growth and inflation forecasts and the implications for monetary policy. He also wasn<sup>’</sup>t too concerned about the recent upside surprises to inflation, describing the disinflationary process as bumpy no fewer than five times.

In his discussion on the balance sheet, Chair Powell noted that QT tapering will begin “fairly soon.<sup>”</sup> His remarks seem to suggest that they want to taper before dislocations emerge in the money markets as they “want to avoid any kind of turbulence<sup>”</sup> like that we saw in 2019, particularly as ON RRP reaches zero or close to zero. In essence, an earlier taper will allow QT to go farther.

All things considered, we continue to expect the first 25bp cut to take place in June, followed by a quarterly pace of cuts thereafter (see The Jays beat the Hawks, M. Feroli, 3/20/24). Given Chair Powell<sup>’</sup>s remarks on QT, we<sup>’</sup>ve also revised our QT taper call, and now expect the Fed to announce a reduction in the monthly caps at the May meeting for implementation in mid-May, instead of tapering to be announced in June and implemented in July. As Figure 1 shows, even with an earlier start to tapering, we still estimate ON RRP balances will hit \$300bn in December, a threshold we believe the Fed wants to maintain to effect continued smooth functioning in the money markets. If we are right, this means QT will continue through 2024, leaving \$3tn of reserves in the banking system by year-end (see Interest Rate Derivatives).

## Upcoming tax season could shed light on the path of QT

With the expectation that a QT taper begins in May, the upcoming April tax season bears watching as it could shed light on how far QT can run. Typically, the April tax season results in a concomitant drop in RRP and reserve balances as individuals and corporations draw down on their MMFs and bank deposits to fund their tax payments. In the decade leading up to and through 2021, the magnitude of these draw-downs around April 15 were on average around \$30bn and \$150bn for MMFs and reserves, respectively. However, in 2022 outflows were particularly sharp, as MMF AUMs and reserve balances declined by \$145bn and \$465bn, respectively (Figure 2Outflows arndhe Apix y c m b 203 MF… and Figure 3…and foresv, whick bgt p ul y). Recall the year 2022 reflected meaningful capital gains taxes for the prior year. In contrast, 2023 saw much more muted outflows as negative returns dominated nearly every part of the financial markets the year prior.

Assuming 2022 and 2023 to be the worst- and best-case scenario, respectively, of what taxrelated outflows could look like this year, this year<sup>’</sup>s drawdown could reasonably fall somewhere in between \$40-140bn for MMFs and \$215-465bn for reserves. There is risk that reserve balances bear a greater proportion of the tax-related outflows this year considering the attractive yields MMFs offer in the current interest rate environment relative to deposits.

To that end, while reserves are still more than ample, registering \$3.49tn as of 3/20, it<sup>’</sup>s plausible reserves could fall to between \$3.0tn-\$3.275tn in the week following April 15, right within the range classified as the lowest comfortable level by primary dealers (Figure 4It’s plauibe rvcod fwn \$3.0-275 hkg A1,    m y ). While we don<sup>’</sup>t necessarily think this will result in a funding crisis—given what we think will be a continued positive balance at the ON RRP in addition to the availability of the SRF—it could still be telling in terms of how far QT can continue to run as RRP reaches near-zero.

## FRN spreads finally saw some widening

Credit FRNs finally saw some spread widening in the money markets this month, in a reversal from the aggressive tightening we<sup>’</sup>ve witnessed throughout most of 1Q24. On average, 6m and 12m spreads widened by about 1bp since last Friday, and about 1-2bp month-to-date (Figure 5On averg,6m d12sp hwi byout cl F - ). This marginal increase in spread widening, which is not significant on its own but directionally different from what we have seen over the past couple of months, could be a reflection of some investors positioning themselves conservatively heading into quarterend and the individual April tax deadline. On the margin, with the new MMF reform liquidity requirements and liquidity fee framework going into effect in April and October, respectively, there is likely some hesitation among prime institutional MMFs to extend in fear of potential outflows. Instead, they are building their liquidity buffers. Indeed, of the remaining prime institutional MMFs that have not announced their conversion to a government fund, their WLAs increased by 1.1%-pt week-to-date and 0.7%-pt month-to-date (Figure 6Of thermaing psuol MFv cd  , WLA’ by1.%- wk 07  ).

On the topic of MMF reforms, this past week, Vanguard filed with the SEC to convert their internal prime institutional MMF to a government institutional MMF (see here). This marks the second 2a-7 institutional prime fund (first was Capital Group) that has decided to convert from prime to government after the final MMF rules were adopted in July 2023. This is also the second prime fund that Vanguard has decided to convert to government (they converted their prime retail fund to a government retail fund in 2020).

Vanguard<sup>’</sup>s prime institutional fund currently has close to \$80bn in assets, representing about 12% of the prime institutional market. Combining that with Capital Group<sup>’</sup>s prime AUM, once these two funds convert, prime institutional balances will drop by approximately \$210bn, leaving about \$445bn of balances sitting in remaining prime institutional funds.

While this might seem like a significant decline in terms of the the availability of credit, it<sup>’</sup>s worth noting that Vanguard<sup>’</sup>s prime fund, similar to Capital Group<sup>’</sup>s prime fund, has largely operated like a government fund and hence has little credit exposure currently. Indeed, in aggregate, Vanguard<sup>’</sup>s and Capital Group<sup>’</sup>s prime funds held only about \$40bn (or 19% of their portfolios) combined in credit exposure, concentrated across CP, CD, TD and ABCP as of February month-end (Figure 7In agret,Vud’s Cpil Gom fh yb \$40( 19% ) c x  PD TAB F-). As such, we do not think their conversion will have an outsized impact on credit spreads.

As for the remaining prime institutional funds, credit exposure consisted of nearly \$240bn (or 54%) of their holdings as of February-end. While it remains to be seen whether more conversions are on the way, even in a worst-case scenario where the remaining funds convert, we suspect other liquidity investors can easily absorb the capacity left behind by institutional prime funds, limiting any impact on spreads over the longer term.

Looking ahead, investor positioning could continue to push spreads wider heading into quarter-end and the April tax day deadline. But once the calendar moves past these events, absent some exogenous shock, spreads should tighten again in 2Q. On the margin, MMF reforms might add to the spread widening pressure, but as noted above, we do not expect this to result in any significant spread widening event.
