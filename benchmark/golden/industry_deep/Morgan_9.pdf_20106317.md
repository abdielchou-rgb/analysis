## United States

FOMC signals rate cuts are coming, but March meeting likely is too early

Inflation developments are being viewed favorably by the Fed

• Job growth momentum has stayed strong along with other signs of solid job market

• Next week: watch Fedspeak and CPI revisions

There was a lot of economic news this past week. The January FOMC meeting left us confident that the Committee will not be ready to cut rates as early as the March meeting but that rate cuts are coming at some point later this year, and potentially soon. Chair Powell seemed happy with the inflation news from recent quarters, with the main PCE price indexes coming in close to the FOMC<sup>’</sup>s 2% target on a quarter-on-quarter sequential basis. And he didn<sup>’</sup>t seem concerned by the gap between CPI and PCE inflation or the composition underlying the trend-like inflation, suggesting that more of the same in the coming months could be enough to give the FOMC enough confidence to start cutting rates. We continue to look for the FOMC to start lowering rates at the June meeting, but a cut at the May meeting is also a clear possibility.

Importantly, Chair Powell seems to believe that inflation can be close to the FOMC<sup>’</sup>s target even if the labor market stays strong. And the January employment report along with some other news from the past week suggested that conditions remained favorable in the job market pretty recently. Nonfarm employment surged by 353,000 in January while the unemployment rate stayed at 3.7% and average hourly earnings increased 4.5%oya. Monthly figures can be noisy, and we think seasonal adjustment issues may have played a role in supporting the January jobs gain and also that weather may have been a complicating factor for some details. But overall the signs are clear that job growth momentum has remained solid lately while the unemployment rate has stayed low (Figure 1).

Things should quiet down a lot next week according to the scheduled data flow. We will get some news on the CPI data on February 9 but we don<sup>’</sup>t expect major changes coming from either updated seasonal factors or new relative importance weights. The Fed<sup>’</sup>s senior loan officer survey also could be interesting to see given the easing in financial conditions lately. We will get some Fedspeak next week, which could allow policymakers to share individual views beyond the official FOMC communications from the January meeting. But we think the data (and the inflation data in particular) may speak louder than words in the coming weeks in terms of signaling when the Fed will start cutting rates.

## March unlikely

The January FOMC statement and related communication from Chair Powell pushed back against expectations for a near-term rate cut. However, both the statement and the chair opened the door for easing after the next FOMC meeting in mid-March. The statement indicated that “The Committee does not expect it will be appropriate to reduce the target range until it has gained greater confidence that inflation is moving sustainably toward 2 percent.<sup>”</sup> That could mean a lot of things, but Chair Powell narrowed it down a little when he said: “I don<sup>’</sup>t think it<sup>’</sup>s likely that the Committee will reach [that] level of confidence by the time of the March meeting.<sup>”</sup>

While pushing back against March, the communications were not uniformly hawkish, as easier policy is clearly on the Committee<sup>’</sup>s mind. The statement noted that risks to growth and inflation were getting into “better balance<sup>”</sup>—a move to a neutral bias is presumably a precursor to moving to an easing bias. And Chair Powell readily confirmed that almost everyone on the Committee thinks rates should come down this year. One of the more dovish aspects of Powell<sup>’</sup>s remarks was the asymmetry on employment: strong employment gains won<sup>’</sup>t necessarily forestall rate cuts, but weak employment gains would “absolutely<sup>”</sup> hasten rate cuts. We are sticking with our call for a first cut in June. But after Powell<sup>’</sup>s remarks it<sup>’</sup>s not hard to see a configuration of employment and inflation data that gets the Committee cutting by May.

In Powell<sup>’</sup>s prepared remarks, he added the word “notably<sup>”</sup> in describing how much inflation had come down. While the prepared remarks sounded more upbeat about inflation and the risk balance, they only predicted rates would be cut “at some point this year.<sup>”</sup> In much of the Q&A the mood was upbeat, as he said: “Let<sup>’</sup>s be honest, this is a good economy. 99 This mood was underscored by Powell<sup>’</sup>s clarification that the Committee has confidence that inflation will come down in the context of a healthy labor market, but that it was seeking “greater confidence.<sup>”</sup> Similarly, he has been seeing good data, he just wants to see more good data.

On inflation, Powell viewed the very weak patch of goods inflation fading eventually, though he sounded quite confident that this will be offset by rental inflation coming off. He wasn<sup>’</sup>t going for the argument that neutral may have shifted up materially such that policy may not be so restrictive after all. He saw the effects of restrictive policy in interest-sensitive sectors. Moreover, he saw some of the recent strength of the economy as supply-side healing “that won<sup>’</sup>t go on forever.<sup>”</sup> The comments regarding March were in response to a question about rate cuts being “around the corner.<sup>”</sup> Interpreting that as March, he said “it is probably not the most likely case, or what we would call the base case.<sup>”</sup>

## Labor market still strong

Nonfarm employment increased 353,000 last month (Figure 1), easily beating expectations. This was the fourth consecutive January in which employment growth came in about double what consensus was looking for, as non-seasonally adjusted job growth of -2,635,000 last month was not as bad as the seasonal adjustment was expecting. Job growth in the prior two months was also revised up by 126,000, bucking the recent trend of downward revisions. There was a big upside surprise in average hourly earnings, up 0.6% last month (Figure 2), and a big downside surprise in the average workweek, down 0.2 hour to 34.1 hours. These two surprises may be linked, as adverse weather may have depressed the workweek for some employees, which in turn may have boosted average hourly earnings for salaried workers. The household survey, in contrast, sent a simpler message of a solid, steady labor market. After adjusting for the annual change in population estimates, the household measure of employment increased 239,000 and unemployment declined 116,000, though with rounding the unemployment rate was unchanged at a low 3.7% (Figure 1).

While the latest employment report contained more than the usual amount of noise, the overall picture looks to be one of a still quite strong labor market, and an economy starting 2024 with plenty of forward momentum. This strength was echoed in other reports from the past week, with jobless claims filings remaining low and job openings staying above pre-pandemic norms. Growth in the employment cost index also remained solid through the end of last year, albeit with some moderation in the trend (Figure 2).

## Other news solid on net

Relative to the events discussed above, the other economic reports from the past week were second- (or third-) tier. The national ISM and PMI surveys improved in January, and while the latest prints were still on the soft side, they sent a much more favorable signal than some of the regional manufacturing surveys that had previously shown surprisingly downbeat data for the month. Consumer sentiment also appears to have firmed noticeably lately, with rising stock prices and moderating inflation likely lifting moods.

Housing vacancies remained low in the fourth quarter of last year, and we think limits in available inventory continue to generate a need for homebuilding. In partially related news, the December construction report beat expectations in terms of both the latest monthly change and upward revisions to earlier figures, showing strong growth on net through the end of last year. The news from the construction report pushed our tracking of 4Q GDP growth up from 3.3% to 3.5% saar. We don<sup>’</sup>t have much hard data on economic activity in 1Q at this point, but the pace of light vehicle sales dropped off in January (-7% to 15.0mn saar), pointing to some pullback in consumer spending early this year (Figure 3). We continue to look for slowing in GDP growth in 1Q, with real GDP expected to be up 1.75% saar.

Nonfarm employment increased 353,000 last month, easily beating expectations (JPM: 225,000, Consensus: 180,000). This was the fourth consecutive January in which employment growth came in about double what consensus was looking for, as non-seasonally adjusted job growth of -2,635,000 last month was not as bad as the seasonal adjustment was expecting. Job growth in the prior two months was also revised up by 126,000, bucking the recent trend of downward revisions. There was a big upside surprise in average hourly earnings, up 0.6% last month, and a big downside surprise in the average workweek, down 0.2 hour to 34.1 hours. As discussed below, these two surprises may be linked. The household survey, in contrast, sent a simpler message of a solid, steady labor market. After adjusting for the annual change in population estimates, the household measure of employment increased 239,000 and unemployment declined 116,000, though before rounding the unemployment rate was unchanged at a low 3.7%. While this report contained more than the usual amount of noise, the overall picture looks to be one of a still quite strong labor market, and an economy starting 2024 with plenty of forward momentum. The report also backed up Powell<sup>’</sup>s hunch that the first cut won<sup>’</sup>t come as soon as March, and we continue to look for rates to be first lowered at the June FOMC meeting.

In the establishment survey, government employment increased another 36,000 last month. The industrial breadth of private sector job growth improved last month, as the diffusion index rose to 65.6%. Some industries with notable gains in employment growth last month include health care and social assistance (100,000), professional and business services (74,000), and retail trade (45,000). The 0.55% gain in average hourly earnings was the largest since early <sup>’</sup>22 and takes the year-ago figure up to 4.5%. Meanwhile the 0.2 hour decline in the average workweek was also the largest in two years, and the level of 34.1 hours was the lowest outside of recessions and the period immediately after recessions. In months with adverse weather, the workweek and average hourly earnings often move in opposite directions, as salaried workers may have hours reduced, which arithmetically boosts average hourly earnings. The larger-than-usual number of people not at work due to bad weather in the household survey (588,000) suggests that adverse weather did have a meaningful labor market impact last month. The weather effect is not as large in total labor pay, which increased a steadier 0.2% and has been growing at around a 5% rate over the last year and last three months, enough to support continued good nominal spending growth.

Turning to the household survey, while the unrounded unemployment rate was unchanged, to higher precision it moved down from 3.743% in December to 3.661% last month. The participation rate was unchanged at 62.5% last month. Primeage participation ticked up to 83.3% last month but remains about two ticks below the recent high from the middle of last year. The employment-to-population ratio ticked up to 60.2%, taking back part of the three-tick decline that occurred in December. The annual updating of population estimates didn<sup>’</sup>t affect the unemployment rate, participation rate, or employment-to-population ratio, but it did lower the estimated civilian population by 625,000 and the labor force by 299,000. Mirroring the quits number in the JOLTS report, the number of job leavers fell to 12.8% of all unemployed persons. The U-6 measure of underemployment ticked up to a still-low 7.2%, due to an increase in the number of people working part-time who want full-time jobs.
