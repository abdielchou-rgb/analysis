# APAC Economic Perspectives ASEAN: Gains from trade, losses from trade wars

## Risks of trade barriers in ASEAN, the why and what next

Comments by some officials in ASEAN reveal an increasing worry that China suppliers may displace domestic producers as they look to other export markets. For instance, Indonesia's trade minister recently announced that the country is looking to introduce safeguard duties on textiles and other consumer goods to mitigate the effects of the US-China trade war. In this note, we show Chinese imports have not gained share in ASEAN-6 to the same extent, with Malaysia and Singapore less affected than Indonesia and Thailand. Gains are largely in machinery and electrical machinery, suggesting a reconfiguration of supply chains rather than a concerted effort to export excess capacity. Rising machinery imports from China is directly correlated with increased FDI from China, especially for Indonesia. China's import share gains also include consumer goods such as electric appliances and vehicles in Thailand, and Indonesia textiles, but the overall magnitude is fairly modest. However, the potential impact on employment, and in some cases a higher degree of import substitutability vs. domestic producers, could have triggered concerns by more than what China's import share gains should imply.

## US-China tariffs are bad for growth, due to negative spillovers globally

An across-the-board import tariff of 60% could have a significant negative hit on China's economy. The historical experience of 2018-19 suggests escalating trade tensions sow the seeds for much weaker growth globally, given spillovers to financial markets, consumer and business confidence. At that time, Asia's open economies were more significantly affected by the hit to global demand, and within this region Singapore is likely to be the most vulnerable. Nonetheless, some substitution effects could partly offset the income effect; these are more relevant for Vietnam and Thailand.

## Supply chain shifts benefitted the region, and will continue to do so

Most economies in Asia have gained share in the US import market, but few countries (except Vietnam) had improved goods trade balances vis-à-vis 2017. That alone isn't proof that supply chain shifts did not or will not benefit the rest of the region, as comparisons need to account for the energy drag and tech cycle. Supply chain shifts are likely improving the export mix. For instance, a rapid increase in Thailand's electronic components exports, from a low base, suggests production is becoming more diversified and less reliant on disk drives. Indonesia's gains in nickel-related exports continue to buffer the cyclical slowdown in commodity exports. Domestic value added embedded in exports has increased or at least remained stable, which contradicts the wholesale triangulation thesis. The rationale and intentions for supply chain relocation remain intact as UBS Evidence Lab's China CFO survey showed. The effects of supply chain shifts are still ongoing, we think the best is yet to come.

## Implications on sectors, stocks, and overall ASEAN outlook

We believe ASEAN is still more likely to welcome than resist trade integration on the whole. We previously highlighted how some sectors benefit from deepening trade and FDI patterns (ports, industrial estates, Indonesia's nickel industry, Malaysia's electronics sector) while others such as retail that import a lot of its inventory could suffer if there is an escalation of import tariffs such as that being contemplated by Indonesia. Beneficiaries of trade integration include: Ports (Westports, ICTSI); Industrial estates (WHA); Nickel (Merdeka Battery Materials); Electronics (Vitrox), Banks (Vietcombank). If import tariffs rise to the extent being contemplated by Indonesia (which we think unlikely), Map Aktif and Mitra Adiperkasa could be casualties.

## The context behind import competition fears

## Some country officials sounded concerns over import competition from China

Recently, Indonesia's trade minister announced that the country is looking to introduce safeguard duties of 100-200% on imported beauty care, footwear, apparels, textile products, and ceramics imported from China, potentially affecting USD5bn of imports. The trade minister said this was to mitigate the effects of the US-China trade war, citing the fact that numerous textile companies have closed, resulting in mass layoffs. The Bank of Thailand has previously noted that excess capacity in China had led to an influx of goods into global markets. Thailand's manufacturers have repeated concerns over an influx of low-cost steel imports from China. Yet, Thailand's concerns are not new— safeguard duties against some Chinese steel producers have been in place since 2017.

## China's gains were largely in machinery (capital goods) and electrical machinery

Digging deeper into the sectors, we find that most gains are in electronics and machinery (i.e. capital, intermediate goods), and that this is because China had gained more share within these sectors rather than because ASEAN is importing a lot more machinery per se. We believe this suggests a reconfiguration of supply chains, and highlights the fact that China is embedded in regional production networks. That China's market share gains are concentrated in machinery & parts and electrical machinery, and not across all product types, is probably more associated with increased FDI from Chinese companies (especially in the case of Indonesia), rather than a wholesale effort to export China's excess capacity to the region.

## But this is different from outright triangulation—FDI likely played a role

However, whilst some triangulation could be taking place, shifts in actual production patterns are real in that the assembly or production is still taking place in ASEAN, albeit with imported capital and intermediate goods alongside some domestic value added. We believe this is not just pure triangulation effects, in which goods traverse country borders in order to evade tariffs without much domestic value added. As shown in next section "Supply chain shifts are still ongoing", data from the OECD shows there has been a general increase in the domestic value added content of exports to the US since 2017 for most of ASEAN-6.

Rising machinery imports from China likely went hand-in-hand with increased FDI from China to ASEAN. This was most evident in Indonesia's case. Increased imports from China in just four categories, "machinery that is handling minerals" (HS 843149), "mechanical shovels (HS 8429)", "machinery handling treating materials by a change in temperature (HS 8419)", and "goods transport vehicles (HS 870410)", have increased by about US\$2-3bn per year since 2019, positively correlating to the increase in direct investments by Chinese nickel processing companies into Indonesia during that time. Notably, in Indonesia's case, the 'import shift' effect had also played a role for China's rising share of machinery & parts imports. This means that there was capital deepening on the whole for the economy.

## Why the fears about competition with Chinese imports?

The potential impact on employment, and in some cases a higher degree of import substitutability vs. domestic producers, could have triggered domestic concerns by more than what China's import share gains should imply.

Indonesia: In contrast to fears that China had gained a disproportionately large market share in Indonesia's textiles and apparels, it seems that the incremental change was quite modest, of about 8% points (39% to 47%) or about US\$0.7bn (less than 0.3% of total imports), and offset by the import shift effect. Indonesia's imports of textiles and apparels from China comprise only USD5bn, which is about 2% of total imports. However, the textile industry still relies on the local market, with exports accounting for only 30% of sales, while 70% is still held by the local market. Overall, the domestic textile industry in Indonesia is a US\$39bn industry, based on our estimates (3% of GDP). It also comprises about 27% of manufacturing employment, according to a 2022 study by ILO. Given total manufacturing employment of around 19 million, that could be as much as 5 million workers at risk from import competition.

Thailand: Imports from China have made larger inroads in Thailand's iron, steel, and other base metals imports. China's increased share in Thailand's "base metals" imports of about 9ppts since 2017 was quite meaningful, contributing about 1% point to the overall rise in China's import share. There was also an increase in imports of some consumer goods, such as electrical household appliances, but not clothing & footwear.

For Thailand, the impact of China export diversion could seem to be more substitutionary (than complementary) for domestic producers. That would be because China's export similarity is closest to that of Thailand within the region, this suggests that Thailand's manufacturing structure is similar to China's. A growing concern would be the auto sector. Auto sales have been on the decline mostly due to tight credit standards, and a shift in demand towards EVs partly due to incentives. Even though Chinese EV makers have invested in building production facilities in Thailand, providing a significant offset to the fears about a hollowing out of Thailand's auto industry, underlying growth concerns about Thailand could still limit the amount of overall FDI into Thailand by auto manufacturers from other countries. If more auto factories close down with detrimental employment effects, the cost-benefit analysis of granting EV incentives could be called into question. At this juncture though, we still see more factory openings (which are rising) than factory cessations (which are also rising, but at a slower pace).

## The possible pushback from increasing protectionist policies

The inclination to increase protectionist policies would likely be higher in strategic sectors in which the market size of domestic production is much larger than the market supplied by imports, and where imports are seen as substitutionary rather than complementary to domestic production. Countries at lower GDP per capita levels might be more susceptible to price competition at the lower end in their domestic markets, as consumers could be more price sensitive. However, some protectionist measures that might be deemed overly prohibitive, such as those which Indonesia is thinking of imposing (100-200% safeguard tariff), could be detrimental to growth and inflation. They might also encourage distortions, such as the deflection of trade to inefficient producers and illegal imports. Uncertainty on tariffs could also dampen plans for FDI into the country. A more effective approach could be to focus on curbing illegal imports.

Even if all of the value added from China's loss of market share to the US after the 2018- 19 trade tariff escalations were transplanted to other countries, this might not mean an improvement in goods balances, if there are offsetting factors dragging goods balances down. ## Negative impact of trade wars on growth

## That's what happened in 2018-19

Historically, growth in Asia ex China slowed in 2018-19, as the trade war’s impact on business uncertainty and sentiment led to a significant global demand slowdown. Export growth slowed across all of Asia then, not just China. Even if Asia ex China is spared from a 10% US import tariff, we do expect significant spillover from global trade and confidence linkages from escalating US-China trade tensions. The non-linear effects of a prohibitively high tariff would likely result in much larger volume effects. An immediate, across-the-board import tariff of 60% could have a significantly negative hit on China's economy, of about 250bps over a 12-month period over 2025E and 2026E.

## Open economies likely to feel the largest blowback

Asian countries more affected by the slowdown in global growth back in 2018-19 were the open economies of Singapore, Korea, Malaysia, Thailand. We believe that a similar relative impact could also take place in the event of a 2025 all-out trade war; within ASEAN, that would be Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, and Vietnam. We believe the impact could spillover beyond China's goods import demand, including weaker confidence effects, a global trade and investment downturn, and declining tourist arrivals from China. Apart from the impact on China, the equilibrium impact on overall global growth and inflation, the potential for retaliatory tariffs, and policy responses by the major economies, would be key parameters to consider as well.

## Vietnam still stands to be the biggest winner from any trade diversion

As we've highlighted in our recent China trade note, within this region, Vietnam still stands to be the biggest winner from any potential trade diversion gains from further escalation of trade tariffs. Based on a one-dimensional export similarity index, which measures the extent to which the export profiles of two economies (China vs. other Asia) overlap, implying they are closer competitors in the US import market, Vietnam remains the closest in terms of its profile to China. Thailand, Taiwan, Mexico, rank next in terms of export similarity. Even so, the index does not consider size or the level of exports. China's market share in the US import market remains more than 3x times that of Vietnam, and 6x of Thailand.

It is difficult to completely substitute away from China. Another way of highlighting the substitution challenge is as follows. By product category, there are 224/1227 (or 18%) of HS4 categories in which China's market share in the US is dominant (>30%). A list of these imports is given below. US imports of these categories of goods from China amounted to USD265bn in 2023, or slightly more than half of China's total exports to the US. For these categories, total imports from Mexico totalled - USD71bn, followed by Vietnam - USD53bn, with the rest of the world trailing far behind. Within ASEAN, Thailand is a far third (USD16bn). Despite the difficulties of ramping up production quickly, there could be some trade diversion gains for ASEAN, particularly for Vietnam, and Thailand to a lesser degree.
