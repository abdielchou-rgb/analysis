# 2024 Outlook: A year of stability?

Thematics - Is bad news good news in a way? There hasn't been much to cheer for China at the start of 2024 - either on data front or in terms of market price action. Is bad news good news in a way though? Over the past few days, the government has been ramping up its announcement of measures both on the fiscal and property fronts to support growth. To be sure, we still need to see (a) speedier and more coordinated implementation; and (b) sizing up in the support measures (particularly in the property sector). But the willingness of the authorities to do more in stabilizing growth, versus the prevailing level of bearishness on the China narrative, makes the risk-reward relatively more attractive to go long Chinese assets.

Economics - Waiting for policy tail wind. China's trend growth has shifted downward in recent years. We believe that potential growth is now in the 4.5-5% range. This means that the economy, growing by 4.1% on average in 2022-23, is operating below potential. However, the economy lacks the endogenous momentum to rebound. As such, only the government can jump-start the economy through forceful and coordinated policy responses. A 1.5% - 2% of GDP expansion in fiscal spending, combined with further PBoC rate cuts, should be sufficient to lift GDP growth to 4.7% and inflation to 0.9% in 2024. In addition, government-led urban redevelopment and social housing projects should finally see the property sector not being a drag on growth in 2024.

Macro Strategy - A harder pivot to come? The markets have turned increasingly bearish on China assets, setting a relatively low hurdle for China growth to surprise to the upside. The key to the balance of risks on China growth, we believe, lies with whether the authorities there are willing to pivot harder on their policy choices. Indeed, that does seem to be the direction of travel. If so, the risk to USD/RMB is on the downside.

Equity Strategy - Will HK equity market recover? After four down years in a row, the HK Indices sit in a very small pocket of market history. In the rare cases where we can find such ongoing drops, they have tended to follow major bull market - of a type HK hasn't seen in close to 20 years. Valuations are extremely low, and prospective catalysts include a plateauing in the bad- news cycle, liquidity shifts from bank accounts as rates ease (and even a potential skew from bonds to equities at HK's Exchange Fund) , rising earnings, and buybacks. While the CSI300 also appears oversold, it lacks the investor exhaustion and possible depth of value of HK.

## Thematics

## Is bad news good news in a way?

2024 hasn't started on a particularly good note for Chinese assets. The HSI China enterprise and the CSI 300 have underperformed regional peers. Although RMB weakness has been less pronounced vs rest of Asia (partly due to ongoing, strongerthan-expected fix), USD/CNH has again breached the 7.20 level.

Data hasn't been very encouraging either, particularly in the property sector. According to the NBS, property sales values declined -17% YoY in December (much more than in November, when property sales contracted by -9% YoY). In volume terms, property sales also contracted by 13% YoY (worse than November's -10% YoY contraction). For full-year 2023, property sales values and volumes declined by -9% and - 3% YoY, respectively, highlighting the weakness in the sector. Privately owned property companies continue to find it difficult to get the necessary financing in both the onshore and offshore markets.

Is bad news good news in a way? Over the past few days, there have been more substantial measures announced by the authorities in an attempt to stabilize the situation.

n Accelerating the Urban Village Redevelopment Plans. Over the past few weeks, we have seen a number of megacities unveil plans to support the property sector. For example, Guangzhou has become the first Tier 1 city to launch a housing voucher scheme, which compensates for families affected by urban renovation projects in an effort to revive the sluggish housing market. The scheme is also different from those of the past. Under previous schemes, residents affected by urban renovation projects have normally been relocated to reconstructed projects, designated by the authorities. This time around, the vouchers can be used to purchase property listed on the city's government-run housing website. The vouchers have a maximum validity of 18 months – although the local government is biased toward shortening the validity in order to encourage home purchasing. To incentivise participation, families affected by urban renovation projects can freely choose between resettlement, buying new property on the government-approved website, or obtaining monetary compensation.

Another example is Shenzhen, which has also announced plans for its urban renovation programme. The plan breaks the project up into three main categories of transformation: demolition and new construction, renovation and improvement, and a mixture of the two. In other words, urban villages that meet the conditions will be demolished and rebuilt; those that do not meet the conditions will be renovated and upgraded; and those in between will be demolished and renovated. According to the draft, the total construction area involved in the renovation is about 2.2mn square meters, accounting for more than 40% of the city's construction area, and the residential population accounts for about 60% of the city's population.

This includes assessing the construction status of projects in their regions and the financial soundness of the developers involved in the projects, as well as drawing up a list of those that are eligible for financial support. On the back of the notice, earlier this week, Ping An Bank published a list of 41 developers eligible for funding support. The bank told its departments and branches that they should extend full lending support to developers on the list of those that are operating normally, and refrain from cutting or suspending credit lines. Although the list that Bloomberg reported only consists of: (1) SOEs and quasi-SOEs and (2) non-distressed POEs (Hong Kong developers and distressed companies are excluded from the list), the list limits the risk of banks pulling credit from property developers. In addition, we could see more banks releasing their whitelists, which should help to ensure that financing conditions do not deteriorate further.

Resumption of the PSL programme. Early this month, the People's Bank of China (PBoC) released its December lending facilities data. For the first time since November 2022, China has again started to deploy funding via its Pledged Supplementary Lending (PSL) programme, with the facility providing RMB350bn of funding to policy banks. Moving forward in 2024, the PSL programme is expected to continue, with a total potential size of RMB1tn.

This is important, given that the PSL programme has been used twice over the years in order to support growth. It was used for the first time in 2014-18 in order to support the property sector via shantytown redevelopment. The programme was used a second time in September 2022, when the authorities injected a net total of RMB630bn over the course of three months in order to support infrastructure spending. Caixin reported that the PSL programme could be used again to support the property sector via urban village redevelopment and affordable housing programmes in order to stabilise the sector. If this should come to pass, it would help to stabilise sentiment on surrounding property.

Apart from the property measures, the government has also flagged the likelihood of a further increase in fiscal support. Finance Minister Lan Fo’an recently has reiterated the need to ramp up fiscal spending in order to help stimulate domestic demand. He noted that the government would maintain appropriate fiscal expenditure intensity, further optimise tax- and fee-reduction policies, and continue to utilise special bonds to promote investment.

Most notably, the government is planning to issue another CNY1tr (0.8% of GDP) of ultra-long maturity special sovereign bonds to fund public investment projects according to a recent Bloomberg report. The news does not come as a surprise, in fact it coincides with an earlier report by Reuters in late-December that the government is considering CNY1tr of special sovereign bond issuance. Chinese local news also reported that local governments are preparing project proposals in the areas of food, energy, supply chains and urbanization, whose funding will come from ultra-long special sovereign bonds. It is likely that these bonds would have a maturity of \~30-50 years, so as to: (a) lower funding costs, (b) improve the government's debt maturity structure without increasing near-term debt repayment pressure, and (c) manage off the budget (like those issued in 2020).

What does this imply for China's fiscal stance? We think it will be 1.5% to 2% of GDP more expansionary in 2024. This fiscal stimulus in 2024 will be comprised of: (1) the budget revision in October 2023, when the government issued CNY 1tr of additional CGB, and the bulk of them will likely be spent in 2024; (2) another CNY 1tr of ultra-long sovereign bonds; and (3) the PBOC's PSL facility, which will likely continue be tapped in 2024. Although PSL is not part of China's official fiscal accounts, its economic impact is similar to fiscal spending.

China's fiscal spending growth should see a notable jump in 2024. By our calculations, by implementing the measures listed above, total fiscal spending growth will surge to 8% in 2024, from average annual growth of 0.2% in the past 3 years. This will have significant implications for growth. We thus expect China's economy will likely strengthen in 2024 - sequential growth will likely rebound to 1.3% QoQ in Q1 and Q2 2024 from 1% in the most recent quarter.

Although much as the above measures are a good starting point to stabilise growth, we believe that the government still needs to: (a) speed up the introduction/ implementation of its macro policies and (b) size up the support measures (particularly in the property sector) given how poor household and business confidences are.

## Waiting for policy tailwind

China's trend growth has shifted downward in recent years. We believe that potential growth is now in the 4.5-5% range. This means that the economy, growing by 4.1% on average in 2022-23, is operating below potential. However, the economy lacks the endogenous momentum to rebound from a prolonged property sector downturn and a sharp drop in inflation.

Only the government can jump-start the economy through forceful and coordinated policy responses. The most important policy variable is its 2024 growth target, which we believe will be set at "above 4.5%". A 1.5% - 2% of GDP expansion in fiscal spending, combined with further PBoC rate cuts, should be sufficient to lift GDP growth to 4.7% and inflation to 0.9% in 2024.

The property sector may finally stop being a drag on growth in 2024. Its share in GDP will likely stabilize, thanks to government-led urban redevelopment and social housing projects. Recovery in the private housing market could still take time.

The renminbi is expected to stabilize in H1 and strengthen in H2 against the USD, as trade flows remain favorable and capital outflow pressures will likely ease in the scenario of a mild US recession followed by Fed rate cuts.

## China's growth is below potential

China's economic outlook remains challenging for 2024. Economic recovery still lacks momentum one year after its reopening after the pandemic. Growth averaged 4.5% since 2020 and only 4.1% in the past two years, significantly lower than the 6% or above growth in 2019 and before. It is now commonly believed that China's trend growth has permanently shifted downward in the past few years, although people's views vary on what should be the "new normal" for China's growth.

One thing is clear: China's economy is currently operating below potential. A sizable negative output gap emerged in 2022 and persisted in 2023, evident from the low capacity utilization rates, labor market slack, weak credit demand, and, most importantly, negative consumer and producer price inflation. Therefore, China's potential growth should be higher than the 4.1% average growth in 2022-23. It should be in the 4.5-5% range, in our view. Growth could be even higher for a short period if the economy can enter a cyclical rebound to close the negative output gap.

That cyclical rebound still has not happened yet, primarily because after a prolonged property sector downturn and a sharp drop in inflation, a vicious loop has emerged among consumer spending, business profits, and forward expectations for income and prices. A positive shock is required to break the loop. Unfortunately, both external demand and public spending have contracted for the most part of 2023, reinforcing rather than breaking this vicious circle.

## Government's growth target would be key

The most important policy variable is the government's 2024 growth target. Only the government can jump-start the economy through forceful and coordinated policy responses. It tends to adjust growth targets gradually; it has never revised down the growth target by more than 0.5ppt in the past two decades. For 2024, it could either keep the growth target unchanged at 5% or revise it down to 4.5%.

Our base case is that the government will likely commit to supporting the economy by setting a growth target at "above 4.5%" for 2024, which is moderately higher than the 4.1% average growth in 2022-23. Although a 5% growth target may be desirable for the economy, the government will likely consider the chances of missing the growth target in the event of unexpected negative shocks (such as a deeper US and Europe recession). Meanwhile, a 4.5% growth target may be too low to inject confidence in the economy. An "above 4.5%" growth target would be a balanced choice, in our view.

We forecast real GDP growth of 4.7% in 2024 and 4.5% in 2025. Consistent with an above-4.5% growth target, our forecast assumes that the government's fiscal stance will be 1.5-2% of GDP more expansionary in 2024 compared to 2023, taking into account that most of the recently announced measures, such as the CNY 1tr infrastructure investment and the scaling up of public housing projects, would be implemented only in 2024. We also expect the PBoC to cut the MLF rate by another 45bps before mid-2024 and inject liquidity through RRR cuts, the PSL, and other long-term lending facilities to accommodate the upcoming fiscal expansion.

A 1.5-2ppt rebound in nominal GDP growth would help improve business sentiment. Nominal GDP growth was only 4.2% in 2023, which was lower than 2022's 5.3% growth. In fact, it was the lowest annual growth in China's history since 1980, with the exception of 2020. Low nominal GDP growth means low revenue growth and narrow profit margins for corporates, and could be quite negative for business sentiment and future plans. With the support of government spending, we expect both CPI and PPI inflation to rise gradually throughout 2024, pushing nominal GDP growth higher to around 5.5% in 2024 and 6% in 2025.

An upside scenario would be the government setting a 5% growth target. This target should be achievable if the government's fiscal stimulus package is larger than we have assumed and implementation is front-loaded. In this scenario, a sharper rebound in prices can be expected and, thus, even higher nominal GDP growth, in the 7-8% range. Conversely, a lower growth target would lead to doubt about the government’s policy commitment, resulting in lower growth than our forecast and the risk of deflation getting entrenched.

## Property's drag on growth may come to an end

In its third year of downturn, the property sector has been a big drag on China's growth. The combined GDP share of real estate services and property investment has declined from 21.2% in 2020 to an estimated 15.4% in 2023, suggesting a negative contribution of almost 2ppt every year. This calculation may be overly simplistic; nonetheless, it suggests that the property sector's drag on growth has been sizable.

The prolonged downturn is primarily due to entrenched weak home-buyer sentiment. Property sector activity remained subdued across the board, with real estate investment, housing sales, and housing new starts hovering at 70%, 50%, and 35% of their peak levels, respectively. Despite intensifying policy support since August, property sector activity saw only a modest rebound in September and softened again in October. Chinese families have accumulated large amounts of bank savings in recent years. They remain hesitant to spend on home purchases, concerned about delays in delivery of homes and declining home prices. Instead, they used excess savings to pay down mortgage loans, by about CNY 4-5tr cumulatively since 2021, by our estimates. This, in turn, further suppressed developer cashflows and their funding for finishing pre-sold homes. Coincidentally, we estimate that developers would also need another CNY 4-5tr of cash to ensure the delivery of unfinished homes sold in the past few years. This deadlock situation has prevented any meaningful rebound of home sales.

Government spending on housing would be crucial to breaking the negative cycle. The government has been encouraging financial institutions to provide more credit to developers, including through PBoC credit lending facilities, but uptake of those facilities has remained low,<sup>1</sup> as credit risks associated with lending to developers are paramount. At this point, what will likely make a difference in 2024 and beyond is the so-called "Three Major Projects" led by the government, namely urban redevelopment, affordable housing, and emergency public facilities. With government backing, banks would be more willing to lend to these projects. The PBoC's PSL facility will likely be tapped to provide additional low-cost funding for banks to lend. These projects are expected to help developers as well as their suppliers. With an estimated size of CNY 1tr or more, these projects could account for almost 10% of property investment, effectively putting an end to its continued decline. Meanwhile, recovery in the private housing market will likely lag that of investment and construction.

Thus, the property sector may finally stop being a drag on growth in 2024, although a meaningful rebound still looks elusive. We expect another small drop in real estate services/GDP owing to a lagged recovery in the private housing market. This drag will likely be offset by a small increase in property investment/ GDP, due to government-led housing projects. The share of GDP of the property sector as a whole would stabilize at slightly more than 15% in 2024, with the hope that it rebounds further down the road when private housing demand finally recovers.

## The challenging task of bringing back inflation

We are more concerned of market consensus about China's deflation risks. The economy has been on the brink of deflation for almost a year: on a seasonally adjusted basis, China's consumer prices have stayed flat since mid-2022, while producer prices have dropped by about 5% in the past year. We judge that China is "on the brink" of deflation only because core CPI inflation still stayed positive, in part due to higher services prices after its reopening. But core CPI inflation has also been experiencing a downtrend since H2-2021.

Low inflation has a "gravity" effect that, absent external shocks, tends to persist rather than revert to the mean. This is because low inflation reduces corporate revenue and profit growth, which in turn leads to reduced capital spending and hiring. Consumers also delay big-item purchases if they are uncertain about future income and employment prospects. Additionally, declining inflation expectations, induced by prolonged low inflation periods, could become self-fulfilling and further weigh on future prices. Inflation expectations have been trending down since 2020, dropping to the lowest level in recent years as of Q2 2023. They are still not as low as during China's last deflation period in the late 1990s to the early 2000s, but the risk of entrenched low inflation expectations cannot be overlooked.

To bringing back inflation, China's negative output gap would need to be closed... Our estimated output gap appears to be a good leading indicator of China's CPI and PPI inflation. China escaped from deflation in the early 2000s, when its accession to the WTO prompted a surge of exports and capital inflows, propping up the output gap to positive territory for a sustained period. This time, though, external demand is unlikely to generate a sufficiently large shock to China's output gap, given the challenging world growth outlook for 2024.

...which in turn would require stronger domestic investment. China's output gap in the post-GFC period is well correlated with investment's contribution to growth. Indeed, with domestic consumption largely stable and the importance of exports diminishing, demand volatility largely came from investment. Investment has been weak in the past two years owing to pandemic-related disruptions, a property sector downturn, and the government's pursuit of a less-debt-driven growth model. While we have a positive outlook on investment (its contribution to growth will likely improve to close to 2ppt in 2024 from 1-1.5ppt in 2022-23), such a moderate improvement may not bring the output gap to sufficiently positive territory. Thus, our inflation forecast is lower than consensus: we expect only a gradual improvement in CPI and PPI inflation, to 1-1.5% by end-2024. Average CPI and PPI inflation would be 0.9% and 0.4%, respectively, in 2024.

## Expansionary fiscal stance would be positive for growth

Fiscal expansion is the most effective policy tool to stimulate demand and boost prices in today's low-growth, low-inflation environment. China's fiscal multiplier is estimated to range between 1 and 2, but vary a lot over time. We found that the fiscal multiplier tends to be higher in China when growth and interest rates are low, likely because the crowd-out effect on private demand would be smaller. We also find that the fiscal multiplier is likely much larger on spending measures than on revenue measures.

However, the government withdrew fiscal support in late 2022 and H1 2023, resulting in a sharp reduction in the fiscal deficit by more than 3% of GDP. Spending growth was negative for most of this period, in part owing to land sales revenue shortfalls. Given the high fiscal multiplier, such fiscal tightening likely had a sizable negative impact on aggregate demand and prices. Fortunately, the government changed its fiscal stance in Q3 by accelerating spending, helping local governments with their debt problems, and, more recently, announcing stimulus packages for infrastructure investment and housing construction.

We expect the fiscal stance to be 1.5-2% of GDP more expansionary in 2024 versus 2023.

n China's overall fiscal deficit in 2023 is estimated at 6.3% of GDP, which largely came from (1) the 2023 annual budget deficit, which was set at 3% of GDP in March 2023; and (2) a CNY 3.8tr of local government special bonds. In 2024, we expect the government will keep these two items broadly unchanged: budgetary deficit will likely still be kept at 3% of GDP, and the local government special bond quota will likely be set at CNY 4tr. The sum of the two items will still be 6% of GDP.

n Fiscal stimulus in 2024 will come from a few discretionary items: (1) the budget revision in October 2023. The government revised up deficit to 3.8% of GDP and issued CNY 1tr of additional CGB in 2023. The bulk of this CNY1tr funding will likely be carried over for spending in 2024; (2) the CNY 1tr of ultra-long sovereign bonds to be issued in 2024; and (3) the PBOC's PSL facility. PSL is not part of China's official fiscal accounts, but its economic impact is similar to fiscal spending. We expect a total of CNY 1tr funding from PSL will be utilized for the funding of public housing and urban redevelopment projects, the bulk of which will be spent in 2024.

If the above assumptions are correct, China's fiscal spending growth should see a notable jump in 2024. By our calculations, total fiscal spending growth will surge to 8% in 2024, from average annual growth of 0.2% in the past 3 years. This will have significant implications for growth. We estimate that China's fiscal multiplier has been around 1.3 in recent years. We thus expect China's economy will likely strengthen in 2024 - sequential growth will likely rebound to 1.3% QoQ in Q1 and Q2 2024 from 1% in the most recent quarter.

It remains to be seen, though, to what extent the central government's more expansionary stance will be offset by tightening in local government borrowing. In an attempt to comprehensively address the local government debt issue, a few highly indebted cities and regions have been restricted from issuing new debt, including through their LGFVs. We believe that the restrictions so far should have a limited impact on a few regions and not impact the overall fiscal stance, as the total special bond quota for all local governments will likely be unchanged at CNY 3.8tr for 2024. However, it remains to be seen whether restrictions will be broadened to wider regions and especially, whether they would be applied to offbudget borrowing through LGFVs. If so, our assumption of the size of overall fiscal stimulus would need to be adjusted downward, with negative implications for growth and inflation.

## Additional monetary easing is also warranted

China's real interest rate is still too high relative to the natural rate. This is because (1) China’s natural interest rate may have dropped rapidly in recent years owing to slower trend growth and (2) more recently, inflation has also fallen, which has raised the real interest rate despite PBoC cuts to nominal interest rates.

We expect the PBoC to cut the one-year MLF policy rate by another 45bps in 2024. The PBOC did not change its policy rate in January against market expectations, but we think rate cuts is only delayed, it still has to happen to bring bank interest rates to below the natural rate. The first rate cut will likely be in March, after the government's budget is announced at the National People's Congress, followed by 2 more rate cuts in Q2 and Q3, respectively. In the near term, the PBOC will likely cut the RRR and inject liquidity through the PSL and other long-term lending facilities, to make sure there is sufficient liquidity in the financial system to accommodate the upcoming fiscal expansion.

Our assumption on PBoC rate cuts would depend heavily on inflation-related developments. Our non-consensus view on PBoC rate cuts is based on a more benign outlook for inflation: we expect CPI to stay below 1% until late 2024. The market was expecting a faster rebound in inflation, to \~1% in Q1 and almost 2% by end-2024. A faster rebound in inflation would lower real rates and reduce the need for PBoC rate cuts, but we think these will be unlikely before mid-2024 given that it will take time for fiscal expansion to reduce the output gap and assert positive pressures on prices.

In any case, the interest rate policy's near-term impact on growth and inflation would be limited. The interest rate policy tends to be more effective in cooling down an overheated economy; this was demonstrated again by the Fed and the European Central Bank (ECB) in their most recent rate-hiking cycles. Conversely, it is much less effective during economic downturns and in a low interest rate environment. Despite the PBoC's recent rate cuts, M1 growth remained low, as households and corporates still prefer fixed-term deposits over time deposits and are insensitive to the drop in deposit rates. Fiscal policy is in the driving seat in closing China's output gap and lifting inflation, while monetary policy would largely play a supporting role.

A medium-term challenge for the PBoC and financial regulators is how to strengthen China's financial sector. The twice-a-decade Financial Work Conference in November called for comprehensively strengthening financial supervision and better management of financial risks. Banks and financial institutions have made sizable lending to local government debt and the property sector in the past decade, the asset quality of which has deteriorated in the past few years. A banking crisis is unlikely, given the government owns most of the banks and is backing the debt of local governments as well as supporting the property sector. Nevertheless, the government would need to find ways to identify and deal with non-performing assets in the banking system and beef up bank capital where necessary, rather than letting low-quality assets stay on bank balance sheets, eroding their profitability and reducing their capacity to make new, productive loans. It has much work to do to ensure the financial sector remains efficient and supportive of economic growth.

## External sector

The renminbi's depreciation against the USD in 2023 was driven largely by two factors: relative growth surprises and widening interest rate differentials. The US economy beat expectations while China's growth disappointed, driving FDI and equity market outflows. Higher USD yields have made CNY bonds less attractive, resulting in bond market and bank lending outflows. The PBoC's "line in the sand" approach has been effective in stabilizing the bilateral exchange rate.

Trade and fundamentals remain strong for the renminbi. China's current account surplus is expected to narrow modestly in 2023-24 owing to a wider services trade deficit after border reopening. The goods trade surplus will likely be sustained at around USD 600bn per year, despite a contraction in global trade. China's manufacturing sector is becoming more competitive in products such as autos and green-energy products. It has also benefitted from a large inflation differential between domestic and foreign markets.

Capital flows will likely improve. Official BoP statistics reported a sharp drop in direct investment inflow since mid-2022, which we think is actually largely owing to reduced financing by Chinese offshore companies, such as through Hong Kong/ US IPOs. The interest rate differential has also contributed as foreign-owned companies in China increasingly tap into renminbi financing instead of financing from parent companies. In other words, the drop in FDI flows was not so much due to longer-term factors, but to short-term growth and interest rate differentials, similar to portfolio flows. Both growth and interest rate differentials against the USD are expected to narrow in 2024-25, given that the US economy is expected to experience a mild recession in H1 and the Fed is expected to cut by 175bps in 2024 and another 50bps in 2025. As a result, we think capital outflow pressures will ease substantially going forward, especially from mid-2024 once the Fed (and also the ECB) enters a rate-cutting cycle.

Thus, we expect renminbi depreciation to come to an end. We expect that it will be stable against a trade-weighted basket in 2024 while appreciating against the USD. We expect the CNY at around 7.0/USD by end-2024 and 6.7/USD by end-2025.

# Macro strategy

## A harder pivot to come?

2023 was a challenging year for China, with a) the initial momentum from reopening failing on follow through; b) increased drag from the property sector; c) rising defaults in the bond market; and d) relations with the US drifting worse. Chinese equities and FX were among the worst performers in the region in 2023.

However, as mentioned in our Macro strategy section, there have been signs of a shift in narrative on China policy, with announcements of more fiscal spending, more monetary support, and promises of more funding support to property developers. The proof is, of course, in the pudding, and that is why Chinese assets have been slow to react to this shift in narrative (CSI is trading at the low of 2019), and will be until conviction increases about a harder pivot from the authorities on support to the property sector, and growth overall.

Two policy measures are arguably more key than others; 1) the expected size of the Pledged Supplementary Lending (PSL) program that is used for support the property sector; and 2) a bigger fiscal boost to growth. Our China economist argues (see the economic section) that the economy lacks the endogenous momentum to close the negative gap, and is looking for fiscal stimuli to be 1.5-2% of GDP more expansionary this year than in 2023.

Hence, for now, our base case is that a shift lower in global bond yields, and easing external headwinds should support a modestly more constructive view on the RMB. The pace and quantum of the move will be determined by multiple factors.

n Widening in the tourism deficit. With the opening up of China's borders and the rise in Chinese students studying abroad, the tourism deficit has been widening. On a 3M moving average, this gap has widened to \~\$16bn, close to the pre-pandemic deficit level (the 2019 tourism deficit average was \~\$19bn). If the tourism deficit were to widen to the 2019 monthly average, it could end up recycling \~40% of China's 2024 trade surplus, as per DB forecasts.

n Further monetary policy support. As the government pushes forward with more potential monetary easing (DB expects a total of 45bps of rate cuts over the next 12 months and at least one RRR cut), this could cause more equity outflows to Hong Kong equity markets via the Southbound Connect, given that the majority of top-tier Chinese companies are listed in Hong Kong.

FDI outflows – more structural than cyclical. Unlike in 2015/16, when the notable decline in China's net FDI flows was mainly driven by cyclical factors, the FDI outlook this time is more punctuated by longer-term structural factors, and in particular the impact of geopolitical tensions, and the diversification of supply chains away from China. Even with the economy rebounding, global corporates might see less reason to (a) retain their earnings in China (estimated to be \~\$530bn); and (b) bring in fresh capital, including because of how much cheaper (and increasingly liquid) onshore financing is getting in China. Chinese corporates, meanwhile, are also looking to expand their supply chains abroad.

The markets have turned increasingly bearish on China assets, setting a relatively low hurdle for China growth to surprise to the upside. The key to the balance of risks on China growth, we believe, lies with whether the authorities there are willing to pivot harder on their policy choices. Indeed, that does seem to be the direction of travel. If so, the risk to USD/RMB is on the downside.

Based on the historical correlation between RMB and the US-China interest rate gap, and given DB rates forecasts for 2024, USD/RMB should be trading below 7 in 2024.

However, if we do see a much harder pivot on policy (and increasingly that is the risk) a stronger RMB price action should emerge, particularly given how bearish the market is. A bigger push by the authorities – and particularly one driven more by fiscal efforts, and combining measures to address the property sector bottlenecks, rather than just monetary easing – similar to in 2020, could cause RMB to appreciate more notably. A key lever for the same could be a pick-up in the corporate FX conversion ratio, which, at the current juncture, is exceptionally low (\~48%). History suggests that during periods of stronger growth recovery, corporates have shown a stronger tendency to repatriate earnings (2017 and 2021) – i.e., higher exporter FX conversion (averaging \~60%). Indeed, in 2017 and again in 2021, FX conversion (on a net trade balance basis) increased by \~\$7-8bn per month.

On China rates, we project net supply of bonds (i.e., CGBs, LGBs and policy bank bonds) to be higher than that of 2023, driven by; (a) the central government keeping its fiscal deficit at 3.8% of GDP, resulting in a net increase of \~RMB200bn of additional CGB issuance vs. 2023; and (b) a potential increase in LGB refinancing bonds, given \~\$2.3tn of LGFV debt maturing in this year. The risk is, if anything, skewed to the upside (higher) on issuance, given (a) the latest Politburo statement suggests a potentially wider fiscal deficit (\~4%), and/or (b) higher issuance of LG special bonds and/or policy bank bonds to support growth.

H1 issuance is traditionally skewed to LG bond issuance, with H2 more towards central government bonds. Although this was not the case in 2023, when LGB issuance was more evenly distributed, this year we suspect could be a repeat of the 2020 and 2022 experience, given the urgency to support growth recovery.

However, rising bond issuance in China is not directionally associated with higher bond yields. Mostly because sizeable issuance has generally been associated with increased fiscal-monetary coordination. DB Economics expects the PBoC to ease monetary policy further, including at least one RRR cut. Rate cuts will likely prompt banks to become more active buyers of CGBs. Indeed, in 2023, commercial banks accounted for \~92% of total net CGB supply (similar to 2022), given easing by the PBoC, resulting in ongoing compression of banks' net interest margins. With the PBoC expected to cut rates by another 45bp, we would not be surprised to see banks remain active buyers of CGBs this year.

## Equity strategy

## Will HK recover, or suffer a once in a century fifth down year?

It's so easy to give up on HK/CH, especially HK - where its forward P/E of 8.1x trades 10.5 points below Bloomberg's World Index forward P/E. Investor exhaustion prevents people recognizing just how unusual it is to have three down years in a row, (or four in the case of the HK indices). The oldest stock index in the world is the Dow Jones Transport Index, and in its 126 years, it has only had two instances of being down four years in a row, at the start of the Great Depression, and as the dot.dom boom turned to bust. In the dot.com case, even after four down years investors were still up 94% on a 10Y rolling view - whereas the HSI had a 10Y rolling return of -15% through the end of 2023. Now the DJTI did have a fifth down year after that, but its total five-year drawdown was 29% vs. the four year drawdown in HK of 39%.

If we look further into the data, the US had many of its best years after being down multiple years in a row, as you would expect, as stock performance is not independent of prior returns.

After four down years in a row, the HK Indices sit in a very small pocket of market history. In the rare cases where we can find such ongoing drops, they have tended to follow major bull market - of a type HK hasn't seen in close to 20 years. Valuations are extremely low, and prospective catalysts include a plateauing in the badnews cycle, liquidity shifts from bank accounts and bonds as rates come off, rising earnings, and buybacks. In 2002 we recall many were reluctant to listen to potential answers to that question.

Usually downturns are preceded my a major bull market. The HSI however is down on almost any time frame you pick after 2006 and is at approximately the same level it was at the city's handover in 1997, when some investors were still in nappies. Trading at 0.9x book value and on a single digit P/E, there's no heat in the market; it had only risen by 29% in the 10 years prior to its four year run of down years. We think investors should be heavily positioned in HK to reflect its potential to rebound.

## What about the CSI300?

The CSI300 had risen 66% in the decade preceding its three down years, which is a similar set up to DJTA Index's performance in the lead up to three successive down years. As such, it may be in danger of the 1% return that has been average in the limited times this has happened.

On the three instances of four down years in a row, as HK has had, the DJTA rose 55%, -13% and 30% the following year. We favour investors being in the HSI over the CSI300, as it offers deeper value, and is well stocked with central SOEs that are likely to be favoured by government policy, and by companies that have gone through the regulatory wringer, and on-balance seem more likely to see on an improving part of the regulatory cycle. Both indices feature market heavyweights with strong balance sheets to allow capital returns or opportunistic acquisitions, but the HK listed companies have more companies looking to lean into capital returns. Our preference remains to invest in national champions, and we particularly like those in energy, telecoms and e-commerce.

## Capital discipline potentially a catalyst

We think it's difficult for large economies to launch new policies that make anything but a temporary impact to GDP. Obviously you can keep stimulating, but then end up stimulating just to avoid the downturn of not repeating the stimulus. Japan is a cautionary tale of that, but other countries seem to be following. Liberalization however can be relied upon, if followed through, and China's latest work plan suggests it is partly on that track, with a commitment to treat private firms equally and embrace foreign firms and people warmly. That should allow for a pick up in sentiment off a low base, and that can be built on. If the government is serious about this, it will eventually reverse the freeze that foreign investors are putting on the China market.

Improving earnings will be a driver, and doubly so if HK/CH EPS outgrows that of the world market, something that is possible due to China's higher GDP growth and high investment share of GDP, which reflects expansion of productive assets by corporates, which should drive earnings.

Corporates are well cashed up, with half of the index constituents in both HK and CH in a net cash position, and that can be used to fund buybacks, as we have seen at the internet companies, or lift dividend payouts as we have seen at SOE telcos in particular. Our main recommendation is to buy companies with strong balance sheets, as they can withstand the macro challenges, consolidate in their industries and support their own stock prices. We are particularly attracted to Hang Seng Industrials, as they generally fit these criteria and are particularly cheap vs. history.
