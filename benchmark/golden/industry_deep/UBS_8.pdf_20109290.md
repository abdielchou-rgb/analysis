# Global Equity Strategy Postcard from America

The key points that came up during our US marketing trip in order:

The most dominant issue by far was market breadth. Most believe it will widen. We show why it should generally especially in Europe and UK. The risk is that in bubbles (which we do not believe we are yet in, but have 7 of the 8 preconditions) breadth tends to remain abnormally narrow for an extended period of time.

Equity Risk Premium. We had more questions on the ERP than any other visit I can recall in the past 35 years. Clients are trying to understand whether a rise in productivity is factored into the market. We don’t believe that it is.

Weak economic data being good or bad for equities. Clients now in general believe that weak economic data will be interpreted negatively by markets (given 2.5 US rates cuts are expected by year end). We disagree.

Gen AI. Obviously the market is focussed on the initial winners (Meta seemed the consensus, Tesla the short while most believed MSFT was exceptionally well placed but questioned its valuation -despite its FCF yield being almost on a par with say Boston Scientific). Investors are now looking at the second round beneficiaries (areas such as big cap pharma, healthcare, retail banks came up).

Europe/UK. Investors were asking about the optimal entry, not exit, point. Most see political events in France as representing a buying opportunity. The rates sensitivity of Europe GDP seemed to have been underestimated (1% off rates adds 1% to GDP growth after a year). The UK was getting a lot more focus because of its relative political stability (banks seem to be preferred UK domestic play).

China: the slow slip in deflation. We were surprised how little China came up as a focus (especially as we highlighted that there has been triple bubble of credit, real estate and investment- which has now burst). China has accounted for 36% of the increase in global growth since 2010. A few clients suggest that we are entering classic debt deflation spiral with a highly inadequate policy response as of now. The popular shorts to play this theme were luxury, iron ore, and RmB proxies. Most remain bullish on copper despite China accounting for 55% of copper demand.

Elsewhere: no strong views on cyclicals versus defensives (we stay overweight growth defensives largely). A President Trump victory was felt to be good for equites (which is a debate as many factors are dramatically different from 2017) but add to inflation (we agree) . Most cited financials as the clear winner (we agree). Many experienced Japan investors believe it to be different this time around Japan and we agree (see UBS Q Series highlights ).

## 2) Equity valuation and Gen AI.

We have never had so many questions in North America on how we calculate the equity risk premium and how it can be adjusted for changes in productivity.

The vast majority of clients use a simple bond to earnings yield ratio or gap (which was rather pompously mislabelled as ‘the Fed Model’). Some go a stage further and look at the TIPS yield versus the cyclically adjusted earnings yield.

Investors need to adjust for any change in future trend earnings.

The Equity Risk Premium= the bond yield + EPS growth - (constant)\*earnings yield

Hence looking at the earnings yield relative to either the bond yield or corporate bond yield does not adjust for future productivity growth (the corporate bond to earnings yield gap is shown in Appendix 2).

This is contrary to the equity risk premium on our models where we use a DDM. Our ERP uses consensus EPS growth numbers for the first 5 years, which are linearly faded out to 15 years to long-term consensus GDP growth rates (provided by Consensus Economics). We also fade margins back to their 10-year average over a 10-year period after year 5 (i.e. in years 6-15). We take a 45% payout ratio (which has been the average seen in the

If we adjust for a 1% increase in productivity from 2028, then the ERP is 4.8% (without an improvement in productivity from 2028, it is 3.9%). 4.8% is below its long run average of 5.4% but above the warranted ERP. The warranted ERP is 4.4% (with the warranted ERP being driven by credit spreads, ISM and PMIs).

# 3) Clients see weak economic data as being bad news from here…we disagree.

The market flipped quite rapidly in June to react positively to weaker hard (ie actual) and soft (ie survey) economic data as we can see that below in Sean Simonds model. Most investors we speak with now believe that with nearly 2.5 rate cuts priced into the market by year-end the rate news can’t get any better without a sharp deteriorating in the macro data and thus believe weak data is bad again. However, very recently the correlation between the market and economic data has turned positive.

Figure 20: Soft Data and Hard data macro surprises correlation with the market.
![](images/ad644481245a447e019e27cf1a940311cfa475f9ee0eb5bf86eca8caa754344a.jpg)
Source: UBS, Bloomberg

We think that weak data is good news because of three conditions below:

First, as long as there is no inflation constraint on the Fed to react to weak data, and there is no constraint on the UBS forecast on inflation (see here) .

Second, eventually monetary policy will work to cause GDP growth to recover to trend. We can’t see any reasons why the monetary transmission mechanism would be broken given that bank, corporate and household balance sheets are in good shape. A low interest sensitivity of the economy is a good thing as long as a certain level of rates does cause GDP to recover.

Thirdly, that bond yields fall in response to falling short rates (as we have seen in the past week).

Thus the impact the increase in the NPV of future earnings from both a lower bond yield and a postponed economic recovery more than offsets the near term hit to EPS (that occurs under a temporary slowdown in GDP growth).

## 4) The second-round winners from Gen AI.

Clients are examining who could be the second-round winners from AI (outside the ‘picks and shovels’). Retail Banks, big cap pharma, and healthcare equipment came up as highly probable winners. The losers were felt to be: advertising agencies, commercial real estate and non-proprietary data aggregators.

For the macro guys, the debate was whether Gen AI and China disinflation would offset all the inflationary forces (Trump, tariffs, deglobalisation, decarbonisation, re-regulation, demographics and tightening up on immigration) with the 1 year inflation rate implied in 1 years time at 2.55%.

## 5) Magnificent 7:

We found that there was a consensus short of Tesla, consensus long of Meta and big debates on the rest. MSFT was seen to have extraordinary pricing power (with its 410m users of Office 365) but the debate was valuation (though its valuation at 2.3% FCF yield for 2025E is the same as Boston Scientific 2.6% in 2024E, for example). Outside of the Mag 7 a name that kept popping up as being abnormally cheap for its growth prospects was Salesforce.

## 6) Europe and especially the UK.. US looking to buy not sell.

The UK is now seen as a haven of relative political stability and is getting more focus. We can see that 2 days ago Blackrock upgraded the UK to overweight (see here). There are lots of cheap stocks that have underperformed their peer group as we show in our pack (see here). UK banks were often mentioned. We were asked occasionally about the impact of the Labour government to which we believe it particularly helps FTSE 250, brick manufacturers/homebuilders, a higher price of UK carbon, retailing and sterling (see here).

In general, we found clients looking to buy rather than sell Europe and did not see French politics changing this in a meaningful way.

To us, it is very simple, valuations are abnormally cheap, growth is accelerating, and rates are falling and 1% off rates adds 1% to GDP after a year (see here). We think the EU Recovery Fund Europe, of which just 35% has been disbursed helps increase productivity and mutualise debt. In dollar terms in the past month, Europe has kept pace with the US (and most US investor buy unhedged European exposure).

## 7) China…the slow drift into deflation.

We were surprised how few questions we have had on China in general (stock market or economy especially bearing in mind 36% of the increase in global growth has come from China since 2010).

Some clients are beginning to think we are slowing entering a very extended period of deflation and deleveraging. We believe that the triple bubble has now burst (namely in credit, real estate and investment) with secondary house prices falling at record rates and both producer prices and export deflators falling in absolute terms.

The experience elsewhere (when double bubbles burst- credit and real estate) was that the adjustment process was long and required very much greater fiscal and monetary response than seen in China so far (in peripheral Europe, Japan and the US QE, zero rates and a very large fiscal stimulus was required).

The particularly worry is the starting point: nominal GDP growing at just 4% in Q2 YoY (closer to 2% in Q2 quarterly annualised), government debt to GDP (on IMF data) at 89% of GDP with a primary budget deficit of 7.3% of GDP suggesting limited fiscal headroom. This is before we get potentially a 60% tariff assuming a Trump administration which initially is estimated by UBS economists to take 2.5% off Chinese growth (assuming perhaps optimistically that there are no additional tariffs on Chinese goods exported to other regions - see their note ). This potentially would leave nominal GDP growth very close to contracting.

We can see that the China relative themes have not been particularly well correlated.

To us the risk/reward in luxury still looks poor in: luxury, L'Oreal, iron ore, German autos and China exposed capital goods (see here). The European equity strategy team has a detailed note on China exposure which highlights Sell-rated Burberry (26% of revenue exposure to China), VW (42% of exposure to China) and Schaeffler (23% of exposure to China) amongst many companies, see their note.

Moreover, some China exposure would be vulnerable from nationalistic buying on the back of Trump tariffs (e.g. Apple- where 18% of its sales come from China).

Around 20% of semis revenues come from China (and in some instances such as ASML it was in the past quarter 45%).

The debate is whether the degree to which this extends to commodities. While just 13% of oil demand comes from China, it is closer to 55% for copper (our commodity team highlight that only c20% of China copper demand is related to real estate and thus the structural positive story associated with electrification and undersupply dominates concerns about China slowdown despite SHFE inventories being abnormally high).

## 8) Potential impact of a Trump Presidency/GOP clean sweep.

There were less questions than 6 months ago on this subject but this question still came up quite frequently.

In general, US investors still see a Trump Presidency as being a net positive for equities (the impact of lower taxes, easier regulation for banks, energy, real estate offsetting the impact of higher tariffs, lower growth and potentially higher bond yields). The Europeans are a bit more sceptical. American investors believe that Trump would be measured and that some of his tariff rhetoric is a bargaining tool.

Most clients believed that long term inflation would be higher (less immigration, more tariffs, potentially more fiscal easing).

The general agreement was that a Trump Presidency helps financials (life companies like a steeper yield curve and banks benefit from a higher for longer rate cycle on the back of higher inflation. De-regulation also favour more M&A which helps investment banks).

Clearly the issue is that this time around, unlike 2018, inflation is above not below the Fed target ( the 10 year breakeven was 1.7% in November 2017 versus 2.3% now) and government deficits and government debt are in far worse shape (government debt to GDP is nearly 17pp higher and the budget deficit as % of GDP is 2pp of GDP than 2017). Risk assets are priced more richly (the P/E on 12 month forward earnings was 17.7 in 2017 compared to 21.2 now and high yield spreads are now in the bottom 10% at 3.1% of their historic range compared to 30% at 3.7% in November 2017).

Thus, applying the 2017/18 template would appear to be a mistake with the economy much later cycle and risk assets already much more highly priced.

## The two potential key debates in our mind are:

i) How sensitive would President Trump be to a negative equity market reaction to his policies (recall that Trump only implemented tariffs in 2018 after we had had a very strong rally in equities).

ii) How much retaliatory action on tariffs we might see. The fear is that this time around that the tariffs are much more widespread and retaliatory action is more likely (for example, both Mexico and Europe have imposed tariffs on Chinese products that limits the degree to which China can redirect its exports to these regions).

What happens in September/October ?

Corporates might postpone investment until there is greater visibility post-election policies (especially with regards to the tax credits of IRA and tariffs) meanwhile bonds might sell off on fear the inflationary and fiscal consequences of a potential GOP clean sweep (with for example the CBO saying that renewing the Trump tax cuts would add \$4.6trn to US government debt see here).
