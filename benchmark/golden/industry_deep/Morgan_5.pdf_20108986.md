# The Viewpoint: How US elections could affect Asia

After the US presidential debate, investors are asking what a Trump win and/or Republican sweep would mean for Asia. We see three channels of transmission: (1) tariffs, (2) US and global growth, (3) financial conditions – US rates and dollar. Our top concern: the growth downside from higher tariffs.

## Key Takeaways

Tariff risks are rising given recent developments. Tariffs slow growth meaningfully and pose a deflationary shock to Asia. China is most exposed, India least.

■ For context, when trade tensions emerged in 2018-19, Asia's GDP growth slowed 2.1ppt, a greater impact than we initially projected.

■ If 60% tariffs on imports from China are imposed, the potential acceleration in supply chain diversification could provide an offset for Asia ex China.

But if additional 10% tariffs were imposed on all US imports, the significant damage to corporate confidence and capex could mean a potential repeat of 2018-19.

Given the experience of the previous cycle, the risks are skewed towards a further rise in trade tensions and elevated levels of uncertainty weighing on growth.

## What a potential Trump win could mean for Asia

For the US, higher tariffs, slower immigration, and wider deficits: Following last week’s developments, markets are beginning to evaluate the possibility of a Trump win (see here) and maybe even a Republican sweep. In that light, we have been getting questions from investors as to what that means for Asia. Our US research team has detailed the possible policy choices and implications for growth and markets under a variety of scenarios as they relate to the election results.

From an economic standpoint, three main areas stand out – tariffs, immigration, and fiscal policy. In the case of a Republican president with a divided government, our US economics team sees tariffs moving higher and immigration slowing. In a Republican sweep, the prospects of fiscal expansion would move higher too. As Matthew Hornbach, our Global Head of Macro Strategy, notes, upward pressures could build on long-end term premiums in that scenario.

For Asia, increased risk of growth downside from rise in trade tensions: For Asia, there are three channels through which the US elections outcome will matter:

1. Financial conditions – how the US dollar and rates will move ahead of and after elections;

2. Tariffs; and

3. US growth outcomes (which will affect global growth and end demand for Asian exports).

In our mid-year outlook, we had indicated that growth in Asia is broadening out with the export recovery helping economies that depend on external demand. But at the same time, we had noted that the key risk to the region’s growth would be a potential rise in trade tensions and imposition of broad-based tariffs on US imports from China and the rest of the region. That risk appears to be rising given recent developments.

## The spillover effects on Asia – the channels and the scenarios

We explore two tariff scenarios which have already been talked about by former President Trump and how they would affect Asia. As we have learned from the 2018 experience (see What did the 2018 experience tell us? ), it is not the direct effect of tariffs that would play the most dominant role in affecting the macro outcomes, but rather the transmission through corporate confidence, capex, global demand, and financial conditions.

For context, during the previous round of trade tensions in 2018-19, we had modeled the GDP impact of tariffs to be around 1.1ppt. In reality, aggregate real GDP growth for the region slowed from a peak of 6%Y in 1Q18 to 3.9%Y in 4Q19. The final effect was almost double our estimate and mirrored our caution then that the model would not capture the transmission through corporate confidence.

## Scenario: A Trump win with divided government

## (1) The US imposes 60% tariffs on imports from China

China would be more affected than Asia ex China: In this scenario, the quantum of the tariff hikes would be larger than in 2018-19 – but at the same time, there are other factors which differ from the 2018-19 experience. There would be a meaningful adverse effect on Asia's growth, but the final drag on growth would depend on the eventual set of trade policy measures taken up by the US and how those measures would weigh on corporate confidence – see more on this below).

China would remain most exposed as compared to the rest of the region, which has reduced its export exposure to China over time. Some of the economies, like India and Japan, are in a better position – both cyclically and structurally – as compared to 2018-19.

Feed-through to corporate confidence is crucial to track: During 2018-19, the bigger drag came in the form of weak corporate confidence, resulting in a sharp slowdown in corporate capex, rather than from the direct impact of tariff increases. This time around, if tariffs do rise, the drag on corporate confidence could be mitigated because it would be a recurrence/escalation of a pre-existing issue rather than emergence of a new trend.

Indeed, US-China trade tensions have been persisting for a long time – unlike 2018-19, where the rapid shift in attitudes and the deterioration of the US-China trade relationship weighed heavily on corporate confidence.

Moreover, we believe that there would be an acceleration in supply chain diversification, which could help provide some offset to growth in the region excluding China over a period of time.

How the drag on China's growth could play out: We consider three factors in thinking about the overall impact on growth:

## 1) The direct effect of tariffs on China’s exports

China could mitigate the drag of tariffs on its exports: In the previous round of trade tensions, China’s real exports broadly continued to outperform global real exports and its market share in global exports held stable in the initial period after trade tensions. This meant that even though the tariffs were directed at China, China did not face an outsized effect on its exports growth path.

• The currency effect: While US trade-weighted average tariff rate on imports from China increased 17.8ppt between February 18 and September 2019, the RMB depreciated 10.9% against the USD over the same period, suggesting that about 60% of the tariff increase could have been offset by currency depreciation.

• The rewiring effect: Some rewiring of supply chains has already taken place, as we have written previously. For instance, local manufacturing facilities have already been set up in Vietnam and Mexico. For Vietnam, the implied sourcing from China for exports to the US has decreased from about two-thirds in December 2019 to 38% in April 2024. These channels could be utilised more as trade tensions rise.

• Exporting new products, to new geographies: Moreover, China has been able to export new products (such as solar panels, lithium batteries, EVs and oldergeneration chips) to new markets (i.e., Europe and other EMs).

We think some of these factors should still help to mitigate the direct effects of tariffs this time around. Indeed, China's market share in global exports has actually risen from 12.7% in January 2018 to 14.4% currently. While the US's share may have declined, China's trade with the rest of the world has increased to such an extent that it has increased its overall market share.

## 2) The spillovers to domestic and global corporate confidence, capex, and growth

Arguably the downside to corporate confidence should be smaller this time around: Over 2018-19, the deeper downside to growth emerged from the spillover effects from weaker global growth and a sharper drop in domestic corporate confidence and capex. We think that the drag on domestic capex momentum could be less this time around, given that trade tensions have persisted for some time. However, we are mindful that the hit to corporate confidence would very much depend on the intensity of the trade measures taken.

## 3) The state of the economy before rise in trade tensions and the scope for policy response

The economy is in a weaker state currently with a lesser scope for easing: China is currently facing significant structural challenges in the form of weaker demographics, high levels of debt, and persistent deflationary pressures (the 3Ds). It is already in deflation at the starting point and an aggressive policy response to offset the downdraft to growth appears less likely. Unlike 2018-19, it will not be able to stimulate the property sector to offset weaker external demand. The implementation of tariffs would likely add to the deflationary pressures that it is already facing. A weaker external demand environment would likely further exacerbate the existing excess capacity issue in the manufacturing sector.

## (2) The US imposes 60% tariffs on imports from China and 10% tariffs on imports from the rest of the world

In this scenario, we could see a much broader impact on corporate confidence and global capex to the extent that we could get a repeat of the 2018-19 episode. The corresponding impact on financial markets could also be much larger than in Scenario #1. Indeed, compared to December 2017, for the region excluding China and Hong Kong, the share of exports to the US has gone up to 15.2% from 12.3%. In other words, the US has become an even more important source of end demand for the region excluding China and Hong Kong.

At the same time, given the broad-based nature of the tariffs, this would likely give the push for companies to take up onshoring rather than near-/friend-shoring – which would mean bigger disruption for the corporate sector globally and risk more fragmentation in the global supply chain. Indeed, as we have been highlighting, it is this channel of corporate confidence and capex that would have a material effect on the region's growth trajectory.

## Scenario: Trump win with Republican sweep

In this scenario, in addition to the implications from tariffs, we would also be watching the potential fiscal policy outcomes and how they would shift the yields and US dollar. This means that the potential tightening of financial conditions would pose further growth downside to Asia, over and above the effects of tariffs.

## Which Asia ex China economies are more exposed?

Scenario #1 – 60% tariffs on imports from China: We assess the exposures of the other Asia ex China economies to China by looking at the share of exports to China for the respective economies. From that perspective, we’d rank Australia and Indonesia as being more exposed considering the higher share of exports. Korea, Taiwan, Malaysia, and Thailand would be moderately exposed. India, Japan, and the Philippines are less exposed given low share of exports to China.

Scenario #2 – 60% tariffs on imports from China plus 10% tariffs on imports across the board: In this scenario, where tariffs are imposed across the region in addition to the 60% tariffs on China’s exports, economies like Korea, Taiwan, Malaysia, and Thailand would be more exposed considering the high trade orientation of their economies. India, Japan, and the Philippines are still the least exposed. But on a relative basis as compared to Scenario #1, they would face slightly greater growth downside because they export more to the US.

## How would Asia's policymakers respond?

Fiscal response first because monetary policy space is limited: Undoubtedly, the materialization of trade tensions would pose downside to the region’s growth outlook and therefore prompt a policy response. We expect the policy response to unfold in two stages – first with fiscal policy turning easier at the margin before rate cuts kick in. In this cycle, the monetary policy space is much more limited than in previous cycles because nominal rates in Asia are for the most part are lower than in the US at the starting point. The differing effects on financial conditions in the US and Asia mean that Asia’s growth would slow more on a relative basis as compared to the US but yet Asian central banks would be constrained in the near term to act.

The constraint on cutting rates/easing monetary policy would be even more acute in the case of a Republican sweep given that the US dollar and yields would remain stronger for longer. In that scenario, fiscal policy would remain the key policy to support growth and the extent of the monetary policy support would likely be smaller than in the case of a divided government under a Republican president.

## What happened to key economic variables?

• Asia's real GDP growth for the region slowed from a peak of 6%Y in 1Q18 to 5% as of 4Q18 and 3.9% as of 4Q19. In China, growth decelerated from 6.9%Y in 2Q18 to 5.8% as of 4Q19. In Asia ex China, the deceleration was sharper, with growth slowing from 5.3%Y in 1Q18 to 2.2% in 4Q19 – though in part this reflected domestic developments in India and Japan.

• Asia's nominal dollar exports growth – which had recovered in 2017 – decelerated meaningfully from a peak of \~14%Y 3MMA in February 2018 to -3.7% as of February 2019, with much of the slowdown unfolding from 2H18 once trade tensions ratcheted up. The deceleration was not limited to China but was broadbased across Asia ex China economies as well.

• On a level basis, China’s 12-month trailing nominal dollar exports also stagnated at US\$2.5trn in 2019, having risen \~US\$0.2trn in 2017 and 2018, respectively. Meanwhile, Asia ex China exports actually fell US\$0.2trn in 2019, to US\$3.8trn, having risen US\$0.4trn in 2017 and US\$0.3trn in and 2018.

• Asia's real exports growth also began slowing from 6.5%Y 3MMA in October 2018. It fell into negative %Y territory in January 2019, averaging -0.3% in 2019 with similar downtrends across China and Asia ex China in the period.

• Asia's real investment growth peaked at 9.4%Y in 1Q18 before slowing sharpy to 2.7%Y in 1Q19. Despite similar movement, the impact was more profound on China than on the Asia ex China region.

## What happened to financial variables?

• US nominal 10-year bond yield rose from 2.4% at end-2017 to 2.9% in February 2018 against the backdrop of Fed rate hikes. Thereafter, the 10Y remained largely range-bound until trade tensions intensified in summer 2018, and 10Y bond yield rose from 2.8% in late August 2018 to a peak of 3.2% in October 2018. As the Fed paused on its rate hike path from end-2018, the US 10Y bond yield declined sharply to 2.5% before once again staying range-bound. When trade tensions intensified further in May 2019 and growth concerns began to deepen, bond yields fell to 1.9% by August 2019 and then troughed at 1.5% in September 2019. Real 10- year bond yield also followed a similar trajectory through this period.

• The US dollar appreciated in two major bouts against the RMB in 2018-19, first appreciating 11% against the RMB between April 2018 and November 2018, before depreciating 4% from November 2018 to February 2019 as US-China trade talks progressed. When trade tensions once again intensified from May 2019, the USD appreciated 7% against the RMB between April 2019 and September 2019.

• Asian currencies also broadly depreciated against the dollar during 2018-19 amid USD strength, though most currencies except AUD and INR depreciated to a lesser extent than RMB did.

## What was the policy response?

Policy response constrained by external backdrop: Even though trade tensions had clearly weighed on growth and external demand by 2H18, the response from policymakers in the region was not as forthcoming given that the financial conditions backdrop of rising US rates and a stronger dollar amid Fed rate hikes. Several central banks such as RBI, BNM, BSP, and RBA eventually cut ahead of the Fed – which implemented its first rate cut in July 2019 – but with the exception of RBI, most central banks only cut a few months ahead while others such as BI and BoK cut in the same month. For China, the PBoC did not cut benchmark rates until November 2019, despite its headline PMI having first dipped below 50 in December 2018, 11 months prior. Instead, policymakers responded with fiscal easing, and the on-budget fiscal deficit widened 1.7ppt, from 3.2% of GDP in August 2018 to 4.9% in February 2019. Overall augmented fiscal deficit widened 1.5ppt, from 12.7% in 2018 to 14.2% in 2019.

## Risks

Risks are skewed towards further escalation: In 2018-19, trade tensions persisted for some time and remained in an escalatory cycle for longer before the signing of the Phase 1 trade deal eased tensions. Informed by this backdrop, we think that the risks are skewed towards further escalation and that elevated uncertainty will keep risks skewed to the downside. This could have the scope to amplify the shock through even tighter financial conditions, which would weigh heavily on corporate confidence and the capex cycle. While we do expect that policymakers would respond to the downside to growth, the challenge is that the monetary response would likely be delayed, which would keep the risks to the growth cycle alive.
