# FOMC Reaction: Firm Hold with an Easing Bias

Committee is not focused on a hike scenario; next move likely a cut; focus remains on length of hold. Powell's confidence in inflation is somewhat less, but he still expects inflation to move lower by year-end. Our agency MBS strategists move overweight. Our rates team maintains 3m30y receivers.

## Key Takeaways

• Rates decision: The FOMC held the policy rate unchanged in a range of 5.25-5.50%, where it has remained since July 2023. Despite a lack of further progress in 1Q24, disinflation has come a long way over the past year, and the Committee remains focused on the length of hold time. QT taper announced to begin in June, as expected.

• Outlook: We continue to see three cuts this year, starting in July, though inflation surprises in 1Q have narrowed the path to get there. The Committee saw upside risks to inflation in March, and these have likely grown: it takes just two policymakers to lower the median in June to two cuts in 2024. QT taper ends around February 2025, with risk it goes on longer.

• Rates: Our rates strategists see the markets pricing out risks of a hike, but ultimately watching data. They prefer to engage via duration as opposed to curve steepeners, and continue to suggest long 3m30y receivers.

• FX: The USD declined over the course of the May FOMC meeting as Chair Powell signaled that the Fed is highly unlikely to raise rates. Our strategists expect EUR/USD to decline over the coming months as inflation pressures decelerate in the euro area relative to the US.

• MBS: Our MBS strategists move from neutral to overweight MBS as index spreads hit YTD wides and a dovish Fed outlook should be supportive of MBS.

## Awaiting More Confidence to Cut

Chair Powell kept an asymmetric easing bias, strongly resisting saying the word "hike" (the count for the word in the Q&A transcript shows a final tally of Reporters 6, Powell 1). Reaching the confidence to cut is taking longer than previously expected, but Powell still expects inflation to have moved back down over the course of the past year and rates to be lower by the end of the year. Despite the lack of further progress in slack this year (in terms of both inflation and the labor market), the Committee has made meaningful progress toward its 2% goal over the past year. We continue to see inflation moving lower, unemployment higher, and three cuts this year.

## Statement and Presser

The FOMC statement recognized the lack of further progress in recent inflation data, but the statement still acknowledged that inflation has eased over the past year and maintained an overall easing bias. The Fed decision was unanimous.

As expected, the Fed signaled the start of tapering its balance sheet, which will begin in June. The Fed will cut the pace of QT in half to \$25bn a month for Treasuries and maintain the \$35bn cap on MBS. This is the "slow taper" we have expected, and our baseline is tapering QT ends in February 2025, with the risk of continuing longer.

In the press conference, Powell kept an easing bias but removed the comment that he expects to lower policy "at some time this year," which he had said at the last meeting. The two introduction paragraphs of Chair Powell's prepared remarks were largely unchanged from the prior meeting, but he added that inflation had eased "over the past year" and a comment on the "lack of further progress" in recent months. Nonetheless, he commented that inflation has eased substantially and the data are moving toward better balance.

On the policy path forward, the Committee needs greater confidence inflation is moving sustainably toward 2% to reduce the federal funds rate, and it is taking longer than previously expected. According to Powell, the policymakers are "prepared to maintain the current target range for the federal funds rate as long as appropriate. We're also prepared to respond to an unexpected weakening in the labor market." The scenario where they keep rates on hold for longer would be if inflation proves to be more

persistent than expected (inflation moving sideways) and the labor market remains strong. In our view, this still highlights that the two likely policy outcomes are keeping rates on hold or cutting.

When asked explicitly about a rate hike, Powell replied that he thinks policy is currently restrictive enough to get inflation back to 2%: "If we were to conclude that policy is not sufficiently restrictive to bring inflation sustainably to under 2%, then that would be what it would take for us to want to increase rates. We don't see that. We don't see evidence of that. That's where we are." Powell thinks "that the evidence shows pretty clearly that policy is restrictive and is weighing on demand" but just need more time to do its job – which seems to be keeping the rate hike scenario off the table for now.

The first-quarter data signal that it will take the Committee longer to gain confidence that inflation is on a sustainable path. Powell said he does expect inflation to move back down over the course of the year. In particular, there is confidence that lower shelter inflation will come through.

In light of the rise in the ECI this week, Powell made it clear they do not target wage growth, and he thinks there will be more alleviation on the supply side. Between restrictive monetary policy weighing on demand and the recovery in the supply side of the economy, wages and inflation are moving down incrementally towards more sustainable levels.

We continue to expect three 25bp cuts this year starting in July, but have highlighted the path to cut in July has gotten narrower. There are three road signs to watch for that get us to further disinflation and a July cut ( Exhibit 1 ):

(1) Two weeks ago, the Bureau of Labor Statistics released its New Tenants Repeat Rent Index (NTRR) for 1Q24. It suggested an even faster pace of deceleration than we had previously anticipated. The data went largely unnoticed, though the Fed follows it closely. We have flagged that several leading indicators point to lower shelter inflation, especially in the second half of this year.

(2) Financial services, and within this category, portfolio management and advice, is a volatile component of inflation that is linked with stock market returns. April-to-date, the S&P 500 is down 3.9% M/M following increases of 4.8% in February and 3.1% M/M in March (end-of-period values). The reversal in the S&P 500 in April will likely push financial services inflation down in April/May.

(3) Another key driver of the 1Q24 upswing was goods inflation, but the acceleration in core goods PCE has not been broad-based – apparel, computer software, and videotapes explain two-thirds of the acceleration in the three-month pace. We think that better supply chains and continued China deflation should keep goods prices broadly in deflation. For example, we find that a deceleration of 1pp in China PPI consumer goods leads to a \~40bp deceleration in US CPI core consumer goods. China PPI for consumer goods has been declining since May 2023.

## Easing asymmetry on display?

Bottom line: Chopping off the hike risks: Powell declined to entertain rate hikes and repeatedly emphasized the need for more time – to get additional confidence on inflation, for policy to be "sufficiently restrictive," and to understand the recent uptick in inflation. He discussed multiple scenarios, but none in which the Fed would need to hike rates.

In reaction to the FOMC meeting, yields endured a round-trip, falling initially but reverting quite a bit by the close of day, ending modestly lower on the day. The yield curve did bullsteepen eventually but showed signs of resistance to steepening for a large part of the day ( Exhibit 3 ). While we agree with bull-steepening pathways, we do see risks of bullflattening, too, which keeps us in favor of duration trades as opposed to curve trades – see The Problem with Steepeners. We stay long 3m30y receivers.

Data-dependent, not FOMC-dependent markets: Ultimately, tracking intra-day price action, much like the March FOMC it became clear that the May meeting was not going to offer a clear direction to markets – only data will, particularly inflation data. That has been the case through the year, anyway ( Exhibit 2 ).

Steering clear of rate hike paths: Powell was asked in multiple, indirect ways about the possibility of rate hikes, and he counteracted most questions on the subject with the possibility of a longer "on hold" being the most hawkish outcome in his mind (see gray box above). He mentioned three scenarios: two in which the Fed could cut, based on cooling inflation or weakening of labor markets, and one in which the Fed remains on hold for longer if inflation moved sideways.

Powell explicitly discussed falling quits rates and hiring rates, calling them "normalized," though if the 2019 labor market is the ideal (to which Powell has referenced in the past), recent data suggest things are weaker than the ideal ( Exhibit 5 ).

Powell stuck to the bigger-but-not-tighter theme; markets behind the curve: Powell reemphasized the role of the supply side, and how high growth or a strong labor market by itself would not worry him with regard to inflation. This remains a key disconnect in the way long-end yields reacted to tight labor markets ( Exhibit 4 ) and labor surprises through last year – which admittedly were not as tight as the labor data surprises might suggest.

As our economists have noted, the breakeven payrolls in the last year were much higher than previously estimated, which in turn has probably led to low forecasts on payrolls and more upside surprises than there truly were – see Supply Side Curveball.

Taper time: After a signal from the March meeting that the decision to slow the pace of balance sheet run-off would be made "fairly soon," the May statement confirmed the FOMC would begin slowing the decline of its securities holdings in June.

Specifically, the monthly redemption cap on Treasury securities will be lowered from \$60bn to \$25bn, but the monthly redemption cap on agency and agency mortgage-backed securities will remain at \$35bn. The FOMC will also reinvest any principal payments in excess of the MBS cap into Treasury securities.

Securities holdings in the SOMA portfolio have declined on average between \$75bn and \$80bn per month since the runoff caps were raised to current levels in September 2022. MBS paydowns have remained well below the cap at \$15-20bn per month. The Fed lowered the monthly Treasury redemption cap, by a bit more than our base case, in an effort to reduce the overall pace of monthly runoff by "roughly half."

We update our monthly net UST issuance projections ( Exhibit 6 ) and note the QT taper decision reduces the Treasury's additional financing needs in 2024 to \$475bn, from a potential \$720bn in the event QT would not have been tapered – a scenario noted in the New York Fed’s 2023 Open Market Operations report. After holding coupons unchanged at May refunding, we continue to think the Treasury will reduce bill issuance to the private market to accommodate the impact of QT tapering.

## USD outlook

The USD declined over the course of the May FOMC meeting. The decline is likely due to the clarity with which Powell signaled that the Fed is highly unlikely to raise rates. He signaled a high bar for the committee "to conclude that policy is not sufficiently restrictive to bring inflation sustainably to under 2%," and noted that "We don't see that. We don't see evidence of that."

We continue to expect the broad USD to gain over the coming quarters, particularly against the EUR.

Over the past three years, EUR/USD has been sensitive to the overall trajectory of inflation and central bank policy rates. From 2020 to early 2022, core inflation was higher in the US than in the euro area. From late 2022 until recently, core inflation has been higher in the euro area than in the US ( Exhibit 7 ).

The Fed and ECB tightening cycles reflected this divergence. First, the Fed was priced to hike rates quickly, then the ECB as European core inflation rose above US core inflation ( Exhibit 8 ).

In recent months, US and euro area core inflation pressures have converged just below 3% ( Exhibit 7 ). Reflecting that convergence, Fed and ECB pricing have converged. The redshaded area of Exhibit 8 illustrates the degree to which the ECB and the Fed are priced to cut at a similar pace.

Post today's FOMC meeting, where Chair Powell was quite clear that the Fed is unlikely to hike ("I think it's unlikely that the next policy rate move will be a hike"), we think the tail risks of higher rates are much more remote than they were before. When you combine this reduced tail risk with index valuations at the wides of the year, we recommend investors go long mortgages vs rates. We have a slight preference for production MBS vs index given the view that the forward path could see lower vol, which is more beneficial to bonds with higher option cost, but in general we think the backdrop is supportive for MBS.

Our first reason to be constructive is valuations. We thought after the December FOMC meeting that the mortgage index would trade in a 40-55bp range this year. Last night, the index closed wide of 55bp for the first time this year at 56bp ( Exhibit 11 ). While the market has clearly priced in fewer cuts now than it had in December, mortgages have lagged many other risk assets. For instance, YTD the IG index has tightened 12bp, and now is trading within 7bp of the post-GFC tights. On the other hand, the mortgage index is 9bp wider YTD ( Exhibit 12 ).

Third, the carry profile of MBS has improved. Carry on TBA traded cheap vs SOFR for much of 2022 and 2023 as the lack of CUSIP buyers weighted on the sector. However, roll specialness has improved somewhat for conventional investors and significantly for Ginnie investors over the last month. We calculated the implied funding rate on current coupon MBS compared to SOFR, and note that it went from trading slightly cheap to SOFR earlier this year to slightly rich ( Exhibit 14 ). We think that new rules like the adoption of FINRA 4210 can help rolls trade a little more special on a go-forward basis. Ginnie current coupon funding is extremely special ( Exhibit 15 ), though the longer-term outlook for Ginnie funding is that it is unlikely to remain at SOFR -450bp – however, if it were, then valuations are even more attractive for those that can roll TBA.

Fourth, beyond a supportive macro backdrop and improved carry, there are some other technical positive factors on the supply and demand front. Supply has been running below expectations, with net issuance at just \$27bn YTD. Demand from Chinese commercial banks is likely to be quite strong this year, in the context of \$40bn. Price-to-book ratios on the largest agency REITs appear to be at or above 1. Furthermore, the largest holder of

MBS is domestic banks, who have been sidelined from the MBS market for the last two years due to higher rates and regulatory uncertainty. Bloomberg reported today that bank capital rules could be finalized as soon as August (please see our large-cap banking analyst team's thoughts here). We've been highlighting four factors that would keep banks on the sideline since last year, and if regulations are finalized in August and the first cut is either July (our economists predictions) or November (market expectations), we think it's likely that expectations of bank demand may need to be increased. We could have more conviction on this when we receive finalized 1Q demand numbers in roughly two weeks.

Of course, there are clear risks to the long basis trade. The biggest one is if inflation stays elevated and the market prices in a higher-for-longer Fed. While also not our base case, a hard landing in which mortgages realize negative convexity would be a problem too, though we'll point out that the unchanged \$35bn MBS cap is likely not binding until primary mortgage rates fall below 4.5% (we last reached that cap in April 2022). While the convexity profile the conventional TBA seems to be better than we thought, risks exist both on production MBS (title insurance, for instance) and discount MBS (second liens).

Finally, we'll lay out a few other general thoughts for investors looking for alternative perspectives

• For those investors who are open to steepeners, IIO barely hedged would be a much more positive carry expression than vanilla curve steepeners

• For those that like mortgage spreads but don't like duration, CMO floaters could be attractive here

• For macro investors who find spreads attractive but don’t want to worry about delta hedging as much, long slight discount mortgages in the mid \$90 price range (which would benefit from lower vol but have a more muted convexity profile hedged with rates) could be attractive

## Disclosure Section

Mortgage Backed Securities (MBS) and Collateralized Mortgage Obligations (CMO)

Principal is returned on a monthly basis over the life of the security. Principal prepayment can significantly affect the monthly income stream and the maturity of any type of MBS, including standard MBS, CMOs and Lottery Bonds. Yields and average lives are estimated based on prepayment assumptions and are subject to change based on actual prepayment of the mortgages in the underlying pools. The level of predictability of an MBS/CMO's average life, and its market price, depends on the type of MBS/CMO class purchased and interest rate movements. In general, as interest rates fall, prepayment speeds are likely to increase, thus shortening the MBS/CMO's average life and likely causing its market price to rise. Conversely, as interest rates rise, prepayment speeds are likely to decrease, thus lengthening average life and likely causing the MBS/CMO's market price to fall. Some MBS/CMOs may have "original issue discount" (OID). OID occurs if the MBS/CMO’s original issue price is below its stated redemption price at maturity, and results in "imputed interest" that must be reported annually for tax purposes, resulting in a tax liability even though interest was not received. Investors are urged to consult their tax advisors for more information. Government agency backing applies only to the face value of the CMO and not to any premium paid
