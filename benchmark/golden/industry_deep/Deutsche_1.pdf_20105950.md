# TWD: Post election thoughts

Taiwan concluded its latest round of elections over the weekend, with Mr Lai Chinge of the Democratic Progressive Party (DPP) winning the Presidency with 40.1% of all votes (5.6m, Figure 1), beating the KMT’s Mr Hou You-ih (33.5%; 4.7m) and the TPP’s Mr Ko Wen-je (26.5%; 3.7m). The margin of victory for Mr.Lai was better than in the last opinion poll, though narrower than that for outgoing President Tsai in 2020. This is the first time since Taiwan began holding direct Presidential elections in 1996 that any party has held power beyond two four-year terms.

None of the three parties contesting the elections for the Legislative Yuan managed to secure an absolute majority (Figure 2). It is the first time in 20 years that the President's party has failed to secure an absolute majority in the legislature, and the first time that a third party – TPP with 8 seats – gets the balance vote. Following the results, the new President-in-waiting has talked about "the need to work across party lines, calling for communication, consultation, participation, and cooperation, under a new structure of the legislature.”

The new Legislative Yuan will begin its session on February 1<sup>st</sup> with the election of a new House Speaker and Deputy House Speaker, while the new President will be inaugurated on 20<sup>th</sup> May.

## Implications for cross-strait relations

The election result should not, in and of itself, move the needle in any material fashion on the market's perception of cross-strait relations. The initial response from key stakeholders has been well contained.

n The President elect has argued for maintaining the status quo on crossstrait relations. In his victory statement, he appealed to China to find ways to communicate and lower tensions. As long as the "two sides could face each other in dignity and parity", Mr.Lai said he hoped to “replace confrontation with dialogue and confidently pursue exchanges with China." He also avoided mentioning President Tsai's 'four commitments' framework for cross-strait relations.

n Beijing's initial response has also been relatively muted, with no direct criticism of the new President-elect. In his initial comments post the elections, US President Biden has stated that the US does not support independence for Taiwan, while reaffirming the one-China policy.

Unlike in the first two terms of DPP Presidency, the party no longer holds a simple majority in the legislative council. Passing bills - including, for example, the US-Taiwan Initiative on 21st Century Trade and US arms sales to Taiwan - will be contingent to more active negotiations with the opposition. Political experts have suggested this would also reduce the risk of the government introducing any "key actions to change Taiwan’s status, such as a constitutional revision or a referendum" given this needs a super majority, which the DPP does not have.

There is plenty of water to still potential flow under this bridge, however. The three things markets would be watching for on cross-strait risks in the near term would be; (a) any major military exercises carried out by China ahead of the Presidential inauguration; (b) China's response to the US delegation led by ex-National Security Advisor Stephen Hadley and former Deputy Secretary of State James Steinberg, who arrived on Sunday in Taiwan for a three-day visit; and (c) whether US House Speaker Mike Johnson will dispatch a delegation from the relevant House Committees (including himself) to Taiwan post the May inauguration.

## Implications for domestic economic policy

On the domestic side, the key watch point ahead will be the beginning of the new Legislative Yuan session, and where the new House Speaker and Deputy House Speaker will be elected. The dynamics there would indicate whether KMT and TPP are looking to convert their pre-poll understanding into a more formal coalition. TPP leader Mr Ko has thus far said that "in order to play the role of a critical minority, the TPP will not work with a fixed side. We will cooperate with whoever is right.”

## What are likely to be the priorities of the Lai administration?

Reducing economic reliance on China. Since the pandemic and the introduction of US export controls on semiconductors to China, Taiwan exports to China have been on the decline – dropping from a peak of 30% in 2020 to 22% in 2023 (Figure 3). This is mainly due to the reduction of machinery exports (in which electronic exports make up a sizable portion) to China, which make up \~70% of Taiwan's exports to China (Figure 4). Indeed, electronic exports to China (as a % of total machinery exports by Taiwan) have declined from 31% in 2020 to 23% in 2023 (Figure 5).

It is likely that this number will continue to fall, not just due to the tightening of US export controls on China, but also due to: (a) the ongoing diversification in electronics supply chains away from China towards countries like Vietnam and India; and (b) Taiwan's desire to reduce its economic reliance on China, particularly with China trying to limit Taiwan's exports to the Mainland.

Chinese authorities recently announced they would suspend tariff concessions on 12 types of petrochemical products from Taiwan. These products had enjoyed zero tariffs since 2013 as part of the so-called “Early-Harvest” list under the Economic Cooperation Framework Agreement (ECFA) signed in June 2010 between Taiwan and mainland China. According to China, more restrictions could be forthcoming. These include exports of agricultural, fishery, machinery, auto parts and textile products. Although for now these measures should have a limited impact on Taiwan's exports, since most of these products make up only a small part of Taiwan's exports to China, the Taiwanese authorities will still likely take preemptive steps to reduce their dependence on the Chinese market.

Corporates have also been actively repositioning their investments. For example,

Taiwanese investments into China have been on the decline (Figure 6), with investments into ASEAN and the US on the rise (Figure 7). The ongoing investments into ASEAN are in line with the government's adoption of the “New Southbound Policy”, which aims to promote relations with South and Southeast Asia, Australia, and New Zealand.

The same can also be said about Taiwan's tourism sector. Chinese tourists are comprising less of the total number of tourists going to Taiwan. During the prepandemic period, Chinese tourists made up \~30% of total tourists arriving in Taiwan. However, after the pandemic period, it is now less than 5% (Figure 8), partly because of travel restrictions the government has imposed on Chinese tourists, and also steps taken to encourage tourism from other nations, particularly ASEAN.

Increased focus on growth and housing prices. During the campaign, the incoming President had pledged GDP growth at no lower than 3.5% (which is higher than the CBC’s 2024 GDP growth forecast of 3.1%; DB: 3%). This would suggest the government will maintain spending growth of around 7% YoY in 2024 (as budgeted) and continue to push for higher consumption activities, including by raising minimum wages (Figure 9).

On property, Lai has stated he will try to curb rising home prices (Figure 10) by building more social housing units. In addition, the government might try to further tighten regulations such as income tax on house and land transactions to reduce speculative transactions. The government will also likely be comfortable with the CBC keeping rates on hold to limit price rises, since inflation remains above the CBC's comfort zone of around 2%.

## Implications for the market

As mentioned in our 2024 outlook, we are optimistic TWD will reverse some of its underperformance from last year. Why?

Rebound in demand for electronics. Taiwan is seeing more signs of stability in its semiconductor exports, driven by the ongoing recovery in global semi sales, which tend to lead exports by at least two months (Figure 11). Taiwan's electronic component imports – which typically lead electronic exports by three months – are also rising, arguing for a better outlook for electronic exports (electronics make up \~60% of total exports). Two drivers to consider here are: (a) rising demand for AI products, with Taiwan accounting for \~90% of the world's advanced chip production; and (b) a recovery in other electronics sectors, such as PCs.

Stronger equity flows. The recovery in the electronics cycle should also support a return of global equity inflows, with earnings getting revised upwards. Although in 2023 Taiwan saw \$6.5bn worth of inflows (the largest since 2019), foreign ownership in its equity market has yet to return to the pre-pandemic period. In terms of ownership as a % of market share, foreigners' holdings are at \~38% (vs 45% in 2021, Figure 12), while equity outflows since 2020 are still at \~-\$70bn (Figure 13). Hence, with the improving prospect of a recovery in electronics demand, flows into Taiwan should rise, especially if the market pivots from a carry to growth and value investment stance. In addition, with the election over, flows should return. Looking at the previous six elections, the TWSE on average gained 3.2% in the month after the election, though there was some short-term volatility.

The pace of any TWD appreciation however will likely be moderated by two key factors.

n A rise in outbound investments by Taiwanese lifers. Following the turn in risk sentiment and the peak in US yields, we have seen a pick-up in lifers' offshore investments, driven by a search for yield via the ETF route (Figure 15). Further, their investments in ETF holdings are still well below the 10% of total AUM limit (\~5% as of January). Every 1% increase implies \$11bn of portfolio outflows.

Lingering geopolitical tensions will likely continue to encourage more overseas investments and slower repatriation in earnings. We proxy the geopolitical risk premium priced into the TWD as the residual versus our financial 'fair value' model (Figure 16). That suggests some give back post elections to the increase in premium since US Speaker Pelosi's visit last year; but will need to be watched for further cross Straits risks.

Forward points to become more negative. Given that the overall hedging ratio of lifers is relatively low – around 60% as of September (Figure 17) – we think hedging activities should pick up, particularly if foreign investments are on the rise. Unlike in the past where lifers could rely on their FX volatility fund to reduce their hedging needs, this might not be the case in 2024. As of November, the fund had declined by 40% to TWD129bn (from 215bn, Figure 18). Given the ongoing reduction in the size of the fund and the recent pullback in USD/TWD, we could see hedging activities picking up again. Based on our estimates, every 1% increase in the hedging ratio is equal to \~\$8bn of LHS flows into the forward market, driving both onshore and offshore forward points further into negative territory.

Steepening in the local bond curve. In 2024, the Taiwan Ministry of Finance (MOF) plans to have 19 TGB auctions (including a 2y special bond auction in March and a 10y special bond auction in May). This would be two more auctions than in 2023. Looking at the split across the various tenors, the number of auctions will remain unchanged from 2023 for 10y, 20y and 30y TGBs, but the $2 \gamma$ and 5y will both increase by one. Based on the number of auctions in 2024, and the issuance size per auction in 2023, we estimate the gross issuance for TGB to be \~TWD560bn vs 478bn in 2023 while the net issuance to be TWD138bn vs 113bn in 2023. This will be similar to 2021-23 average. Given that auctions in the longer end of the curve over the past few months have been relatively poor (with lifers buying of TGBs weak, Figure 19) and small upsize in supply, we could see 2Y/10Y TGB curve steepening with the current spread (10bp) below the 2023 average of \~30bps.
