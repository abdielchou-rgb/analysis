## Key Takeaways

We focus on 8 key questions in '24, centered on energy transition, climate change, longevity, AI, carbon, supply chains, elections, and regulation.

The most actionable investment ideas: high quality energy transition business models, climate adaptation, using our social investment framework, and AI Adopters.

Stock selection resources: We include links to our preferred stocks list in Americas and EMEA; and '24 Sustainability outlooks from Fixed Income and APAC.

The resilience and evolution of sustainability. The "survival of the fittest" applies not only to biology but to the investment process. Companies that cannot evolve or innovate frequently lose market share to those that can, and we should not be surprised to see this principle in the world of Sustainability investing. We believe evolution in Sustainability investing in 2024 and beyond will center on prioritizing investments that drive alpha while meeting Sustainability goals. We continue to see an expansion of integration of ESG criteria into investors' decision-making process, and not surprisingly, we are seeing a wide range of approaches to the integration process. We believe 2024 will be an unusually complex year to navigate for Sustainability investing, given new rules and regulations, increasing physical damage from climate change, a likely rapid acceleration in the development of GenAI and the adoption of this technology across many sectors, a perhaps unavoidably chaotic energy transition, heightened geopolitical concerns/questions, and an increasing focus on "moving beyond the E in ESG" to consider other important investment criteria.

As we think about where key risks and opportunities lie for the year, we highlight 8 key questions we believe will matter to the sustainability investment community as we take to capitalize on the continued evolution of Sustainability investing:

3. Will carbon markets reflect the current pace of decarbonization? Major compliance markets and voluntary carbon markets (VCM) were oversupplied in 2023 and price action reflects both 1) lower demand due to lower emissions in Europe and the UK and 2) a significant sentiment shift on the authenticity of carbon neutral claims in the VCM. Global emissions rose in 2023, however, and limiting temperature to 1.5C is nearly scientifically unattainable. All else equal, higher emissions should translate to a higher cost of carbon, but the market is presently disconnected because of 1) where the emissions are currently being taxed and 2) a pullback from corporates to offset their residual emissions. In 2024, we think compliance markets will continue to reflect regional trends rather than global as we are still years from sufficient implementation of carbon pricing in markets in which emissions are rising and border taxes to combat leakage. On the voluntary side, we think the market will continue to be oversupplied not because of a lack of emissions to offset but because of continued tepid desire to use carbon-reducing credits against real emissions. That said, we expect to see greatest interest in carbon removal technologies (e.g., DAC, CCS, BECCS, and afforestation/reforestation) and in programs such as the ICVCM, and are most constructive on price action in these markets.

4. Will growing inequality and interest in longevity influence the rise in social investing? Growing global population and resulting trends provide opportunities for corporates to address social needs. This is why we continue to believe the investment opportunity for social will grow. We see investment opportunities in our global "culture club" plays (see Exhibit 4 ). We also highlight 5 core themes in social investing we expect to get increased attention in coming months & years, namely Access to Healthcare, Nutrition & Fitness, Social Infrastructure, Access to Finance and Education/Reskilling.

6. How will geopolitical tensions (continue) to rewire global supply chains? Supply chain shortages during Covid-19, the war in Ukraine, and geopolitical issues (such as US-China trade tensions) have exacerbated trends around the rewiring of the global economy. As our economists, policy experts, and sector teams have written, companies are going to spend a significant amount of capital trying to make their operations more resilient to geopolitical risks and reduce choke points. Manufacturing companies, industrial automation and robotics are at the core of this rewiring, benefiting the US, Southeast Asia, and Mexico. We see environmental and social implications from this global rewiring.

7. Will global elections impact ESG and progress toward sustainability goals? More than 60 countries, representing half of the world population will hold regional, legislative, or presidential elections. These election results, in our view, will be key catalysts for what's next in the energy transition and sustainability broadly. We discuss our views on why these elections matter and how will they impact key legislation by region.

# Is the Energy Transition Investable Globally?

We are biased toward investment strategies that favor stock selectivity over thematic investing for the energy transition. We expect investors to capitalize off of negative sentiment toward the energy transition with particular focus on players with scale that have high barriers to entry / large economic moats, strong balance sheets, and adequate access to capital. In our view, we believe that companies that face risks of commoditization, are cash flow constrained, and/or are exposed to significant execution risks will continue to struggle. This is why we are biased toward investment strategies that favor stock selectivity over thematic investing for the energy transition.

Why we believe the energy transition remains investable? The favorable economics of renewables in many parts of the world coupled with the need for the world to decarbonize will continue to drive the energy transition. While there are real challenges that must be considered largely the economics of the energy transition, we believe the value proposition from the shift to cleaner business models continues to be highly attractive in much of the world, and see continuing signs of further cost reductions across a wide range of decarbonization technologies. See 'Global Renewables – New Interactive Cost Map: Value Creation Alive and Well' and 'Clean Power: Deflation Path Supercharges Adoption' for global cost projections by technology.

Although challenges with the energy transition continues to exist ... such as higher cost of capital, interconnection bottlenecks and permitting issues in the US, inventory destocking, and labor shortages, among others issues … we do not believe they impact all players equally. We highlight our preferred players where growth potential is not currently reflected in the stock prices. See Exhibit 2 for our list of preferred stocks.

Additionally, there are technologies that could have a greater than expected disruptive effect on the energy transition and we expect greater appreciation for within the investment community. Three key examples include CCUS, long duration energy storage, and generative AI.

1. Carbon capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS): Carbon capture is an important decarbonization tool, particularly for hard-to-abate industries, such as cement, steel and chemicals. Morgan Stanley's Energy team has estimated that the TAM for carbon capture could be \$30 billion by 2030 based on only 40% of the growth in the IEA’s Net Zero scenario, this increases to \$225 billion by 2050 (see note). We believe 2024 will bring increased focus on CCUS; we will see 'first capture' for several new CCUS projects, with \~20 Mtpa of additional global capture capacity expected to start up — a \~40% increase in total operating capacity. In our recent note How to Capture CO , we delve into available carbon capture technologies, exploring applications, cost, commercialization, and ways to play.

2. Long duration energy storage (LDES): We are paying close attention to long-duration iron-air batteries that allow for high renewables penetration while maintaining grid reliability and avoiding high power price inflation. The potential capital cost of iron-air storage technology product, such as the iron-air product developed by private company Form Energy, could be well below \$20/kWh, which is a small fraction of the cost of lithium-ion batteries used by the EV sector and compares very favorably to the variable cost of a natural gas-fired turbine in the US of \~\$50/MWh (\$5 nat gas \* 9.1 heat rate, plus \$5/MWh variable O&M as per EIA data). Several US utilities have entered into definitive agreements with Form Energy for commercialization of this technology (see here). We believe LDES could entirely displace natural gas-fired power plants in the Power sector.

3. Generative AI: In our view, the most promising uses of GenAI to reduce ${ \mathsf { C O } } _ { 2 }$ -equivalent emissions and/or mitigate the impacts of climate change are: material science, power grid optimization, smart agriculture, climate modeling, development of new clean energy sources, reducing its own emissions from model training, and many other use cases. While we are still in the early stages of the full capabilities of genAI, we think this year could be a monumental year as more models come into production and research and development continues to evolve. We discuss the impact of genAI and its benefits to climate change further in the section What's Next for AI & ESG and Where Are the Opportunities? .

## How Should We Think About Climate Adaptation and Resilience?

According to Copernicus, 2023 was the warmest year on record, with global temperatures set to reach near the 1.5 degree C limit. The World Meteorological Organization (WMO) stated that the "warming El Niño event, which emerged during the Northern Hemisphere spring of 2023 and developed rapidly during summer, is likely to further fuel the heat in 2024 because El Niño typically has the greatest impact on global temperatures after it peaks." In addition to warming temperatures, in 2023, we also saw record ocean temperatures which intensified extreme weather. Given these data points, we believe that investor interest on the physical risk of climate change will grow with specific interest in the interrelated impacts of climate change — increased damage from storms, water challenges and food impacts, and how a warmer climate may drive the need for more energy to cool the world. We believe the Insurance sector can provide important "early warning" signals that investors should spend more time assessing, as we showed in our joint note written with our Insurance and Real Estate colleagues (here).

We believe investors underappreciated climate related risks (physical and transitional) despite material evidence showing that climate-related risks negatively impact financial returns. # Will Carbon Markets Reflect the Current Pace of Global Decarbonization?

Price action in both major compliance and voluntary markets reflected weak demand in 2023 for different reasons: in the UK's and the EU's emissions trading schemes, demand softened as power demand and industrial output and emissions fell; in the voluntary market, the benefits of buying carbon credits rapidly dissipated as public sentiment turned against credit usage as part of a corporate's decarbonization strategy. On the other hand, global emissions are likely to be up 1.1% in 2023 over 2022 levels per the Global Carbon project and even declines in major developed countries will leave the Earth's warming likely well above 1.5C.

With that said, we think the big question for 2024 for the carbon markets will be whether or not global markets reflect the lagging pace of decarbonization. We look ahead to Europe's carbon border adjustment mechanism to drive carbon pricing outside of the EU (and thus, into regions where emissions continue to rise). On the voluntary side, we believe that quasi-regulation due to begin in 2024 will provide support for some project types, but that demand could remain tepid as corporates continue to grapple with the reputational risks associated with being vocal about credit purchases in a somewhat hostile environment.

## Domestic Policies and Border Taxes

We are increasingly focused on the preparation for and expansion of Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanisms (CBAMs). In Europe, the initial phase of required reporting has already begun ahead of scheduled full launch on January 1, 2026. Our base case scenario is that the EU's CBAM proceeds as expected and as currently legislated. The most immediate consequence to the EU's CBAM is, in our view, the proliferation of 1) other domestic carbon pricing schemes, by countries that have trade exposure to the EU and 2) other carbon border taxes, as those countries with domestic pricing schemes similarly adopt border taxes to reduce carbon leakage and preserve domestic competitiveness.

We have begun to see a number of examples in recent months of countries embarking on these approaches. As of 2023, there are 73 carbon pricing initiatives implemented covering 23% of global carbon emissions, per the World Bank. There are numerous other domestic schemes under consideration, with announcements from Turkey and Brazil in recent weeks alone. In the UK, the government recently announced a carbon tax on imported goods beginning in 2027. We are monitoring other countries that are considering such policies, such as Canada (that also just announced a new federal oil and gas cap-and-trade program) and the US (see a summary of four proposed bills here).

Governments are incentivized to establish their own carbon pricing system at a level equivalent to Europe's by the EU CBAM as companies will be taxed on the embedded carbon of the good net any carbon tax already paid. Therefore, all else equal, it is better for exporting nations to retain that carbon levy if it would instead be taxed by and paid to the EU. However, there are other paths forward for companies that export into regions with border levies, most obviously by reducing embedded emissions directly (for importers the corollary is to purchase low-carbon goods and avoid the pass-through of the tax).

## Voluntary Carbon Markets

## Pricing

We anticipate a slight rebound in some market segments for carbon offsets in 2024, but do not expect the futures market as is to meaningfully reflect this change. Prices fell significantly in the voluntary carbon markets in 2023 in both spot and futures markets, driven by a dwindling market sentiment that carbon credit purchases represent a legitimate sustainability strategy by corporates in the market as it is today. There are technical reasons as to why the futures market in particular suffered a material price drop of >90% from peak pricing; the heterogeneity of the market works to the disadvantage of the futures contracts, with the perceived lowest-quality projects eligible for delivery into the contracts despite little appetite from buyers to engage with these lower-integrity products. With the market facing a reckoning amidst a continuous stream of news around concerns that the vast majority of carbon offsets are "worthless" it comes as no surprise that projects that have fallen out of favor are being priced into the futures delivery. In addition, while many projects have largely held their price despite this criticism, the uncertainty around what will actually get delivered weighs on prices.

In the first half of 2024, we anticipate that the ICVCM will begin to ratify that certain project methodologies align with its Core Carbon Principles (CCP). In the absence of formal progress on Article 6 at COP28, we expect that the ICVCM's standard will be the most widely accepted by the market. Projects that are issued under approved methodologies should be met with greater demand than those that do not, though it is not clear yet to us how much damage the past year's reckoning will have done to the market overall. We continue to think that the demand for high-cost, highly measurable products like Direct Air Capture (DAC) signal the market's continued appetite for complementary carbon solutions but that the price point is too prohibitive for most corporates (and even for companies flush with cash, cancelling out 100% of its remaining emissions with DAC is impossible given serious supply constraints). Thus, corporates that otherwise would have looked to credits are left with two options: 1) step back from carbon credit projects altogether and 2) circle back to lower-cost, higher-risk projects and find those with a third-party stamp of approval, such as by the ICVCM.

With that said, there are numerous other initiatives underway which may prolong the market fragmentation that currently exists. We view the failure to operationalize Article 6 at COP28 as a material setback for the market, and see risk in having too many NGOs, quasi-regulators and regulators looking to get involved with this market. Indeed, we view the product heterogeneity as one of the largest headwinds to market development in the VCM and believe that with too many cooks in the kitchen the market will remain fractured through 2024. We look to the ICVCM as the front-runner at the moment but think that if there's always a feeling that another standard or regulation is just around the corner that the wait-and-see atmosphere will persist.

## Regulatory Outlook

As we wrote in Carbon Offsets: A Reckoning, then Progress, there are myriad efforts underway that seek to build integrity within the voluntary carbon markets and to create a product that is reliable and widely commercial. However, failure to forge an agreement on Article 6 (and thus to create a UN-backed framework for an international carbon credit trading system) is a setback for the market that is already struggling to agree on 1) what actually constitutes a "good" credit; 2) the merits of avoidance credits, particularly nature based ones; and 3) how to adequately communicate carbon credit usage and its role as part of a broader corporate decarbonization strategy.

2024 will see the beginning of labelling of carbon offset project types that pass through rigorous assessment by the leading supply-side integrity initiative in the market — the ICVCM. This important step will provide a fresh start for a market that has experienced a 90% selloff in futures, and we expect projects that are granted the CCP label by the ICVCM to be strongly preferred by buyers. While we expect some avoidance projects (both nature and tech-based) to benefit from this restored confidence, we think the market will by and large continue to favor removals and prices for these projects should remain elevated. CORSIA Phase 1 also begins, and so we expect demand for projects from airlines to be a dominant market force. # How Will Geopolitical Tensions Continue to Rewire Global Supply Chains?

Supply chain shortages during Covid-19, the war in Ukraine, and geopolitical issues (such as US-China trade tensions) have exacerbated trends around the rewiring of the global economy. As our economists, policy experts, and sector teams have written, companies are going to spend a significant amount of capital trying to make their operations more resilient to geopolitical risks and reduce choke points. Manufacturing companies, industrial automation and robotics are at the core of this rewiring, benefiting the US, Southeast Asia, and Mexico. There are numerous ESG implications of this rewiring that investors should pay attention to, in our view, including:

• On the Environmental front, carbon footprints are set to change drastically as high energy intensive activities are relocated, with the ultimate impacts dependent on the energy mix of local electric grids (see here for the carbon intensity of the electric grid by country). A favorable dynamic is the shortening of transportation routes to get products to their final destination. We expect supply chain management to gain relevance as regulation in the EU and the US (in draft form) requires the disclosure of scope 3 emissions and, at least in the case of the EU, the disclosure of impacts and dependencies to nature and biodiversity.

• On the Social front, in 2023 we saw increased focus on supply chain risks particularly relating to human/labor rights dynamics (more on this below), a trend that we expect will continue as the rewiring of global economies and the energy transition take shape. Beyond the clear labor implications (positive for some countries and detrimental for other), we believe nearshoring provides better oversight and reduces financial, reputational, and legal risks.

• Other points to consider: We continue to monitor the impact of geopolitical risks in respect to the energy transition specifically related to EV battery supply chain and what it may mean for growing EV adoption around the world. We discussed this extensively here. We also monitor AI supply chain.

See our podcasts series Thinking Thematics for our global teams' work on the topic of a Multipolar World.

Supply-chain related controversies can cause severe stock price reaction. For companies that are flagged as potentially being in violation of human rights standards (such as the UN Guiding Principles (UNGPs) on Business and Human Rights, the OECD's Due Diligence Guidance for Responsible Business Conduct and the CSDDD for companies operating in Europe), we have seen many instances of fairly rapid decisions to divest, especially in cases in which it appears challenging for a company to clearly, quickly and unequivocally refute the allegation. In many markets, we see increasing conflict over land, water, and air use, between First Nations/indigenous people and extractive industries — an area that warrants more attention as the energy transition and political trade issues unfold.

## EMEA

In February 2023, the European Commission unveiled new temporary measures to ease strict rules on national subsidies (“state aid”), allowing countries to match offers of third countries if they would otherwise result in production being lured away from Europe. On January 8, 2023, Germany became the first country to utilise the scheme and grant Swedish battery maker Northvolt €902 million to build its production site in Heide vs it being built in Nebraska, USA.

Furthermore, mandatory due diligence is driving increased focus on sustainable supply chain practices. In addition to CSDR (see Sustainability: Question of the Moment: Who is reporting what and when? (28 Nov 2023), the EU has proposed the Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive (CSDDD), which will set obligations for large companies regarding the adverse impacts of their supply chains on human rights and the environment. The directive also lays down rules on penalties and civil liability for infringing those obligations. Furthermore, it requires companies to adopt a plan ensuring that their business model and strategy are compatible with the Paris agreement on climate change. So far, a provisional agreement has been reached between the two colegislators. The scope of the directive is set to apply to large EU companies with >500 employees and a net worldwide turnover of €150 million and to non-EU companies with a €300 million net turnover generated in the EU three years from the entry into force of the directive — a list of non-EU companies which will fall under the directive will be published by the EU Commission. The financial sector will be temporarily excluded from the scope of the directive, pending a review for possible future inclusion based on a sufficient impact assessment. We note that Germany also recently enacted the Supply Chain Due Diligence Act, which obliges German organizations to comply with human rights and environment-related standards in their supply chains.

The EU Deforestation Regulation will also come into force toward the end of 2024. The regulation will require companies trading specific commodities (cattle, wood, cocoa, soy, palm oil, coffee, rubber, as well as some of their derived products) to conduct extensive diligence on their value chains to ensure products do not originate from recent deforestation or contribute to forest degradation. The regulation is already impacting staples companies' sourcing strategies, with reports that importers into the EU have started to scale back purchases from some small-scale coffee farmers in Africa where mapping supply chains can be prohibitively complex and laborious (see Reuters article).

# Will Global Elections Impact ESG and Progress Toward Sustainability Goals?

Some 4 billion people in more than 50 countries, which is nearly half of the world's population, are set to vote in national elections, with outcomes likely to sharpe global politics for years or decades to come. From a sustainability perspective, these election outcomes could have an impact on progress on investments related to the energy transition, climate adaptation and resilience, education, healthcare, diversity and inclusion, among other topics. We highlight key elections across regions:

## Americas

As our public policy experts wrote (here), absent a recession, the 2024 US presidential election is expected to be focused on issues like AI regulation, energy permitting, and trade policy, with an eye toward the economic relationship with China — all key topics for the energy transition theme. Focusing on the IRA, in the event of a Democratic presidential victory (whether there is a Dem sweep or divided government), we expect the IRA to roll out as planned and a focus on AI regulation. In the event of a Republican presidential victory we anticipate the attempt of a full or partial IRA repeal, as well as efforts at the Treasury to implement criteria that limit how much of the approved tax credits could be used in practice. A Republican sweep theoretically makes both outcomes more likely, but legislative efforts are less likely in a divided R Congress. Although we believe that a full-scale repeal is unlikely, a partial repeal or tightening of tax credit requirements could impair unit economics and the growth profile of renewable energy. The stock implications of this outcome vary considerably depending on what areas of the IRA the administration attempts to repeal or restrict. Our sector team details stock implications here. On trade policy, we are particularly cautious of what a R presidency could mean for industries highly dependent on China, particularly if IRA benefits are flowing to Chinese companies — as is the case of the EV battery industry, which could hinder EV penetration rates and thus the decarbonization potential of the Autos sector.

Outside the US, there are several 2024 presidential elections in the Americas that could have large implications for discussions surrounding climate change in the near and medium terms. # What Are the Rules & Regulations Worth Watching in 2024?

## Americas

We are watchful of 3 key topics: 1) The SEC's final ruling on the Climate Disclosure Rule, expected anytime now. As we published earlier this year (here), we believe the most likely changes would be the elimination or delay of Scope 3 emissions reporting requirements, as well as the financial impact disclosure threshold potentially going from 1% to 5% in terms of materiality for each financial statement line. We see likely legal challenges brought up against the ruling. Similarly, at the state level, we anticipate to see amendments to the recently enacted California climate bills (SB 253 and SB 261 specifically) and related legal battles (see here). 2) SEC updated rules on Human Capital Management (HCM) Disclosures — following the adoption of new HCM rules in 2020 that required companies to disclose human capital risks and resources as well as a July 2023 Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB) proposal to mandate that companies disaggregate the reporting of major operating costs included in the income statement, including employee compensation costs, the SEC is expected to release proposed rules to strengthen existing workforce disclosure rules and allow for better assessment of risks and opportunities. 3) The anti-ESG movement in the country — in 2023 alone, over 150 bills were introduced against ESG investment criteria according to Green Central Banking (see here), though nearly half of the bills failed. Today, \~75% of US States have brought at least one bill that restricts the use of ESG factors (see here).

## EMEA

The European Sustainability Reporting Standards (ESRS) will be implemented from January 2024. ESRS is intended to provide a standardised framework for implementation and reporting of a broad range of environmental, social and governance issues under the Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD) law. Our recent note Who is reporting what and when? discusses ESRS, outlining what the standards include, who will have to report under them and what the feedback has been.

We are also looking forward to the outcome of the latest consultation on SFDR that seeks to address the implementation challenges with the current regime and the potential introduction of a new product categorisation system. As the EU Deforestation law comes into force at the end of 2024, there might be more guidance on its implementation and ensuring traceability of products. Finally, in the UK, FCA will consult on updating its TCFDaligned disclosure rules for listed companies to refer to UK-endorsed ISSB standards, which might result in certain reporting requirements becoming mandatory for listed firms in the UK.

For more details on upcoming EU & UK regulation in 2024, please see Sustainability: EU & UK ESG Regulatory Tracker: Jan 2024 (9 Jan 2024).

## APAC

Starting from 2024, we will see staggered alignment of corporate sustainability disclosures with the ISSB standards. Pursuant to the publishing of disclosure standards under International Sustainability Standards Board (ISSB), we see regulators announcing their roadmaps to align disclosure frameworks with the ISSB standards. However, progress could be staggered by jurisdiction. While Australia is looking to introduce sustainability reporting from 3Q24, Japan is releasing its initial draft for sustainability disclosure standards in 1Q24. Hong Kong delayed implementation of enhanced climate disclosures to January 2025, hinging on the finalization of the ISSB Adoption Guide in 2024.

Regulatory focus on defining sustainability investments and the crackdown on greenwashing will not go away. IOSCO published a document on supervisory practices for addressing greenwashing in December 2023, laying out initiatives including: 1) asset manager practices and disclosures; 2) product disclosures and 3) code of conduct for ESG ratings and data providers. We believe these approaches will continue to be replicated by regulators.

APAC regulators are also expected to lay out criteria and guidance on the transition. Singapore released its Multi-Sector Transition Taxonomy in December 2023, setting out detailed thresholds for "transition activities." Similarly, Australia's Sustainable Finance Taxonomy is likely to treat "green" and "transition" activities separately, which should commence public consultation in March 2024.

For more details on upcoming APAC ESG regulations in 2024, please see Asia Sustainability: ESG Regulatory Tracker – January 2024 (9 Jan 2024).
