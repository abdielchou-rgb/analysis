# European Economic Perspectives

## Germany's growth weakness: Facts and some hope

## Weak German GDP growth in focus

In recent client conversations about Europe, the ongoing growth weakness in Germany was a key topic. While most clients thought that we may have seen the cyclical trough (even though recent survey data remained mixed), they worried about structural headwinds that could prolong Germany's underperformance vis-à-vis its peers. In this chart-based report, we take a closer look at the facts and offer our assessment.

## Fact no. 1: Four-year stagnation in real GDP, significantly lagging peers

Real GDP in Germany is up just 0.1% since Q4 2019, while it is up 3.6% in Italy, 1.8% in France, 2.9% in Spain and 3% in the Eurozone (Fig. 3). Breaking the overall period down into Covid (Q4 2019-Q1 2022) and the energy crisis (Q1 2022-Q3 2023) shows that the German economy was almost equally weak in both stretches of time (Fig. 5). This suggests that the underperformance is not just reflecting the energy price shock hitting manufacturing (which at 22% has a larger share in GDP than elsewhere, Fig. 4), but is a broader phenomenon.

## Fact no. 2: Manufacturing was not the main problem

Looking at the demand side of the economy shows that German GDP performance was particularly weak in household consumption and fixed investment (Fig. 7). More surprising is the picture on the supply side of the economy. This shows that German services sector activity was much weaker than in all other large Eurozone countries (Fig. 6). Underperformance is particularly visible in the trade, travel and accommodation sectors. This is in contrast to conventional wisdom, which often argues that manufacturing is the main culprit behind Germany's underperformance. For example, French manufacturing is down 4.3% over the past four years, much weaker than Germany (-0.8%), Fig. 8.

## Our assessment: Weaker potential growth

The prolonged and broad-based growth weakness in Germany suggests that this is more than just cyclical weakness and points to structural factors weighing on growth. Indeed, estimates of potential growth, such as those by the OECD, show that Germany's is now the lowest among the large Eurozone countries. Various estimates suggest that potential growth, or Germany's "new normal" growth rate, is now likely between 0.5% and 1%, down from 1½% during the 2010s, Fig. 14.

## Headwind no. 1: Population ageing

A key factor behind the decline in potential growth estimates is the ageing of the workforce, which is progressing faster in Germany than elsewhere in this decade. UN population projections suggest that the working-age population (15-64 years) in Germany is declining by c.8% during the 2020s (about 500k persons per year), compared with just 1½% in France, Fig. 15. According to estimates by the Council of Economic Experts, the declining labour supply is subtracting ½pp from potential growth on average during the 2020s (after adding 0.4pp during the 2010s, Fig. 13). Widespread labour shortages in Germany, which are higher than elsewhere, are likely related to the scarcity of available labour even as labour force participation of the 55-64 age cohort at 76.8% is 10pp higher than in the Eurozone.

## Headwind no. 3: Exposure to and competition from China

In addition, the car sector is most exposed to exports from China and is faced with competition from EVs, not least from Chinese producers. German motor vehicles do not only have the largest share in German exports, they also have the largest export exposure to China across all goods, Fig. 21. As such, they are vulnerable to weaker demand from China. In addition, the car sector faces competition in world markets. ## Bottom line and three reasons not to become too pessimistic

(1) If we are right with our view about a decline in potential growth related to ageing, Germany would remain a growth laggard. While efforts are being made to increase skilled migration, the scale and speed of the decline in working-age population make it unlikely that it can be offset by policy measures. Even so, the current stagnation of real GDP overstates Germany's structural decline and there is room for a cyclical upswing (we project GDP to grow 0.8% in 2025, up from 0.3% in 2024, but weaker than the Eurozone at 1.2%). After all, export orders in surveys are rising, and the labour market is very robust, Fig. 27/28. (2) Given the headwinds noted above, we would expect the share of manufacturing in GDP to decline from its elevated level over time, as it has done for some years. However, the performance in past years has also shown that one should not underestimate the ability of the sector to adjust to shocks. For example, energy intensity in energy-intensive industries has declined in recent years and in particular during the energy crisis, Fig. 30. (3) Lastly, Germany's fiscal situation remains much more comfortable than in other countries. In marked contrast to other countries, debt/GDP is at about the same level as 25 years ago, giving economic policy the space to buffer the adjustment, Fig. 31. Potential policy areas include lowering corporate tax rates (which are among the highest in the OECD) and increasing public investment (lower as a share of GDP than in many other large economies), Fig. 32/33. The question is will this fiscal space be used, which would arguably require some relaxation of the strict debt-brake fiscal rule? We would expect some steps in this direction, but would caution that a fullscale overhaul faces political constraints, at least in the near term, with looming elections in 2025 and a lack of support for the government coalition, according to opinion polls.

## Fact no. 1: Four-year stagnation in real GDP, significantly lagging peers

German activity has remained essentially unchanged since pre-Covid, with real GDP standing just 0.1% above its Q4 2019 level. Over the same time, Eurozone real GDP has risen 3%, French GDP has increased 1.8%, Italy is up 3.6% and Spain 2.9% (Fig. 3). Cyclical survey indicators do not yet show convincing signs that the growth weakness is gradually fading, but indicate that GDP may at least be bottoming out (Fig. 1/2). However, there is a concern that Germany's relative underperformance is here to stay. After all, other countries have been facing similar cyclical headwinds from weak global demand, tighter financing conditions and fiscal consolidation, even though Germany's large manufacturing sector leaves it particularly exposed to global demand (Fig. 4).

## Fact no. 2: Manufacturing was not the main problem

The past four years were characterised by two very different shocks: Covid and the energy crisis. While Germany was particularly exposed to the energy crisis, given its reliance on gas and the high share of manufacturing, it was arguably less exposed to Covid, given its smaller services sector (which suffered in particular during the lockdowns). However, when we decompose the GDP performance into different periods, it becomes apparent that Germany underperformed the Eurozone both during Covid and during the energy crisis (Fig. 5).

Next, we dissect Germany's growth performance both via the expenditure side (GDP) and the sectoral detail on the supply side (gross value added).

Looking at the expenditure side, it is particularly the weakness of German household consumption, and to a lesser extent fixed investment, that stands out (Fig. 7). Household consumption has deducted 0.9pp from GDP growth since Q4 2019, while it is up elsewhere except for Italy. And German gross fixed investment has deducted 1pp from the growth performance since Q4 2019, explaining almost all of the difference in GDP performance vis-à-vis Italy and France (where investment has added 4.1pp and 1.1pp, respectively). Were it not for a large positive inventory contribution, German growth would have been even weaker.

Looking at the sectoral breakdown on the supply side, Germany's growth performance since Q4 2019 has been particularly weak in services sectors, notably trade, travel, accommodation, and arts and entertainment (Fig. 6). Despite having seen the smallest decline during Covid in the trade, travel and accommodation sectors, Germany has lagged behind especially over the past 1½ years when inflation was high (fitting with the weakness in private consumption since then) while the other countries benefited from rebounding tourism (Fig. 9/11). Somewhat surprisingly, while the performance of German manufacturing was weak (deducting 0.2pp from growth since Q4 2019), this was still better than in France (-0.5pp) (Fig. 8). So one conclusion contrary to conventional wisdom is that manufacturing has not been the only, and not even the most pressing, problem facing Germany's economy, but rather large parts of the services sector.

## Box: Manufacturing – the divergence between production and value added statistics

When discussing the theme of "deindustrialisation", it matters which data points are looked at: the monthly industrial production index or the quarterly statistics on gross value added. In the short term, fluctuations in both measures are correlated, but over the past few years, a large level divergence has opened up. Notably, the manufacturing production index today is around 7% lower than it was at the beginning of 2015 while gross value added in manufacturing is around 7% higher since then. In other words, while industrial production statistics fuel the impression that there is substantial "deindustrialisation", this is less apparent when looking at value added, which has held up better. While this divergence already increased pre-Covid, it has accelerated since then.

Both statistics measure different things: the production index measures the volume of produced goods, while gross value added measures the additional value created in the production process. Also, different measurement methods could play a role here; for example, the monthly production index excludes very small firms. Another explanation could be structural changes in the production process (see ifo Schnelldienst, 2024, 77, No. 2). For example, capital-intensive industrial production may have been outsourced abroad and research-intensive qualified production maintained domestically. This could explain why value added has increased despite less domestic production. To this point, ifo (2024) notes that the share of equipment investment in total corporate investment has declined since the 1990s while the share of other investment (e.g. R&D) has increased. Similarly, domestic production of motor vehicles has declined over the past 20 years while production abroad has more than doubled over the same period and domestic value added has increased. Lastly, the divergence between production and value added is more pronounced in Germany than elsewhere.

## Our assessment: Weaker potential growth

Taking a broader perspective, while relative German underperformance has stood out over the past four years, this should not be entirely surprising, given that potential growth is weakening, according to most estimates. For example, the OECD estimates that German potential growth has declined from around 1½% during most of the 2010s to just below 1% (Fig. 14). The German Council of Economic Experts (Fig. 13) and the Bundesbank are more pessimistic, with potential growth estimates of ½% currently.

## Headwind no. 1: Population ageing

A key reason for these declining estimates is the fall in working-age population due to demographic ageing. UN population projections suggest that the working-age population (15-64 years) in Germany is declining by c.8% during the 2020s (about 500k persons per year), compared with just 1½% in France (Fig. 15). Historically lower fertility rates in Germany help explain the difference between Germany and France (Fig. 16). According to estimates by the Council of Economic Experts, the declining labour supply is deducting ½pp from potential growth on average during the 2020s (after adding 0.4pp during the 2010s), Fig. 13. Widespread labour shortages in Germany, which are higher than elsewhere, are likely related to the scarcity of available labour even though labour force participation of the 55-64 age cohort at 76.8% is 10pp higher than in the Eurozone.

## Headwind no. 2: High energy prices

Even though Germany's structural problems cannot all be blamed on manufacturing, the sector undoubtedly faces multiple headwinds. One of these is high energy prices. The sectoral composition of industrial production shows that energy-intensive production remains 22% lower than two years ago, while in other sectors it is down 3%. However, even prior to the energy crisis of 2022/23, electricity and gas prices in Germany were higher than elsewhere, notably compared with the US (Fig. 17/18). Fo example, EU gas prices were 6x more expensive than US natural gas prices on average during 2010-19, and are currently still 15x more expensive, despite the recent declines. Industrial electricity prices in Germany were around twice as high as in the US on average during 2010-19 and also higher than in France. One consequence is that energy-intensive manufacturing has seen a steady decline in its share of GDP (from 7.5% in the early 1990s to 5% in 2021).

# Bottom line and three reasons not to become too pessimistic

## Structural weakness does not prevent a cyclical upswing

If we are right with our view about a decline in potential growth related to ageing, Germany would remain a growth laggard. While efforts are being made to increase skilled migration, the scale and speed of the decline in working-age population make it unlikely that it can be offset by policy measures. Even so, the current stagnation of real GDP overstates Germany's structural decline and there is room for a cyclical upswing (we project GDP to grow 0.8% in 2025, up from 0.3% in 2024, but weaker than the Eurozone at 1.2%). After all, new orders in surveys seem to have bottomed, and the labour market is very robust.

## Ability of the economy to adjust to shocks

Given the headwinds noted above, we would expect the share of manufacturing in GDP to decline from its elevated level over time, as it has done for some years. However, the performance in past years has also shown that the sector can adjust. For example, energy intensity in energy-intensive industries has declined in recent years, and in particular during the energy crisis. More broadly, German manufacturing was phased with many shocks in the past (not least currency appreciation in the pre-Euro period), suggesting that one should not underestimate the ability of manufacturing firms to adapt.

Germany's fiscal situation remains much more comfortable than in other countries. In marked contrast to other countries, debt/GDP is at about the same level as 25 years ago, giving economic policy the space to buffer the adjustment, Fig. 31. The question is will this fiscal space be used, which would arguably require some relaxation of the strict debt-brake fiscal rule? We would expect some steps in this direction, but would caution that a full-scale overhaul faces political constraints, at least in the near term, with looming elections in 2025 and a lack of support for the government coalition, according to opinion polls.
