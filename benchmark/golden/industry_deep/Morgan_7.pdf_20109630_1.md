## US Fixed Income Overview

## That escalated quickly

Economics: Nonfarm payrolls rose 114k in July while the unemployment rate increased 0.2%-pts to 4.3%. Against the backdrop of moderation in the labor market, we now expect the Fed to ease by 50bp at the September and November FOMC meetings, and cut by 25bp at each meeting thereafter

● Treasuries: Fed easing cyclicals suggest further room for yields to fall, but we stay neutral duration after a very large rally this week given the lack of near-term catalysts, but hold 5s/30s steepeners. Treasury yields gapped lower and credit spreads wider following weaker-than-expected labor market data (Figure 1). Most concerningly, the unemployment rate rose for the fourth consecutive month to 4.3%, 0.9%-pts off its cycle lows, as employment growth cooled last month to 114k. Labor demand is likely slowing as both hours and earnings softened last month while the diffusion index showed only 49.6% of industries added jobs in July, and notably this indicator has only dipped below 50 once outside of a recession (see Weak jobs to prompt strong Fed action, Michael Feroli, 8/2/24). This slowing narrative was corroborated by a suite of second tier reports including a large step down in the July ISM employment index, a substantial revision and move lower in the Consumer Confidence survey’s labor market differential, and continued cooling in June hiring and job openings which brought the vacancy/unemployment ratio to a new cycle low (see US: Confidence and

JOLTS consistent with easing labor market, Abiel Reinhart, 7/30/24). Moreover initial claims continues to move higher, jumping to a new 2024 high of 249k for the week ending July 27, but we caveat the impact of Hurricane Beryl could be biasing higher recent reports, and we look for any significant breakout of the NSA data from its typical summer plateau to discern further weakening (see US: Claims rise again, though Beryl likely still a factor, Abiel Reinhart, 8/1/24).

One key takeaway from Wednesday’s FOMC meeting is that the Committee is now “attentive to both sides of its dual mandate,” implying the risks of higher inflation and weaker labor markets are now more balanced (see Powell has labor market bee in his bonnet, Michael Feroli, 7/31/24). With Friday’s labor market data reinforcing the downside risks to employment, we think the Fed would like to have a policy setting closer to neutral. Hence, we now look for the FOMC to cut rates by 50bp at both the September and November meetings, followed by 25bp cuts at every meeting thereafter. From a risk management perspective, we think there’s a strong case to act before September 18<sup>th</sup>. But perhaps Powell doesn’t want to add more noise to what has already been an event-filled summer (see Economics). Historically, unscheduled monetary policy actions are uncommon, only occurring seven times since 2001.

Against this backdrop OIS markets are pricing in a full 25bp ease at the September FOMC meeting and 77% chance of a 50bp cut, as well as 119bp of total easing this year, not too far from our own forecast. However, rates markets are priced for a terminal rate near 3.00%, still well above our own forecast (Figure 2). Against this backdrop, we think there’s further room for yields to fall, and we revise our interest rate forecasts materially lower to reflect our revised Fed call: we now project 2- and 10-year Treasuries will reach 3.20% and 3.50%, respectively, by year end (Figure 3). Despite this medium-term outlook, locally we are hesitant to chase this rally. Intermediate yields dropped 40bp this week, the most over a 5-day window since the regional banking crisis, suggesting we could see yields stabilize in the near term, especially given a lighter data calendar next week. Thus, we remain neutral on duration, but would use any back up in yields to add duration exposure. We also recommend maintaining 5s/30s steepeners as an expression of our bullish view, which offers better carry and should benefit from structurally higher term premium. Moving in the curve, we think 2s/5s steepeners will become more attractive as we get closer to September (Treasuries).

Alongside the revisions to our nominal yield forecasts, we adjust our TIPS breakeven targets lower as well. Though breakevens across the curve are now somewhat cheap to our fair value framework, breakevens are likely to remain near current levels over coming months. We think there is room for the money market curve to invert somewhat further and expect commodity markets may remain focused on bearish 2025 dynamics. Hence, we adjust our year-end breakeven forecasts lower by 15-30bp lower across the curve, and we now see 5- and 10-year breakevens ending the year at 200bp (Figure 4We rvisoubakn dly tgw ch ). Combined with our lower nominal yield forecasts, we now forecast 10-year real yields falling to 1.50% by year-end and reaching 1.05% by mid-2025. Risks around this forecast remain two-sided. If recession dynamics take hold, breakevens could narrow further. However, we could see inflation risk premium start to get built back in along the curve, particularly if growth data do not weaken as much as feared over coming months. In light of the shift in our outlook, we remain neutral on breakevens at current levels (see TIPS).

Turning to derivatives markets, the pace and cumulative extent of the upcoming easing cycle remains highly uncertain. Indeed decomposing the Dec’24 SOFR futures options into specific rate outcomes reveal a sharp switch in market expectations from shallow to deep cutting expectations and an overall reduction in policy clarity further out. A backdrop of heightened policy uncertainty, combined with the seasonal trend of worsening liquidity as we enter August, is supportive of turning bullish on volatility, and we recommend buying 6Mx5Y swaption straddles on a delta-hedged basis. Given the sharp move lower in the expected path of policy rates, we turn more cautious with respect to seeking carry on the curve. Indeed with forward OIS rates no longer appearing rich relative to our revised expectations, we recommend stopping out of yield curve trades where positive carry was a key element. Meanwhile, suddenly-accelerated expectations of Fed easing should bias bond fund inflows higher, support lower term premium, and steepen the swap spread curve. Therefore, we turn neutral on the swap spread curve, while maintaining a widening bias on swap spreads (see Interest rate derivatives).

Away from monetary policy, Treasury held its August quarterly refunding process this week where it indicated it expects to keep nominal coupon auction sizes unchanged “for at least the next several quarters.” This was not surprising to us as our prior work indicated the current schedule leaves Treasury well funded until 2H25. Although the decisions were in line with our expectations, markets appeared relieved and pushed longer maturity swap spreads wider as well as term funding premium lower. Outside of this debt management announcement, TBAC also raised the recommended optimal T-bill share of debt to an average 20% over time. The presenter argued that 15% should remain a lower bound which supports healthy market functioning, but given structurally higher demand and that current valuations do not indicate an oversupply of T-bills, this supports a higher average over time and we think this effectively widens the optimal range from 15-20% to 15-25% (see US Treasury Market Daily, Jay Barry, 7/31/24).

Indeed over the near term we should see increased demand for T-bills as liquidity investors are likely to extend further out the money market curve with more aggressive Fed cuts coming into view. To be sure we have already witnessed better demand for 17-week and 26-week maturities as bid-to-cover ratios have steadily increased in recent weeks. However this is likely not to be the case in the credit space: the upcoming easing cycle remains highly uncertain, prime funds are looking for yield advantage over government funds ahead of key MMF reforms in October, and 6M SOFR FRNs still offer higher coupons than 6m fixed CP/CDs, at least until SOFR rests lower. Regardless, ON RRP balances should gradually decline to low-to-mid \$300bn levels in the near-term. Meanwhile SOFR at 5.35% as of August 1<sup>st</sup> has come down from elevated month-end levels of 5.38% but still remains above pre-monthend levels of 5.33%. Similar to past months, there has been a bit of a month-end hangover and dealer balance sheets still remain elevated, but we anticipate SOFR will decline, albeit at a slower pace (see Short term fixed income).

Lower coupon mortgage spreads moved towards the tighter ends of recent ranges while higher coupons were unwound as investors positioned down the stack into longer duration collateral following weak economic data. We think current Fed pricing promises to take some pressure off bank deposits while the BoJ decision this week to raise its policy rate brings FX-hedged buyers closer to finding MBS attractive. We caution that at these tight spread levels mortgages should still command some true risk premium, even in the upper belly where FN 5s offer reasonable ZVs and should benefit from lower vol, but only offer low teens OAS in our model. That said, we are seeing more green shoots in demand, as money manager inflows have been surprisingly robust and mREIT buying has been stronger this year than we had anticipated. As we inch closer to a Fed easing regime, we have received more questions on the evolution of the primary mortgage rate. Feeding forward rates into our model together with expectations around steeper curves and declining vol, we think primary rates could drop 60bp over the next two years under current market pricing - not enough to move the refi needle for most of the stack, but it could pull the higher coupons into the money (Figure 5It wouldake mrsignfc yh p   v- , see Agency MBS).

Down the credit spectrum, mortgage credit spreads have held in well despite seasonally unusually heavy issuance, which we expect will continue at a busy clip in August. While rates markets debate over two or three Fed cuts this fall, we think the end result is less consequential for mortgage credit and expect spreads will be sideways as we inch closer to the presidential election (see RMBS).

ABS spreads were mixed over the week, with heavy selling down the capital stack leading our indicative AAA to BB subprime auto credit curve to twist slightly steeper as AAA spreads tightened 5bp to Treasuries while BB spreads widened by 10bp to Treasuries. Monetary policy reactions aside, we expect demand for ABS will remain healthy given the attractive relative value, even with a bias toward being up in quality for sponsors/assets/structures. We continue to expect the consumer will remain healthy as we forecast the unemployment rate to remain low and we note it would need to increase significantly to adversely affect ABS credit ratings given excess spread cushioning, robust enhancements, and strong structures/triggers that protect bondholders. However balancing labor market concerns with relative value, we recommend IG rated subprime ABS. We think AAA consumer ABS should tighten given superb fundamentals while offering sizeable spread premia on high quality unsecured corporates (see ABS).

CMBS spreads were similarly little changed, with 5yr and 10yr conduit LCF AAAs each 1bp tighter week-over-week. Meanwhile Conduit CMBS AS to single-As underperformed similar duration single-A and BBB REIT bonds. We still like Conduit CMBS single-As as, while volatile, they offer adequate loss coverage, attractive spread pickup to corporates, and greater investor depth compared to BBBs with real money engagement. Meanwhile Q2 Costar data show a robust multifamily demand with a rebound in absorptions and firming rent growth. Robust demand against moderating supply deliveries should turn net absorptions positive in coming quarters, leaving occupancy rates to start to bottom out and setting the stage for potentially better rent growth into late 2025 (Figure 6). One offsetting feature may be an uptick in construction starts as we expect a modest reversal in the recent widening in cap rates (see CMBS).

High grade credit spreads widened 11bp to 119bp on the JULI, their highest levels YTD, on the back of lower yields and weaker economic data. Notably the 10s/30s spread curve has steepened to 22bp despite limited long-end supply, as the recent decline in yields has likely muted demand. With supply likely to shift from Financials to non-Financials as earning season progresses, issuance trends may add further steepening pressure to the spread curve (see JPM Daily Credit Strategy & CDS/CDX am update, Eric Beinstein, 7/31/24). Meanwhile we have seen a rebound in fund inflows while the decision of the BoJ to increase policy rates helps improve the hedging costs for overseas investors. To the extent these investors focus on the intermediate sector, this could further add steepening pressure to the 10s/30s spread curve. Away from market pricing, we note that secondary trading activity in the high grade market rose 21% y/y in 1H24, mostly concentrated in portfolio trading, and this led turnover to increase to a record 0.35%. Meanwhile lower market volatility over the first half of the year versus prior years led to a decline in CDX and credit ETF trading - products often used to hedge cash positions and manage volatility (see US Credit Market Liquidity: 1H24 Update, Eric Beinstein et al., 8/1/24).

High yield bond yields declined 7bp to a 2-year low of 7.71% while spreads widened 21bp to a 6-month high of 371bp as weaker data spurred concern the Fed is waiting too long to start cutting rates. More encouraging, the tone around earnings has improved while capital market activity has been the most elevated since February. By rating, BB and B spreads widened by 25bp while CCC spreads were roughly unchanged. Following changes to our rates forecasts, we now raise our YE24 HY spread forecast from 380bp to 500bp, which equates to 8.15% YTW and lowers our full-year total return forecast from 7.5% to 5.75%. Over the near-term the spike in VIX to a 9-month high is likely to be accompanied by wider HY spreads (see High Yield).

Amid swings in the political landscape, our credit strategists outline the potential impact of a Republican sweep on HG and HY markets, with four main takeaways. First, when it comes to tax policy, we think the extension of the TCJA and any modest reduction in the corporate tax rate would have a muted impact on markets. Instead new/extended tax cuts would represent further fiscal deterioration and increased long-term rates and rate volatility would weigh on markets, though there could be some offsetting technical factors for HG. Second, outside of growth and inflation impacts, higher tariffs would likely have more of a direct impact on HG markets given their heavy weighting towards multi-nationals. Third, while the Republican party has historically been critical of large industrial policy bills which have boosted capex spending, we think it is unlikely these policies are unwound under a Trump presidency given political optics and changing views among the Republican base. Likewise, while it is expected a Trump administration could lessen the strong anti-trust focus of the current administration, there could be support for a continued strong anti-trust approach toward the technology industry from certain Republican lawmakers. Fourth, less regulatory oversight proposed by Trump could be a positive for credit, but given near-record bullish valuations, it is likely to have a limited impact (see US Credit Impacts of Trump’s Economic Proposals: Thoughts on the potential macro and micro impacts across HG and HY and by sector, Eric Beinstein et al., 7/30/24).

Finally, our CLO strategists investigate the best time to invest in CLO equity, finding there is no one period that provides the best market timing. That said, purchasing CLO equity during times of stress - even with wider liability spreads - delivered strong six month forward returns in the past. Indeed, they show that equity performance is often higher when US CLO AAA primary spreads widen beyond the historical 65<sup>th</sup> percentile. Such environments are associated with steepening yield curves and that in turn is highly correlated with stronger growth. While this may seem counter-intuitive for strong returns to occur after periods of wider AAA/richer loans, we note this may be due to certain historical idiosyncracies in the data. Finally, they estimate that US CLO equity cashflows are on track to end the year with \~15% growth, with newer vintages (2018-2023) outperforming largely as they avoid the seasoning of collateral which previous vintages experienced (see CLOs).
