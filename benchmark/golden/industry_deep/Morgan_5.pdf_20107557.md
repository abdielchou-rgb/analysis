## Touching from a distance, further all the time

michael.s.hanson@jpmchase.com

The global expansion has proven far more resilient than many had anticipated. However, hopes for a Goldilocks soft-landing are being challenged by the accompanying sticky inflation that is cautioning central banks from starting their easing cycles too soon. Our conviction in both the sustainability of growth and in the stickiness of inflation leads us this week to reconfigure our global economic scenario probability tree and add a “High-for-long soft-landing<sup>”</sup> (HFLSL) scenario (Figure 1). This change has two key implications. First, rates that stay high-for-long are no longer a sufficient condition to “boil the frog<sup>”</sup> (i.e., cause a recession)—a possibility we have long recognized as underlying the interest insensitivity of mid-cycle fundamentals. A combination of strong fundamentals and more patient central banks could keep short-run neutral rates much higher than the long-run rate, yet sustain the expansion alongside elevated inflation. The second key implication is that, with this new scenario garnering a 30% probability, the odds of rates staying high for long is now 75%.

The latest data underscore the likelihood of our new HFLSL scenario. The US clearly leads the pack in both robust growth and sticky inflation. Last week<sup>’</sup>s payroll report showed businesses remain expansionary; we look for next week<sup>’</sup>s March retail sales report to show a healthy 0.5% jump in control. With growth strong, the risk is that inflation too will remain hot—a message sent clearly by this week<sup>’</sup>s March CPI report showing broad-based gains across the services categories and super-core accelerating to a super-hot 8.2%3m, saar (Figure 2). Chair Powell<sup>’</sup>s fading of the Jan-Feb surprises now looks to have been a bit hasty. Two points make a line, but three set a trend. Last week, we pushed out our call for the start of the Fed cutting cycle from June to July, but the case is building for an even later start.

Growth resilience and sticky inflation go beyond the boomy US. In Europe, the credit impulse looks to be turning up as this week<sup>’</sup>s 1Q Bank Lending Surveys show no further tightening of standards (Figure 3). We now see UK GDP having expanded 1.5%ar last quarter and raise our confidence that EMU GDP expands near 1%ar this quarter. In the EM, our nowcasters flag upside growth risks across much of the region and we expect China to report a strong 6.6%saar gain last quarter. Core services inflation is also proving far stickier around the world. This is seen in Western Europe but also underscored this week by CPI reports from Norway, Brazil, and Czechia.

The interactions among growth, inflation, and markets pose risks that temper the “soft-landing<sup>”</sup> likelihood in the HFLSL scenario:

Inflation risk: With inflation still running well above central bank comfort zones, and the latest reports proving stronger than expected, we also revise up this week to 20% the odds of a “Too darn hot<sup>”</sup> scenario that sees further policy tightening and a recession by next year. This remains our lowest probability scenario, but the revision up is a reminder that, as Dallas Fed president Logan continues to state, “rate hikes are not off the table.<sup>”</sup>

Market risk: The trimming of expected central bank easing in the HFLSL scenario is likely to threaten financial markets more broadly. The significant repricing of cuts over the past five months has had no impact on risk markets, but that owed in large part to a fading of recession concerns. The repricing that is now underway, which sees just two Fed cuts this year, is already spooking risk markets. It is unclear how well the HFLSL scenario can absorb this tightening.

Divergence risk: As we approach the start of easing cycles, the divergences of macro conditions that were masked by the overwhelming drive to get inflation down are coming into sharper relief. The strength of recoveries and inflation progress vary. This risk is that central banks become too aligned with the Fed and global policy tightens more than necessary. With the recovery stalling last year, the ECB this week expressed a desire to begin cuts soon and strongly argued it was not Fed dependent but rather data dependent. If the data include the currency, however, it may be hard to ignore the Fed so easily. Currency concerns are more prevalent in smaller, open economies (such as the Scandies and Canada), while financial stability concerns are an issue for a number of EM economies. Notably, despite a contraction in jobs last month and inflation well within its target zone, the BoC stayed on hold this week.

## Firm services inflation challenges Europe

Although the acceleration in US inflation raises some questions about spillovers elsewhere, much of Europe has its own sticky inflation to worry about. The biggest challenge to the ECB<sup>’</sup>s signal for a June rate cut is that services inflation is running at a 5%ar so far this year. We have long incorporated sticky inflation into our call for a later move by the BoE, and a recent pickup in growth reinforces that picture. Meanwhile, across the CEE a fall in headline CPI inflation is obscuring underlying strength in core inflation dynamics, particularly for services.

While we stick with our June call for the ECB to start cutting, risks are shifting toward a slower easing cycle. Unit labor costs remain elevated with firm growth in both employment and wages as labor productivity remains weak. The pickup in the PMIs also casts doubt on the ECB<sup>’</sup>s expectations that a squeeze in profit margins will relieve inflationary pressures. As a result, the June ECB staff forecasts could acknowledge upward pressure on inflation, denying the ECB the confidence it seeks to cut then. For the UK, a strong February GDP report this week shows first-quarter GDP tracking 1%-pt above the BoE<sup>’</sup>s forecast. We continue to look for a first cut in August.

One central bank bucking this trend has been the Riksbank. Currency weakness has introduced some risk into our expectation for a May cut, but this week<sup>’</sup>s weaker-than-expected CPI report has put that call back on track. With Sweden having recently been through a recession, the risk of passthrough from stronger wages or FX weakness into higher inflation there is seen as low.

## Asia FX magnifies Fed spillover risk

Asia is particularly sensitive to the potential spillover from the repricing of the Fed cutting cycle. This is because it is a region that saw a slower recovery and less of an inflationary surge than elsewhere, and one where central banks remain sensitive to currency volatility and capital outflow risks from widening interest rate differentials. The Bank of Japan may be at the top of this list: with the yen sliding past 152 against the US dollar, the threat of official intervention remains live.

BoJ governor Ueda<sup>’</sup>s otherwise dovish support for easier financial conditions and policy continuity should not obscure his earlier remarks that inflationary pressure from a weaker currency could warrant a policy response. Recent inflation and wage data—including a 20-year high for full-time wage growth (Figure 4)—are already making the case for another BoJ rate hike by July, and we see yen depreciation as risking an acceleration of the BoJ<sup>’</sup>s policy tightening.

Central banks across EM Asia also may be forced to recali brate their gradual easing plans. Lingering deflation in China could justify further monetary easing and we still see another (10bp) rate cut in 2Q, but domestic pressure on bank net interest margins and external pressure on CNY depreciation potentially constrain the PBOC. Given the gap between the CNY daily fixing and the daily spot currency rate, our FX team revised weaker their USD/CNY forecast for both the near term (to 7.30, from 7.25) and year-end (to 7.20, from 7.10 previously).

Elsewhere in EM Asia, a repricing of the Fed<sup>’</sup>s policy path is interacting with signs of somewhat elevated inflation pressure to nudge many central banks to remain on hold longer than previously forecast. This week we removed our projected 50bp of 2H24 cuts in Thailand and now see the BoT on hold this year. We also now look for a shallower (25bp cut every six months instead of every quarter) and later (starting in 4Q24 instead of 3Q) easing cycle in Korea. Earlier we delayed rate cuts in India to August. Further hikes are still unlikely in the region, although we see some risk in Indonesia to mitigate the impact of FX volatility, and a second modest hike in Taiwan cannot be ruled out after the surprise 12.5bp move in March.

## China’s imbalancing act

Despite expectations that incoming activity data will reaffirm a solid recovery in 1Q, structural imbalances in China<sup>’</sup>s economy remain. Growth of production has been stronger than consumption, while manufacturing fixed investment should continue to outperform that of private companies—particularly in the distressed real estate sector. These imbalances are perpetuating deflationary forces: declining March CPI and PPI point to over-supply and a lack of corporate pricing power. While we expect strong 6.6%ar real GDP growth in next week<sup>’</sup>s 1Q release, the risk is to the upside in part because the deflator could be more persistently negative than previously believed. We look for the March activity indicators to moderate from the strong Jan-Feb readings on fading Lunar New Year effects, with IP growth at 0.7% and retail sales rising 0.4%. These remain consistent with strong near-term growth, and even the 1.9% contraction in March exports left the sequential trend at 33%3m/3m, saar. Meanwhile, TSF growth last month appeared soft at 8.7%oya, but that figure largely reflects earlier front-loading rather than credit tightening. We thus look for a rebound consistent with our forecast for 9.5%oya growth this year.

## The BoI remains cautious

As anticipated, the Bank of Israel left the policy rate unchanged at 4.5%, with heightened geopolitical uncertainty and the repricing of DM rates inducing caution. However, we still think that fairly well-behaved inflation, a negative output gap, and likely transitory currency weakness together leave space for further easing—and the BoI<sup>’</sup>s forecasts broadly align with this view. Our forecast sees 75bp of cuts to yearend, but risks are skewed toward a shallower easing cycle, especially if the ongoing conflict leads to further fiscal relaxation or global easing cycles themselves turn even more shallow.

## By contrast, moderating inflation in LatAm

Core inflation surprised lower in Brazil, Mexico, Chile, and Colombia last month—only Peru, where pressures have deepened recently, was the exception in LatAm. Nonetheless, tightening global financial conditions eclipsed lower-than-expected CPI, reinforcing the gap between higher market pricing and our expectations for the region<sup>’</sup>s major central banks. We keep our terminal rate forecasts unchanged, but acknowledge that central banks will cautiously thread the needle. Chile and Peru have already done so to some extent, respectively decelerating the pace of easing and skipping a meeting. We already expect a slow-moving easing cycle by Banxico, while for Brazil we continue to expect 25bp moves from June after one last 50bp cut in May. We keep BCB<sup>’</sup>s terminal rate at 9.50%. In Colombia we look for BanRep to again cut 50bp this month and accelerate the pace in 2H24, but recognize the risk for slower cuts there as well.
