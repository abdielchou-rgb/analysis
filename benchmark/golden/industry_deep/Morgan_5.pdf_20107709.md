While this marks a clear shift in tone relative to Powell's earlier remarks, it also brings him in line with most other members of the committee. As a result, in a way, nothing has changed for markets and policy uncertainty remains considerably elevated. After all, policy uncertainty in recent weeks has been considerably elevated not because of unclear messaging, but because that data has failed to deliver the corroboration of disinflation sought by the Fed. Therefore, Chair Powell's acknowledgement of that fact has done little to lessen policy uncertainty, which remains as high as ever. As readers may recall, we examine policy uncertainty by looking at calls and puts at various strikes on Z4 3M SOFR futures options, and decomposing the probability distribution implied by these prices into a weighted sum of Normal distributions with fixed means and (mostly) fixed standard deviations. Such an approach distils the information in the tails of the distribution into easier-to-intuit weights on individual pre-determined Normal distributions, and also allows the dispersion of these weights to serve as a quantification of the extent of policy uncertainty. I.e., should the weights (which must sum to one by design) be concentrated in one or two nearby distributions, that would signify considerably policy clarity. Conversely, weights that are spread out over widely separated distributions would signify considerably policy uncertainty. As we can see in Figure 2, we remain in a state of elevated policy uncertainty.

Policy uncertainty translates into an expectation of rangebound yields on the yield curve, which in turn favors positioning for carry on the swap yield curve. Unfortunately, however, the sharp repricing of the front end of the curve has impacted carry in two ways. First, with OIS forwards now pricing in much less easing, carry in front end shorts is now much less attractive than it was just a few months ago (Figure 3). But even more importantly, if one is interested in constructing well hedged carry trades, beta-adjusted carry is now much more homogenous at different points in the front end of the curve (Figure 4). In other words, different sectors exhibit different levels of volatility and thus different betas with respect to a common reference tenor (say, the 2Y) even in the front end SOFR futures sector. Since a well hedged curve carry trade would incorporate these different betas into hedge ratios, it is useful to examine the beta-adjusted carry (i.e., carry divided by the beta with respect to the common reference tenor) in different sectors. When there is much variation in these beta-adjusted carry values, well-hedged carry trades can be constructed, by positioning to earn carry in sectors with high beta-adjusted carry and hedging with offsetting positions in sectors with lower beta-adjusted carry. Unfortunately, as the details in Figure 3 also show, recent declines in front end carry have been such that the betaadjusted carry is now fairly similar in various different points in the SOFR futures sectors. This means that although there is still positive carry to be found in trades such as curve flatteners, well hedged trades will likely offer little carry. Given that the macro risk in outright curve trades is still significant, and given little prospects for attractive carry in well hedged trades, we recommend taking advantage of relative value opportunities instead.

Such relative value trading opportunities continue to exist on the curve, fortunately. One such trade that currently looks attractive is buying the belly of a Z5/U6/H7 3M SOFR futures butterfly (33:67 weights on the wings). As seen in Figure 5, the 18Mx3M / 27Mx3M forward swap curve appears too steep relative to its (fairly strong) relationship with the 27Mx3M / 33Mx3M forward swap curve. Thus, 18Mx3M / 27Mx3M flatteners paired with twice the risk weight in 27Mx3M/33Mx3M steepeners are attractive - this reduces to buying the belly of a 33:67 weighted 18Mx3M / 27Mx3M / 33Mx3M swap butterfly (or its SOFR futures equivalent). Therefore, we recommend this trade (see Trade recommendations). Carry on this trade is now significantly lower than it would have been at other times in the recent past, in line with the discussion above, but is still modestly positive at 1bp/month.

Looking ahead, we maintain our widening bias at the front end of the curve, and turn bullish on spread wideners in the intermediate maturity sector. At the front end, the key driver of this view is negative T-bill net issuance, that is likely to richen the front end on a spread basis going forward. But there are other factors in support of wider spreads as well. For instance, we expect healthy demand for USTs on a swapped basis from banks going forward. The messaging during earnings calls, as well as the H.8 data that we have commented on previously, all suggest that banks are growing securities portfolios in general, especially USTs on a duration hedged basis. This demand will likely serve to anchor swap spreads in the 2- to 5-year sector of the curve. Also, high grade corporate issuance will likely decline from its recent heady pace (which should help lessen the headwind from issuancerelated swapping), implied volatility appears likely to stabilize near these levels rather than increase further (which will help to lessen yet another source of narrowing pressure on spreads), and valuations appear cheap (especially in the 10-year sector). Given these factors, we now recommend a widening bias on swap spreads in the belly of the curve.

There are two key risks to our expectation of wider spreads. First of these is the dollar - the trade weighted dollar has historically exhibited negative correlation with swap spreads, with a stronger dollar implying narrower swap spreads (likely due to the impact of reduced foreign demand for USTs). However, that relationship has been weakening in numerical significance, and we see this risk as likely modest in comparison to the other impacts discussed above. Second, with Fed easing once again no longer imminent, and with growing tail risks of higher rates, demand for fixed income in the aggregate could weaken, which would lead to weaker AUM growth in bond funds. This is especially significant at the long end, where the impact of AUM growth on swap spreads is most noticeable and significant. In light of this risk, we maintain a neutral stance on swap spreads at the long end of the curve, even as we turn bullish on swap spreads in the belly.

Looking ahead, we turn neutral on volatility across much of the vol surface. Although implieds remain below fair value (as also seen in Figure 7), a significant amount of retracement towards fair value has already occurred since late March, and the risk-reward consideration is weaker now for long volatility positions. One, our empirical model for delta hedged monthly returns from long gamma positions (which includes ex-ante levels of residual as a measure of relative value, as well as other observable variables) is now pointing to negative returns (Figure 8). Although only modestly negative, it is indicative of conditions that are shifting to become more favorable for gamma sellers. Two, we are likely to be in a rangebound, mean-reverting environment for some time going forward, given the considerable policy uncertainty as we noted earlier. Historically, range bound and mean-reverting markets tend to produce significant gamma selling, as infrequent delta hedgers are will likely experience lower realized volatility even as frequent delta hedgers experience higher levels of volatility that they can monetize (see The sun is the same, in a relative way, but vol is lower, 3/8/2024). In other words, this kind of environment could bring back the gamma selling and carry seeking strategies, which could pressure implieds lower from current levels. Therefore, risks are likely more balanced and we think further residual convergence could stall in the near term, because of which we turn neutral on volatility and recommend unwinding outright longs (see Trade recommendations).

The exception to this view is in 30-year tails, where we maintain our bearish view. Implied volatility on 30-year tails continues to appear rich to our measure of fair value and therefore we recommend maintaining shorts in 30-year tails. In line with our expectation of rangebound markets, we recommend delta hedging less frequently in order to take advantage of mean reversion.

## Swaption Skew

For some time now, implied volatility has exhibited significant directionality with yield levels, and this is baked into our fair value framework (see, for instance, Interest Rate Derivatives 2024 Outlook). Indeed, much of the rise in the fair value of implied volatility shown in Figure 7 is due to the rise in yield levels. This of course means that vol-rate correlation is a key determinant of the skew. Moreover, with structural demand-supply dynamics such as vol supply from callable issuance unlikely to emerge in the foreseeable future, it is safe to say that vol-rate correlations are the dominant driver of swaption skews currently.

That said, as the Fed has gone on hold and markets await rate cuts at some point, the nature of vol-rate correlations has become more subtle. In particular, this relationship has become somewhat asymmetric, with implieds exhibiting positive correlation with yields in a selloff, but much more muted correlation in a rally (Figure 9). To be sure, such an asymmetric relationship cannot be sustained in the long term, but could be a factor to contend with when contemplating the outlook for swaption skews in the near term.

What does all this mean for swaption skews? To answer this question, we look at the current A+/-100bp payer and receiver swaption skews, and compare them to estimates of its fair value derived in two different ways. First, we use our fair value model to project the amount by which ATM implied volatility will rise if rates were to rise 100bp, and use onehalf of that as the fair value for the A+100 minus A+0 payer swaption skew. This method is repeated for a -100bp shift to calculate the estimated fair value for the receiver skew. Second, we repeat essentially the same exercise, but instead of using our fair value model to calculate the rise/fall in ATM implied vol in the event of a +/- 100bp shock, we instead use recent empirical behavior. Specifically, we use the "up-beta" and "down-beta" shown in Figure 9 (for 1Yx5Y, and repeat the process for other structures), to calculate the expected change in ATM implied volatility, and then use one-half of that as the estimated fair value for today's payer/receiver swaption skews in various different sectors.

Current payer and receiver swaptions in various sectors, as well as the estimated fair value under these two different approaches, are shown in Figure 10. We make several observations from this Figure. One, our fair value framework assumes a somewhat symmetric volrate beta (with some asymmetry because of non-linearity in our backbone), but recent history has produced much lower betas in a down scenario compared to an up scenario across all structures. As a result, fair value estimates for receiver skews based the second approach will generally be higher (i.e., less negative) than fair value estimates that rely on our implied volatility fair value model. Two, for most structures, the current payer skew appears to be roughly in line with what's to be expected under these two frameworks; thus, we see payer skews as fairly priced.Three, current receiver skew appears to be fairly mixed with respect to the two frameworks. For example, 5Yx5Y receiver skew appears rich with respect to our fair value framework, but cheap with respect to recent empirical observation. Thus, it would appear that receiver skews are somewhat rich, but not convincingly so given that the recent empirical vol-rate correlation is quite different from the longer-run structural correlations assumed in our backbone. Finally, there are a few sectors, such as the 1Yx2Y and 1Yx5Y sectors, where payer swaption skews appear cheap under both frameworks and receiver skews appear rich relative to both frameworks. In these sectors, we can have greater conviction in the view that receiver skews are rich and payer skews are cheap. Therefore, we recommend positioning for a correction in skew by buying A+100 1Yx5Y payer swaptions and selling A-100 1Yx5Y receiver swaptions on a delta hedged basis (see Trade recommendations).
