## European Economic Perspectives Switzerland quarterly: All you need to know

## Our key views ahead of the June SNB meeting

This is the second edition of our quarterly publication on the Swiss macro and policy outlook. It summarises our current views, key charts and data points to watch. The focus article in this edition dives deeper into the question of how low the SNB policy rate will decline in this easing cycle.

## Macro outlook: Growth, inflation, labour market (p.3)

Manufacturing activity remains depressed amid sluggish external demand, but the more domestic-oriented services sector partially compensates the weakness, as was reflected in the Q1 flash GDP release (0.2% q/q). We project real GDP growth of 1.3% in 2024, including a positive effect from international sports events. This should also support the labour market, where we expect only a modest softening in light of structural recruitment shortages (unemployment rate end-2024E: 2.4%). Inflation has risen to 1.4% y/y in April and is now tracking the SNB's forecast from March. We expect some acceleration from rent increases in May, but project average inflation in 2024 and 2025 at just 1.3%.

## What next for the SNB? (p.4)

Following the first rate cut in March, we expect two more 25bp rate cuts this year (June and September), to a terminal rate of 1%. However, we stress the risk that the SNB may lower policy rates by less, given that the inflation forecast is at target and Chairman Jordan said that neutral rates may be in the range of 1-1.5%. Also, if inflation were to surprise to the upside (after rising markedly in April), if the CHF continues to weaken or if the ECB were to cut rates by less than expected, this could imply less SNB rate cuts. We discuss the outlook for monetary policy on p.4.

## Focus article: How low will the SNB cut rates in this easing cycle? (p.7)

In our focus article, we argue that the likely SNB terminal rate in the current easing cycle (which we forecast to be 1%, see above) depends on: (1) the level of the Swiss neutral rate; (2) whether there is a need to go below neutral; and (3) what the split is between using policy rate cuts and FX interventions. We argue that there are difficulties with applying the concept of a neutral rate to a small open economy such as Switzerland. We rather view the Swiss neutral rate as a function of the ECB neutral rate, and using the historical policy rate differential between the ECB and the SNB points to a rate of around 1% for Switzerland. Weaker growth and inflation outcomes than we project could lead to a lower rate at the end of this easing cycle, and vice versa. At the same time, the lower policy rates drop, the more likely FX interventions to weaken the currency become in case more monetary easing is needed.

## CHF rates strategy (p.6)

We continue to see value in cross-market trades involving either ESTR or SONIA 1y1y rates against SARON. This is primarily driven by expectations that policy rates between the ECB/BoE and the SNB will converge over time as inflation improves. From a valuation point of view, the cross-market spreads remain at historically elevated levels.

## CHF FX strategy (p.6)

As we expect volatility in FX markets to remain low due to the absence of catalysts in the near term to trigger a vol event, we project continued strong performance in FX carry strategies. Hence, we still like using CHF as a near-term funding currency, pairing it against currencies such as USD and AUD where we anticipate the persistence of the "higher for longer" narrative.

## Swiss economy at a glance

## Growth

Manufacturing activity in Switzerland remains weak, with the Manufacturing PMI back at a six-month low of 41.4 points in April. The latest decline was much more pronounced than the Eurozone Manufacturing PMI and came despite relief from a weaker exchange rate. This indicates that the post-pandemic order backlog has been largely depleted by now and the impact of weak demand is felt in full. Going forward, we draw some comfort from improving Eurozone growth and the pick-up in German sentiment, but any recovery is likely to be slow and gradual.

Meanwhile, the more domestic-oriented services sector is holding up well, helped by robust private consumption. After an outlier drop below 50 in March, the latest Services PMI (55.6 points) has recovered to expansionary territory. Overall, we see little convincing signs of manufacturing weakness spilling over into the broader economy. Both the KOF leading indicator and Composite PMI in April stood above their Q1 2024 average, indicating a solid start into the second quarter. The flash Q1 GDP estimate for Q1 was 0.2% q/q (adjusted for the impact of sports events). We project annual GDP growth of 1.3% in 2024, including a positive impact from international sports events.

Key to watch: Q1 2024 GDP (released 30 May), May KOF index (released 30 May), May PMIs (released 3 June)

## Labour market

Our growth forecast hinges on the assumption of a relatively resilient labour market. More pronounced employment weakness could lead to a decline in household income and perceived job security and thus dampen private consumption. Given that the Swiss labour market is relatively flexible, we are slightly more worried about employment risks in Switzerland than in the Eurozone.

Since the beginning of the year, manufacturing employment has deteriorated somewhat while the metrics for the services labour market still look robust, mirroring the diverging trends in activity. Accordingly, the unemployment rate has risen slightly faster than expected (from 1.9% in early 2023 to 2.3% in April 2024), driven by manufacturing. Employment growth has moderated from its post-pandemic peak of 3.2% y/y in Q2 2022 to 1.7% y/y in Q4 2023.

In light of structural labour shortages, we continue to expect the unemployment rate to remain below 2.5% this year and next. This would be a moderate rise of around 0.5pp from trough to peak − compared with a sharp deterioration during past recessions when unemployment rose by 1.2-1.7pp.

Key to watch: May labour market data (released 6 June)

We expect inflation to rise to 1.5% in May and then to fluctuate just below that level in the coming months. Overall, we expect inflation to average 1.3% in 2024 and 2025 (the SNB projects 1.4% and 1.2% in 2024 and 2025).

Swiss inflation rose 40bp to 1.4% y/y in April, the highest level so far this year and well within the SNB's target range of "below 2%". Inflation at the start of Q2 is now tracking the SNB's conditional inflation forecast from March, which expected a pick-up from 1.2% in Q1 to 1.4% on average in Q2, and to 1.5% in Q3.

Inflation is solely driven by domestic price increases, with higher rents (the largest CPI component) becoming more important. We think the full impact of the rise in the mortgage reference rate on rent inflation is yet to come through in May. We think that the mortgage reference rate increase in December 2023 was the last one in this cycle.

## SNB

Next meeting: 20 June 2024

Market view: The market is currently pricing -17bp for June, -11bp for September, and a cumulative -33bp by the end of this year.

UBS view: Next cut in June 2024 (-25bp to 1.25%), followed by one more cut in September to 1% by end-2024

Key to watch: April balance sheet data (31 May), May CPI (4 June); ECB meeting (6 June); news regarding a successor for Chairman Jordan; speeches/interviews by SNB board members

As the first G10 central bank, the SNB lowered its policy rate by 25bp to 1.5% on 21 March. The policy statement noted: "The easing of monetary policy has been made possible because the fight against inflation over the past two and a half years has been effective." Accordingly, the inflation forecast path was shifted lower by around 50bp across the board, with the end-point showing 1.1% in 2026, essentially at target. The SNB also became more concerned about the appreciation of the real exchange rate over the past year. In this regard, the policy statement noted that the policy rate cut also supports economic activity. Since the March meeting, the nominal and real exchange rate has depreciated by c.2%. The March statement retained the sentence: "The SNB also remains willing to be active in the foreign exchange market as necessary." In subsequent speeches, SNB board members have noted the complementary function of FX interventions, even as the policy rate remained "at the core" of monetary policy implementation.

In our view, the policy outlook has become more uncertain. First, the March SNB forecast showed inflation essentially at target with an end-point in 2026 of 1.1%, raising the question as to how much more policy easing the SNB actually needs to deliver. April inflation rose more than expected, to 1.4%, and now matches the average SNB inflation forecast for Q2. Hence, after a series of inflation undershoots, the SNB may have more confidence in its March inflation projection. Second, Chairman Jordan added to this uncertainty by indicating at the March press conference that the nominal neutral rate could be in the range of 1-1.5%, close to the current policy rate. Third, the extent of FX interventions adds an additional layer of uncertainty, given that the March balance sheet data indicated that the SNB may have purchased FX to weaken the CHF. As we discuss here, we think these estimates may be distorted and FX purchases would contrast with the recent communication by the SNB. Nevertheless, to be really sure, we have to wait for the next balance sheet data and eventually the actual SNB FX interventions data for Q1 (which will be published only after the June SNB meeting). Should the SNB − contrary to our baseline expectation − use FX interventions to ease monetary conditions through a weaker CHF, the scope for rate cuts would accordingly be smaller. After all, FX interventions were also used to strengthen the CHF when monetary policy was tightening, and limited the extent of policy rate increases.

We expect two more rate cuts this year, in June and September, to a terminal rate of 1% in our baseline forecast (see focus article, p.7). However, given the uncertainties noted above, we stress the risk that the SNB may lower policy rates by less (given Jordan's reference to neutral rates) and may also decide to not cut every meeting, in case other central banks delay rate cuts, inflation surprises to the upside or if the CHF continues to weaken in the coming months.

Prior to the SNB meeting on 20 June, May inflation will be released on 4 June (we expect 1.5% y/y) and the ECB − if our expectations prove correct − will have delivered its first rate cut on 6 June. Along with the developments in the CHF (and any information about potential FX interventions that may be indicated in the April balance sheet data released on 31 May), both May inflation and the ECB will be key inputs to the June SNB decision. Should the CHF continue to weaken, inflation surprise to the upside in May, or the ECB deliver a hawkish message that puts the speed of further rate cuts in doubt, the SNB could refrain from lowering the policy rate in June and wait for the September meeting. However, at this stage, we think the bar for no SNB rate cut in June is high.

## Strategy views: CHF rates and FX

## Rates: Value in cross-market trades vs SARON

We continue to see value in cross-market trades involving either ESTR or SONIA 1y1y rates against SARON. This is primarily driven by expectations that policy rates between the ECB/BoE and the SNB will converge over time as inflation improves. From a valuation point of view, the cross-market spreads remain at historically elevated levels.

The robust US economic data and a slow-moving Fed may have an impact on the ECB and BoE policy outlook. Economic and financial conditions in the US could affect macroeconomic conditions in Europe, thus indirectly influencing policy decisions. Furthermore, if the ECB/BoE embark on a cutting cycle earlier than the Fed, this may have implications for exchange rates and, consequently, imported inflation. These factors are likely to keep EUR/UK vs CHF spreads elevated for an extended period. But economic conditions in the US and Europe differ, and therefore the ECB and BoE have room to partially decouple from the Fed.

In addition, we think the SNB's capacity to cut rates significantly, beyond current market expectations, is limited. SARON 1y1y is currently trading around 1%, which aligns with our terminal rate expectations. Swiss inflation surprised to the upside in April, increasing by 40bp to 1.4%, marking the highest level this year. UBS expects inflation to rise to 1.5% in May due to the impact of rents following the increase in mortgage reference rates in December. Near-term inflation uncertainty and the use of FX interventions to ease monetary conditions through a weaker CHF would limit the scope for rate cuts from the SNB.

## In focus: How far will the SNB cut policy rates?

Following the surprise rate cut by the SNB in March, the focus has shifted towards the question of how many more cuts are to be expected. Or more precisely: at what level will the SNB policy rate settle once the easing cycle is over? Views are split in our client conversations: while some see the early March rate cut as evidence of a more dovish SNB reaction function that could lead to more cuts than previously thought, others question the need for several more cuts, given that the SNB's medium-term inflation forecast is already at target under the assumption of an unchanged policy rate. Our baseline remains that the SNB will cut policy rates in June and September to a terminal rate of 1%, but with the risk of fewer or later cuts (current market pricing is for c.37bp of rate cuts until March 2025). In the following, we review the debate about the terminal policy rate in this easing cycle. We see at least three questions: (1) Where is the neutral rate for Switzerland? (2) Is there a need to lower policy rates below neutral? and (3) What is the balance between rate cuts and FX interventions?

The neutral or natural rate of interest is the level of the short-term interest rate that
provides a neutral policy stance, corresponding to a situation where inflation is at target The neutral rate is a theoretical concept and its level is
unobservable, but is often related to fundamental factors, such as a country's potential Declining potential growth rates, in part driven by ageing populations, (3)
are often seen as having led to lower neutral rates in many advanced economies.

Apart from the uncertainties around determining the level of neutral rates, we see difficulties in applying this concept to Switzerland. Most importantly, we think the concept of a neutral policy rate applies more to relatively closed economies where inflation is primarily driven by domestic factors. In those countries, a below-neutral policy rate level leads, for example, to stronger investment and eventually higher inflation. By contrast, in small open economies such as Switzerland, inflation is much more driven by external factors such as the exchange rate (Figure 16, 17). On top of these considerations, determining the neutral rate level is arguably more difficult in the Swiss case than elsewhere, given that estimates typically find that potential GDP growth is higher in Switzerland than in the Eurozone, which could imply a higher Swiss neutral rate. For example, according to SECO estimates, Swiss potential growth has averaged 1.8% over the last decade, while the EC's estimate for the Eurozone was closer to 1.1% (Figure 14). Actual annual real GDP growth has, on average, been 50bp higher in Switzerland than in the Eurozone since 1999. However, despite a higher Swiss potential growth rate, which could imply a higher neutral policy rate, nominal Swiss policy rates have been consistently below those of the Eurozone by an average of 90bp since 2000, their neutral rate, given that inflation over the last decade has not overshot the SNB's target range − by contrast, inflation was deemed too low pre-Covid. Swiss inflation has averaged 0.6% since 1999, compared with 2.1% in the Eurozone.

As a result of the difficulties mentioned above, and given the importance of the exchange rate and policy rate differentials, we view the Swiss neutral policy rate as a and applying the historical policy rate differential of c.90bp would imply a neutral or terminal policy rate for the SNB of around 1%. Of course, this is an imprecise measure and also neglects that the policy rate differential has fluctuated substantially over time, depending on the exchange rate and overall conditions for growth and inflation (Figure 18). Uncertainty about the neutral rate was also raised by Chairman Jordan at the March press conference. While he said that the SNB does not normally comment on the level of the SNB neutral rate, he referred to estimates made by others that suggest the neutral real rate could be in the range of 0-0.5%. Combining this with an implicit inflation target of 1% (i.e. the mid-point of the "below 2%" target range) would give a 1-1.5% range for the nominal neutral SNB policy rate.

Following on from the assumption that the Swiss neutral rate could be around 1%, the second question is whether the SNB would need to deviate from it. A lower-than-neutral policy rate would be required if inflation were seen below target in the medium term, and vice versa. From a domestic perspective, this would be the case if GDP growth were below its potential rate with labour market slack. Our forecast assumes GDP growth forecast would be in line with a return of the policy rate to neutral. From an external angle, as discussed above, ECB policy plays a key role. Should the ECB give indications that it intends to lower the depo rate by less or more than our assumed 2% mediumterm target, this would have implications for the SNB as well, not least through an appreciation or depreciation of the exchange rate. And of course, exchange rate strength or weakness for reasons other than rate differentials would matter for the SNB, and potentially require a policy stance that differs from neutral. Such a situation then directly leads to the third question: which instruments would the SNB use in case it needs to deviate from a neutral policy stance?

This third question is Swiss-specific: even if one were able to determine the desired policy stance based on the neutral rate and potential deviations from it, would the SNB implement this stance purely with policy rate changes or also by intervening in FX markets to influence the CHF? The SNB has been very explicit that it operates two different instruments − policy rates and FX interventions − and even mentions this in its monetary policy strategy. If the SNB deems that monetary conditions need to be eased further beyond a neutral policy rate (perhaps as a result of persistent exchange rate strength), would it choose to lower policy rates or purchase FX reserves to weaken the CHF? This question becomes especially relevant, given that policy rates in Switzerland are already much lower than elsewhere (in part, as the SNB stresses, because of its increased use of FX interventions to strengthen the CHF during the tightening cycle). While the SNB clearly stated recently that the policy rate is the primary tool for influencing monetary conditions<sup>(9)</sup> , we think that ideally the SNB would like to maintain a buffer to negative rates. How large this buffer would need to be is unclear as the extent of policy rate cuts has differed in downturns: while the SNB eased policy rates by c.300bp in 2001-03 and 250bp in 2008-09, it cut policy rates by just 75bp in 2014-15. In our view, once policy rates decline towards 1%, such considerations may become more prominent. If, at this stage, the need for a more accommodative stance persisted, we think the SNB would lean more towards FX interventions to weaken the CHF. Recent communication (speeches here and here, 2023 Annual Report and implicitly through the findings of a recent working paper) suggest that FX purchases would be used in instances where the interest rate is at, or close to, the effective lower bound, or in response to strong safe-haven inflows into the CHF.

Taking all together, we think a reasonable baseline assumption is that the SNB policy rate will decline to 1%, and this remains our baseline forecast. However, should growth turn out much weaker than assumed, implying less inflationary pressure (or vice versa), the SNB could lower policy rates by more. Similarly, should the ECB cut the depo rate by more or less than we assume in our baseline, the SNB terminal rate may also be different. However, we think that once the SNB reaches 1% and rates move closer to zero, the SNB would increasingly resort to FX interventions in case monetary conditions would need to be eased further.
