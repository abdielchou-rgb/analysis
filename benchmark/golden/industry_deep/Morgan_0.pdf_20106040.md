## Polar vortex duration extension

A lot has happened in the two weeks since our last publication. While much of the country has grappled with multiple cold fronts, markets appear to be heating up fairly early in the year. Economic data (CPI, retail sales, jobless claims) have come in strong, and bank earnings season has kicked off, with banks posting strong AOCI gains thanks to the decline in yields in 4Q23. The past two weeks were also heavy on Fedspeak, which continues to lean against premature rate cuts but supports QT taper expectations, and also suggests that the odds of Basel III Endgame implementation in the near term are now lower (Figure 1). Policy rate expectations effectively round-tripped over the past two weeks, with markets pricing in much more easing by the end of last week, before reversing course, on the back of stronger data and Fedspeak (Figure 2). As if all this weren<sup>’</sup>t enough, somewhat higher rates at the long end and a steeper 10s/20s Treasury curve has brought the US bond basis back in the picture as CTD extensions have become possible again (see discussion below).

As we look ahead, we think there is more to go and maintain our widening bias on swap spreads in intermediate maturities, for the following reasons. First, from a tactical standpoint, the seasonal headwind from swapped issuance is likely now mostly behind us. As seen in Figure 4, high grade financial issuance tends to be heaviest in the first three weeks of January, and the heavier-than-usual issuance in the second week this time suggests that most of this issuance is by now behind us. Second, the odds continue to favor a taper-down of QT, as we noted recently (see It<sup>’</sup>s all over now, QT, J. Barry et al., 1/12/2024). Recent Fedspeak also continues to be supportive of this view, with Waller articulating a higher terminal size of Reserves than previously assumed (Figure 1). As we noted in our last publication (see Happy new taper, 1/5/2024), an earlier-than-previously-thought end to QT is supportive of wider spreads for two reasons - (i) the passive reinvestment demand for USTs from the Fed once it ends, and (ii) the removal of balance sheet policy as a headwind, which could result in greater demand for USTs on an asset-swap basis from investors such as banks.

Another factor that - on the margin - should also support greater bank appetite for UST asset swaps is the steady rise in SOFR towards IOER. As RRP balances have continued their relentless decline, one consequence has been the upward drift in SOFR towards IOER (Figure 5). This is important for banks (which can hold Reserves earning IOR) because the marginal attractiveness of USTs is measured relative to IOR and not SOFR. As SOFR itself rises within the target range, asset swapping USTs becomes correspondingly more attractive to banks. In addition, we note that recent Fedspeak also appears to lower the odds of a near-term implementation of the so-called "Basel III endgame" capital proposals, which (on the margin) lowers the associated risk of higher systemwide balance sheet costs and narrowing pressure on swap spreads. With Waller's as well as Bowman's comments suggesting more work is needed on this front, odds of a near-term implementation are likely lower which is supportive on the margin.

Finally, valuations remain supportive of our view despite the recent widening. As seen in Figure 6, swap spreads in the 5Y sector (for instance) are beginning to converge towards fair value, but there is more to go. Therefore, given this confluence of supportive factors, we remain biased towards wider swap spreads in the belly of the curve.

One way to position for wider swap spreads in the belly of the curve, while also benefiting from slide on the term structure of swap spreads, is to asset swap old 10-year notes that have a remaining maturity of just over 5 years. As seen in Figure 7, there tends to be far less differentiation based on original issue tenors once bonds slide into the less-than-5Y sector. This means that the original issue 10-year notes maturing in Feb 2029, which is currently at least 3bp narrower than spreads in the 5Y sector, are likely to experience attractive slide as they age into the <5Y maturity sector. Therefore, we recommend asset-swapping the 2.625% Feb '29. Although we have used proceeds asset swap spreads (which controls for the curve risk arising from the fact that the bond has a below-par coupon) in our analysis, we recommend expressing this view via a plain vanilla maturity matched swap spread widener, as the curve risk is less significant in shorter maturities. Therefore, we recommend paying in the 2.625% Feb '29 maturity matched swap spreads (see Trade recommendations).

One key risk to this trade is a potential increase in implied volatility. As we have noted elsewhere (see Interest Rate Derivatives 2024 Outlook, 11/21/2023), implied volatility has become a significant factor driving swap spreads, and a good rule of thumb in this sector is that a 1bp/day rise in 2Yx2Y implied volatility could pressure spreads narrower by 0.65bp in the 5Y sector. Given our bullish view on implieds (see Options section below), we recommend that investors overlay a long vega position to hedge this risk, as a second variation on the theme (see Trade recommendations).

## Swap yield curve

As we noted earlier, we continue to see the funds rate as anchored in the near term (eventually followed by rate cuts but not until June) given the combination of stronger economic data and Fedspeak that leaned against premature rate cuts. Thus, our approach towards trading the swap yield curve continues to revolve around carry and roll. As Figure 8 shows, the 6M slide in receive-fixed positions is most punitive in short tenors and in near-spot sectors; conversely, anchoring shorts in those sectors can be a source of attractive slide. In other words, outright front end shorts and/or flatteners anchored in those sectors offer attractive slide.

However, it also remains the case that Fed expectations remain volatile, and the carry in such outright trades can be illusory given the macro risk inherent in such trades. Therefore, our strategy on the swap yield curve has been to seek pairs of curve trades (i.e., a steepener in one curve paired with a flattener in the other) that can still offer attractive slide while hedging out the curve risk and lowering the volatility of the position. Depending on the sectors chosen, these trades can sometimes net out to three legs, but more generally involve four legs.

This week, we present one such trade motivated by the slide characteristics shown in Figure 8. We begin with a short in the 1Yx1Y sector (which offers 49bp of 6M slide) and a long in the 10Yx5Y (which has modestly positive slide over 6M) - i.e., a Reds/10Yx5Y flattener. Given the wide span of this curve, we will need a similar wide ranging curve steepener to hedge the macro risk, but with less punitive carry. We accomplish this by anchoring the front end long in the 1Yx2Y sector (which has half the negative carry of Reds), and a short in the 30Y sector (which has slightly positive carry for a short position) - i.e., we choose a 1Yx2Y / 3Mx30Y curve steepener. These two curves have been extremely well correlated with each other, and the 1Yx2Y / 3Mx30Y curve has exhibited a beta of about 65% with respect to the Reds / 10Yx5Y curve (Figure 9). However, the Reds/10Yx5Y curve flattener offers nearly 21bp of slide over 3 months (or about 14bp after accounting for the 65% risk weight that would be appropriate on this flattener), while the 1Yx2Y / 3Mx30Y steepener has carry of about -7bp per quarter. Thus, the net package still offers attractive slide, at approximately 7bp per quarter, while hedging macro risk. Therefore, we recommend this trade (see Trade recommendations).

In a similar vein, we recommend receiving fixed in the belly of a 6M forward 2s/7s/30s swap yield butterfly (40:69 wing weights). As seen in Figure 10, this butterfly spread has been pretty stable and mean reverting over the past 9 months, and is currently about 2bp cheap relative to its historical average. But more importantly, this butterfly offers nearly 5bp of positive slide over three months, making it another attractive way to position for carry and slide while mitigating the macro risk. Therefore, we recommend this trade (see Trade recommendations).

## Back in the limelight- the bond basis

Barely three months ago the classic bond contract was in the spotlight, as higher yields and a steeper curve caused CTDs to shift, and additionally resulted in significantly increased option values in the contract because of the probability of even more material CTD switches. With yields in the 15Y sector of the Treasury curve having risen 30bp in recent weeks, and with the 10s/20s curve showing signs of steepening (Figure 11), this topic is unsurprisingly back in the spotlight.

The CTD into the USH4 contract is the 4.5% Aug '39, and thus far there have been no shifts in the CTD. In addition, there are several issues extending up to mid-2041 that are "near-CTD", in the sense of having net bases that are within a few ticks of the Aug '39's net basis (Figure 12). This is important because if the CTD were to shift to one of these issues, the resulting impact on futures pricing would likely not be very significant, and such a shift would be a case of "more bark, less bite". Indeed, our projections suggest that the CTD could indeed shift in a modest selloff, but this will likely be to the 4 3/4 Feb '41 issue which, while significantly longer in maturity, will only have a modest cheapening impact on futures pricing. For the CTD to shift longer still would require yields to rise by \~70bp or more, or for the curve to steepen significantly.

Given current market conditions, therefore, we see CTD shifts as resulting in relatively modest option value - we estimate that the value of is about 1.6/32nds, while the USH4 CTD net basis is near 4.5/32nds (as of Thursday COB). Thus we see futures as currently overpricing this option. That said, a steady and relentless steepening of the curve could undoubtedly occur, as it did in late September / early October, which could trigger more material CTD extension.

What can an investor do to protect against such CTD shifts? Consider an investor who has chosen to replace a \$100mn notional position in the Aug '39 with a bpv-equivalent (which is also approximately conversion factor weighted) amount of USH4 contracts. Effectively, this investor is short the Aug '39 USH4 basis as an overlay to her / his target portfolio. This investor can hedge the resulting exposure by constructing a hedge portfolio that mimics a long CTD basis position. In particular, we project that a \$100mn notional exposure in the Aug '39 USH4 factor-weighted basis can be approximately replicated by 130 March USH4 puts with a strike of 118 (Figure 13). Therefore, for investors such as bond funds who may be utilizing USH4 contracts as a synthetic proxy for the equivalent cash position in Treasuries, we recommend buying USH4 puts (with a strike of 118) to mitigate the risk of CTD extension.

Finally, it is also worth asking how the cost of the puts to offset CTD extension risk compares to the CTD net basis. At Thursday's close, we estimate that replication using 130 of the USH4 118 puts is the equivalent of 4.5/32nds in net basis terms, which is quite close to the actual net basis. In other words, adding USH4 puts would protect against underperformance in the event of CTD shifts, at the expense of giving up the benefit derived from the cheapness of futures in a synthetic strategy in a scenario where the CTD does not shift.

On a relative basis, we recommend buying 2-year tails versus selling 5-year tails. First, as we noted in our 2024 Outlook, as the Fed easing becomes more imminent, 2- year tails start to deliver more than 5-year tails, and thus a theta neutral strategy that involves buying 2-year tails and selling 5-year tails tends to become profitable (Exhibits 38 and 39 in 2024 Interest Rate Derivatives Outlook). We are already seeing that the 2- year tails have begun to deliver increasingly more than 5-year tails (Figure 17), and we think this trend is likely to persist, as we move steadily closer to eventual Fed easing in coming months. Also, implied vol ratios ratios have not increased as much, making this trade attractive. Second, while we like this trade going into the first ease, the trade is also likely to benefit from a correction in residuals in the near-term, where 2-year tails appear cheaper than 5-year tails. Therefore, we recommend buying 6Mx2Y straddles and selling a theta-neutral amount of 6Mx5Y straddles (see Trade recommendations).

Separately, on a relative value basis, we recommend overweighting 2Yx2Y volatility versus 5Yx5Y volatility. As our readers may recall, we use to vol surface factors to explain all implied vol differentials - the so called “expiry curve<sup>”</sup> (defined as 1Yx1Y minus 10Yx1Y) and “tail curve<sup>”</sup> (defined as 1Yx1Y minus 1Yx10Y) to summarize the volatility surface. The 2Yx2Y / 5Yx5Y volatility differential appears mispriced with respect to these volatility surface factors (Figure 18). Additionally, both the expiry and tail curves themselves appear cheap based on our fair value framework, and we expect them to increase by \~0.8 bp/day and \~0.5 bp/day, respectively, from current levels. The

2Yx2Y / 5Yx5Y volatility differential exhibits a beta to expiry curve that is positive, and slightly higher in magnitude compared to the beta to the tail curve, resulting in an overall positive exposure to a correction in the expiry and tail curves. Therefore, given these attractive characteristics, we recommend buying 2Yx2Y straddles versus selling vega-neutral amount of 5Yx5Y straddles (see Trade recommendations).

## Bank AOCI update

The bank earnings season for the quarter kicked off last Friday. One notable observation from earnings is the large AOCI gains that banks have reported so far. The top 4 banks that we track (JPM, C, BAC and WFC) reported aggregate AOCI gains of \~\$16.5bn and 6 of the next ten largest banks that have reported earnings so far (USB, PNC, TFC, SCHW, BNY, STT) reported aggregate AOCI gains of \~\$12bn.

These numbers are well in excess of our expectations from a few weeks ago, and suggest that we erred in overestimating the degree to which banks had de-risked their AFS securities books after a punishing selloff in 3Q23. Since bank' securities holdings, their risk characteristics and their impacts on bank capital all feed into their UST demand, this remains a topic of importance and we adjust our framework for estimating AOCI changes on bank balance sheets. As readers may recall, our empirical approach to estimating AOCI changes (aggregated across the largest four and the next ten banks) is based on an empirical regression versus change in 5Y UST yields and the squared change in yields. The latter coefficient of course represents convexity effects, largely stemming from MBS holdings. As the hiking cycle took off and caused yields to trade in entirely new regimes where mortgage convexity became far less significant, we were forced to manually adjust these coefficients in order to represent our best guess about banks' evolving risks. In addition, we made best-guess assumptions regarding the extent to which banks had actively managed their duration lower, in the aftermath of March 2023 when AOCI losses become a subject of intense focus.

Going forward, we tweak our methodology in two main ways. First, we incorporate data from recent quarters, allowing our estimation to benefit from recent experience. Of course, since this means we would be using data from many different quarters during which MBS convexity has varied considerably, we need to somehow account for this. We do so by modifying our nonlinear term to be the squared change in 5Y yields times the magnitude of the average mortgage (negative) convexity in that quarter - this permits the regression model to be estimated with a stable nonlinear coefficient, while still permitting the variation in MBS convexity to be properly taken into account in the quadratic term.

Details of this framework, estimated using data from 4Q19 to 3Q23 (i.e., excluding 4Q23, for out-of-sample comparison purposes) are shown in Figure 19. Such a framework would have estimated AOCI gains of \$14.4bn across the four largest banks in 4Q23, which is reasonably close to the actual \$16.5bn. Moreover, this approach also suggests that the remaining four banks (in the next ten) which have yet to report earnings also likely had good AOCI gains. Given our aggregate estimate of \~\$17.5bn for the next ten banks, of which the six who have reported have accounted for \~\$12bn, we expect the remaining banks likely experienced AOCI gains of \~\$5.5bn in 4Q23.
