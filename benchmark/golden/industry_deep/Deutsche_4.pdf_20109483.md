# On the cusp of change

A quiet summer is not to be had this year – not least due to the ever-shifting expectation on US rates and the global election cycle. We had already expected the upcoming US election to be the most consequential one in decades for Asia, especially in the context of global geo-economics today. If nothing else, elections in the EU and the dramatic events that have unfolded in the US in recent weeks suggest that nothing can be taken for granted.

Despite the market bringing forward the timing of rate cuts by the Fed, Asia FX has failed to gain much ground - far from resembling their sharp moves in late 2023 - given the potential impact of US policies on Asia's external balance and growth post the US election. Global fragmentation also points to higher inflation, leaving a rather limited room for central banks to maneuver beyond this year.

While China's Third Plenum underlined the urgency in fiscal reform, populist policies have gained greater traction in the South, amid political fragmentation, raising concerns over the subregion's fiscal sustainability. And, while the PBoC's rate cuts came hard on the heels of policymakers vowing to “resolutely achieve the annual economic goals” at the Third Plenum, it remains to be seen whether policymakers elsewhere in Asia have nerves of steel to move ahead of the Fed. Assuming that data evolves as expected, we expect BSP and BoK to ease in August, ahead of the Fed. RBI, BI and BoT would follow the Fed in October, while MAS may wait until next year as BNM and CBC remain on the sidelines.

In this report, we take a closer look at all 12 economies under our coverage and provided detailed forecasts.

n Facing these challenges, policymakers affirmed their commitment to achieve the 5% annual growth target at the 3<sup>rd</sup> Plenum last week. ## Economy: striving to achieve 5% growth target

China's real GDP growth decelerated to 4.7% YoY in Q2, missing market expectations by 0.4ppt. The drag mainly came from a weaker-than-expected domestic demand. Services sector growth slowed to 4.2% in Q2, attributed to property sector downturn and slower credit by the financial sector. Retail sales also fell to 2% YoY in June, representing the lowest reading since 2023. Consequently, the capacity utilization rate and sales ratio stayed low at 74.5% and 95.3%, respectively. In contrast, the industrial sector maintained 5.6% growth in Q2, in part thanks to strong exports. Sequential GDP growth also halved to 0.7% QoQ in Q2 from 1.5% QoQ in Q1. QoQ growth was actually higher than we had expected (0.5% QoQ) but this is largely owing to revisions to historical QoQ growth figures. Taking the revised numbers, the 0.7% QoQ growth in Q2 is still the lowest QoQ growth China has seen since Q4 2022. Meanwhile, nominal GDP grew 4.0% YoY in Q2. GDP deflator was negative again at -0.7% YoY, staying below zero for a fifth consecutive quarter. Although China's CPI and PPI have both been improving in the past few months, the momentum of improvement was not as strong as we had expected.

Slower fiscal spending was an important driver of the recent growth slowdown. The government had planned to increase fiscal spending by 8% in 2024 to support the economy. Instead, Government spending declined by -1.5% YoY in Q1, and decelerated further to -4% YoY in Q2. Although the government increased bond financing in Q2, it was offset by a -3% YoY drop in budgetary revenue, and a larger -25% YoY drop in land sales revenue. This deceleration of public sector activity, we think, is likely an important reason why GDP growth slowed in Q2.

Property sector downturn continued in Q2 despite the new round of easing since May. Housing prices continued to decline in both the new home and secondary markets. Housing new starts have also further deteriorated to 30% of 2019 levels, as developers were still struggling to fund them. Property investment and sales have appeared stable to some extent, potentially underpinned by government projects. According to NBS, the "three major projects" contributed 0.9ppt to property investment growth in the first half of 2024, indicating an investment volume of RMB 50bn.

Consumer spending recovery also faced headwinds in Q2, due to slowed income growth. Employment was resilient in, as unemployment rate remained steady and migrant worker population also increased to its historical high of 190 million. But income growth was relatively weaker. Income growth slowed to 4.5% YoY in Q2 from 6.2% in Q1, among which salary income decelerated the most by 2.4ppts to 4.4% YoY. Migrant worker monthly income also fell back slightly by 0.5% QoQ. Household spending followed a similar trend, slowing by 3.3ppts to 5.0% YoY in Q2, declining more significantly than income growth. Accordingly, the household savings rate on a seasonally adjusted basis stayed largely unchanged at 31%, and has yet to reach pre-Covid levels of 30%.

These developments have led us to revise down our 2024 GDP growth forecast to 4.9% (from 5.0%). We expect growth will stay just below 5% in the next two quarters at 4.8% in Q3 and 4.7% in Q4. Our 2025 growth forecast is unchanged at 4.5%. We also revise down our annual average CPI inflation forecast for 2024 and 2025 by 0.1ppt each to 0.3% and 1.4%, respectively.

## Policy outlook

Policymakers have expressed their commitment to achieving the 5% annual growth target... On the Third Plenum of the 20th CPC Central Committee, policymakers vowed to “resolutely achieve the annual economic goals”, which was rare in such conferences. This raises the hope of more comprehensive policy support, after disappointing Q2 economic performance. The Third Plenum also discussed long-term reform plans. We see fiscal reforms, urbanization, private sector development, attracting foreign investment, and the property sector, among others, as the key reform areas in the next 5 years.

… and PBOC cut policy rate earlier than expected as a response. After the 3rd Plenum, PBOC held an internal meeting to discuss monetary policy reforms needed to deliver “sustained improvement and high-quality development” of the economy. In the following Monday, PBOC unexpectedly cut the 7d OMO policy rate by 10bps to 1.7%, and bank 1-year and 5-year LPR were also lowered by 10bps to 3.35% and 3.85, respectively. Future rate decision will likely depend on PBOC's management on exchange rate: If PBOC starts to allow the CNY exchange rate to fluctuate around a wider band, we see scope for PBOC to cut policy rate more, likely by another 10- 20bps, before year-end. Otherwise, we would expect PBOC not to cut again for the rest of the year.

We expect additional policy support to come from the Politburo meeting in late July. Policymakers' tone and PBOC's move have raised the expectation of further easing. Today’s situation is similar to what happened in mid-2023: after growth disappointed in Q2 2023, PBOC started easing first, while the government followed up with further property and fiscal easing measures afterwards. The upcoming Politburo meeting before end-July will be a key venue for policymakers to signal there intentions for further policy easing. We expect the government will likely announce more property sector easing, and additional fiscal measures (such as a budget revision to increase this year’s deficit by CNY1tr, if not more).

## Hong Kong

Hong Kong's economy outlook remains challenging in a high (US) interest rate and low (China) growth environment. Financial sector activity have not yet recovered, retail sales dropped as inbound travels stalled, while property prices have dropped further despite the removal of restrictions. An expansionary fiscal stance will provide some support to the economy, which is expected to grow 2.6% in 2024 and 2.2% in 2025.

## More headwinds

Hong Kong's economy continues to struggle in a high (US) interest rate and low (China) growth environment. We forecast the economy will grow 2.6% in 2024, revising down from 3.0% previously. Similar to Taiwan, Hong Kong is also benefiting from a recovery in external trade; its exports and imports are expected to grow by 8% and 6%, respectively, in 2024. But its domestic demand continued to face multiple headwinds.

Challenges to Hong Kong's economy was evident from the recent double-digit drop in retail sales in April (-15% YoY) and May (-12% YoY). A popular explanation was that many Hong Kong residents travelled to mainland and overseas during holidays and weekends, reducing their local spending. But while it is true that outbound travels have indeed increased, the number of outbound visitors and their spending this year are broadly at par with pre-Covid levels, and does not appear abnormal. It seems to us that a bigger issue is the lack of recovery in inbound travelers. The number of inbound travelers stalled at around 60-70% of their peak in 2018, and even dropped somewhat in recent months.

After the HKSAR government scrapped all restrictions on property purchases on 28 Feb, residential property transactions surged in March-April, but has slowed again from May onwards. Despite the increase in transactions, property prices continued to drop and is now 25% below its peak in end-2021. Meanwhile, rent increased modestly by about 1.5% from end-2023. As a result, average rental yield have risen to 3.1% lately from 2.4% in 2022, but remains almost 1 percentage point lower than the HKD mortgage rate/money market rate.

The hope of Hong Kong's economic recovery still hinges on a rebound in financial sector activities. This has not happened yet; IPO activities remained subdued in the first half of 2024 amid still-high USD interest rates and an underperforming Chinese economy. To support the economy, the government has further delayed its fiscal consolidation plan in this year's budget. It now aims to run a 4.5% fiscal deficit for FY2024-25, and will keep running deficits until 2027.

## India

FY25 union budget set a fiscal deficit target of 4.9% of GDP, 20bps lower than the interim budget estimate of 5.1% of GDP. This should help reduce cost of capital in the economy on a structural basis and pave the way for an eventual ratings upgrade in the coming years.

n June CPI inflation came at 5.1%yoy, broadly in line with our forecast, but considerably higher than Bloomberg consensus estimate of 4.80%. Inflation volatility is set to rise led by spike in food prices.

n Assuming the Fed cuts 150bps in this cycle, then the RBI can potentially deliver 50-75bps rate cut.

## India budget review

Post the general elections in April-May of 2024, the new government announced the Union Budget for FY25 on 23 July. Striking a delicate balance between the dual objectives of supporting growth and employment, while remaining on the path of fiscal consolidation, the budget set a fiscal deficit target of 4.94% of GDP, 20bps lower than the interim budget estimate of 5.14% of GDP. This was in line with our expectations.

The budget focused on creating jobs, continuing with the infrastructure push and reiterating the government's commitment to stick to the fiscal consolidation path and reduce the budget deficit in FY26 to below 4.5% of GDP. This should help reduce cost of capital in the economy on a structural basis and pave the way for an eventual ratings upgrade in the coming years. Assuming states fiscal deficit at 2.8% of GDP, India's consolidated fiscal deficit is likely to be around 7.7% of GDP in FY25 vs. 8.4% of GDP in FY24. This is 80bps consolidation within one fiscal year, which is commendable. Despite the sharp reduction in fiscal deficit, gross (INR14.01 trn vs INR14.13 trn) and net (INR11.63 trn vs INR11.75 trn) market borrowing reduced only marginally.

Bottom line: Out of the 0.5% of GDP increased proceeds from non-tax revenue, particularly led by higher dividend transfer by the RBI, roughly 0.3% of GDP got offset on account of higher expenditure, which still left 0.2% of GDP worth savings, in the form of lower fiscal deficit, which resulted in a fiscal deficit target of 4.94% for FY25, 20bps lower than the interim budget projection of 5.14% of GDP.

## Ten key takeaways:

1. Capex boost continued: The budget announced a INR11.1trn allocation for FY25 capital expenditure (17%yoy increase; 3.4% of GDP) – unchanged from the interim budget estimate - continuing with the virtuous trend of the last few years. This is not only likely to help create more jobs and support non-inflationary growth, but also should act as an incentive for the private corporate sector to increase its allocation of fresh capex, after remaining on the sidelines through most of the last decade due to a variety of factors. We think even the INR11.1trn allocation is quite large and the authorities may fall short of spending the entire allocation in the remaining eight months of the fiscal year. The capital expenditure allocation therefore incorporates a buffer savings, in our view, to meet the fiscal deficit target, in case revenue expenditure allocation were to increase higher than the budget estimate.

2. Commitment towards fiscal consolidation: The government set a fiscal deficit target of 4.9% of GDP for FY25 vs. an actual outturn of 5.6% of GDP in FY24. The

FY24 budget estimate for fiscal deficit was 5.9% of GDP, which eventually came in lower at 5.6% of GDP, due to strong tax collection and lower-than-anticipated spending in the previous fiscal year. The 4.9% of GDP fiscal deficit target for FY25 indicates a strong commitment on the government's part to stick with the mediumterm goal of bringing centre’s fiscal deficit down to 4.5% of GDP by FY26. Given the improved tax buoyancy and support from higher RBI dividend, we will not be surprised if the fiscal deficit for FY25 ultimately ends up lower below 4.9% of GDP, which would imply that the centre's fiscal deficit can easily fall below 4.5% of GDP in FY26.

3. Change in tax rates: With the aim of rationalizing the capital gains tax regime, the period of holding and tax rates have been adjusted in the budget proposals. Firstly, the current long-term capital gains tax exemption of INR 100,000 has been increased to INR 125,000. The period of holding for listed financial assets is proposed to be fixed at one year, and for other assets (unlisted financial assets and all non-financial assets), it is set at two years to qualify as 'long-term capital assets.' Long-term capital gains across listed and unlisted assets are proposed to be taxed at a uniform rate of 12.5%, bringing uniformity to the current tax rates of 10% and 20%. However, the indexation benefit for long-term capital assets is proposed to be removed. Security Transaction Tax (STT) rates for Derivatives have been increased to 0.1% and futures to 0.02%, while custom duty on gold and silver was reduced to 6%. As far as income tax is concerned, the budget has announced an increase in the standard deduction from INR 50,000 to INR 75,000, alongside adjustments to the tax slabs under the New Tax Regime (NTR). These changes are set to provide individual taxpayers with an annual tax saving of up to INR17,500 under the NTR.

4. Nominal GDP growth assumption conservative: The nominal GDP growth assumption of 10.5%yoy is slightly on the lower side, in our view. As per Deutsche Bank (DB) estimates, India’s real GDP is likely to grow 6.9%yoy in FY25, with nominal GDP growth likely to rise to 11.0%yoy, from about 9.6%yoy in FY24. In FY25, the GDP deflator will normalize, as WPI and CPI inflation converge and average between 3.5-4.5%. The conservative estimate regarding nominal GDP growth lends more credibility to the tax collection projections (net tax revenue is estimated to grow by 11%yoy) and raises the possibility of higher tax collection than budget estimates.

5. Revenue and expenditure growth assumption for FY25 realistic: On the back of a strong revenue growth outturn in FY24, even after accommodating for a lower nominal GDP growth, overall revenue collection is estimated to grow by 15.0%yoy,thanks to a larger-than-expected dividend transfer by the RBI to Government of India. Indeed, the budget has estimated non tax revenue to grow 36%yoy in FY25 or 1.67% of GDP (vs. 1.36% of GDP in FY24) This, in our view, is a realistic estimate. We have factored in slightly lower disinvestment proceeds compared to the budget estimate of INR500bn. With the expenditure base already having risen significantly in the last few years to support growth and accommodate higher subsidies, we are not surprised that total expenditure growth has been estimated at 8.5%yoy, lower than the nominal GDP growth estimate.

6. FY25 fiscal deficit target of 4.9% of GDP is not only achievable; it could be lower: Our revenue and expenditure assumptions yield a fiscal deficit of 4.9% of GDP for FY25, same as the budget estimate, with total revenue/GDP rising to 9.8% of GDP, from 9.4% of GDP in FY24 and expenditure/GDP moderating to about 14.7% (from 15.0% of GDP in FY24). With tax collection continuing to surprise to the upside, it is possible that the fiscal deficit for FY25 could be lower at 4.8% of GDP, but we will wait for a few more months' data to reassess our current fiscal estimate.

7. Quality of fiscal spending has improved significantly: An indicator to track in this regard is the revenue deficit/fiscal deficit ratio (lower the ratio, the better). Since FY17 (which recorded a low of 59.1%), this ratio moved up to a peak of nearly 80% in FY21, but it is encouraging to note that it has improved once again to 46.3% by FY24 and is likely to fall further to 36.0% by FY25, a record low. We expect the authorities to aim at keeping this ratio as low as possible, by continuing to boost capital expenditure while reducing revenue expenditure, particularly subsidies, as soon as the global geopolitical situation improves.

8. Dependence on small savings to fund fiscal deficit has increased significantly and will likely continue: In the last few years, India's dependence on funding its fiscal deficit through small savings component has increased significantly, with a commensurate fall in net market borrowing. In FY24 small savings have helped finance 27% of fiscal deficit. For FY25 also, the government has assumed small savings to finance 26% of the fiscal deficit, which is achievable in our view.

9. Bond index inclusion, a key positive: JP Morgan Global Bond Index team including India in the GBI-EM Global Series from June 2024 is indeed positive, as this will be a welcome source of incremental demand for local currency government bonds which should bring about USD25bn inflows in a staggered manner through FY25. This should lead to a fundamental change in India’s Fixed Income market, with demand-supply dynamics of bonds improving in FY25. India's inclusion in global bond index will help reduce the cost of capital in the economy with a lag.

10. Fiscal consolidation of states, caution warranted: While the centre remains committed to the fiscal consolidation agenda, caution is warranted regarding certain states’ fiscal profile. States that will remain the most vulnerable to debt sustainability risks are Punjab, West Bengal, Bihar, Rajasthan and Kerala, given their weak fiscal and debt metrics even prior to the Covid-19 crisis.

## Inflation outlook

June CPI inflation came at 5.08%yoy, broadly in line with our forecast of 5%yoy, but considerably higher than Bloomberg consensus estimate of 4.80%. The June outturn was also higher than the 4.8%yoy outturn in April and May. This constituted a rise in headline CPI by +1.3%mom in June vs. +0.5%mom in May. Over April-June'24, CPI averaged 4.9%, in line with RBI's estimate. The uptick in June CPI inflation to 5.08%yoy was on account of higher food and beverages prices, particularly led by vegetables. Food & beverages price momentum were up +2.69%mom (8.4%yoy) in June vs. +0.7%mom (7.9%yoy) in May, while core inflation (CPI ex food and fuel) increased +0.12%mom (3.13%yoy) in June vs. +0.33%mom (3.07%yoy) in May.

In June, housing prices recorded a decline of -0.6%mom, as per the seasonal pattern, which pulled down core inflation momentum to +0.12%mom. But this will reverse in July. Additionally, the impact of telecom tariff hikes will also get reflected in July, which will likely push the core inflation momentum closer to +0.85%mom, as per our current estimates. Overall, core inflation (CPI ex food and fuel) will likely rise by 20bps in FY25 due to the telecom tariff hikes. Factoring this, we have already revised up our FY25 core inflation forecast to 3.8%, from 3.6% average earlier.

Core CPI is likely to average 3.4% between April-Sep'24 vs. 4.0% average in Oct-Dec'23, 4.8% in July-Sep'23, 5.1% in April-June'23 and 6%+ in FY23. Thereafter, core CPI average is likely to rise to 4.0%yoy in Oct-Dec'24 and to 4.5%yoy in Jan-March'25. In 2HFY25 (Oct'24 to March'25), we are forecasting core inflation to average 4.3%, which leads to an average of 3.80% for FY25 full year.

We were expecting CPI inflation to fall below 3%yoy in July, due to a favorable base effect of the last year (July'23 CPI was at 7.44%yoy), but given the sharp spike in vegetable prices, and telecom tariff hike, we think CPI inflation could touch 4%yoy or move even slightly higher in July. But then we would also expect sharp disinflation in vegetable prices through August and September, like last year and therefore the average for the quarter (RBI forecast is 3.8%) may not be severely impacted. Despite the food price volatility and telecom tariff hike, we maintain our FY25 CPI inflation forecast of 4.6% average at this stage (RBI's and consensus forecast is at 4.5%). While this may not change the annual CPI average, it could lead to increased intra-quarter volatility and raise the uncertainty premium regarding the prospect of future monetary policy action, in our view.

## Monetary policy outlook

It is evident that the next move from the RBI will likely be a rate cut rather than a hike (please see Special report: How much and how fast can the repo rate fall? 10 July 2024). Indeed, two out of the six MPC members have already called for a 25bps rate cut and change in monetary policy stance in the June'24 policy, but the three RBI members (Governor, Deputy Governor and Executive Director) and the majority are still in favour of maintaining a pause at this stage, given strong growth and persistent uncertainty on the inflation front. The RBI delivered the last rate hike way back in Feb 2023, and since then has maintained a status quo on policy rates, patiently waiting for monetary transmission to work its way through the real economy. By keeping liquidity tight and also tightening macro prudential norms from Nov'23, the central bank has been able to achieve considerable monetary transmission in the current rate hiking cycle.

We have our first 25bps rate cut pencilled in for October, but that could be easily pushed to December or early 2025 depending on India's inflation trajectory and the Fed easing cycle. As far as the quantum of rate cuts are concerned, assuming the Fed cuts 150bps in this cycle, then the RBI can potentially deliver 50-75bps rate cut. But the quantum of rate cuts could be limited to only 50bps, if the Fed cuts lesser than 150bps in this cycle.

## Indonesia

n Bank Indonesia's (BI) focus on the near term is on rupiah stability amid fiscal uncertainty, as it waits for the Federal Reserve to begin easing before starting its own rate cuts.

n Near-term growth and inflation outcomes are stable, but some softness in sentiment hints at slight moderation in economic activity ahead.

## Uncertainty hangs over Indonesia's fiscal picture as BI stands pat

We expect BI to begin easing in October by 25bps, after the Fed’s first expected move in September and also when Prabowo will formally assume office. We also expect BI to complete its total 75bps easing cycle by 2Q 2025 to support the President-elect’s growth plans, with the exact timing contingent on market reaction and rupiah movement. BI made clear that “in the near term, the focus on monetary policy is oriented towards strengthening the effectiveness of Rupiah stabilization measures” in its July meeting, when it kept its policy rate (6.25%) and macroeconomic growth targets unchanged (GDP: 4.7-5.5%; CPI: 1.5-3.5%). CPI inflation at 2.5% YoY in June (YTD: 2.8%) sits comfortably within BI’s target range, while core inflation stood largely stable at 1.9%. Notwithstanding the moderate inflation environment, BI’s focus on rupiah stability is pertinent given its potential impact on not only Indonesia’s fiscal deficits as we discuss below, but also on investor sentiment.

Indonesia revised its fiscal deficit estimates to 2.7% of GDP from 2.3% for 2024 earlier this month. The wider deficit projection was due to both a shortfall in tax revenue, and larger subsidy and personnel spending. Finance minister Sri Mulyani said that lower commodity prices had hit corporates’ income and thus tax payments, while the weaker rupiah had pushed up the energy subsidy expenses for the country, which is a net importer of oil. Subsidies comprised 8.6% of the 2024 draft budget (2019-23 avg: 8.1%). Consequently, Indonesia’s budget deficit for 1H 2024 was 0.34%, compared to an average surplus of 1.1% in 1H for both 2022 and 2023. (Figure 15) Considering the normal pickup in spending towards the end of the year, the downward revision is not unexpected, but at 2.7% it would be the largest estimated deficit since data was available from 2005.

Much of the market discussion over the past month has (again) been on Indonesia’s fiscal and debt approach for the incoming Prabowo administration, in which inconsistent messages by Prabowo’s transition team were made public and thus considerable uncertainty remains. This could weigh on Indonesia’s FDI prospects in the coming years; direct investment flows into the country had already recorded four consecutive quarters of contraction up to Q1 2024 (avg: -13.2%). Most recently, the administration announced that Thomas Djiwandono, who is Prabowo’s nephew, has been appointed as deputy finance minister. Djiwandono had pledged fiscal continuity and discipline for the incoming administration and has been a key part of Prabowo’s transition team on economic and fiscal matters. Nonetheless, this did not immediately assuage market concerns. The rupiah weakened and CDS rates rose following the announcement. A conflicting message came a week before Djiwandono’s appointment, when the media reported Hashim Djojohadikusumo, Prabowo’s brother, saying that the President-elect will allow the debt-GDP ratio to rise to 50% in 5 years, from the current \~39%. Our earlier analysis found that this was improbable. There will be more information to be gleaned during the President’s budgetary discourse and speech during Indonesia’s Independence Day in mid-August, but we expect continued uncertainty until the budget is signed into law in October.

## Sri Lanka

Political noise is likely to rise, in the run-up to the Presidential elections between Sep-Oct, but with the debt restructuring plan in place, it is unlikely that the IMF programme will be derailed, irrespective of the political outcome, given the lack of any credible alternative options.

n Sri Lanka looks poised for a ratings upgrade, with the debt restructuring plan being in place.

n While there may be scope for appreciation in the near-term, we think LKR is unlikely to sustain below 300 vs. the USD, particularly with political uncertainty looming on the horizon.

## Debt restructuring plan in place

After defaulting on its external debt in April 2022, the Sri Lankan authorities finally entered into a deal with its private creditors on 3<sup>rd</sup> July 2024, to restructure USD12.6bn of sovereign bonds (ISBs), which would require the investors to take a 28% haircut on the nominal amount of existing bonds. Sri Lanka's total external debt is about USD37bn. With this deal, Sri Lanka achieved the final step in restructuring private, bilateral and domestic debt (note that Sri Lanka owes China Development Bank USD2.2bn; we await details of the restructuring plan related to this component of the debt). This was a key pre-requisite for debt sustainability as part of the USD2.9bn four-year financial assistance programme that Sri Lankan authorities entered with the IMF starting in March 2023.

Apart from 28% haircut, the debt restructuring framework also proposes 11% reduction on past interest with payments on the interest component to start from September. The outline proposes to swap four existing dollar-denominated bonds for a bundle of three fixed income instruments:

1. Plain vanilla bonds: This has a coupon of 4% and matures in 2028.

2. Macro-linked bonds: Payouts and principal will be adjusted according to the country's economic performance - lower in case the economy fails to hit the IMF baseline projections, and higher if the economy outperforms.

3. Governance linked bonds: While the statement did not detail the parameters to which the payout was linked, it will likely be linked to the achievement of the reforms outlined by the IMF and meeting of the tax revenue targets.

The agreement now remains to be confirmed by (i) the Secretariat of Sri Lanka's Official Creditor Committee (the "OCC") to ensure comparability of treatment with the terms agreed between Sri Lanka and the OCC ("Comparability of Treatment") and (ii) the IMF staff to ensure consistency with the parameters and debt sustainability objectives of Sri Lanka's IMF-supported Program. The deal with private creditors comes on the back of an agreement signed with official creditors to restructure about USD10bn of debt a week earlier. Official creditors will not be taking a haircut on the principal, but have agreed to reduce interest rates to an average of 2.1% and extend the term of the loans by a further eight years. The Sri Lankan authorities has secured a moratorium on bilateral debt repayments until 2028, but ISB repayments are due to start in September. Under the agreement, the Sri Lankan government would be paying USD225 mn upfront to bond holders.

## Macro outlook post debt restructuring

This is indeed a positive development and once finalized is likely to lead to a ratings upgrade for Sri Lanka. It will also allow the Sri Lankan economy to move on with its economic agenda, although political noise is likely to rise from hereon, in the run-up to the Presidential elections between Sep-Oct. With the debt restructuring plan in place, it is unlikely that the IMF programme will be derailed, irrespective of the political outturn, given lack of any credible alternative options.

## We summarise our key macro outlook below:

1. Growth: The Sri Lankan economy grew by 5.3%yoy in 1Q'24, higher than 4.5%yoy in 4Q'23 and 1.6%yoy growth in 3Q'23. The economy is on the path of recovery, aided by tourism revenues and buoyant remittances, albeit from a very low base. Indeed, the Sri Lankan economy contracted by 2.3%yoy in 2023, on the back of a 7.3%yoy decline in 2022. We are forecasting Sri Lanka's real GDP to grow 3.0%yoy and 3.5%yoy in 2024 and 2025, respectively, with some upside risks, given the better-than-anticipated 1Q'24 real GDP outturn. However, a 3-3.5%yoy growth outturn is not impressive, considering the favourable base of last year, but given the multi-prong challenges, Sri Lanka will likely have to contend with lower growth for the time being.

2. Inflation: Sri Lanka's CPI inflation came at 1.7%yoy in June, after falling to 0.9% in May. Inflation was also low in April at 1.5%yoy and March at 0.9%yoy, led by reduction in electricity tariff, fuel and LPG price. The authorities have cut power prices once again this month by 22.5% and this should keep inflation in low-singledigits for now. Credit growth has bottomed and expanded in May and June, but we expect a gradual recovery in the months ahead to mid-single digit levels, as the lagged impact of previous rate cuts and liquidity operations likely result in further monetary transmission. Overall, we expect CPI inflation to average around 2.5- 3.0% in 2024 vs. 20.5% in 2023.

3. Monetary policy: The central bank delivered 25bps rate cut in July 2024. CBSL has already delivered 725bps of rate cut thus far, but we expect another 25bps rate cut in Nov'24 or Jan'25 policy, as real rates remain positive by over 500bps on a forward looking basis, with inflation having fallen sharply. With 725bps of rate cuts already delivered, the CBSL expects market interest rates to drift lower.

4. FX: Gross FX reserves increased to USD5.6bn (including the PBOC swap) by end-June 2024, supported by considerable net purchases by CBSL from the domestic foreign exchange market amidst increased foreign currency inflows. This has helped LKR to appreciate by about 6.5% against the USD thus far in 2024. Over the coming months and quarters, there should be more inflows as financial assistance from multilateral institutions come in, but the CBSL will need to continue buying USD to improve the FX reserves position further. Also, political noise and uncertainty will likely increase from hereon, in the run up to the Presidential elections in Sep-Oct, and at some point the remaining restrictions on automobile imports will likely have to be removed, which should then lead to a proper price discovery in the FX market based on true demand and supply for Dollars. Therefore, on balance, while there may be scope for appreciation in the near-term, we think LKR is unlikely to sustain below 300 vs. the USD, particularly with political uncertainty looming on the horizon. We are forecasting LKR/USD to end the year around 310 levels.

Government spending surged 49.6% YoY (48.7%) in April/May, after contracting 10.7% in Q1, while public investment spending rose to +30.7%, from a 93.9% fall in the same period. Private investment growth also improved (expanding 1.7% in April/May vs. a 0.2% fall in Q1), while private consumption growth moved largely sideways (to 1.7% from 1.8% in the same period), when discounted by inflation, data point to weaker private domestic demand. In particular, private consumption growth, discounted by CPI inflation, fell sharply to 0.9% in April/May, from 2.6% in Q1. Moreover, individual credit contracted 0.5% in April/May, vs. 0.4% growth in Q1, while business credit growth moved largely sideways, to +2% from +1.8% in the same period, public sector credit growth rebounded sharply to +0.8% from -7.2%. Worse still, deterioration in labour market conditions point to rising headwinds to private consumption. Employment fell 0.8% YoY in April/May vs. 0.2% growth in Q1, leaving Thailand's unemployment rate higher at 1.2% in May vs. 1% in March. Both consumer confidence and business sentiment continued to trend lower.

## Vietnam

n After delivering a stronger-than-expected growth in 1H, Vietnam may take a temporary breather in Q3 amid national mourning and political transition.

## An early completion of political transition could be growth positive

Vietnam expanded 6.4% YoY ytd in 1H, led by 16.9% growth in exports, while investment and consumption rose by 6.7% and 5.8%, respectively. On a quarterly basis, GDP growth quickened to 6.9% YoY (3% QoQ sa) in Q2, from 5.9% (0.1%) in Q1, with high-frequency data pointing to improvement in consumption. Retail sales growth rose to 8.9% YoY (4.5% when discounted by inflation) in Q2, from 6.8% (3%) in Q1. FDI also expanded at a faster pace of 9% in Q2 (vs. 7.6% in Q1), partly countering the slowdown in state investment growth (to 4% from 8.1%) in the same period. Goods import growth accelerated to 19.6% in Q2 (from 14.5% in Q1), outpacing goods export growth, as the latter slowed to 12.2% from 17.8% in the same period, resulting in a smaller trade balance of USD3.8bn in Q2 (down from USD7.8bn in Q1). By sector, industrial production rose 8.6% in Q2 (up from 6.2% in Q1), leading GDP growth higher, while services growth rose to 7.1% from 6.2% in the same period, as growth in the number of tourists slowed to 45.8% in Q2 (from 72.1% growth in Q1). Meanwhile, improvements in agricultural production and construction were more modest, rising to 3.3% and 7.1% in Q2, respectively, albeit still stronger than 3% and 6.8% respective growth reported in Q1.

Looking ahead, Vietnam's manufacturing PMI points to stronger export growth ahead, led by electronics, as suggested by global electronics PMIs. Although recent hikes in the country's minimum wage (6%) and civil servant basic wage (30%) should support consumption, we believe we could see moderation in domestic demand in Q3 – in public spending and investment, in particular, as the country enters a period of mourning and political transition, following the passing of Community Party General-Secretary Nguyen Phu Trong, with President To Lam to serve as the interim leader. While a formal designation of new party leader may take until the 2026 party congress, an earlier completion of the political transition would pave the way for faster implementation of the government's economic policies. It remains to be seen whether the government will manage to achieve its 2024 budget plan in 2H.

The government has sought to spend 95% of its 2024 budget, after a significant shortfall in 1H amid the country's anti-corruption drive. While government revenue rose 17.7% YoY to VND1038.1tn in 1H (61% of the 2024 target), current expenditure and investment/development spending stood at VND803.6tn (37.9%) in total, with the latter type of spending lagging behind. This revenue and expenditure gap resulted in a budget surplus of VND217tn (4.1% of GDP) (vs. the 2024 budget deficit target of VND399.4tn, 3.6% of GDP). In fact, Prime Minister Pham Minh Chinh called on state officials to show “determination to overcome the situation of avoiding taking responsibility and fear of making mistakes”. A further lapse in budget execution could result in a worse-than-expected payback in growth in Q3, despite the surge in sequential growth in Q2, which has placed the economy on a better path to meeting the government growth target range of 6% to 6.5% this year.

On the monetary policy front, the SBV plans to continue to prioritise macro stability while seeking bad debt resolution. According to the SBV deputy governor, the banking system's NPL ratio stood at 6.9% – with NPLs on banks' balance sheets at 5% (vs. 4.55% at end-2023 and 2.03% at end-2022) and the remainder either being sold to VAMC (Vietnam Asset Management Company) or comprising rescheduled loans that are at risk of turning into NPLs, despite extending Circular 02/2022/TT-NHNN (extended for six months, until end-2024), which allowed commercial banks to reschedule their debt payment periods and maintain their debt groups for certain sectors. The government's Circular 3/2024/TT-NHNN also requires VAMC to carry out bad debt recovery appraisals before purchasing bad debt, being allowed to do so only if there is a prospect for recovery and being purchased at market price (in line with its assessment by qualified price appraisal organizations). The SBV seeks to bring the banking system's NPL ratio down to 3% by end-2025. Credit growth accelerated sharply in June, with credit by mid-year 6% above that at end-2023, up sharply from 2.41% growth reported at end-May – suggesting that the government's credit growth target of 15% may be reached this year. Credit for the industry and construction sectors rose 4.2% in 1H from end-2023, while that for real estate stood at 4.6%. The SBV has also proposed lowering lending interest rates for home buyers (to 3% [vs. 2% currently] below the market rate) who borrow from the social housing credit programme.

The SBV noted that its intention is to continue its monetary policy in a "flexible manner", "to achieve the objectives of maintaining macro-economic stability, controlling inflation, being consistent in gradually shifting from the relationship of mobilization-lending to foreign currency buying-selling and anti-dollarisation, in accordance with the government's policy." In this regard, it underlined the importance of its application of a 0% interest rate policy for USD deposits. While coming off from recent highs, interbank rates have remained relatively elevated, in support of the dong. Meanwhile, the SBV struck a relatively sanguine view on inflation, noting that it "has been controlled at a low level". Inflation fell in June to 4.3% YoY from 4.4% in May, within the government's target range at 4% to 4.5%.

Macroeconomic fluctuations often account for most of the risks associated with exposures to instruments that promise to pay fixed or variable interest rates. For an investor who is long fixed-rate instruments (thus receiving these cash flows), increases in interest rates naturally lift the discount factors applied to the expected cash flows and thus cause a loss. The longer the maturity of a certain cash flow and the higher the move in the discount factor, the higher will be the loss. Upside surprises in inflation, fiscal funding needs, and FX depreciation rates are among the most common adverse macroeconomic shocks to receivers. But counterparty exposure, issuer creditworthiness, client segmentation, regulation (including changes in assets holding limits for different types of investors), changes in tax policies, currency convertibility (which may constrain currency conversion, repatriation of profits and/or liquidation of positions), and settlement issues related to local clearing houses are also important risk factors. The sensitivity of fixed-income instruments to macroeconomic shocks may be mitigated by indexing the contracted cash flows to inflation, to FX depreciation, or to specified interest rates - these are common in emerging markets. The index fixings may - by construction - lag or mis-measure the actual move in the underlying variables they are intended to track. The choice of the proper fixing (or metric) is particularly important in swaps markets, where floating coupon rates (i.e., coupons indexed to a typically short-dated interest rate reference index) are exchanged for fixed coupons. Funding in a currency that differs from the currency in which coupons are denominated carries FX risk.
