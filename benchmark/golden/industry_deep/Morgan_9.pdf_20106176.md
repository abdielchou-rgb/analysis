## United States

Real GDP grew at a 3.3% rate last quarter and we now look for a 1.75% gain this quarter

• Core PCE inflation increased at only a 2.0% rate last quarter and 2.9% in the year ending December

• While welcoming the inflation progress, we expect the FOMC to be cautious in signaling cuts

• We expect nonfarm employment growth of 225,000 in January, on fewer layoffs than usual

In the second half of last year the US economy achieved the elusive soft landing: core inflation gliding toward 2% with few bumps in the labor market or economic activity. To the contrary, growth was quite firm. This week saw the first report on GDP growth in 4Q23, which came in at a strong 3.3%. For the second half as a whole output growth was an even stronger 4.1%. Meanwhile core PCE inflation last quarter increased at only a 2.0% rate—effectively a repeat of the 3Q outcome and spot on the nose of the Fed<sup>’</sup>s inflation target.

After the immaculate disinflation of 2H23 will the favorable configuration of growth and inflation continue in 2024? We won<sup>’</sup>t get our first real look at developments early in the year until next Friday<sup>’</sup>s January payroll report. However, consumer spending momentum increased late in 4Q with a solid 0.5% gain in real consumption in December and we revised up our estimate of 1Q24 growth from 1.25% to 1.75%. We also believe core PCE inflation will run in the low 2s this year: moderating declines in goods prices are expected to be about offset by cooling in rental inflation. While this wouldn<sup>’</sup>t quite be the Goldilocks outcome we saw in 2H23, it would be much better than many forecasters were expecting during the worst of the <sup>’</sup>21-<sup>’</sup>22 inflation surge.

Against this benign backdrop the FOMC is scheduled to meet next week. We think Fed officials have probably seen enough progress on inflation that they can officially drop their bias to tighten further. However, we think it<sup>’</sup>s too soon for them to communicate that they are preparing to loosen policy. Fed leaders have stated a desire to be confident that inflation is on course to sustainably return to 2% before they cut rates. The one missing piece that would give them such confidence is a softer labor market.

We don<sup>’</sup>t forecast that softening coming in next week<sup>’</sup>s January labor market report. Instead we look for job growth to quicken to 225,000 last month. The prior seasonal pattern of large layoffs in January seems to have diminished in the postpandemic years, a regularity that the seasonal adjustment routines may not have fully learned about yet.

## Still going strong

The marquee report of the week was the first look at 4Q GDP growth. The 3.3% outturn was an upside surprise, driven mostly by a larger-than-anticipated inventory build. Strong inventories last quarter could detract from growth this quarter: with flusher stockpiles at the end of 4Q firms may see less need to ramp up production to meet demand this quarter. Even so, away from inventories it was a solid report. Private domestic demand, sometimes considered a sort of “core GDP<sup>”</sup> measure, grew at a respectable 2.6% rate (Figure 1).

## Inflation looking tame

Core PCE inflation increased only 0.17% last month, bringing the year-ago measure down from 3.2% to 2.9%. The more recent run rates look even more favorable, with the three- and six-month annualized running 1.5% and 1.9%, respectively (Figure 4).

Figure 4: Core PCE inflation run rates
![](images/9bfbeb02b1d85b16ff4ba44533e031d89de9587d3309c5a833356618e9aa2520.jpg)

Similarly, the super-core measure (core services ex-housing) has also looked well-behaved lately, with the three- and sixmonth run rates at 2.2% and 2.8%. (Given the normal goodsservices inflation gap, super-core at 2.75% is probably about consistent with overall core running close to the Fed<sup>’</sup>s 2% target).

One shouldn<sup>’</sup>t lose sight of the fact that headline PCE inflation has also cooled, with the year-ago reading edging down to 2.6% in December. While core inflation provides a better signal of near-term inflation trends, headline inflation likely figures into workers<sup>’</sup> wage bargaining. The decline in headline inflation from a peak of 7.1% may be one reason wage growth has cooled even without any material rise in the unemployment rate.

## Housing gets a heartbeat

After peaking at around 8.3% in late October 30-year fixed mortgage rates have come off about 100bp, though remain high by the standards of the past two decades. And so it is not surprising to see some indicators of housing demand pick up, albeit to levels that are still relatively low. This week<sup>’</sup>s December pending home sales report was a case in point (Figure 5).

## A half step from the Fed

At next week<sup>’</sup>s FOMC meeting the Committee is universally expected to leave the funds rate unchanged for the fourth consecutive meeting. In that period the statement has conveyed a bias to tighten, by talking about conditions that would prompt “additional policy firming.<sup>”</sup> At next week<sup>’</sup>s meeting we expect the Committee will turn to a neutral stance, conveying no bias to either tighten or ease at the subsequent meeting.

Since September 2022 Chair Powell has said that the Committee is committed to achieving a stance of policy that is “sufficiently restrictive to bring inflation sustainably down to 2 percent over time.<sup>”</sup> In place of December<sup>’</sup>s bias to deliver “additional policy firming<sup>”</sup> we think next week<sup>’</sup>s statement will describe the unchanged stance of policy as “sufficiently restrictive.<sup>”</sup> In the last two press conferences, after discussing sufficiently restrictive policy, Powell appended a vow to “[keep] policy restrictive until we are confident that inflation is on a path to that objective.<sup>”</sup> We believe the Committee could add this phrase to the forward guidance if they want to push out market expectations for the first rate cut.

January is usually a month with net job losses before seasonal adjustment as cold weather weighs on employment in some sectors and the holiday shopping season, which temporary lifts activity, is wound down. But we think these job losses won<sup>’</sup>t be as significant as anticipated by the seasonal factors given the recent tightness in the labor market and that this will result in strong job growth after seasonal adjustment. A hint of this has come in the jobless claims data, which have shown very favorable seasonally adjusted readings in much of the first half of January as the jump in filings that the seasonal factors anticipated was not fully realized and the seasonally adjusted data improved. A similar thing occurred in January 2023, with seasonally adjusted initial claims filings declining temporarily that month and this corresponding with a surge in seasonally adjusted nonfarm employment that month (472,000) that was well above the trend for job growth from surrounding months. January 2024 looks different than January 2023 is many respects (fewer unfilled job openings, softer preceding trend for job growth, less favorable changes in temperatures, and some recent downbeat signals on employment like the ISM services survey<sup>’</sup>s employment index) so we don<sup>’</sup>t look for as meaningful of a pickup in job growth in January 2024 as we saw in January 2023.
