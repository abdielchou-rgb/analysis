# Oil & Gas: How Could the US Election Impact Energy Policy?

We evaluate executive authority on energy policy and associated impacts on oil & gas markets, as well as the energy & clean power sector. We find that executive action with respect to supply is limited, but other policy factors could drive nuanced impacts in the energy & clean power sectors.

![](images/527386fd18fe527ad5affc7f462feb34a1897fbd1db47dbfba67278d060944b2.jpg)

Key debate: can the election lead to a near-term boost to energy production, with knock-on effects to inflation & stock prices for energy companies?

• Former President Trump has cited the linkage between permissive energy policy (i.e. promoting more domestic production) and lower levels of inflation, arguing that higher levels of supply should drive down costs for consumers.

• We find that there are limitations to the president's authority on energy policy - with some exceptions - but there are other policy variables that could impact supply & demand and consequently companies in the oil & gas sector in the event of a potential second Trump administration.

Note that in this report we focus on the on oil & gas markets, and the energy & clean power sector, in a hypothetical Trump win scenario because we expect a second Democrat term would signal a continuation of the status quo for these markets and sectors, as noted below. Hence, we encourage investors to interpret our views as an expression of a particular scenario outcome rather than a base case or explicit expectation for this outcome.

Much Ado About Energy?

Former President Trump has cited energy policy as a key antidote to inflation levels, arguing that higher levels of domestic production will drive lower costs. Hence, we evaluate the potential policy actions a president could take to enable higher levels of production and to materially change supply levels.

Presidential authorities with respect to energy policy are limited, but can be impactful at the margin over time, as we explain below. Nonetheless, energy tends to feature heavily on the campaign trail, likely due to political linkages between support for "traditional" energy sources and the longer-term transition to lower carbon alternatives as well as the relationship between energy prices and voters' perception of the economy. For those reasons, it's not surprising that former President Trump's campaign, as well as the Republican party platform, focus heavily on energy as a key policy item in a potential Republican win scenario. The former president also cited higher levels of energy production as an offsetting force to higher inflation (see gray box below) several times in a recent Bloomberg Businessweek interview.

## Energy Policy Has Been a Feature of Former President Trump's Campaign Messaging

• From the GOP platform: "Republicans will increase Energy Production across the board, streamline permitting, and end market-distorting restrictions on Oil, Natural Gas, and Coal. The Republican Party will once again make America Energy Independent, and then Energy Dominant, lowering Energy prices even below the record lows achieved during President Trump’s first term…we will DRILL, BABY, DRILL and we will become energy independent."

• From Trump's recent interview with Bloomberg: "So inflation was caused by energy. And what he did is he started immediately cutting my energy."

• On whether or not the Fed should hold off on lowering interest rates: "Unless they cut other costs commensurate with interest, interest is a very big cost...I have a plan to make up for that with energy cutting. Energy. We can cut energy way down. You know, I always say we have more liquid gold under our feet, which is true. We have more liquid gold than anybody. If we could get energy—energy is such a big cost and interest is such a big cost."

But how much can the president actually influence energy policy (and consequently, production levels)? Former President Trump's campaign messaging is very supportive of the oil & gas sector, but production and investment levels are reasonably insulated from federal politics: \~25% of oil and \~10% of natural gas is produced on federal land and waters in the US (per the US Department of the Interior), and FERC (the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission) is responsible for pipelines that span multiple states, but not within individual states. Indeed, President Biden campaigned on ending new oil & gas

drilling on federal lands and replacing relaxed energy regulatory requirements with stricter rules designed to restrict investment, but US oil and gas production have both hit all time highs in recent quarters.

Further, any policy changes are unlikely to quickly translate to changes in supply levels. Time is needed for such policy changes to take effect: deregulation itself can be a lengthy process (as we note here, "re-regulation" as a process takes a few years on average). Similarly, there's a lag between the policy implementation and a change in production levels. For example, leasing changes on federal land can have an approximately 10 year tail between policy change and production impacts, as policy changes are typically proposed for future, not existing, leases.

But there are some potential measures that the newly-elected president could take to affect production levels, that could matter at the margin or over the longer term, some of which can be accomplished through the executive branch alone. As described below, our strategists do not expect such policy changes, if implemented, would result in significant changes in production, but we expect many of them would likely feature in a potential second Trump term:

• Permitting reform: Relaxing standards that facilitate building interstate pipelines could increase supply, but would require Congressional consent (i.e., a Republican sweep). That being said, the president does have some unilateral authority to approve key pipelines that transfer oil (i.e., Keystone XL).

• Foreign policy/sanctions enforcement: As we highlighted in our 2024 election early guide we think stricter sanctions enforcement would be feasible in a Trump win scenario, in particular on countries like Iran/Venezuela, and there are also implications for oil & gas in the event of a ceasefire in Russia/Ukraine, as more Russian gas would come back to the market (see the next section). Similarly, we have written about the potential to rejoin the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action (the JCPOA) as a potential catalyst for higher levels of supply, though such an agreement would likely be off the table in a potential second Trump term given the former President withdrew from that agreement.

• Environmentally-focused deregulation to facilitate energy production: The GOP platform calls for "lifting restrictions on American Energy Production" and "unleashing energy production from all sources, including nuclear." It's possible that Trump, if elected, instructs agencies like the EPA to loosen the regulatory burden on oil & gas companies, for example rolling back Biden-era rules on climaterelated emissions targets (including the methane fee or reversing the President's pause on gas export permits), or easing the environmental review period required for project approvals (which the former President did in 2020 but was later reversed by the Biden administration in 2022).

• Leasing of federal lands and waters for oil & gas development: The administration can control the pace of sales and review/facilitate development of projects on certain federal lands (like Alaska or the Gulf of Mexico), although it's worth noting that lease approvals do not spur instantaneous drilling as time is needed to build the infrastructure and development prior to seeing an increase in production.

# What Would a Trump Win Mean for Oil Supply & Demand?

Martijn Rats

Although it's unlikely, in our view, that former President Trump could leverage executive authority to rapidly increase production levels (as outlined below), his re-election could still have significant implications for oil and gas markets. In particular, three issues stand out to us:

1. Sanctions on Iran: In 2018, the Trump administration started a campaign of "maximum pressure" on Iran, in order to force the country to reverse its nuclear program. In practice, this was done via the implied potential imposition of secondary sanctions on buyers of Iranian oil. As shown in Exhibit 1, Iran's crude oil exports fell sharply in 2018/19 as a result of this.

The Biden administration implemented those sanctions more softly, which resulted in a significant rebound in Iran's oil exports of nearly 1 mb/d.

A second Trump administration could reverse this once again. In fact, in our view, there is a considerable likelihood of a return to the "maximum pressure campaign". VP candidate JD Vance recently commented that "if you want to check Iran, the way to do it is to, one, withdraw their oil money." If that happens, that could put several hundred thousand barrels per day of Iranian oil exports at risk, which could drive oil prices higher.

2. Import tariffs and their impact on inflation, growth and commodity demand: Trump has called for import tariffs of 60% on all goods brought in from China, and possibly as much as 10% on all other imports. Such protectionist measures could likely drive inflation higher and global trade lower. In turn, this could weigh on commodity demand, possibly sharply. In contrast to the first issue, the threat of significant import tariffs is a bearish risk for oil prices.

3. Subsidies on Electric Vehicles in the Inflation Reduction Act: Trump has pushed back against EV subsidies, which has raised the probability that, in the event of a Republican win, his administration could reverse the electric vehicle credit in the IRA. In the short term, this would have only a modest impact on domestic oil demand, in our view. However, this could become more important over time.

## Domestic US Oil Production

Trump has indicated he favours a significant increase in domestic oil production. In his convention speech, Trump said "we will drill, baby, drill [...] we will lead a large-scale decline in prices [...] we will do it at levels that nobody's ever seen before." This is in sharp contrast to Biden's campaign promises from 2020, which included the promise of "no more drilling on federal land."

However, significantly accelerating US oil production would be no mean feat, for two reasons:

First, US oil production is already at an all-time high and continues to grow sharply.

This is highlighted by Exhibit 2 . As shown, US oil production has grown faster under Biden than under any other US president with the exception of Obama. Also, over the last several years, US oil production has also grown faster than that of any other country .

Second, there is tension between growing oil production and lowering prices at the same time. The vast majority of oil production growth has come from shale basins, such as those in the Permian, Bakken or Eagle Ford formations. On average, break-even oil prices in those basins are relatively low - around \~\$50/bbl.

However, the variation around that average is very wide. This is shown in Exhibit 3 , which shows the distribution of break-even oil prices of the 650 wells that were put-onproduction in December 2023. By now, there is enough data available to calculate a break even for every well.

The mode of the distribution in that chart is even a little below \$50/bbl. However, there is also a long tail of wells that require much higher oil prices - close to 20% of wells that came on-stream that month had a break-even above \$70/bbl.

To raise output significantly, more wells need to be drilled each month. However, the additional wells will likely be of the \$70 break-even variety, not the \$50 ones.

In our experience, when more than 20% of wells drilled have break-even below prevailing oil prices, activity levels decline. In fact, recently WTI has been around \$80/bbl and the US oil-directed rig count has been falling steadily in recent months, already down >30 units since April. Therefore, this effect already seems to be playing out.

Perhaps a potential second Trump administration could try to lower these break-evens through de-regulation and cutting "red tape". However, the key factors driving break-evens are simply related to geology of the formations and the operations of drilling and fracking. As a result, the economics of raising production substantially and lowering prices at the same time are challenging.

## Oil prices, inflation, and the consumer

## Diego Anzoategui

A substantial and persistent drop in oil prices under a potential Trump administration seems unlikely and, therefore, our base case is a minor overall economic impact through energy prices. But if a set of policies manages to decrease oil prices, the final effect on inflation and the consumer will depend on the size and persistence of the drop.

When studying the pass-through to broader consumer prices, our previous work finds that a 10% drop in oil prices coming from a supply shock takes 35bp from headline CPI for 3 months, but just 3bp from core CPI. The main core component affected by lower energy prices is transportation services, especially airfares. Lower energy prices must be sustained for some time to have a greater, more durable effect on core prices ( Exhibit 4 ). The Fed will likely look through this shock.

Of course lower energy prices can be a boost to consumption, but just like for inflation, the decrease in oil prices has to be meaningful and persistent to have a measurable effect on consumption. In a recent publication, we find that in a scenario where a 100% drop in WTI prices persists for a full year, real consumption would be reduced by up to a cumulative 3.7% over that period, with a boost led by durable goods. Importantly, a large fraction of the effect occurs 3-8 months after the initial oil shock.

## US Natural Gas

On the cusp of a new growth cycle. After a multiyear pause, the US gas market is now on the cusp of another cycle of demand growth, driven by LNG (liquefied natural gas) exports and, to a lesser extent, rising electricity consumption. At the same time, constraints on supply from a maturing industry and pipeline bottlenecks make this next growth cycle look different than history. The US went through an LNG expansion wave last decade, but Henry Hub prices moved lower from robust supply growth and a wave of pipeline expansions that "unlocked" low-cost gas. Now the E&P industry is more mature, some shale regions are facing inventory exhaustion, and lack of new pipeline capacity will limit further growth from Appalachia. As a result, meeting the next wave of demand growth will be more costly than the last (see On the Cusp of a New Growth Cycle). We do not expect the US election outcome to materially impact our price outlook, but highlight some potential considerations:

• Permitting natural gas pipeline infrastructure in the US is currently a long and difficult process. While there have been permitting reform proposals, we continue to see limited potential for meaningful legislation absent a Republican sweep. Under a scenario where the process to construct interstate pipelines becomes easier, this could "unlock" low-cost Marcellus supply, pressuring US gas prices. That said, our base case expectation is no change to domestic supply, and we retain our constructive 2025 outlook for US gas (see here).

• Looking into 2026, the global LNG market is oversupplied, driving the risk of cargo cancellations from US facilities (see here). Should Russia pipeline gas flows to Europe increase (at the expense of LNG imports), this would add to the oversupplied environment, pressuring LNG prices while also increasing the risk of US export facility shut-ins, a headwind for Henry Hub.

• Reversal of the LNG permitting pause (see here) could add more long-term domestic LNG feedgas demand.

• In the longer term, increasing electricity consumption tied to data center growth from AI (artificial intelligence), industrial onshoring, and a broader trend of electrification should be a tailwind to US natural gas consumption (see here).

Under a potential Republican victory in the upcoming US election, natural gas could take a larger share of this incremental demand.

# What Would a Trump Win Mean for the Power Sector?

## Dave Arcaro

We see limited impacts overall to the power space (CEG, NRG, PEG, VST) given the base case view that the gas price outlook is unchanged, but with some downside tail risk. Power prices in the US are influenced by natural gas prices – in key US markets, natural gas-fired power plants represent a significant portion of overall power generation and often set the price for the market. As a result, increases in gas prices lead to higher power prices, and lower gas prices serve to lower power prices. As noted in the previous section, the Morgan Stanley base case expectation is no change to domestic natural gas supply and a constructive 2025 outlook for US gas prices – rising gas prices should support rising power prices and EBITDA for power stocks. The potential downside risks include: 1) A scenario in which the process to construct interstate pipelines becomes easier – this could "unlock" low-cost Marcellus supply, pressuring US gas prices, and 2) The risk of cargo cancellations from US facilities or lower LNG demand for US deliveries to Europe could pressure LNG prices while also increasing the risk of US export facility shut-ins, a headwind for Henry Hub. These downside risks to gas prices would likely result in lower power prices. We previously calculated a \$3-4/MWh impact on power prices for a \$0.50/ mmBTU change in the price of gas.

We don't expect any changes to our outlook regarding the pace of data center buildouts in the US, the nuclear production tax credit (PTC), or the potential for data centers to collocate with nuclear plants regardless of who wins the presidency. No change in view for CEG or VST on this opportunity. The outcome of the election is unlikely to change the desirability of the US overall as a venue for data center development, change the pace of the data center buildout, or alter the locations of these data centers. The US is still a primary market for the development and use of proprietary data and LLMs – and we think incentives for data center development stemming from state and local governments and the power market dynamics for locating data centers are unlikely to change based on the elections. We also expect the nuclear PTC to remain intact regardless of the outcome – particularly because the IRA is unlikely to be fully repealed, in our view, and because nuclear has robust bipartisan support. We think the opportunity set for data centers collocating with nuclear plants should remain the same regardless of the election outcome, important for CEG and VST.

Less risk for coal plant shutdowns. A potential Trump administration could revisit EPA rules such as the recently proposed carbon standards for coal plants and new gas plants. These rules require lower operating levels or carbon capture technology to be installed on these plants by the 2030s. A potential pullback on these initiatives could lower the risk of new environmental requirements on these plants for the time being. We don't expect any near-term implications of these rules on power markets.
