## US Fixed Income Overview

## Markets brace for key policy week

The Treasury curve steepened and spreads ground tighter as DM central banks inched closer to rate cuts and economic data was favorable

● We will receive key monetary and fiscal announcements next week. We expect the Fed to leave policy rates unchanged at the upcoming FOMC meeting while adjusting forward guidance from a hawkish to neutral bias. We look for Treasury to announce a final round of increases to nominal auction sizes in its quarterly refunding announcement

● The Fed tweaked the BTFP to erase the riskless arbitrage offered to banks, which should limit further growth in the facility. We see limited implications for money markets. We see risks to an earlier end to QT, which should be supportive for swap spreads

● Economics: We look for non-farm payrolls to have increased 225k and the unemployment rate to have held steady at 3.7% in January

Treasuries: We continue to hold longs in 5-year Treasuries and 5s/30s steepeners as yields should fall and the curve should steepen as we approach the first ease. We look for Treasury to announce a final round of increases to nominal auction sizes: Treasury remains underfinanced and the T-bill share of debt should remain above 20%. Hold energy-hedged 10-year TIPS breakeven narrowers

● Interest Rate Derivatives: Swap spreads in the belly remain narrow relative to fair value, although the gap has shrunk steadily since year end. We continue to see value in swap spread wideners in the 5Y sector. At the front end, with spreads wide to fair value, with the 2s/5s curve likely biased flatter and with RRP balances expected to decline further, we now look for narrower swap spreads. We recommend positioning for this via 2s/5s weighted spread curve steepeners, given the widening risk posed by spreads in the belly

● Securitized Products: Remain neutral on the mortgage basis; spreads are still on the tighter end of the fair range, but may have more support from banks than we had anticipated. We have seen strong performance across CMBS, RMBS, and ABS sectors but generally turn cautious at these tight spread levels

Corporates: HG bond spreads reached their post-GFC tights as demand continues to outstrip supply, and we look for the 10s30s spread curve to widen. HY mutual funds are becoming increasingly conservatively positioned

● Near-term catalysts: FOMC meeting (1/30-1/31), Jan employment (2/2), Jan CPI (2/13), Jan retail sales (2/15)

Following a relatively sleepy week in US fixed income markets, the Treasury yield curve twisted steeper and spreads across high-grade and high yield corporates continued to grind tighter, as DM central banks inch closer to rate cuts. Though the economic calendar was light, the data we received was positive, with Friday<sup>’</sup>s PCE report showing a 0.5% increase in real consumption, the strongest gain since January 2023, leading our economists to revise up their 1Q24 real GDP growth forecast from 1.25% to 1.75% saar (see Consumers pick up the pace in Dec. as inflation cools, Daniel Silver, 1/26/24). Moderating inflation likely has helped drive real spending gains recently—core PCE rose just 0.17% m/m, bringing the year-ago rate down to 2.9%, and the 3-month annualized run rate down to 1.5%.

Given the significant progress on inflation, market participants will be focused on the forward guidance in the FOMC statement next week Wednesday, and the messaging delivered by Chair Powell for clues around the timing of the first rate cut. At the last three FOMC meetings, the Committee has left policy rates unchanged while conveying a bias to tighten, by talking about conditions that would prompt “additional policy firming.<sup>”</sup> At next week<sup>’</sup>s meeting, we expect the Committee will turn to a neutral stance, conveying no bias to either tighten or ease at the subsequent meeting. We also believe there is a chance the inflation discussion gets edited, which would be a dovish development as well (see FOMC Preview, Michael Feroli, 1/26/24). Meanwhile, in the January employment report released Friday, we forecast a 225k increase in non-farm payrolls (consensus: 180k) and see the unemployment rate unchanged at 3.7% (consensus: 3.8%) (see Economics).

The other key event for rates markets next week will be Treasury<sup>’</sup>s quarterly refunding announcement on Wednesday morning ahead of the FOMC. Recall that heightened attention on Treasury<sup>’</sup>s refunding announcement last August, and the looming growth in Treasury supply, kickstarted an aggressive rise in long-end yields, while the November refunding announcement helped to turn the yield move around. However, we don<sup>’</sup>t expect Treasury<sup>’</sup>s announcement next week to be as impactful for markets this time around. We look for Treasury to announce a final round of increases to nominal auction sizes, similar to the increases announced in November and consistent with the guidance offered in the latest policy statement. Further, intermediate Treasuries appear fairly valued, and duration positioning looks broadly neutral, reducing the risk of large reactions to next week<sup>’</sup>s events. Figure 1 shows the share of neutrals in our Treasury Client Survey has risen to 71%, the highest in more than 9 months, and sits in the 97th percentile of levels observed over the last decade. Overall, with the Fed likely to move toward a neutral bias next week alongside other DM central banks, we think this supports being long duration as we approach the first expected ease in June and hold longs in 5-year Treasuries. Additionally, we hold 5s/30s yield curve steepeners as a structural position ahead of the first ease (see Treasuries).

A further narrowing in inflation breakevens in coming months should also help push nominal yields lower, as the run rate on core CPI resumes its decline, growth moderates, and structural demand for TIPS remains tepid. Breakeven valuations still appear rich versus commodities and the money market curve (Figure 2), and the 10-year sector remains somewhat rich along the inflation curve. However, the next CPI report is still three weeks away, and meanwhile, growth and labor market data have so far pointed to still strong momentum to start the year. Additionally, retail demand for TIPS has improved over the past month, in contrast to the heavy outflows from TIPS funds observed in December. Thus, we continue to hold 10-year breakeven narrowers paired with an energy hedge, though we recognize we may not see material satisfaction on this position over the very near term (see TIPS). Our technical strategists highlight that a renewed tightening trend in 10-year breakevens would gain traction through the 228bp 200-day moving average and 223bp Nov-Jan pattern breakout area (see Technicals).

Turning to Fed balance sheet developments, in a surprise after-market announcement the Fed tweaked the financing terms of the Bank Term Funding Program (BTFP) on Wednesday, likely motivated by the rising use of the facility as merely a source of attractive funding. Since the BTFP offers funding at term OIS plus 10bp, a bank that draws on this facility can obtain 1Y term funding (prepayable without penalty) at about 50bp below IOR (Figure 3 ). Thus, a bank drawing from this facility and holding the cash as Reserve funds can earn a riskless \~50bp spread, since the trade can be terminated if the Fed were to cut rates by over 50bp. Of course, none of this is new, and most banks have generally refrained from using an emergency funding facility opportunistically. Nevertheless, usage has risen by enough to prompt the Fed to modify the terms of the facility to floor the rate at IOR on the day the funds are drawn. This erases the arbitrage that was previously permitted (see Fed shuts the BTFP barn door, Michael Feroli, 1/24/24). We do not think one should draw any monetary policy inferences from this week<sup>’</sup>s announcement, but the decision could have implications for the Fed<sup>’</sup>s balance sheet.

The actual scale of reduction in usage remains to be seen, but on the margin we project a somewhat faster ramp-down of BTFP balances in the aftermath of this week<sup>’</sup>s announcement. This is reflected in our revised projections for the Fed's balance sheet size and liabilities mix (Figure 4). Recall we expect the FOMC will craft an outline for a prospective QT taper at next week<sup>’</sup>s meeting (communicated to the public in the minutes to be released on Feb 21st), before formal agreement at the March FOMC meeting for implementation starting in April. At that time, we expect the Fed will reduce the Treasury runoff cap from \$60bn to \$30bn, and we assume that QT will end when domestic O/N RRP balances fall below \$300bn (see It<sup>’</sup>s all over now, QT, Economics, Rates, and Securitized Products Research, 1/12/24). Given the larger than expected drop in O/N RRP balances over recent weeks, it now appears we could see QT ending in September.

An earlier end to QT would be favorable for swap spreads, and with 5-year spreads still narrow to fair value, we maintain our widening bias in the belly. Meanwhile 2-year swap spread appear too wide to fair value. A flattening 2s/5s cash curve, declining RRP balances, rising 1Y1Y volatility, and the potential for BTFP shrinkage to result in replacement funding demand are all sources of widening pressure. In light of these factors, we recommend 2s/5s swap spread curve steepeners (see Interest Rate Derivatives). Meanwhile we see little material impact of the changes to the BTFP program on money markets, as there continues to be plenty of liquidity in the front end. Indeed, bank CP/CD outstandings have risen by \~\$158bn over the past 2 years, mainly from US GSIBs, while spreads have remained relatively unchanged during the same period. When the program expires and the facility usage declines, we suspect liquidity will be drained from the system, which could add upward pressure to SOFR on the margin. To the extent banks need to roll their borrowings to fund their assets, large banks will likely turn to the CP/CD market, while smaller/regional banks will probably tap the fed funds, FHLBs, or secured funding markets for their liquidity needs (see Short-Term Fixed Income). We also don<sup>’</sup>t expect much of an impact on the mortgage basis, given the availability of other funding sources. Since the term of the loans can be up to a year, banks truly reliant on this funding to finance their holdings and plug a deposit outflow hole can still kick the can down the road until early 2025, if needed. Agency MBS spreads are still on the tighter end of the fair range, but may have more support from banks than we had anticipated, and we remain neutral on the basis at current levels (see Agency MBS).

Elsewhere in securitized product space, spreads continue to grind tighter in a strong start to the year. CMBS new issue demand has been strong out of the CREFC conference and IG mezz in particular has performed in line or outperformed LCF AAAs over the past month. We view LCF AAAs as fully valued at current levels and we see risk of widening if HG credit spreads soften into February. Instead we recommend adding on-the-run or new issue 5yr conduit CMBS XAs as they afford upside into recessionary scenarios and historically have exhibited slower CPY than 10yr loans (see CMBS). Meanwhile CRT spreads also tightened further, likely as it is the only sectors in our opinion where one can buy exposure to mortgage credit <sup>‘</sup>in size<sup>’</sup> and as we project net issuance to turn negative (see RMBS). Moreover, January has been a record month for the primary ABS market with new issues totalling \$34.1bn, beating the previous post-GFC highs of \$32bn achieved in Oct <sup>’</sup>19 and Nov <sup>’</sup>21. Demand remained strong over the month, as reflected by another week of tighter-than-guidance pricing spreads, heavy oversubscription levels, and upsized transactions across asset classes. While ABS remains cheap to comparable unsecured corporates, we think it will be a challenge for spreads to tighten much further given elevated recession and inflation risks (Figure 5). As such, we continue to recommend high quality ABS for their attractive relative values versus other credits but remain cautious on higher yielding ABS given potential macro market volatility and recession concerns (see ABS).

At the risk of sounding like a broken record, high grade credit spreads are once more tighter by 3bp, bringing the spread on our JULI to 105bp, now reaching their post-GFC lows which were last achieved in September of 2021. Despite the \$168bn MTD of supply dwarfing the average \$129bn we typically observe in January over the last five years (ex-2020), demand has proved strong with fund inflows in particular remaining robust to start the year. Furthermore, this rally has been broad based—as opposed to the recent surge in equity markets—as dispersion among subsectors in our index has narrowed to 1.5 year lows. Looking ahead we could see some weakness, as is typical in February, as we think investors rushing into longs to start the year at such tight valuations may expose themselves to future negative idiosyncratic events (see JPM Daily Credit Strategy & CDS/CDX am update: Driven by idiosyncratic events. spreads have widened in February in recent years following strong Januarys, Eric Beinstein, 1/24/24). Moreover, we expect the 10s30s spread curve to steepen in the coming weeks: historically extreme curve flatness has been short-lived and 30yr supply as a percent of total supply should rise as non-Financials come out of earnings blackout periods. We could see around 5-10bp of steepening and, as the JULI is both market cap and duration weighted, this should apply upward pressure on the index (Figure 6 and see JPM Daily Credit Strategy & CDS/CDX am update: 10s30s spread curve remains very flat, Eric Beinstein, 1/24/24).

High yield spreads also continued tighter, narrowing 13bp on the week to 376bp which is the tightest level since April 2022. Thus far this year Single-Bs are outperforming (-9bp YTD) whereas CCCs are underperforming (+16bp YTD). Meanwhile the loan primary market has been active this month, with \$111bn of repricing/refinancing/other representing the most active backdrop since February 2021 (see High Yield). Separately, our analysts note that mutual funds in the space are becoming increasingly conservatively positioned through building cash balances back up to near long-term averages, sharply reducing allocation to loans, and decreasing exposure to CCCs to their second largest UWs on record while increasing exposure to BBs to their largest OWs on record (see 4Q23 High Yield Fund Analysis: HY funds most conservatively positioned on record, Nelson Jantzen, 1/23/24). Finally, US CLO AAA Tier 1 new issue tightened 10bp YTD to our MY24 spread forecast of 150bp as technicals have been strong with modest new CLO supply, slow creation of the raw material, and high repayments. We think Private Credit CLO AAA looks attractive for buy-andhold investors seeking spread pickup and higher carry. Meanwhile the basis between US BSL AAA and European AAA new issue spreads are at historic tights (see CLOs).
