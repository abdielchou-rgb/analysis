# Earlier Start, Slower Taper

Fed communication has put QT in the spotlight. We look for Fed discussions at upcoming meetings resulting in a June start to taper, earlier than our previous expectation. But the market narrative that the end of QT is coming soon has been overdone. Earlier start will be paired with a slower taper.

## Key Takeaways

• The Fed will start discussing tapering QT soon, but will not end QT soon. Recent Fed communication has stirred market discussion about the end of QT. That narrative is overdone. We see the Fed discussing QT at upcoming meetings, likely announcing the parameters of a taper to QT in May and initiating the taper in June.

• Our baseline is the Fed cuts the pace of QT in half for Treasuries to \$30bn/month, but leaves MBS to run off with prepayments. If we're wrong, it is more likely the taper pace will be slower, not faster.

• We discuss mechanics of our projections, but we expect the SOMA portfolio to fall about \$750bn from the current level. We see the RRP facility falling to roughly \$0, and QT ending in early 2025 when reserves are about \$3.2tn.

• Enormous uncertainty surrounds the ultimate size of the balance sheet and the end of QT. The Fed has clearly said it will monitor market conditions to determine when to stop. Two key parameters stand out among many it will monitor. If the SOFR-IORB spread goes to zero or turns positive, it will be important. If the standing repo facility (SRF) is used in size, it will also be a meaningful signal.

• Although markets will pay close attention to the Fed's balance sheet, we see the implications for fixed income markets as limited. The shift in supply of Treasuries relative to expectations will likely not be substantial, and the effects will be most noticeable in the front end and swap spreads, rather than longer-dated yields. MBS spreads will consequently be little affected, but marginal passthrough from funding conditions could arise from time to time. And likewise, we think credit markets are not meaningfully affected for all of the above reasons.

• One key misconception is that QT necessarily means destruction in bank deposits. Indeed, over the past couple of quarters as the Fed's balance sheet has contracted, deposits have moved mostly sideways but have also actually edged up.

In early 2022, the Fed laid out its plans for quantitative tightening (QT) (see here and here). Those plans were to run down the balance sheet, reducing assets by \$60bn Treasuries per month and up to \$35bn MBS per month, and at some point to be determined in the future, the Fed planned to slow that pace of runoff. Finally, before getting all the way to "ample" reserves, it would stop QT. Since then, Fed officials have reiterated that plan over time (see here and here, for examples from 2023). Despite the rise in market discussion of QT recently, we think there is only limited new information. Consequently, our view has evolved, but only modestly.

We had said the Fed would taper QT beginning September of this year and end QT February next year. We now expect that over the next three FOMC meetings, the Fed will discuss QT in detail and then announce the size and magnitude of the taper in May. For now, we pencil in a starting date of June and a shift to \$30bn per month in Treasury QT, but no change to MBS. After that shift, we continue to see QT running through the end of this year, and ending February 2025, although that outcome is subject to uncertainty.

The market narrative following recent Fed communication has contemplated an imminent end to QT, which we believe is overdone. It was always the case that the taper in QT would happen in 2024. The minutes to the December 2023 FOMC meeting noted that the Committee might begin to discuss conditions for the taper of QT before long. But as of December, those discussions had not begun, and indeed the discussions concerned tapering, not ending QT. Moreover, the minutes said that the Committee would make decisions and communicate "well before" it happened so that markets would have a great deal of advanced notice. In the past, such discussions have taken place over a couple of meetings, implying that the earliest taper announcement would seem to be at the May meeting, following discussions in January and March. A May announcement could indicate taper to begin June 1, a risk to our baseline view.

Hints about the conditions for a taper came in speeches from Fed officials, most clearly from President Logan and Governor Waller. President Logan (who previously ran the SOMA Portfolio at the New York Fed) expressed a preference for the RRP facility to be drawn down to zero, and linked getting very close to that level as a time to taper QT. Governor Waller also said last year that draining the RRP facility was an objective and recently he said at Brookings on January 16, "there's no reason to have anything in it," he said at Brookings and then re-emphasized that getting to that point could prompt a taper. In our baseline, the RRP facility will be roughly \$250bn at the time of the May or June FOMC meeting, and thus would be a natural point to announce a taper in QT. Even if the RRP falls more rapidly than we expect, the result would be a higher level of reserves, given that the Fed's balance sheet would have to balance. Consequently, even an earlier draining of the RRP and a possible earlier taper would not imply an earlier end to QT.

Our tentative view is that the Fed will cut the runoff in Treasuries in half to \$30bn per month but leave the runoff of MBS unchanged at "up to" \$35bn, but in practice only \$10bn to \$20bn. That pace of runoff would be a material slowing, as Logan suggested would be desirable, but would also coincide roughly with the experience from the previous QT episode. Given the disruption to funding markets in September 2019, the risk is that the Fed opts for a slower pace than this baseline. In the next section, we discuss the details of the mechanics of the runoff of the balance sheet, with projections for the RRP and reserves. In broad terms, however, after the anticipated taper, QT could continue for another 9 months, so lasting into early 2025. If the RRP falls faster than the current QT, then tapering could come sooner, but reserves would necessarily be higher, and thus the ending point for QT would be roughly unchanged. The likely end to QT is still far off in the future, barring a disruption to money markets.

## Does QT destroy deposits? Not necessarily

One narrative about QT has been an exacerbating of bank funding stress because QT destroys deposits. We argue that this view has a kernel of truth, but is a bit misleading. The logic is simple. When the Fed allows a Treasury to mature without reinvestment, the Fed loses that asset and debits the Treasury's account (a liability) the same amount – the Fed's balance sheet shrinks. But the Treasury must refund that debt in the market, so when an investor buys the security, it wires money to the Treasury from the investor's bank. That bank loses reserves and debits the investor's deposits an equal amount. QT destroys deposits.

![](images/98714592a7ca4bc348a242f55752bf033b1d06ed9ca73a204b64e6945445e5f3.jpg)
Source: Fed H.4.1. release, Fed H.8 release, Morgan Stanley Research

A couple of issues arise that mean deposits need not actually fall with QT. First, if a bank is the entity buying the extra Treasury issuance, the bank can swap reserves for T-bills (which for example have similar applicability for the liquidity coverage ratio), and deposits need not change. More generally, however, even if deposits fall in the first instance, banks can still choose to seek out more wholesale deposits to replace those deposits that are running off.

Indeed, since May of 2023, total assets of the Fed have fallen \$800bn and yet deposits are roughly unchanged, and actually have risen just a touch. The composition of deposits at banks has changed, with banks replacing deposits that they are losing either from QT or the normal cyclical behavior of falling deposit growth when short-term interest rates rise ( Exhibit 1 ).

Of course, that shift in composition has brought higher funding costs for banks. Most of the deposits that are running off are lower-cost deposits, and wholesale deposits cost money market rates. Such a shift happens in all rates cycles, whether QT is happening or not, so the direct effect of QT on bank deposits is typically vastly overstated, but the increase in funding costs should not be ignored. The point is one of cost of deposits, not quantity of deposits, so arguments made on the basis of the "money supply" are misguided.

## Destination: Ample reserves

As discussed in Talking about Talking about QT, January 6, 2024, we characterize reserves as abundant when the opportunity cost of reserves (captured by IORB) is above the cost of liquidity for the market (SOFR) and ample when they are fairly close to each other. In an ample reserves regime, banks are neither rewarded (IORB > SOFR) nor penalized for holding reserves (IORB < SOFR).

In other worlds, "abundant" reserves occur when there is too much liquidity and money market rates fall materially below IORB (SOFR < IORB). In the extreme case of too much liquidity, SOFR tends to print at or below the RRP rate, which would be consistent with a "super abundant" reserves regime. At the other extreme, SOFR being materially above IORB shows that banks are being penalized for holding reserves even when it is economically better not to do so (SOFR > IORB).

As front-end markets continue to come into balance, SOFR has started to gradually climb above the RRP rate over the past year. In our view, the eventual depletion of the RRP is what will ultimately allow SOFR to come in consistently at or slightly above IORB as banks become the marginal lenders of cash (right now, dealers can meet surges in demand for repo financing by bidding up MMF cash in the RRP, earning the fixed rate of 5.3%). Current conditions remain consistent with abundant reserves ( Exhibit 7 ).

Into 2H24, SOFR consistently at or above IORB should serve as an indicator that the Fed has achieved an ample reserves regime. By the same token, stopping QT soon (particularly before the RRP is depleted) would leave the Fed implementing monetary policy under an abundant reserves regime and using the RRP as a ceiling for money market rates instead of a floor.

Once SOFR > IORB is achieved, the question then turns to how low reserves can go before reaching banks' minimum comfortable level and causing disruptions in the repo market. This would be against the effective implementation of monetary policy (money market rates such as SOFR and EFFR spike above the target range) and the Fed's goal of maintaining financial stability while achieving its dual mandate.

The Fed would then have to be careful to not do "too much" QT to avoid SOFR consistently spiking above the target range and having the Fed effectively become the lender of last resort (via the Standing Repo Facility or SRF, which currently sits at the fed funds upper target of 5.5%).

Another consideration is that the "ample" reserves corridor is currently at 10bp (spread between SRF and IORB). This could create the incentive for the Fed to align its taper plans with technical adjustments that lower IORB and RRP by up to 10bp, widening the ample reserve corridor to up to 20bp. This would create an even greater incentive for banks to lend cash in a post-RRP world (more below).

## SOFR - IORB spread

A positive and wider SOFR - IORB spread over time should serve as a clear indicator that banks are not comfortable holding lower reserves balances even when it is profitable to do so by lending in the repo market (SOFR > IORB). In addition, paying close attention to market signals is likely to be a preferred avenue for Fed officials this time around. In 2019, expectations on when to taper and end QT were shaped around estimates of reserves that proved to be too low.

Today, the SOFR - IORB spread currently sits at -8bp with the 20-day moving average also \~6-7bp below IORB while starting to increase gradually ( Exhibit 8 ). This compares to 2018/19, in which the SOFR - IORB spread became increasingly positive since late 2018, reaching a peak of 315bp on September 17, 2019 and forcing the Fed to purchase bills and conduct repo operations to restore order to funding markets.

By the same token, SOFR increasing due to declines in reserves would not be consistent with an ample reserves regime. To assess this relationship, we compare reserves as a percentage of total bank assets (to account for the structural growth in demand for reserves over time) and the SOFR - IORB spread. ## Usage of the Standing Repo Facility (SRF)

As the RRP continues to be depleted with MMFs extending into bills and capturing attractive repo rates in the market (see Exhibit 12 and here for more), banks should eventually start lending excess reserves to opportunistically capture returns above IORB. The uncertainty comes from not knowing the level of reserves that banks will be willing to lend in a post-RRP world before disruption in funding markets occurs.

Still, in 2H24 we should observe a pick-up in reverse repo and fed funds sold by all banks (assets) in the weekly H.8 release once the RRP is depleted ( Exhibit 13 ). This same dynamic occurred in 2018 and into 2019 as demand for repo financing (reflected via higher SOFR volumes) continued to grow and banks acted as the marginal lenders of cash.

Where the SRF comes into play is when banks no longer become willing and/or able to lend excess reserves while demand for repo financing continues to increase. This backdrop into 2H19 is what contributed to the eventual dysfunction in repo as demand for funding (SOFR volume) continued to climb while reserves deployed in repo plateaued ( Exhibit 13 ).

## Distribution of reserves

Dallas Fed President Logan named the risk that "individual banks can approach scarcity before the system as a whole" as a key reason to taper QT once "ON RRP balances approach a low level". A slower pace of QT should allow the markets to "redistribute liquidity" as reserves continue to shrink and demand for funding continues to grow. Hence, the distribution of reserves and how that informs banks' ability to lend excess reserves and its borrowing needs will also serve as an important indicator for discussions around QT.

In Exhibit 15 , we show reserves as a percentage of assets for large, small and foreign banks (as classified by the H.8 release) over recent years. This analysis helps us to assess how comfortable individual bank types might be with current reserve levels.

As shown in Exhibit 16 , all bank types currently have reserve levels above those seen during the March bank stresses, late 2018 (when SOFR - IORB turned positive) and the September 2019 repo spike. We recognize that this analysis is limited as the Fed is able to see reserve balances for individual banks on a daily basis. This also does not account for the likely shift higher in desired reserve levels.

However, elevated cash holdings for foreign banks give us comfort that some reserves out of the current \$3.5 trillion will be deployed into funding markets once rates become

attractive. Given that foreign banks are not subject to the same regulatory constraints, these banks tend to be more responsive to swings in funding conditions. For instance, as shown in Exhibit 17 , foreign bank reserves as a percentage of assets declined by over 15pp during QT1 as money market rates (SOFR and EFFR) cheapened.

In our base case forecast, we estimate that the bulk of the decline in reserves to \$3.2 trillion will be provided by foreign banks as US banks decide to hold on to cash in line with the recent trend of sticky reserves (see here for more). This conservative assumption will leave foreign banks with \~30% reserves to assets while US banks maintain current levels.

As reserves eventually decline, trends in reserves relative to total assets across different bank types should help to inform bank willingness and ability to lend excess cash and overall funding needs. In particular, cash over assets becoming "sticky" suggests that banks are no longer comfortable holding lower reserves relative to their total holdings of liquid assets.

This would then lead banks to seek other sources of funding to offset reserves and limit the ability of reserves to maintain functioning repo markets as demand for funding continues to increase. As we observed in past episodes of tighter funding conditions, we expect small banks to exhibit this behavior first (for instance, as shown in Exhibit 15 , reserves/assets for small banks became sticky around \~6% in 2018), leaving foreign and large US banks as those willing or able to shed excess reserves as QT continues.

## Fed to remain attentive to money market conditions

Even with a robust framework to appreciate and analyze the impact of QT over time, the timing and size of the eventual taper this year remains highly uncertain as Fed discussions are still in early stages. As these discussions take shape and the Fed formalizes its views around its future balance sheet reduction plans, the accuracy of our views is likely to improve over time.

At the same time, an earlier taper versus our previous base estimate (see here) of September reduces tail risks to funding conditions in the coming months and gives us further comfort that reserve scarcity remains some time away (1H25). We believe that this also significantly reduces the likelihood that the Fed will rush into tapering and ending QT assuming our economists' base case for a soft landing plays out for the rest of the year.

## Remain neutral on SOFR/FF

In 2019? Not Yet, December 8, 2023, we noted that market expectations for funding conditions moved too fast in late 2023. As shown in Exhibit 18 , 1m SOFR/FF spreads as measured by 1m SOFR (SER) and fed funds (FF) futures contracts (negative means SOFR expected to be above EFFR) have reversed some of this overly pessimistic pricing that saw SOFR realizing 5-6bp above EFFR in 2H24.

In particular, this move into year-end appeared excessive when considering that realized SOFR/FF averaged 2bp in 2018 and 4bp in 2019 ( Exhibit 19 ) and contributed to our decision to exit our short 2y SOFR swap spread trade. This continues to present the risk of SOFR/FF realizing above current pricing, particularly in 1H24.

So far, realized SOFR prints continue to show that temporary month-end SOFR spikes are driven by typical dynamics such as dealer intermediation constraints and large net settlements. This is in line with our expectation for repo markets to remain largely well behaved as long as there are still ample balances in the RRP (see here for more).

In addition, recent talk about an eventual taper has likely contributed to the recent widening in SOFR/FF and front-end swap spreads. At the same time, we think that potential market expectations for a quick start to taper and end to QT in the next 1-3 quarters might be overly optimistic when considering current funding conditions, and that recent Fed discussions have only revolved around tapering versus ending QT.

As discussed above, our current read of funding indicators and our updated forecast for the Fed's balance sheet continue to suggest that clearer signs of scarcity that will motivate the Fed to fully end QT are still some time away. This leaves the door open for potential tighter funding conditions, particularly in 2H24 and into 2025. For now, we remain neutral on SOFR/FF and 2y SOFR swap spreads.

## How does QT matter to US rates

Our US economics team now expects the Fed to announce a taper earlier than previously expected – around May 2024, with an end to QT in 1Q25. The timing of the start of tapering would be a little on the sooner end vs. what the December survey of primary dealers (3Q24 tapering) and survey of market participants (2Q24 tapering) imply. It is likely that those survey expectations (recorded in early December) may have already moved to an earlier timeline, suggesting our call on QT tapering starting in May may not be a big surprise to investors at this point.

The bigger surprise in our call on QT tapering would be the Fed keeping QT running longer as an offset to starting tapering earlier. Many market participants associate an earlier start to tapering with an earlier end to QT.

In the recent speech delivered by Dallas Fed Chair Lorie Logan, she floated the idea of tying QT tapering to when RRP levels "approach a low level". While investors did read that as a sign that QT tapering could start sooner, they also extrapolated it to mean that QT could end soon after.

With the Treasury's finances outlook improved by \$110 billion, the Treasury would need to issue \$110 billion less to the market to keep its overall funding profile unchanged. While the \$110bn change in our supply estimates may or may not match the market's expectation, the key thing to note is that the impact of QT on USTs depends on the Treasury itself – it has to decide whether to reduce issuance in bills or coupons.

In this case, the answer might be rather straightforward – the Treasury can reduce bill sizes issued to the market to accommodate the impact of QT tapering.

So as the Fed tapers QT, it reinvests more in maturing coupons, by increasing the sizes of add-ons in US Treasury auctions. This means the tapering of QT will increase the funding of the Treasury via coupons. In a world where total funding needs don't change, and the Treasury is getting more funding via coupons, the Treasury would then have to decide whether to lower coupons sizes or bills.

We think the Treasury will lower bill sizes offered to the market, because the Treasury would not want to lower coupon sizes right after they increased them for three quarters (from August refunding up to the upcoming January refunding) – keeping with the "regular and predictable" issuance strategy for coupons.

The idea of reducing net bill issuance to market also happens to work well because the Treasury's current issuance strategy was going to push t-bills/total outstanding ratio to a bit above their generally preferred 15-20% range. Reducing bills in response to QT tapering would help keep the bills outstanding within the preferred range.

Ultimately, the supply channel will be a lot more influential in affecting T-bills than long-dated yields.

2) The monetary policy signal from QT: Our economics team believes the Fed will frame any changes to QT as linked to the liquidity available in money markets and not as an active tool being used to adjust monetary policy. The perception of QT not being an active monetary policy tool is bolstered by the fact that the ultimate duration impact of changes to QT does in fact depend on changes by the Treasury – as we have explained above.

This perception is bolstered by the fact that QT will continue while rate cuts could start later this year – clearly highlighting the focus on rate cuts as the primary monetary policy tool. And thus, we do not think that investors need to treat QT as a monetary policy signal. However, perception is often reality in markets, and it is possible that investors see changes to QT as a dovish signal and Treasury yields could react lower based on that perception.

Overall, we expect changes to QT in line with our house view to have a limited impact on duration. We expect swap spreads to be much more affected by discussions on QT.

# Why Ending This QT Shouldn't Impact MBS Markets Materially

Similar to how our rates strategists think that ending QT will have a limited impact on duration, we think that ending QT will have an even more limited impact on mortgage markets. It's important to highlight a few things. First, there is expected to be no change to the path of the Fed's mortgage portfolio. FOMC participants have been very consistent (reiterated by Governor Waller on Tuesday when he said that "I personally don't really want to keep MBS on my balance sheet as much") that the Fed's portfolio should be comprised mostly of Treasuries. Thus, even when QT ends, we (and the market) expect that both UST and MBS paydowns should be reinvested into Treasuries. Therefore, the Fed's portfolio should continue to shrink at this rate level by an average of about \$16 billion/month, though seasonals will likely cause that number to vary by \$2-3 billion in either direction.

Of course, some market participants have had the view that ending QT will mean that bank deposits will stop shrinking, and if banks stop having less deposits, they would stop shrinking their security demand. While the concept that QT -> less reserves -> less bank deposits -> less security demand is straightforward, the data do not back this up, as discussed in the first section of this piece, and is apparent below ( Exhibit 22 ). Thus, it's hard to have high conviction that ending QT ends negative bank demand for MBS and thus is positive for MBS.

In fact, it's probably easier to make the argument that ending QT is bad for MBS – right now during QT the Fed is reducing the size of MBS and UST. If it reinvests MBS paydowns into UST, it would go from (if you squint) selling duration to (squinting) selling the MBS basis. Furthermore, almost by definition there will be fewer reserves in the system when QT ends than there are now, which is likely bad for funding markets.

# Corporate Credit – Strong Demand, Little QT Effect

MORGAN STANLEY & CO. LLC

Vishwas Patkar

Vishwas.Patkar@morganstanley.com

+1 212 761-8041

Similar to the views laid out above by our rates and MBS colleagues, we think an earlier tapering of QT should have a limited impact on corporate credit. Two things are worth emphasizing here. First, since the timing of the tapering of QT is driven by the fall of the RRP to near zero, rather than an active monetary policy measure, there is likely not much signal here for risk-asset investors. Second, even if the taper leads to better deposit bases, the read across for corporate credit is limited. Banks are a very small part of the investor base in corporate bonds.

Corporate bonds are not on the Fed's balance sheet, so any technical impact on credit needs to be assessed through the portfolio balance channel. If QT increased the supply of Treasuries and MBS for investors to absorb (thereby taking that demand away from risk assets), tapering would reduce the pace of this supply over time. One investor community where all these factors intersect is the "Agg" benchmarked active mutual funds. These funds invest across high-quality fixed-income products, and represent a big part of the demand base for corporate credit. However, our data shows that, while investor allocations to corporate credit have come down over the past year, they remain high relative to their weight in the index. This is a sign in our view that active investors are still comfortable with credit fundamentals. Corporate credit is also a relatively less liquid asset class, and unless investors expect a large sell-off, trading in and out can be prohibitive. As a result, unless the economic backdrop changes, the implications of higher or lower Treasury supply may not be meaningful.

Overall, the demand backdrop for IG credit has been strong in spite of the Fed cutting its balance sheet in the background. This is the result of many factors. The investor base in high-quality corporate bonds is diversified and global. For instance, a big source of demand for IG bonds over the fall came from domestic yield-based buyers such as pension funds and insurance companies. These liability-driven investors looked to lock in yields at multi-year highs to immunize their liabilities. In addition, overseas buyers represent close to 30% of the ownership base in corporates. In 2023, demand from Taiwan and Japan was robust, given the yield advantage and scale/depth offered by the IG market.

We expect technicals to strengthen further in 2024. Earlier tapering of QT might marginally help improve that, but is not a key part of it. Further, with valuations at fairly rich levels, there is a lot of good news priced in already. So we think the pricing impact of early tapering QT is minimal. If at all, we expect to see the credit market take its cues from assets like Agency MBS that will be more directly affected. Similarly, the evolution of hedging costs for overseas investors based on monetary policy cycles across regions will be a more important driver of demand than QT.
