## United States

• April job growth solid (175k) but below expectations

• Average hourly earnings trend has cooled, but ECI surprised higher

The FOMC stayed on hold as was expected, signaling eventual rate cuts are still the baseline

• Watch Fedspeak next week with limited data releases

The FOMC left rates on hold at its meeting this week and indicated that the economic news from the past few months showed a lack of further progress on inflation. The Committee clearly doesn<sup>’</sup>t have the confidence on inflation it needs to start lowering rates in the very near term. But the FOMC still believes that rates currently are restrictive and that the desired progress on inflation will come at some point, allowing it to lower interest rates.

Many economic indicators have surprised to the upside in recent months, pushing back expectations for the first Fed cut. But the April employment report (released two days after the end of the May FOMC meeting) disappointed in many respects: job growth, wage growth, and the workweek all surprised to the downside, while the unemployment rate unexpectedly moved up. To be sure, the April data were not weak, and on its own the latest jobs report probably is not enough to cause policymakers to meaningfully change their expected path for policy. But if the softening evident in April is followed by additional cooling in future reports, we think this could prompt the Fed to move on rates. We continue to look for the first cut to come at the July FOMC meeting.

The other reports from the past week were somewhat mixed. The ECI showed an upside surprise with respect to labor costs in 1Q, but the JOLTS report suggested that supply and demand in the labor market have been approaching better balance lately. Jobless claims data point to strength in the labor market continuing through most of April, whereas a variety of April survey data (i.e., the ISM reports and the Conference Board<sup>’</sup>s consumer survey) disappointed. There wasn<sup>’</sup>t a lot of news related to GDP, and we continue to forecast 2.5% real GDP growth for 2Q. Next week<sup>’</sup>s scheduled data flow is pretty quiet, but we should start to see some of the reaction to the April jobs report from various Fed officials that are scheduled to speak throughout the week.

## Labor market: Goldilocks is back

As noted above, the April jobs report was a rare downside surprise among a series of stronger-than-expected readings in the recent past. Employment growth eased in April, in addition to softer readings on the average workweek and earnings growth. This welcome news followed a JOLTS report earlier in the week that also showed further signs of cooling labor demand and hiring but no signs of rising separations. Low and steady weekly jobless claims continued to signal that this gradual rebalancing of the labor market is happening without a significant uptick in job loss. One unwelcome surprise this week was the firmer-than-expected ECI print for 1Q.

Nonfarm payroll growth slowed to 175,000 last month, below the 269,000 average gain over the prior three months and the lowest reading since October (Figure 1). The public sector was a big contributor to the step down in job growth reported for April, with only 8,000 government jobs added that month—down from the 60,000 average pace in the prior six months. Private service sector employment grew at a solid clip in April (153,000), with health care and social assistance accounting for over half of these jobs (87,000). In another sign of moderating labor demand, the average workweek ticked down to 34.3 hours (Figure 2). The average workweek for production and non-supervisory workers also ticked down, to 33.7 hours.

In the household survey, the key variables exhibited only moderate changes. The unemployment rate ticked up from 3.8% to 3.9% in April, after rounding. The changes in household employment (25,000) and unemployment (63,000) were relatively modest in April. Even though this gain was much higher (563,000) when adjusted for the payroll concept of employment, the household survey continued to show weaker job growth than the establishment survey on net in recent months. Finally, the participation rate held at 62.7% in April and the employment-to-population ratio declined 0.1%-pt to 60.2% after rising by 0.2%-pt in March.

On the wage front, the April jobs report also delivered a positive surprise for those hoping for softer inflation. Average hourly earnings increased only 0.2% in April and, with downward revisions, the year-ago figure cooled to 3.9%. On a 3-month and 12-month annualized basis, the latest readings on average hourly earnings (2.8% and 3.9%, respectively) were the softest seen in some time. Relative to this positive news, earlier in the week we saw a firmer-than-expected ECI reading for 1Q. Year-ago growth in ECI and average hourly earnings were relatively close by the end of 1Q (Figure 3), but we see reasons that ECI growth might stay a bit firmer for the rest of the year. Since the ECI is seen as a better gauge of wage inflation, policymakers will be looking for further evidence of cooling in wage inflation beyond one month<sup>’</sup>s average hourly earnings. The more volatile unit labor cost data also accelerated in 1Q, rising 4.7%q/q, saar, although the year-ago pace eased to 1.8%oya.

These all point to some softening in labor market conditions with demand and supply likely coming into a better balance into the second quarter.

## Powell still sees eventual rate cuts

The modest cooling in the April employment report was arguably supportive of the tone from the May FOMC meeting, as Chair Powell kept the door open for rate cuts later this year. He acknowledged that the recent run of data going into this week<sup>’</sup>s meeting “have not given us that greater confidence<sup>”</sup> that inflation is coming down, and thus gaining that confidence is likely to “take longer than previously expected.<sup>”</sup> That said, he also steered clear of any suggestion for now that the next move could be a hike. Rather, any further persistence in inflation would lead the Fed to “hold off on rate cuts.<sup>”</sup>

In addition to inflation coming back down, Powell also noted that “an unexpected weakening in the labor market<sup>”</sup> could trigger cuts, but that would have to be more than a few tenths higher on the unemployment rate, and, of course, the Fed won<sup>’</sup>t react to just one softer jobs report. Instead, the latest jobs report likely furthers the progress toward “better balance<sup>”</sup> in the labor market that various Fed officials have sought. While there remains a path for rate cuts to start in July as we expect, risks are still skewed toward a later start. Markets are now pricing nearly a full rate cut by September. Unsurprisingly, Powell was adamant that the election would not preclude the Fed from moving at its September meeting if the data support it.

More substantive changes were made to QT, as the cap on the monthly runoff of Treasury holdings was reduced to \$25bn from \$60bn previously, effective June 1. While a slightly larger reduction than expected (the March minutes had suggested cutting the pace in half), this decision had been flagged at the March meeting and thus was not a big surprise.

## Business surveys stumble in Apri

The softer momentum signaled by the April employment report was amplified by weaker business surveys last month. The headline ISM indexes for both the manufacturing and services surveys disappointed in April, respectively down 1.1 and 2.0 points from their March readings (Figure 4). But these two surveys are seeing notably different trends of late: despite the April setback, the manufacturing ISM has been improving on net, whereas the services ISM plunged to its second-lowest level this cycle—and a relatively weak level historically of 49.2. The April declines were broad-based across the ISM components, with the biggest drops in the manufacturing production and services business activity indexes. While this loss of momentum bears watching, it<sup>’</sup>s important to recognize that the ISMs do not map closely into key economic activity measures.

All the important figures in the April jobs report came in a little softer than expected, and a little softer than the recent trend. Nonfarm employment growth eased to 175,000 last month, below the 269,000 average gain over the prior three months. In another sign of moderating labor demand, the average workweek ticked down to 34.3 hours. The unemployment rate rounded up from 3.8% in March to 3.9% last month. Finally, average hourly earnings increased only 0.2% in April, and with downward revisions, the year-ago figure cooled off to 3.9%. While all these figures were softer than prior months, they were still generally solid. Nominal labor income growth has been running around 5% in recent months, which should continue to provide the fuel for steady consumer spending growth. Overall, we<sup>’</sup>re still comfortable projecting that real GDP growth will pick up to about a 2.5% annualized pace this quarter. Fed officials will likely view this repor as a welcome sign that the labor market is continuing to come into better balance and that policy is restrictive enough to cool off labor demand (particularly with large immigration flows raising the break-even level of monthly employment growth to 200,000 or higher). We<sup>’</sup>re sticking with our call for a first ease in July. The market is not there but we believe that if the next two job reports show continued cooling in labor market activity, then the FOMC will be comfortable taking

back some of its policy restraint.

The 175,000 jobs added in the establishment survey in April marked the softest monthly gain since October. Prior months were revised down 22,000 on net. The public sector was a big contributor to the step down in job growth reported for April, as only 8,000 government jobs were added that month, down from the 60,000 average pace from the prior six months. The private service sector continued to add jobs at a solid clip in April (153,000), with health care and social assistance accounting for over half of these jobs (87,000). The move down in the average workweek also occurred in the workweek for production and non-supervisory workers, which ticked down to 33.7 hours. On a 3-month and 12-month annualized basis, the latest readings on average hourly earnings (2.8% and 3.9%, respectively) were the softest seen in some time. This moderation in wage growth has been reasonably broad-based across industries. The production worker earnings series similarly has shown moderation in wage growth lately with the 3-month annualized gain in this series only 2.6%.

In the household survey, the key variables exhibited only moderate changes. The unemployment rate ticked up from 3.8% to 3.9% in April, although at a higher level of precision the move up was just 4bps. And the changes in household employment (25,000) and unemployment (63,000) were relatively modest in April. The participation rate held at 62.7% in April and the employment-to-population ratio declined 0.1%pt to 60.2% that month after rising by 0.2%pt in March. The household survey did show strong employment growth in April (563,000) when adjusting to be closer to the establishment survey<sup>’</sup>s payroll concept of employment. But even with this strong April gain, the adjusted series in the household survey still has shown weaker job growth than the establishment survey on net in recent months. The U-6 broad measure of labor market underutilization ticked up to 7.4% and is now 0.9%-point above its late <sup>’</sup>22 low of 6.5%.
