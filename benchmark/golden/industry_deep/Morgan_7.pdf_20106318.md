With the balance of risks to the December FOMC forecast on 2024 GDP growth (1.5%) and the unemployment rate (4.1%) now shifting to the upside, rising uncertainty about monetary transmission likely argues for patience.

## Accelerating the Riksbank ease to May

The Bank of England followed the Fed and ECB in delivering a dovish pivot this week, opening the door to lower rates. But the shift was subtle, with two members still voting for higher rates and a set of projections aimed at pushing back on the degree of easing assumed in markets recently. Based on its perception of the current level of inflation persistence, the BoE doesn<sup>’</sup>t appear minded to cut until the second half of this year. This adds weight to our forecast for easing to begin in August. The Riksbank is a notable DM exception, delivering more aggressive than expected dovish guidance this week. With our forecast now pointing to core inflation hitting 2%oya by mid-year, we move forward our expectation of a first cut from June to May. This implies that the Riksbank will be the first to cut rates in Western Europe. The combination of sliding inflation and a cooling labor market should outweigh any currency concerns.

## Global “goods” (but not great)

After surging in the initial stages of the expansion, growth in global manufacturing has trailed services for nearly two years. Demand has rotated in part after an initial burst of goods spending was followed by a mobility-driven rebound in services demand. However, services have now more than recovered their pre-pandemic pace, whereas manufacturing has continued to struggle. A rebound in 2H23 factory output to a 1.4%ar proved disappointing. Moreover, the gains were concentrated in Asian tech and reflected an acceleration in the pace of stockbuilding while final goods demand growth slowed. But the latest news is more encouraging, and points to sustained modest growth in the months ahead.

Final demand will be the ultimate driver of the goods sector. After slowing to a soft 1%ar in early 4Q23, global retail sales look to have ended the quarter at a strong 3.2%ar based on the available data. We estimate global equipment spending recovered last quarter after contracting in 3Q23. A step down in last month<sup>’</sup>s investment goods PMI suggests businesses are still cautious, however, and inventories look to have turned into a drag. We thus expect global industry to expand 2%ar in 2H23. The January PMIs support our call of improving momentum even if the levels remain depressed (Figure 3), with a broadly-based gain in the output and new orders PMIs, even as the inventory PMI ticked lower.

## A positive inflection in EM Asia…

The signal of improvement from the global manufacturing PMI this week is bolstered by the strong gain across EM Asia. Large increases in the new orders PMI are particularly encouraging and temper recent concerns that the tech cycle may be peaking. Some of the confusion owes to the fact that Korea and Taiwan, the two key semiconductors producers, have seen an unusual bifurcation in trade direction, with exports to the G3 rising while exports to Mainland China have slowed. Whether this gap reflects differentiated cyclical postures or shifts in supply chains is unclear and bears watching, given implications for China and for the broader global tech cycle.

## …but policy uncertainty clouds China

The latest surveys show China maintaining solid growth amid indications that material imbalances threaten the mediumterm outlook. Auto sales are tracking a 64%oya gain in January while home sales of the top 100 developers fell 35%oya. Next week<sup>’</sup>s inflation data should show continued downward pressure on prices that is weighing on corporate profits. Against this backdrop, policy supports are building around two significant uncertainties. First, will the local government hidden debt resolution lead to a deleveraging campaign on LGFV borrowing? We have assumed only a modest correction but the risks are skewed to the downside for more fiscal drag. Second, will the banking sector (especially regional banks) be able to maintain stable loan growth, facing the challenges of NIM compression, weakened profit growth, and capital adequacy pressure? For this question, we are watching monthly Total Social Financing, which we forecast to advance 9.5%oya in January.

## Weak consumer promotes BoJ patience

We revised down our 4Q23 GDP growth forecast for Japan to flat after a disappointing December real sales report (Figure 4). Persistent real income drags look likely to result in a third consecutive quarter of contracting real consumption to end last year. That trend may reverse as wage momentum improves and inflation eases, alongside improving consumer sentiment. But the air pocket currently hitting activity will likely make it difficult for the BoJ to gain confidence that a virtuous cycle of wage and price dynamics is underway. That suggests a phased approach toward normalization, in line with our expectations, starting with changes to the BoJ<sup>’</sup>s forward guidance. We expect the start of rate hikes will be postponed at least until midyear, and only after confirming that higher wages are supporting a rebound in consumer spending.

## EM easing proceeds at a steady pace

EM rate-cutting cycles remain well underway amid easing inflation and supportive global financial conditions. While policymakers generally look to favor a steady pace of easing, strong progress on the inflation front is likely to prompt some to accelerate the cutting cycle. This week Colombia<sup>’</sup>s and Hungary<sup>’</sup>s central banks eased 25bp and we expect both to pick up pace to 100bp and 50bp, respectively. Brazil<sup>’</sup>s COPOM predictably maintained its 50bp pace of cuts and is expected to maintain this clip in coming months. Meanwhile, faster-than-expected progress toward its inflation target prompted Chile<sup>’</sup>s CBC to accelerate cuts to 100bp and the council guided toward rates getting down to neutral (4%) by year-end­—50bp lower than our forecast. Next week, we expect Czechia<sup>’</sup>s and Peru<sup>’</sup>s central banks to deliver 25bp cuts but see risks skewed toward 50bp for both as growth remains subdued and inflation within target ranges. Separately, we expect Poland and Thailand to remain on hold. Markets, however, continue to price significantly more rate cuts than we forecast this year across much of the world even as continued growth resilience and stickier core disinflation make the case for deeper cuts that much harder.

## Austere budget leads to softer RBI tone

India<sup>’</sup>s interim budget doubled down on fiscal consolidation, targeting a 5% of GDP central government deficit next year. This fiscal prudence was needed, in our view, given pressure on public sector debt dynamics from a nearly 10% of GDP broader augmented deficit. With fiscal policy weighing on growth, and inflation likely running 4.5% this year (after four years averaging 6%), we expect the RBI to dial back monetary tightening in stages. In addition to continuing to provide overnight liquidity, we expect it to soften its tone next week but not shift to a fully neutral stance. We look for the RBI to start cutting in June provided the Fed has also moved by then. Markets anticipate a shallow easing cycle that begins shortly, but we expect a later and potentially deeper one—particularly if growth disappoints and inflation stays below 5%.

## Egypt close to a deal with IMF

In a statement, the IMF reported "excellent progress" in reaching an SLA for the combined first and second reviews with Egypt. It comes after the IMF MD, Kristalina Georgieva, stated in a briefing that a larger program is being discussed considering the increasing challenges and turmoil in the region. As MENA geopolitics remain complicated, regional and global policymakers will likely act to prevent more severe outcomes. A package greater than US\$10bn from the IMF and partners may be forthcoming but another round of pound devaluation also looks likely. This Thursday the CBE raised the policy rate to 21.25% to offset pressure and anchor inflation expectations with a policy rate at sufficiently restrictive levels. Recent announcements of fiscal tightening measures and discontinuation of tax privileges for state-owned enterprises are large positives and akin to measures that are geared toward agreeing a program with the IMF.
