The Fed finally caught a break with this week's inflation print. April headline and core CPI both came in softer than expected, at 0.3% m/m versus consensus expectations of 0.4%. After last month's re-acceleration, which threw the Fed's campaign into question, CPI's surprise deceleration was warmly welcomed by investors, with positive sentiment flooding the market and rate cut expectations growing closer to two for the year, compared with slightly above 1.5 last week. The data marked the first surprise to the downside for inflation since last October's print, when headline and core came in at 0.0% and 0.2% m/m while consensus was for 0.1% and 0.2% m/m.

Amid the good news, bad news has been ignored. Despite the positive CPI print, economic data over the past week have not been all sunshine and rainbows. PPI came in much hotter than expected (0.5% m/m versus 0.2% m/m expectations), but a strong revision downward to March's print helped ofset concerns, and USTs were actually lower on the day. With Wednesday's focus on CPI, a strong miss to the downside for retail sales (0.0% m/m vs. 0.4% m/m expected, 0.7% m/m prior) did not cause much concern, and Barclays Economics believes that consumers remain well positioned for resilience despite the softer print (see April Retail Sales: Taking a breather). Still, data and companies are both indicating some softness in the consumer environment. On the company side, the message has been mixed. Some companies, including McDonald's and Starbucks, reported softening demand, but Walmart was not as negative. On the data side for 1Q24, 30-day delinquencies (ie, the number of new delinquencies) are growing rapidly for credit cards and autos, reaching the worst levels since the aftermath of the GFC for some parts of the consumer spectrum. This softness on the consumer side, if it persists, should prevent a sustained rally to much tighter spreads.

The risk-on environment is back, with equities at the all-time highs and USTs rallying. In the wake of this week's data, the S&P 500 reached all-time highs, the DJIA crossed 40,000 for the first time on record, and although not as historically elevated, the Russell 2000 also posted gains. The risk-on narrative comes as we enter a lull on the macro front - the next significant round of data will not be released until the end of May - which might give the current rally room to run.

On the credit side, CDX has led the rally while cash has lagged. Immediately after the CPI release, CDX IG traded inside of 50bp and CDX HY rose above \$107 for the first time since March.

## Systematic investors are increasing their footprint in credit

The number of funds and the total deployed assets associated with systematic strategies have ballooned recently. Up until the past few years, systematic strategies occupied a relatively small corner of the credit market. This has been changing rapidly, and the efects on liquidity and price action are already apparent.

We view this as a net positive for credit investors. The models employed by these strategies screen over a much larger universe of bonds, which should inject additional liquidity into the market. Their ability to identify outliers at scale should also make markets more eficient, as they provide more opportunities for price discovery.

The flip side, however, is that systematic investing is an additional source of price action that should be understood by systematic and discretionary investors alike. Systematic investing is one of several recent innovations in credit, such as portfolio trading, that are disrupting the market. This is likely to force asset managers to evolve or risk being left behind, as we discussed in The equitification of credit.

Systematic credit strategies employ quantitative trading strategies that rely on mathematical models and reams of data rather than teams of fundamental analysts. We broadly categorize the investor types into long-only asset managers (who primarily trade cash bonds), CTAs (who trade macro products across several asset classes, but whose involvement in credit is almost exclusively through CDX), and hedge funds (which trade a combination of cash bonds, CDS, and macro products). We focus on the cash side of the market in this note.

These strategies have grown substantially in popularity over the past few years. Figure 1 shows the total assets of US-domiciled mutual funds that are explicitly titled like systematic credit funds (funds with one of "systematic," "quant," or "algorithm" in the name, as well as "corporate," "credit," "bond," or "fixed income"). The fact that this universe has more than doubled in AUM over the past year is noteworthy, even though this exercise captures only a small fraction of the total capital deployed in systematic credit (the vast majority of systematic credit funds are either not explicitly named as such or simply do not report their holdings data to Lipper, most notably hedge funds).

Better quality and more abundant data, the rise of electronic and portfolio trading, and improved liquidity have lowered the barriers to entry for systematic strategies in credit. With credit continuing to look more equity-like, systematic credit investing will likely only grow from here.

## We estimate that \$90-140bn is deployed in systematic strategies in IG and HY cash

Estimating the total AUM deployed in systematic credit strategies is extremely dificult. There is very little transparency in deployed capital, and the line between what does and does not constitute a systematic strategy is extremely blurry. That said, there are several techniques we can use to assess the size of the market. We approach the problem in two distinct ways - a bottom-up and a top-down approach - and find that they yield estimates in the same ballpark, which gives us confidence in our overall projection.

## Bottom-up approach estimates about \$80-100bn

One of the main dificulties in sizing the systematic credit market is simply defining what does and does not count as a systematic strategy. For example, a large number of investors employ some kind of "quantamental" approach that mixes traditional/fundamental and systematic credit investment strategies. For instance, a few systematic rules are applied to generate a screen of potentially attractive bonds as a "first pass," but the final investment decision is made by fundamental analysts and portfolio managers who select from this screen.

A recent survey of hedge fund investors at the Barclays Hedge Fund Symposium found that nearly three-quarters of respondents favored this sort of approach and only 10% used an entirely non-systematic (pure discretionary) strategy (Figure 2). This underscores that there is a spectrum between pure systematic and pure discretionary, even though that fraction is likely much higher for the large asset managers, pension funds, and insurance companies that make up the majority of credit investors. Still, it is likely that a significant portion of the market is using some form of systematic input in its investment process.

That said, we can narrow the scope to focus on the funds we know are employing strategies that are closer to the pure systematic end of the spectrum. Through client conversations, analysis of fund investor literature, and a screen of holdings data with key words related to "systematic" and "credit," we constructed a list of the 25 largest funds that we know to be employing a systematic investment process in either investment grade or high yield cash. The list includes hedge funds, quantitative asset managers, and systematic carve-outs from larger general asset managers. We exclude CTAs because their involvement with credit is almost exclusively limited to CDX.

Sourcing AUM data for this group of funds from either Lipper or client conversations yields a strict lower bound of \$64bn. Based on conversations with portfolio managers at nonsystematic funds, however, many large real money accounts are beginning to deploy systematic strategies within their main funds. If even 1% of the AUM of the Lipper universe of IG and HY funds is deployed systematically, that represents an additional \$20bn of AUM, and it has the potential to be much larger than that. Therefore, we shade our bottom-up estimate upward to \$80-100bn.

## Top-down approach estimates \$100-130bn in AUM (and \$500-600bn in annual volume)

Roughly a sixth of electronic inquiry comes from systematic clients. As a check on our bottom-up approach, we can also employ a separate top-down approach. As discussed in our research on the evolving liquidity landscape of corporate credit (see The equitification of credit, April 30, 2024), we know that about 33% of volumes across IG and HY are processed electronically. From conversations with our algorithmic trading desk, we learned that about one in six electronic requests for quote (RFQ) come from systematic accounts.

That translates to about \$533bn in annual volume. Our own analysis of RFQs also found that the trade-through rate of these requests is the same for systematic and non-systematic accounts (70%). Therefore, we can assume that if about a sixth (16%) of RFQs are from systematic accounts, then about 16% of traded electronic volume and 5%<sup>1</sup> of total IG+HY volume comes from systematic accounts trading electronically (Figure 3). With about \$10trn of traded volume across IG and HY in 2023, we estimate that about \$533bn of that was from systematic accounts trading electronically.

Data on turnover let us connect volumes to AUM. Thanks to our bottom-up analysis, we also have annual traded volume data, as well as AUM data, for a select few known systematic credit hedge funds. The average ratio of annual volume to AUM for these hedge funds is about 7.5x. This ratio cannot be applied to mutual funds, however. Systematic hedge funds typically employ leverage of up to 15x, which magnifies their AUM substantially, whereas long-only systematic funds typically do not. It is unlikely that a long-only fund would turn over its entire portfolio 7-8 times per year, as the transaction costs would materially eat into alpha.

Blending hedge fund and mutual fund turnover estimates gets us to a top-down estimate of \$100-130bn. We think it is more reasonable to assume a lower turnover ratio of 2-3x for systematic mutual funds, which are more likely to employ longer-term factor strategies that rebalance quarterly or even yearly. Their AUM is roughly two-thirds of our bottom-up population, so we can combine that estimate with the 7.5x from the hedge funds composing the other third of the sample and arrive at a final turnover estimate of 4-5x. That fraction, combined with the \$533bn in annual volume, yields \$100-130bn in systematic AUM, which is not far from the \$80-100bn we estimated using our bottom-up approach.

## Caveats (the "known unknowns") cause us to skew our estimate upward to \$90-140bn

We know it's at least \$64bn, but we also know it's higher than that. As stated above, we've established \$64bn as a strict lower-bound on systematic credit AUM, building of a list of known systematic funds. While we believe we have accounted for the largest participants in the systematic credit market, it is very likely that additional funds out there are putting capital to work in systematic credit. Most notably, it does not include risk sleeves of large asset managers that are deployed systematically but not carved out from the broader portfolios (an example could be a \$1bn discretionary IG fund that allocates 1% of its risk to a systematic trading strategy in liquid bonds). Though data on this segment of the market are extremely opaque, the sheer amount of assets held in the category means that even a small foray into systematic investing by this class of investors is liable to increase the total AUM deployed in systematic credit very, very quickly.

Similar to our bottom-up approach, we also think it's possible that our top-down figure of \$100-130bn is an underestimate. Though anecdotal evidence suggests that most systematic credit investors trade electronically, some do transact through traditional voice desks. Such activity would contribute to annual volumes beyond the \$533bn estimated above and therefore also flow through to a larger AUM estimate. For these reasons, we skew our overall estimate upward slightly, to \$90-140bn.

## Portfolio trading: A surprisingly small part of the systematic landscape

Interestingly, our conversations with Barclays' portfolio trading desk also yielded a surprising result. Although systematic accounts make up 16% of electronic trading, they accounted for only 5% of IG portfolio volume and 3% of HY portfolio volume year-to-date. We estimate that portfolio trades account for about 10% of annual cash volume (see The equitification of credit, April 30, 2024). With the above attributions to systematic accounts, that would translate to about \$33bn in annual IG systematic portfolio volume and \$8bn in annual HY systematic portfolio volume. This is a much smaller amount than the \$533bn in electronic volume attributable to systematics.

This is somewhat surprising, because factor investing is frequently discussed as a common systematic strategy, and the factor quintile portfolios used in such strategies tend to include large cross-sections of bonds and are therefore theoretically conducive to portfolio trades as an execution mechanism. There are a couple possible explanations for this. First, factor investing, though around for decades in equity, is still in its infancy in credit. Second, factor strategies tend to be slow moving, rebalancing quarterly in most cases (monthly at most). The entire portfolio is also unlikely to turn over, as many factor strategies will repeat the same bonds in long and short portfolios period over period, thus reducing the required trading. Single-name focused strategies, by comparison, tend to trade much more tactically and focus on a few liquid names, and are therefore ill-suited to portfolio trades. These strategies tend to focus on shorter-term market signals that necessitate more trading, so liquidity and surety of execution are paramount, as transaction costs can quickly eat into alpha, so it's not surprising that the volume from these strategies eclipses portfolio trading.

## Anatomy of a systematic strategy

At their core, systematic strategies are rules based: they define a quantitative signal, construct a set of rules to go long or short based on that signal, and invest entirely according those rules. Unlike in traditional active management, where human discretion is used throughout the investment process (strategy definition, security selection, trade execution, and holding period), human discretion comes into play only in the first step. Once the signal and rules are defined, the machine takes over.

This definition is broad enough that several classes of investors can be construed as practitioners of systematic credit. Hedge funds, commodity trading advisors (CTAs), and asset managers are all examples of investors that might use a systematic credit strategy, but those strategies can difer wildly in style and execution. Some firms may use a systematic process exclusively, but it is increasingly common for large traditional active managers to employ systematic "sleeves" within their overall mandates. Also, as discussed above, many strategies fall under the "quantamental" umbrella, in which a systematic process is used to define the investible universe, but the ultimate security selection is still discretionary.

There are, of course, advantages and disadvantages to each approach. Chief among the advantages of systematic credit is the ability to ofload some of the analysis work to computers, which allows the managers to include a much wider universe of bonds in their investment decisions, while fundamental active managers are limited by the bandwidth of their human analysts, who can cover only so many names.

The flip side, of course, is that fundamental analysts can understand much more about a credit than any algorithm. Humans can extract nuance from language in deal docs, understand complex corporate structures, glean sentiment and tone from earnings calls, and digest pertinent news as it breaks, giving them a better understanding of the "forward-looking"

picture of a credit than systematic strategies, which rely more heavily on historical data. While advances along all of these avenues have been made in the fields of natural language processing, speech recognition, and sentiment analysis, there is still a large gap between the amount of useful investment information a human can derive from a credit and what a computer can understand.

This diference also underscores the necessity for wildly diferent analysis techniques. In forming a view on a credit, fundamental analysts rely on company financial statements, discussions with management, understanding of industry trends, and bespoke, capitalstructure-specific models. This allows analysts to have an extremely detailed, in-depth view of the credit, which is efectively impossible for a computer to replicate. Instead, systematic strategies employ a breadth-first rather than depth-first approach: they build index-spanning models that rely on signals that can be applied to a cross-sectional view of the market as a whole.

As a result, we think the growth in systematic credit will be beneficial for the liquidity and eficiency of the overall market. Since systematic strategies, advantaged by an abundance of data, can screen over the entire market, they are more likely to uncover opportunities for alpha outside of the most highly publicized names. While a fundamental strategy may be able to cover a few hundred capital structures in depth, a systematic strategy will frequently cover an entire market (all \~2,000 bonds in the HY market or \~10,000 bonds in the IG market) to find attractive investment candidates. This breadth-first approach is therefore likely to create demand for bonds in less liquid areas of the market and provide additional price discovery.

In the remainder of this report, we outline three of the most common systematic strategies: factor models, single-name strategies, and CTAs. For more detail on the universe of systematic strategies, see The Rise in Systematic Credit Investing, January 2024.

## The factor zoo

Factor models are one of the most frequently cited systematic strategies. Though they have been extremely popular in equities for the past few decades, they are beginning to make inroads into credit as well. The quantitative signal they define, commonly referred to as a "factor," centers on the theme that bonds exhibiting certain characteristics will consistently outperform. Once the signal (factor) is defined, the investor filters the investible universe for securities aligned with that signal and invests accordingly. Long-only strategies will buy a portfolio of bonds that score highest (most aligned) in a particular factor, while long-short strategies will also short a portfolio of the bonds scoring lowest.

Momentum is perhaps the most common. The underpinning idea is quite simple - securities that have been outperforming will continue to outperform and securities that have been underperforming will continue to underperform. As in every signal definition, the devil is in the details. Choice of time series, measurement window, and benchmark must all be considered, back-tested, and constantly reevaluated as market regimes change. Many momentum strategies define their signal simply as the trailing return or spread change (one-week, onemonth, and three-month are all common windows). Others will use combinations of moving averages, MACD metrics, or RSI bands. Barclays' Quantitative Portfolio Strategy (QPS) group has developed a momentum factor signal using the bond issuer's equity momentum, which has shown robust performance over the past three decades. For more details on this strategy, see Equity Momentum in Credit (EMC), August 18, 2017.

Figure 6 shows an example of how a momentum strategy might be implemented. The momentum factor here is defined as trailing one-month excess return. Every rebalancing period, the universe of investable bonds is sorted by trailing one-month excess return (the "factor score" in this example). The sorted list is then divided into equal-sized quantiles (quintiles are most common, though quantiles of any size (eg, deciles) may be used). Assuming it's quintiles, the top 20% of the excess-return-ranked universe (the top 20% of momentum scorers) is put into the long portfolio. If it's a long-short strategy, the bottom 20% is put into the short portfolio, and the middle three quintiles are ignored. The positions are then held until the next rebalancing period, at which point the investible universe is re-ranked and the portfolios are reconstructed and turn over.

This is the basic anatomy of a factor strategy. The key point is that the portfolio construction process is identical regardless of the factor definition. All that matters is that the factor is defined in such a way to allow the investible universe of bonds to be ranked from most aligned to least aligned with that factor. Once the factor is defined, the investment process is the same.

It is also critical that, within the ranking process, exposures from unintended sources of risk also be minimized. For example, a strategy going long the highest yielding bonds and short the

lowest yielding will very likely introduce a duration bias, with the long portfolio overweight long-duration bonds and the short portfolio overweight short-duration bonds. Many diferent approaches are employed to mitigate this - some strategies rank bonds within sector, rating, and duration buckets and build long and short portfolios from the top and bottom bonds in each bucket.

Value is another popular factor. In credit, however, the value definition can look starkly diferent from the one commonly used in equities. Equity factor models typically define value using fundamental metrics (most commonly price-to-book ratio). In credit, however, many issuers are private (particularly in high yield), so fundamental metrics aren't always as readily available. Instead, systematic credit investors may choose to define value using quantitative models comparing bond spreads with those of their peers while controlling for confounding factors such as duration, sector, and rating. For an example of a quantitative value factor, see Relative Value Investing in Credit, May 9, 2016. The bonds trading cheapest to their peers will score high and go into the long portfolio, while bonds trading richest to their peers will score low and go into the short portfolio.

Carry is another frequently used factor, with the rationale that high-yielding securities will outperform low-yielding securities. In this era of high base rates and relatively subdued defaults, carry strategies have outperformed (Figure 8). The carry factor can be defined quite simply as bond yield or spread, with the widest bonds going into the long portfolio and the tightest going into the short portfolio. Many strategies will also refine the definition to include roll-down, since pull-to-par efects are often considered alongside yield as a form of carry. This addition is most commonly featured in investment grade strategies, as high yield curves are not as frequently upward sloping.

## Tactical strategies: Trading single bonds systematically

Though factor models are most cited in literature (with good reason, as they are an extremely established strategy in equities), there is also a wide variety of systematic strategies that are more tactical in nature and focus on trading single names or bonds opportunistically. Like factor strategies, these "single-bond" strategies rely on quantitative signals to identify bonds as attractive buy or sell candidates. However, rather than deploying large factor portfolios of several hundred bonds and rebalancing monthly or quarterly, these strategies tend to have shorter holding periods and buy or sell bonds when their signals are triggered rather than waiting for a rebalancing date.

Capital structure arbitrage (frequently referred to as "debt-equity") strategies are a good example of this. Debt-equity strategies look for dislocations in market pricing between an issuer's equity and debt - for example, a large rally in an issuer's stock but little movement from its bonds. When such dislocations are identified, the strategy buys the debt and shorts the stock (or vice versa) in hopes that the dislocation will correct itself. Figure 9 shows an example of what this could look like for a hypothetical issuer.

FIGURE 9. A debt-equity fund might buy the stock and sell the bond after a large equity sell-of and then capture return as the relationship converges

![](images/38869c1440bd54443f553fb17e4004fbff6e572f6f5fe45bc6ecfc72dffdf04f.jpg)

## Source: Bloomberg, Barclays Research

The "systematic" part of the strategy comes from the fact that these investors tend to screen systematically for these dislocations and frequently employ quantitative models to determine the fair value of the debt-equity relationship. The models used range in complexity from simple range-bounds to more intricate Merton-inspired models that use equity signals to infer a theoretical fair value of an issuer's debt, but all of them produce a signal for when to put the trade on. The signals are often quite sensitive, so these investors typically prioritize liquidity. For a long time, these strategies would typically use CDS for the debt leg, since for many credits that was the more liquid option. Now, improved liquidity in the bond market thanks to electronic trading means that bond-equity trades are more common.

Debt-equity is just one example of this class of systematic strategy. Others may screen for bonds based on a host of signals such as regression-based relative value models, ratios of spreads to fundamental metrics, and even some of the factor signals defined above (just with fewer

bonds). Regardless, there are a variety of model-based strategies that 1) trade opportunistically, 2) execute at the single-name or single-bond level, and 3) rely on quantitative signals.

Because of the focus on a smaller number of bonds rather than a large portfolio, these strategies are unlikely to contribute materially to price action at the index level, but they can and absolutely do contribute to price action at the single-name level. On days when an issuer's bonds move with little news on the name, systematic signals are often responsible. While this dynamic, as we argued above, should result in more liquidity in the market, it also means that there is a new source of price action for investors to be aware of. Credit investors, whether systematic, discretionary, or in between, should familiarize themselves with the signals discussed here so that they can better understand the underlying drivers of price action and adapt accordingly.

## CTAs primarily use CDX (for now)

Commodity trading advisors (CTAs) have been around since the 1970s. So named because the first regulated CTAs traded commodities futures, the investor class has since expanded to include equities, rates, FX, volatility, and credit. Some CTAs trade within credit exclusively, but many are also cross-asset and deploy risk in whichever asset class their signals dictate. Within credit, CTA strategies and factor strategies are both typically discussed under the systematic umbrella (though discretionary CTAs do exist). Both are rules based, data driven, and rely on quantitative signals. In execution, however, they are quite diferent. Whereas factor strategies are typically executed with portfolios of cash bonds, CTAs typically express their views with macro products. Within credit, these are almost exclusively the derivative indices (CDX.IG and CDX.HY), as they are far and away the most liquid way to get high-level exposure to credit.

While momentum is just one of a panoply of signals employed by factor strategies, momentum and volatility are the chief drivers of CTA strategies, which are based first and foremost on trend following. Figure 10 shows a very simple example of a potential CTA momentum strategy. Momentum is measured via two moving averages of CDX spreads - a short-term three-day window and a longer-term 10-day window. When the short window crests above the long window (indicating shorter-term weakness, a bearish momentum signal), the model goes riskof and buys protection. When the short window dips below the longer window (a bullish momentum signal), the model goes risk-on and sells protection.

FIGURE 10. Example of a momentum signal in CDX.HY of the type that might be used by a CTA
![](images/f1671284c5f1503e5910db1ac3006720cf6ad6a3e0933133f600e7f63921348a.jpg)
Source: Bloomberg, Barclays Research

CTAs use macro products because liquidity and speed of execution are paramount. Whereas factor strategies typically have holding periods that are measured in months, CTA strategies are typically much higher frequency. They typically use signals that are measured in days (and in some cases intraday). The long and short portfolios in a factor strategy require OWIC/BWICs of cash bond portfolios, which take time to price and transact and often involve multiple dealers. Often, the process takes multiple days. That protocol is not feasible for a signal that needs to turn on a dime. CTAs therefore rely on macro products to express their views since they are liquid and can be traded as soon as the signal dictates.

Although CTAs do not trade in cash bonds directly, they are frequently cited as culprits for large market moves anyway since it's easy to read a large swing in a macro product as reflective of broader market sentiment. Although we view systematic strategies as net beneficial for market eficiency, trend-following strategies in particular can exacerbate herding behavior and amplify volatility. With these dynamics at play, alongside the factor and single-name influences discussed above, credit investors of all stripes should understand the signals that can bring systematic investors to the market and drive real price action.

Although much ink is spilled on the largest issuers in the investment grade market, there are plenty of less followed issuers that can influence overall portfolio returns. We define the peer comp set as bonds with similar maturity, rating, and sector in the broader index and exclude extremely illiquid bonds (sub-\$0.5mn average daily trading volumes). In our view, accounts that cannot own high yield bonds should avoid FLEXIH until it is clear that the facilities are fully operational and the rating agencies return to a stable outlook. However, for investors that are able to own non-IG bonds, we think that current pricing ofers an attractive entry point, as we expect the market eventually to tighten spreads to reflect returning gas flows to Freeport LNG, and debt reduction over time. We recommend buying FLEXIH 2031s.

## Sell Sonoco (SON, Not Rated) 2032s

Sonoco has been linked to an ongoing sale process for private metal packaging firm Trivium (TRIVIU), which is jointly owned by Ontario Teachers Pension (58%; OTPP) and Ardagh Group (ARGID; covered by Ed Brucker). The parties previously explored a sale in 2021,<sup>8</sup> and there may be increased motivation this time with Ardagh facing significant maturities. The business is reportedly being marketed of of \$480mn of 2024 EBITDA and a 8.0-8.5x multiple, suggesting a valuation of \$3.8-4.1bn. Sonoco was initially mentioned as a first-round bidder alongside a number of private equity firms<sup>9</sup> , and recent reports suggest that Sonoco, Apollo, and Platinum Equity have moved on to the second round.<sup>10</sup>

Sonoco has not commented on Trivium directly; however, given the product overlap between the Ball metal packaging and Trivium portfolios, it might have an opportunity to extract sourcing and operational synergies. If Sonoco were to proceed with an acquisition, we estimate that leverage would rise to the low 4x area. In past transactions, management indicated that investment grade ratings were a priority, and it seems credible that SON could retain IG status with leverage of 4.0-4.5x, though the current mid-BBB ratings might be pressured. Sonoco has limited cash on hand and modest cash flow after dividends (\~\$100mn in 2024, \~\$300mn in 2025). Trivium's capital structure is also largely secured, suggesting that in a potential deal, a significant portion of the approximately \$4bn enterprise value would have to be funded.

More broadly, Sonoco management has indicated an openness to acquisitions, specifically citing "programmatic M&A" as a future driver of growth. The company has also demonstrated a willingness to use its balance sheet. Sonoco targets operating with net leverage of 2.0-3.0x but has taken leverage above 3.0x for acquisitions in the past, as it did with the \$1.35bn acquisition of Ball metal packaging in 2022. Although leverage is at the higher end of the range today (2.8x), it is expected to be under 2x in 2025, and management views the balance sheet as an asset that it can use to support growth. The nature of SON's balance sheet (low cash balance, limited excess cash flow) also suggests that acquisitions might need to be debt financed. Although we do not view M&A as an outright negative generally (ie, we prefer it over large-scale buybacks), it does warrant some consideration with respect to credit metrics, particularly if a larger transaction were to occur.

In this sense, although we view SON's spreads to be fair for the steady-state business, we do not believe they price in risk associate with leveraging M&A. Sonoco's 2032 notes (Baa2/BBB) are quoted at levels similar to LyondellBasell (Baa2/BBB/BBB) and at a premium to Eastman Chemical (Baa2/BBB). Although we view packaging as a more resilient through-cycle industry than chemicals, we would expect Sonoco to trade at a discount to Eastman if a cash-funded acquisition were to take place. We would also expect Sonoco to trade at a wider discount to packaging peers Amcor and Avery Dennison, which it trades close to currently. As a result, we think it may be prudent to tactically de-risk and sell SON 2032s at these levels.

## Leveraged loans

YE24 price target: \$97.0-97.5 | FY24 total return = 8.5-9.0% | FY24 excess return = 3.5-4.0%

Loans have outperformed significantly in total return terms in 2024, as elevated base rates have boosted carry and positive price performance amid strong technicals has generated positive mark-to-market gains. We expect carry to remain strong for the remainder of 2024: Barclays Rates Strategy expects 3m SOFR to end 2024 at 5.0% (vs. 5.3% today), which is relatively in line with current market pricing showing a 31% chance of the fed funds rate ending at 500-525bp per the CME FedWatch Tool.

The real diferentiator for return outcomes, in our view, will be secondary price action. Our forecast is for the Morningstar LSTA Leveraged Loan Index (LLI) to end 2024 at \$97.0-97.5 (vs. \$96.99 as of Wednesday's close). The LLI has rallied persistently over the past year, up nearly \$4.0 since this point in 2023, largely because of a variety of technical factors: record year-to-date CLO new issue, strong retail inflows, and an increase in non-loan dedicated fund allocations to the asset class. This rally would correspond to an FY24 excess return of 3.5-4.0% for the LLI.

To test our forecast, we deconstruct the loan index by rating to understand how the components of the LLI would have to perform to achieve a YE24 price of \$97.0-97.5, as discussed below:

• BBs: There is little room for price appreciation in BBs, given that the BB index already trades at \$99.8. Our price target assumes that BBs end 2024 near par, with demand for higher-quality loans from both CLOs and non-loan dedicated accounts keeping prices elevated. A risk to this part of our forecast is that another repricing wave in 2H24 after the six-month soft call periods expire on 1Q's approximately \$140bn of repricing volume might deter buying. Still, we would not expect BBs to trade down drastically as a result given the broader technical strength.

• Single-Bs: This rating bucket composes more than 60% of the LLI's par amount, and the B2 and B3 ratings buckets are both approximately 40% of single-Bs (ie, roughly 25% of the LLI's par amount) (Figure 6). While B2s and B3s have both rallied meaningfully in recent months, they maintain a significant discount versus B1s, which are currently trading at \$99.8 (Figure 7). With little room for price appreciation from B1s, in order for the single-B index to rally to \$99 (from \$98.64 currently), B2s and B3s would have to outperform on a net basis versus B1s. If B1s are able to end 2024 at par (ie, +20c), that would would imply that B2s/B3s rally 23c/40c using monthly betas since 1998, which would bring the single-B index price to just under \$99. We believe CLOs will continue to express a preference for lower-quality single-Bs given the volume of year-to-date repricings , CLO CCC buckets at full capacity, and ample cushion on WARF tests (Figure 8) (see Repricing revival).

• CCCs: Although CCC prices have improved materially over the past several quarters, elevated risk is still priced in to the lowest quality segment of the LLI. The CCC index currently trades at \$82, and we assume that the index price will remain fairly range-bound through YE24. Driving this is our view that default rates will likely remain contained for the remainder of the year (see here). In addition, holding our assumptions for BBs/Bs constant, the CCC index can sell of by 4pts, to \$78, before the lower bound of our forecast range is breached. While this is not necessarily our base case given the strength of CLO issuance and the broader loan market year-to-date, it is certainly a possible outcome if base rates remain elevated and fundamentals weaken further (Figure 11).
