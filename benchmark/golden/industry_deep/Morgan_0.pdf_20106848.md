# Asia Macro Strategy: Stay Long Carry

There are good reasons to buy USD over the medium-to-long term, but not now. We expect USD to trade sideways in the short term, providing carry opportunities in Asia FX.

EM FX could be the major driver for Asia local markets: Asia local market spread over UST yield is tight, making a duration rally less likely unless UST yield rallies from here. Hence, EM FX is likely to be the major driver in the next few months.

Medium-term factors in favour of USD: Higher immigration to the US has kept the job market stronger than expected. The increasing labour supply could also alleviate wage pressure. Better growth and lower inflation would allow the Fed to cut rates but only gradually, a probable Goldilocks scenario for US assets.

Meanwhile, US leadership in AI and the increasing geopolitical tension between the US and China could increase the gap between US productivity and its global competitors. FX is driven by growth differentials and the US has its advantage in both labour input and total factor productivity: this is good for USD in the mediumto-long term.

We like carry in the short term: However, in the short term, we stick to the playbook we laid out two weeks ago. We believe that the market should trade range bound for the time being with an eye on the June FOMC for the first rate cut. Compared to central banks in CEE and LatAm, Asia central banks are much more cautious about rate cuts.

This week, we explore the topic of foreign inflows to G-Secs ahead of India's GBI-EM inclusion and do a comparison between KRW and TWD.

## Our current stance on Asia FX and rates

We toned down our caution: After being cautious on EM Asia in early January (see EM Strategy: Asia Macro Strategy: Three Themes Revisited, January 9, 2024), we toned down our caution two weeks ago by closing all long USD exposure (see EM Strategy: Asia Macro Strategy: Can Asia Generate Alpha for GBI-EM Investors? February 19, 2024).

As discussed in FX & EM Fixed Income Strategy: After You, Core Rates, March 4, 2024, we don’t see much value in local bond yields given the tight spreads over UST yields. This makes EM duration a UST trade, i.e., EM only rallies when the UST yield starts to rally. In addition, EM FX needs to appreciate first before EM rates.

Hence, EM FX is likely to be the major driver of EM local market returns in the next few months: From the beginning of 2024, many investors have been confused and surprised by strong US data as the perception of the US economy has been that high interest rates should finally weigh on growth and allow the US to have a soft landing.

Watch out for high population growth in the US: There are some signs of this such as retail sales, but overall US data points have been better than expected, particularly in the job market. One possible explanation could be the rise in immigration. On February 7, the Congressional Budget Office (CBO) published new population estimates showing faster population growth because of an increase in immigration, especially undocumented immigration.

The CBO correspondingly increased prime-age population growth from 0.2% to 0.8% in 2023, from 0.5% to 1.2% in 2024, from 0.4% to 1.2% in 2025, and from 0.2% to 0.9% in 2026 (see Friday Finish – US Economics: Immigration, Labor Supply, and Potential Growth,

February 23, 2024).

What does higher immigration mean? As these immigrants go to the job market, the increased supply could alleviate wage pressure. Meanwhile, their production and consumption could help growth.

Inflation could be lower while growth could be stronger. Isn't this the best combination or Goldilocks for the Fed and the market? Lower inflation allows the Fed to cut interest rates gradually while still decent growth removes the urgency for the Fed to cut aggressively. In other words, improved demographics in the US put it in a better position versus Europe and China.

Medium-term factors favour USD: Meanwhile, US leadership in AI and rising geopolitical tension between the US and China could increase the gap between US productivity and its global competitors. Total output is capital input times labour input and productivity. The US seems to be ahead of other countries in two out of three growth drivers. This would boost USD over the medium-to-long term as FX is driven by growth differentials.

However, in the short term, we stick to the playbook we laid out two weeks ago. We believe that the market should trade range bound for the time being with an eye on the June FOMC for the first rate cut. Compared to central banks in CEE and LatAm, Asia central banks are much more cautious about rate cuts (see Asia Economics: The Viewpoint: Why Asian Central Banks Will Not Cut Ahead of the Fed, February 26, 2024). This is because:

1) Asia's real rates are much lower than those of the US: With the rise in US real rates and more modest hiking cycles of AXJ central banks, Asia's real rate advantage over the US has eroded. AXJ ex China's real rate advantage over the US (calculated based on 12- month forward CPI) peaked in February 2022 and has turned negative since early 2023.

4) Growth momentum is still holding up: Across Asia ex China and Japan, domestic demand momentum seems to be holding up, as seen in resilient import demand. At the same time, external demand conditions are improving, as evidenced by the pick-up in export growth and new export orders. Real rates in the region are also not restrictive yet. We continue to hold short CNH/INR and short TWD/INR. We added short CNH/THB and short EUR/KRW two weeks ago and continue to recommend both trades.

USD/KRW has been around 1,330-1,340, despite better-than-expected exports and strong equity inflows. TWD, a historically low-beta currency in Asia, has been underperforming recently, with USD/TWD hitting a year-to-date high, despite have similar flow dynamics to KRW. We explore this more in Why do we prefer KRW to TWD?

In rates, we keep receiving 1-year CNY NDIRS, long onshore FX-hedged 1-year CGBs and paying 1-year INR NDOIS.

We closed receive 2-year KRW NDIRS versus paying 2-year USD IRS without beta adjustment recently after it reached our target, and pivoted to a 1s3s KRW NDIRS flattener. Markets are pricing in a very modest BoK rate-cutting cycle, with just \~30bp of cuts priced for this year and another \~20bp of cuts priced for next year. This would leave the policy rate \~50bp above the midpoint of the BoK's neutral rate range estimate, even as our economists forecast inflation to move back to hit the 2%Y target by end-2024.

## Foreign inflows to G-Secs since India's GBI-EM inclusion

Foreigners have bought US\$9 billion G-Secs since last September: JP Morgan announced the inclusion of India in the GBI-EM index last September (see India: India Macro Strategy: A Milestone Event for India Bond Market – GBI-EM Inclusion, September 22, 2023). Since then, foreigners have been gradually adding India exposure. Total foreign ownership had reached almost US\$29 billion as of February 27, a US\$9 billion increase versus the level at the end of last September.

Almost all the inflows went to Fully Accessible Route (FAR) bonds, which will be part of the index, while inflows to non-FAR bonds have been very limited. As a result, FAR bonds now account for 70% of foreign ownership in G-Secs.

## Why do we prefer KRW to TWD?

KRW and TWD are often seen as having similar drivers: Both economies are exporters and run current account surpluses. Both have high exposure to the semiconductor/tech cycle, and their currencies are sensitive to equity flows.

Why we are constructive on KRW but not TWD: As a result, some investors have questioned why we are constructive on KRW but like using TWD as a funding currency. There are a few reasons for this divergent view:

1) Carry: TWD is the lowest-yielding currency in AXJ, with the cost of carry of being long TWD punitive at around 3.7% for one year. In comparison, the 1-year cost of being long

KRW is about half of TWD’s, at around 1.8%. Even after normalising for the different level of volatility in the currencies, TWD still offers the lowest carry/vol ratio in AXJ while KRW sits in the middle of the pack.

This makes the timing of any long TWD trades crucial. There is potential for TWD to outperform should USD and US yields fall, but given that we expect more range-bound trading in the next few weeks, we believe that TWD's low yield would render it a funding currency for now.

3) Drivers of equity inflows: While Taiwan and Korea equity inflows both benefit from AI and the tech cycle recovery, Korea equities receives additional support from its corporate reform agenda. Indeed, year to date, foreign inflows into Korea equities are 1.7 times that of Taiwan’s, at US\$8.4 billion versus US\$5 billion. The inflows to Korea equities have been broad-based across sectors, with 20 out of 26 sectors seeing net foreign inflows.

4) Current account improvement: Granted, Taiwan’s exports may benefit more than Korea’s from the tech cycle recovery, given that semiconductors account for \~35% of Taiwan’s total exports versus \~20% for Korea. However, the starting point is also important in terms of the currency impact, in our view.

Driven mainly by the goods trade balance, Taiwan’s current account surplus shrunk by just 4pp of GDP over 2021-23, and remained at an admirable 11.8% of GDP even at its lowest point. In contrast, Korea’s current account surplus declined by 5.4pp of GDP over the period, printing at just 0.2% of GDP at its lowest point as the trade balance dipped into a deficit for the first time since 1997.

The FX impact from a current account rebounding from nearly 0% of GDP may not necessarily pale in comparison to one rebounding from \~12% of GDP, in our view.
