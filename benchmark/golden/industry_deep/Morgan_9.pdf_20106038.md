## United States

● December retail sales capped off a solid holiday shopping season, albeit with some caveats

Housing appears to be getting a modest lift from the recent decline in mortgage rates

Even with progress on inflation, Fed officials look to be in no hurry to cut rates

• Next week brings the first look at 4Q GDP (2.2% saar) and December core PCE inflation (0.22%m/m)

The economic data released this week point to an economy that continues to grow at a trend-like pace around the turn of the year. A solid retail sales report for December was consistent with good growth in real consumer spending at the end of last year, which provided the anchor for what we still think looks like real GDP expanding at a 2.2% annual rate in 4Q. While the news on 1Q is still scant at this point, the very low level of jobless claims in the latest weekly report suggests no deterioration in the health of the labor market. And as long as the labor market remains tight we expect consumer spending will continue to move the expansion forward.

The early January housing indicators have also looked favorable, as the roughly 100bp decline in mortgage rates is having the expected effect on housing demand. The January homebuilders survey perked up and the latest weekly data on mortgage purchase applications also turned higher. To be sure, both of these indicators remain at low levels, and we expect they won<sup>’</sup>t get anywhere near recent highs absent a more substantial move down in mortgage rates. Existing home sales were more downbeat through the latest December print, but we view this as a more of a lagging signal on housing. Through the ups and downs of housing demand over the past two years, housing supply has been steadier. That was the case again in December, as housing starts slipped last month, but the more forward-looking permits data increased and single-family permits rose to the highest level since May 2022.

Manufacturing has looked less impressive. Industrial production edged up 0.1%-pt last month, thanks to returning UAW workers. But in the bigger picture the level of manufacturing output last month was virtually unchanged relative to where it was two years ago (and 10 years ago, for that matter). While manufacturing output has been boringly and disappointingly stagnant, the factory surveys have suggested an even grimmer picture for most of the last year. That was the case again this week as both the Empire State and Philly Fed manufacturing surveys for January were deeply in negative territory.

Compared to last week there was little inflation news this week. Next week we look for the December core PCE measure to increase 0.22% month on month, which would take the year-ago reading down from 3.2% to 3.0%. While progress continues to be made in bringing core inflation back down toward the Fed<sup>’</sup>s 2% goal, Fed officials appear in no rush to cut rates. Notably, this week Fed governor Waller indicated that while policy rates can probably come down this year, the decision to lower the funds rate should be “carefully calibrated and not rushed.<sup>”</sup> We heard this as leaning against a move as soon as March and we continue to look for the first cut at the June FOMC meeting.

Monetary policy hasn<sup>’</sup>t been the only show in town this week. Congress passed last-minute funding for government agencies that otherwise would have closed this weekend. The shortterm patch sets up another funding battle next month. A deal may be in the works to couple an extension of two expiring business tax benefits with a restart of the expanded child tax credit. But news headlines suggested that the two sides were further from a deal to couple Ukraine aid with border reform.

Next week is about numbers, not words: Fed speakers will be in their blackout period ahead of the late January FOMC meeting. Instead, attention will turn to the first look at 4Q GDP, and the December reading of the Fed<sup>’</sup>s preferred inflation gauge, core PCE.

## Retail sales: use your illusion

The December retail sales report beat expectations, with the headline up 0.6% in December and sales for the important control group up 0.8% that month (Figure 1). Revisions for the control group were also modestly positive for both November and October. On the whole, this report shows more momentum for consumers than we (and the consensus) had been anticipating late last year.

Figure 1: Monthly changes in "control" retail sales
![](images/228c46a8ce1cbfcc6bddb42b0e181dee59a7b0e83151e5c580fe226703555d28.jpg)

It now looks like real consumption rose about 0.3% in December and was up around 2.5% saar in 4Q. There are reasons to think that the underlying trend is not as strong as these numbers imply on the surface, as the increase in retail spending during the holiday season was lackluster before seasonal adjustment yet the increases in the seasonally adjusted data were solid. But even discounting the signal to some degree, we think consumers are continuing to spend.

The latest jobless claims report was another indicator that sent an upbeat signal about the economy, but one that we might want to discount to some degree. Initial claims fell to 187,000 during the week ending January 13 after seasonal adjustment, hitting one of the lowest levels in recent years (Figure 2). The seasonal factors expect some big swings in claims filings during the holiday season and in the early part of each year, so individual weekly prints may not be reliable in isolation. But the broader trend still looks favorable in the data and is suggestive of strength in the labor market.

## Manufacturing still sour

Going in the other direction, the Empire State manufacturing survey surprised significantly to the downside, with results coming in at very downbeat levels. We don<sup>’</sup>t think there is a sharp break occurring in the manufacturing sector and the separate regional manufacturing data released by the Philadelphia Fed produced weak results, but ones that were much less alarming. Stepping back, the more lagging (but more reliable) manufacturing data from the Fed<sup>’</sup>s IP report also has shown a soft trend for manufacturing output in recent months, although some bounce back in auto production following strike-related disruptions has helped boost the aggregate readings (Figure 3).

## Housing getting a little life

One of the few reliable regularities in macroeconomics is the inverse relation—with a few months<sup>’</sup> lag—between mortgage rates and housing demand. And so this week<sup>’</sup>s indicators of homebuying appetite shouldn<sup>’</sup>t be too surprising in light of the 4Q decline in mortgage rates. This is one of those cases where one should be clear about levels and changes. While lower mortgage rates are spurring a little more turnover we don<sup>’</sup>t think we<sup>’</sup>re going back to where we were a few years ago absent a larger move down in mortgage rates. The January homebuilders survey highlights this distinction: while it jumped 7pts it has miles to go before it ever gets back to <sup>’</sup>21 levels (Figure 4).

## Back to inflation

One of the more important data releases next week will December<sup>’</sup>s figure for core PCE inflation, the Fed<sup>’</sup>s preferred inflation gauge. If we get our 0.22% anticipated monthly increase the year-ago rate will moderate to 3.0%, while both the three- and six-month annualized gains should be near or below 2% (Figure 5). Recent declines in several inflation expectation measures also support the idea that the inflation trend is cooling.

There are reasons to think that not all Decembers should be equal in unadjusted terms (due to issues like the day of the week on which Christmas falls), but this does suggest solid support from the seasonal factors for the figures reported for December 2023.

We can also look at the unadjusted changes in the retail sales data across recent months to see how performance during the broader holiday season compared to that of prior years. Nominal sales of the control group increased 21% (unadjusted and not annualized) across November and December in 2023, which is a gain about in line with the average for these months in recent years but below the increases in these months in all pre-pandemic years (third chart below). Despite these unexceptional gains in the unadjusted data for recent months, the seasonally adjusted figures showed control sales up 0.6% on average across November and December last year.
