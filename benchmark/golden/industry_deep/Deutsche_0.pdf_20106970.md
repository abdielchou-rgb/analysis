# The world’s tectonic plates of power change slowly but dramatically over time

World economic power shifts over time. The relative growth and decline of countries/empires often leads to global tensions. While China has been growing rapidly in recent decades, that growth is just restoring it closer to its place for much of economic history, a position it feels is the historical norm…

Demographics have always shaped geopolitics, as having more people provides nations with greater economic and military power. Over the last several hundred years, the changing size of major powers’ populations has broadly coincided with shifts in relative power, often resulting in conflict.

Looking forward, the world will likely change dramatically again within our lifetimes. We are exactly at the point where China, India and Africa’s population cross and all go on potentially very different journeys. As an example of the shifting geopolitical landscape through history, in the appendix on pages 34- 54 we show maps of Europe every century from 1000 to 1700, and then with more regular frequency from 1780 as the modern world emerges. It includes a short summary of the major changes that occurred between each point.

The maps show that Europe’s borders have constantly changed over the last millennium, with repeated conflicts driven by forces such as monarchical rivalries, dynastic claims, religious differences, and economic tensions. Over that time, states and empires have come and gone.

After WWII, 6 countries in Western Europe moved to align their economic and political interests, with the creation of the European Coal and Steel Community. Coal and steel were important for military purposes, so with a new common market, they hoped to make war impossible. The ECSC was the precursor to the modern EU, which has helped to foster geopolitical stability on a continent used to incredibly frequent conflicts over prior centuries.

But history suggests we should avoid complacency. Russia’s invasion of Ukraine has led to war in Europe once again. And we know from history that long decades of peace can be shattered with little warning, as seen prior to World War I. Moreover, the balance of power between different states is one that shifts constantly, in response to economic, military and demographic trends. As such, the relative position of states can change dramatically from generation to generation, leading to the emergence of new rivalries and conflicts.

# Post WWII, the market impact of geopolitical events has often been measurable in days/weeks. The market soon moves back to the macro.

Geopolitical events have often created short, sharp market shocks, but with little lasting impact beyond weeks. From there the macro has taken over. So on this basis you should generally buy into geopolitical risk. The question is whether we’re entering a new phase given that tensions are rising, or whether geopolitical risk continues to create more fear than the reality…

# We still live in an era of low realised geopolitical risk on many measures…

Viewed over the very long-run we continue to be in an era of unprecedented calm in actual “Great Power” conflicts… That may be the reality of the nuclear age... However, lots of tension exists in the background..

## Appendix

The following maps show the borders of Europe over the last millennium, which have constantly changed.

The shifting balance of power is an ever-present process, as states experience divergent trends in demography, economic growth and military capacity. That means the relative position of states can change dramatically in just a couple of generations, particularly in response to population growth.

History also tells us that alliances can shift dramatically. France and Germany were historical rivals, but today are allies in the EU and NATO. Likewise, Britain and France were enemies for centuries, but relations improved after the Napoleonic Wars, and by the World Wars of the 20<sup>th</sup> century they were on the same side. Even further back, the Diplomatic Revolution in 1756 led to a major realignment, and Austria shifted from a British ally to a French ally.

• So in the world of geopolitics and international relations, there are few permanent fixtures.

In 1000, Europe was at the start of the “High Middle Ages”, a period in which the political boundaries of the continent became more defined. In part, this was because the barbarian invasions of the previous centuries mostly ceased. But unlike today, Europe was not particularly developed in economic terms, even by international standards. The continent had experienced significant decline and stagnation over previous centuries since the fall of the Roman Empire, and estimates of GDP per capita in Europe at this point were lower than in the Middle East, China and India.

![](images/2a89fbdab769ef4869f42066e5a9622733b6b826c533b281a0fcd69c9af397a5.jpg)
Map of 1000. © Copyright 2002-2007, Centennia Software, Mystic, Connecticut USA, www.HistoricalAtlas.com

Whilst the barbarian invasions had subsided, by 1100 there had been major political changes across Europe.

The Holy Roman Empire was established as a major power and absorbed Burgundy.

The East-West Schism in 1054 saw a formal break between the Catholic and Orthodox Churches.

The Normans conquered England in 1066.

In 1071, the Byzantine Empire experienced a major defeat at Battle of Manzikert by the Seljuk Empire, pushing Byzantine influence out of Anatolia.

In 1095, Pope Urban II called for the First Crusade, which saw the Crusaders conquer Jerusalem in 1099.

![](images/39fc47670e4968365d5337ea691eb6e03a25129b11867ac36e110a73adae7d97.jpg)

The Crusades continued over the 12<sup>th</sup> century, leading to further border changes by 1200. For instance, Lisbon was taken by Crusader forces in 1147. And after the siege of Jerusalem in 1187, it returns to Muslim control under Saladin.

Elsewhere, Bulgaria returns as an independent entity from 1185, following the Uprising of Asen and Peter.

England’s possessions in France undergo expansion in this period as well, collectively known as the Angevin Empire. That comes as the new King Henry II succeeds his father as Duke of Normandy and Count of Anjou, whilst also obtaining the Duchy of Aquitaine via marriage.

First, the Byzantine Empire was Iceland dissolved in 1204, and the Latin Empire established. The Byzantine Empire is then re-established in 1261, but smaller.

Second, the invasions of the Mongol Empire reach Europe in the late-1200s and caused major population displacement.

Third, the Crusader presence in the Middle East ends around this period, and the Siege of Acre in 1291 sees the Crusaders lose control to the Mamluks.

However, in Western Europe, Christian forces push Muslim rulers out of much of Spain, leaving them only in Granada.

England lose much of their possessions to France, with Normandy lost in 1204.

The 1300s was a calamitous century for Europe, leading to substantial change by 1400.

There was significant depopulation because of the Great Famine of 1315-17, and the Black Death in the middle of the century, which is the worst pandemic of the last millennia.

Politically by 1400, the Ottoman Empire expanded into Europe, whilst the Holy Roman Empire was in decline, with individual dynasties gaining in
importance.

The 1300s also marked the start of the Hundred Years’ War between France and England, as English King Edward III claimed the French throne after his uncle King Charles IV of France died.
