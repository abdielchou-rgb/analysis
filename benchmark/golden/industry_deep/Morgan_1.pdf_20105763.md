## EM Edge Data Watch

## Entering 2024 with eyes wide open

0 EM Edge central bank easing trend continues, with more joining

Benign global environment could open space to access markets (mainly Africa) and/or increase FX reserves (mainly Latam)

Egypt and Kazakhstan join Uzbekistan and Zambia in showing BoP weakness

• Early Dec inflation prints somewhat concerning, but unlikely to stop easing

0 Red Sea disruptions to bite Egypt, but impact appears limited (for now)

Despite the holidays, the data flow in the final two weeks of 2023 was rather busy in the Edge space, with key releases on current accounts, growth and inflation along with central bank decisions. As we move into 2024, we digest incoming data against a backdrop of a more benign global environment and a baseline for soft landing in the US, which in itself carries positive implications for Edge countries - see EM Edge 2024 outlook. Thus far, we have highlighted that central banks will likely be able to proceed with easing and more will join (early December edition); but a couple of other angles are worth highlighting.

First, central banks in Europe Edge have indeed shifted more dovish. The National Bank of Georgia cut 50bp (from 25bp prior pace) while Azerbaijan maintained its 50bp easing pace (after starting cuts in November). Latam central banks continued to ease largely as expected (Table 1). We had highlighted (more below) that balance of payments trends are not favourable in DomRep, and BCRD eventually cut 25bp rather than 50bp expected because FX was under weakening pressure. But that was offset by Uruguay<sup>’</sup>s COPOM which delivered a 25bp cut against our call for rates on hold - this should not be seen as dovish because forward guidance was adjusted in a mildly hawkish direction.

However, it is hard to see this dovish impulse/continuous easing reaching Asia or Africa Edge yet. Ex-post real rates have been positive throughout 2023 in both Latam and Europe Edge, but turned positive only late in 2023 in Asia and are still negative in Africa. In Asia, only Vietnam enjoys strong fundamentals while in Africa the nearterm pressure remains more in the hiking direction (see below).

Further ahead, the direction Latam Edge central banks take in the context of Fed easing becomes more nuanced. Central banks in this region have been at the forefront of easing. Inflation is now below pre-COVID rates but normalizing higher and real rates are not as high as they used to be. We believe that, because of domestic factors, regional central banks will not cut rates as much as the Fed (Figure 1). This could translate into appreciation pressure on FX which leads central banks to accumulate FX reserves. In general, our view is that Latam central banks will be more likely to intervene in FX to limit FX appreciation rather than cut rates as much as the Fed.

Inflationary pressures and higher inflation expectations amid curreny pressures are likely to keep most Africa Edge central banks hawkish near term while others cut (Figure 1). We project further rate hikes in 1Q24 with rate cuts possible only in 2H24 when inflation cools. Egypt, Nigeria and Zambia are most in need of tighter monetary conditions as global conditions have inhibited foreign capital inflows needed to fund current account deficits. In Nigeria and Egypt, firm monetary policy tightening will need to be accompanied with tighter fiscal policy in a bid to tame money supply, stabilize prices, ease currency pressures and lower inflation. Lower mining receipts and strong growth have kept Zambia<sup>’</sup>s current account in deficit while adherence of the NIR targets under the IMF programme has limited BoZ<sup>’</sup>s FX interventions. As such, a weaker currency is likely to keep the BoZ on a tightening path and we project a 50bp hike to the policy rate in February to 11.5%. As BoP position reamins weak (see below), the risk is for more hikes.

## Opportunistic issuance?

Besides easing and potential reserve accumulation in Latam, another implication of the more benign global environment could translate into the ability of some countries, mainly in Africa, to access external markets. For context, tight global financing conditions have left most Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) sovereigns shut out of international capital markets over the last couple of years. This has forced a rapid rise in domestic debt (including central bank financing) and funding from multilateral sources under IMF programs. We have been cautious in our forecasts regarding external bond issuances in Africa Edge countries in 2024, only pencilling in tiny issuances for a handful (such as Egypt). However, the <sup>‘</sup>soft landing<sup>’</sup> narrative has led to a decline in yields, particularly in some stressed countries (Figure 2). In fact, Kenya and Angola are not far from the 10% market access threshold — we consider that issuance at yields below this level could be attractive for some countries. Therefore, if a soft landing gains more strength, then Kenya and Nigeria could be the first to come to markets. Kenya would be keen to partly refinance its US\$2bn Eurobond maturity due in June 2024, while Nigeria is in dire need on FX liquidity injection.

While SSA might be the main beneficiary from lower US rates, increased external issuance is also possible in the MENA region. Egypt has been able to access international markets through Panda and Samurai bonds in the last year, and it also issued an expensive Sukuk in February. With yields lower, Egypt might opt for some extra issuance in 2024, assuming it comes against the backdrop of a a credible resumption of the IMF programme.

In the GCC space, Saudi Arabia is without doubt more active in the Eurobond market after issuing US\$16bn in 2023. Our forecast continues to expect a large issuance in 2024 with most of the predicted fiscal deficit, about US\$19bn, to be financed externally. Public issuance is also likely to originate from the SWF PIF which issued US\$9bn in 2023 and is expected to continue to spend and invest heavily in 2024. We expect Bahrain to issue US\$3bn to cover due amortizations and to finance its deficit, but increased issuance is a possibility with favorable markets. Finally, despite our current call for zero gross issuance in 2024 in Oman, lower international yields could be seen as a good occasion to raise some buffers via issuance.

## More countries face weak BoPs

Our past balance of payments analysis identified two countries as being more exposed than others: Zambia and Uzbekistan. The weakness for both was driven mainly by a negative basic balance and an associated loss of FX reserves. Using the latest data available in Edge (3Q BoP), we can see that more countries slipped into negative basic balance territory (Figure 3). Zambia and Uzbekistan remained exposed (suggesting FX weakness and/or loss of FX reserves), but they are joined by DomRep, Paraguay, Kazakhstan and Ukraine. Azerbaijan has CA surpluses and invests abroad, DomRep has benefitted from loans and remittances to increase FX reserves, Paraguay from portfolio flows, while Ukraine has received significant funding from donors to achieve a large increase in FX reserves. This leaves Kazakhstan and Egypt as exposed, along Zambia and Uzbekistan. In the third quarter, Kazakhstan was forced to sell large amounts of its FX reserves (and FX sales continue in early 2024). For Egypt, BoP data (volatile because of import restrictions) underscores the need for IMF programme and FX adjustment. Another country which stands out with large loss of FX reserves in 3Q is El Salvador, but that was driven by repayments of external debt. Its external position is robust and FX reserves have increased in 4Q23.

In the GCC, CA prints continue to be in firm surplus territory, with Qatar and Bahrain both surprising to the upside. In Qatar the picture looks solid with a wide current account surplus. On the other hand, Bahrain continues to lose reserves despite the higher-than-expected surplus in 3Q (reserves are currently \$574mn below Dec-22). Looking ahead, we still see wide current account surplusesthroughout GCC. Despite this, we see limited reserve accumulation as investments abroad remain large.

In CAC, external accounts have improved across the region, in most cases driven by the same forces: narrower trade deficits on lower oil prices and surging tourism. At the same time, financial accounts remain robust (Costa Rica and DomRep) or have at least become healthier (El Salvador). Large FDI in Costa Rica more than triples the country<sup>’</sup>s CAD. El Salvador continued to lose reserves through 3Q23, but has seen FDI become positive and sizable. It could boost its external standing were it to agree a program with the IMF after next month<sup>’</sup>s election, something President Bukele has suggested will be the case. The main concern, as it is growth-wise, has become to us Panama, as the deterioration in the country<sup>’</sup>s risk profile implicit in bond markets conflates with large financing needs to create what could become an even more challenging fiscal outlook than the one we already envision.

## December CPI somewhat concerning

Inflation momentum in December was rather strong in most countries in the Edge space, except for Africa where both Kenya and Zambia were softer (Figure 4). Inflation prints in Asia Edge (Pakistan, Sri Lanka and Vietnam) showed acceleration from November with Pakistan and Sri Lanka surprising to the upside and with stronger momentum. The Vietnam print was softer than expected and core momentum exhibited stability (only a 0.2% rise). With improved supply side dynamics, core<sup>’</sup>s stabilization should lead headline CPI here lower too. Despite the acceleration in headline CPI in Pakistan, we continue to expect inflation to moderate on base effects and inflation expectations.

Momentum was also stronger in Europe Edge - close monitoring is needed to see if the trend continues. In over-yearago, Georgia showed acceleration but only to 0.4% from 0.1%, Uzbekistan was stable at 8.8%oya, and Kazakhstan slowed to 9.8% from 10.3%. In the case of Georgia, inflation should gradually increase but remain below target, while in Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan we see further limited disinflation. In Latam, only Paraguay showed stable momentum with prices in DomRep and Uruguay accelerating. Uruguay<sup>’</sup>s headline CPI logged -0.11%m/m and 5.1%oya in December, in line with expectations. When controlling for seasonality, inflation momentum continues to accelerate to 7.8% 3m/3m, saar, supporting our call for the central bank to stand pat in the short-term amid unanchored inflation expectations.

## Implications of Red Sea disruption

Following a series of security threats off the southern Red Sea relating to attacks originating from Yemen, several major shipping companies as well as some oil companies decided to halt journeys through the Red Sea route. According to high frequency data, the number of vessels transiting through Suez in December was about 5% lower than in 2022. Looking at data since the first companies decided to re-route around Africa, the contraction has actually been around 20%. Cargo ships reported a larger decline than oil tankers. At a global level, insurance and freight costs have already increased with the key question around costs passing through to consumers.

For Egypt, Suez Canal revenues are an important source of FX accounting for almost 10% of CA receipts. Our projections for FY24 Suez revenues were for about US\$9bn. Since disruption started 150-200 vessels re-routed with figures likely to increase. Compared to the Ever Given incident, this time Egypt will suffer some real losses (at that time almost no vessels re-routed). We believe the impact should still be manageable for Egypt, although clearly not welcome as the country is already facing important challenges relating to its difficult external picture. The route around Africa adds 7-10 days to the journey, impacting delivery times and fuel costs. It is unlikely to be a new normal but carries some implications for global inflation.

## Pakistan: Inflation continues to moderate, more room for SBP to deliver cuts Jin Tik Ngai

Headline inflation came in at 29.7%oya and 0.8%m/m in December, in line with our expectations. The rapid decline in monthly price gains, following the gas tariff hikes in November is consistent with our prevailing view that the knock-on effects of the latest administrative price adjustment is limited. We continue to expect inflation to moderate (2H23: 28.8%oya vs. 1H24F: 19.9%oya), driven by favorable base effects, the ongoing easing in supply constraints, and softening inflation expectations. All else equal, this could open up room for the SBP to deliver 100-200bp of rate cuts by the end of FY24 (June 2024). The Fed<sup>’</sup>s policy pivot, if it does happen, will also support the PKR and bolster the case for SBP easing but in the next 3-6 months, the outcome of national elections (scheduled for mid-February) and post-SBA external financing will likely carry more significance.

## Vietnam: Resilient GDP growth in 4Q, SBV to wait and see after easing in advance Jisun Yang, Jin Tik Ngai

We continue to expect the SBV to keep policy rates on hold through 2024. GDP marked the third consecutive quarter of strong growth in 4Q23 (Table 1), suggesting that there<sup>’</sup>s no immediate need for growth stimulus. Exports surprised the most with resilient external demand, as the expected timing and likelihood of a global recession have recently been pushed back and reduced. Domestic demand also grew at an above-par pace, and credit growth was close to the 15% target during the quarter, implying that liquidity was sufficient to support domestic activities. While the Fed<sup>’</sup>s dovish pivot allows more room to maneuver, the SBV has faced a negative interest rate gap with the US since last May as a result of its rate cuts ahead of the Fed, so it will likely be in a wait-andsee mode, as long as there's no rush to deliver additional cuts.

On the inflation front, oil and food prices eased further in December after pushing up headline CPI in 3Q. Prices for transport (motor fuel) fell 0.3%m/m, sa, in December for the third straight monthly drop, and food CPI reduced its increases from 0.8% in September to 0.4% in November and 0.2% in December. Core CPI exhibited a stable 0.2% rise in December, following 0.1% in October and 0.2% in November. Core's year-ago inflation continued to moderate from 5.2%oya in January to 3.0% in December. Given the diminishing supply-side volatility, we maintain our view that core's gradual stabilization should determine the overall inflation path this year.

## Saudi Arabia: Narrower CA surplus on wider service deficit

## Francesco Arcangeli

Saudi Arabia<sup>’</sup>s current account surplus narrowed much more than expected in 3Q linked to a weaker services performance (Figure 1). Despite a still supportive trade balance surplus of US\$30bn, in line with 2Q figures, the current account surplus declined to just US\$5.0bn in 3Q. This was more than US\$11bn lower than in 2Q and the worst performance since 1Q21. Despite lower oil volumes, higher oil prices allowed a small recovery in oil exports as imports moved higher. This was boosted by higher capital spending related to large ongoing investments. As mentioned, services were the key factor behind the worse performance as the deficit expanded to US\$15.4bn from \$8.2bn in the previous quarter. Travel was particularly affected, reporting the first (small) negative net balance since 4Q21. Primary income remained in surplus as secondary reported a similar deficit compared to 3Q with remittances broadly stable and still well below 4Q21 peak.

## Africa Edge: Soft landing narrative could provide opportunistic issuance Gbolahan Taiwo

There might be scope for some African sovereigns to opportunistically tap the external bond markets in the coming months. For context, tight global financing conditions have left most Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) sovereigns shut out of international capital markets over the last couple of years. This has forced a rapid rise in domestic debt (including central bank financing) and funding from multilateral sources under IMF programs. We have been cautious in our forecasts regarding external bond issuances in Africa Edge countries in 2024, only pencilling in tiny issuances for a handful (such as Egypt). However, the <sup>‘</sup>soft landing<sup>’</sup> narrative has led to a recent decline in yields, particularly in some stressed countries. In fact, Kenya and Nigeria are not far from the 10% market access threshold (Figure 1) — we consider that issuance at yields below this level could be attractive for some countries. Therefore, if this view gains more strength, then Kenya and Nigeria could be the first to come to markets. Kenya would be keen to partly refinance its US\$2bn Eurobond maturity due in June 2024, while Nigeria is in dire need of FX liquidity injection.

## More hikes likely in store

Inflationary pressures and higher inflation expectations particularly amid pressure for currency weakness is likely to keep most Africa Edge central banks hawkish while others cut. We project further rate hikes in 1Q24 with rate cuts possible only in 2H24 when Fed is in a rate cutting cycle too and domestic inflation eases. Egypt, Nigeria and Zambia are most in need for tighter monetary conditions as global conditions have inhibited foreign capital inflows needed to fund current account deficits. In Nigeria and Egypt, firm monetary policy tightening will need to be accompanied with tighter fiscal policy in a bid to tame money supply, stabilize prices, ease currency pressures and lower inflation. Lower mining receipts and strong growth impulse have kept Zambia<sup>’</sup>s current account in deficit while adherence of the NIR targets under the IMF programme has limited BoZ<sup>’</sup>s FX interventions. As such, a weaker currency is likely to keep the BoZ on a tightening path as we project a 50bp hike to the policy rate in February to 11.5%. As the BoP position remains weak (see below), the risk is for more hikes.

## Egypt: Wider current account deficit; positive errors and omissions Francesco Arcangeli

Current account deficit reached US\$2.8bn in 3Q23, continuing to show volatility amid FX restrictions and unofficial flows. CA returned to deficit after a small surplus in 2Q. The deterioration was caused by a wider trade deficit which rose by US\$0.3bn due to a wider hydrocarbon deficit and higher non-oil imports. Services balance was below expectation with a surplus at US\$5.2bn from US\$7.4bn in 2Q. This was the reflection of some correction in non-tourism/transport receipts but mostly of much higher services imports. Tourism performed well with 4.2mn visitors in the period bringing receipts at c.US\$4.5bn - the strongest quarter ever on record in nominal terms. Primary income remained a big drag due to the large external interest payments and secondary remained depressed due to low official remittances. These were just US\$4.5bn in the quarter.

On the financial account front, net FDI were broadly stable at US\$2.2bn while the net FPI position recorded a \$0.5bn outflow. This was most likely related to bonds which were down by US\$335.7mn. As no Eurobond repayments were due in the period, this could be a sign of locals increasing their holdings of Egyptian Eurobonds. Other investments were flat with large MLT repayments (US\$1.8bn) offset by ST credit as well as a sharp increase in CBE liabilities. The increase in Central Bank liabilities are linked to UAE currency swap and China Development Bank US\$1bn loan during September and October. Interestingly, net errors and omissions were largely positive at US\$1.3bn reverting a trend of recent negative prints. Interpretation of this is always difficult, however this could be related to less unofficial imports as well as some extra cash originating from unrecorded tourism and remittances flows. We continue to expect a volatile picture until a proper FX market is restored.

## Implications of Red Sea disruption

Following a series of security threats off the southern Red Sea relating to attacks originating from Yemen, several major shipping companies as well as some oil companies decided to halt journeys through the Red Sea route. According to high frequency tracker data, number of vessels transiting through Suez in the full month of December was about 5% lower than in 2022. Looking at data since the first companies reported the decision of re-routing around Africa, the contraction has actually been around 20% and even a few %pts higher in volume terms. Cargo ships reported a larger decline than oil tankers. On a global prospective, insurance and freight costs have already increased with the key question around costs passing through to consumers.

For Egypt, Suez Canal revenues are an important source of FX accounting for almost 10% of CA receipts. Our projections for FY24 Suez revenues were for about US\$9bn. Since disruption started 150-200 vessels re-routed with figures likely to increase as ships already started their journey. Compared to the Ever Given incident, this time Egypt will suffer some real losses (at that time almost no vessels re-routed). We believe the impact should still be manageable for Egypt, although clearly not welcome as the country is already facing important challenges relating to its difficult external picture. The route around Africa adds 7-10 days to the journey, impacting delivery times and fuel costs; it is unlikely to be a new normal but carries some implications for global inflation.

## Dominican Republic: BCRD - On and off, but mostly on

## Steven Palacio

BCRD held the policy rate steady at 7%—we looked for -25bp. The Bank has been on an intermittent easing cycle since May, bringing the policy rate 150bp lower over that time span. While it is difficult to pin down the timing of the fits and starts of the rate cuts, to us it remains clear that the central bank is intent to drive the policy rate close to neutral this year. Thus, we continue to expect the policy rate at 5.25% by 3Q24. That said, BCRD did seem to take the foot off the gas slightly, sounding pleased about the evolution of the economy and the way that monetary policy has contributed to it. It stressed that in addition to policy rate cuts, liquidity measures have allowed credit growth to grow at a rapid 20%oya pace. Looking at sequential data, 4Q is set to have marked the third consecutive quarter in which activity grew at an above-potential 6.5% annualized rate or more. This has eased the central bank<sup>’</sup>s pressing concerns on growth after a dismal start to the year that still has the output gap negative but now closing.

The Bank remains comfortable (as do we) that inflation will remain well under control even under a scenario in which the policy rate is brought further lower. However, it suggested that the depth to which the policy rate can fall will be a function of prevailing global financing conditions, which they expect to become more lax ahead. To this point, it noted that the Fed is done hiking and that it signaled it will cut rates this year, while the ECB is expected to follow suit. Against this backdrop, we think that absent a major reversal in market pricing of DM rates, there should be no major hurdle for BCRD to guide the policy rate toward our neutral-like 5.25% forecast by 3Q24. It was a bit surprising that given the sizable loosening of global financial conditions, the central bank decided to keep rates on hold. This probably reflected the fact that DOP performed poorly in December and offers a glimpse into what will probably be the lens through which the central bank judges just how much additional easing it can do when balanced against external conditions, which are now seemingly more important than domestic conditions.

## Ecuador: A (so far flavourless) popular consultation proposal

## Lucila Barbeito

As promised during the campaign, President Noboa sent 11 questions to the Constitutional Court that he plans to submit for popular consultation. Amid rising crime and violence, the proposal is centered on security and justice issues, including a deeper role of the Armed Forces in combating organized crime, increased penalties, the audit of Judicial officers<sup>’</sup> assets, and the strengthening of migration controls, among others. Out of the 11 questions, there is only one that is unrelated to security or justice issues, which seeks the population approval to reopen casinos and similar businesses after these were closed following a 2011 consultation. The President argues that such reopening could contribute to increasing tourism inflows, the creation of new jobs and additional tax collection for about US\$300mn (0.2% of GDP). That said, the popular consultation proposal is absent of any questions related to specific labor reforms or other economic-related topics (e.g. subsidies focalization). The President indicated in an interview yesterday that he is open to dialogue and to the incorporation of new questions to the popular consultation upon negotiation with other parties.

According to constitutional experts, the main setback of the consultation proposal is that the majority of the issues included do not seek constitutional or legal reforms. The Law of Jurisdictional Guarantees and Constitutional Control indicates that popular consultations must have a certain legal effect so as to avoid an irresponsible mobilization of the consulted citizens and spending of public resources. Yet, the government argues that the questions are focused on the placement on the legislative agenda of issues relevant to citizens, so they would have a potential to produce legal effects. Against this backdrop, the Constitutional Court (CC) will now perform a constitutional control on the questions. According to Art.105 of the Organic Law of Jurisdictional Guarantees and Constitutional Control, the CC has to resolve within 20 days of initiated the respective control. Once concluded, this process and upon CC green light, according to the Ecuadorian Constitution (Art. 106), the National Electoral Council (CNE) will have 15 days to make the consultation call, which should take place in the next 60 days (likely in March).

## Panama Canal: Filling under

## Steven Palacio

Panama has been hit by several shocks over the past several quarters. We<sup>’</sup>ve noted before our expectation for the country to lose its investment grade rating status following the unilateral decision to make void the concession to a foreign firm of one of the largest copper mines globally.

Adding insult to injury, trouble at the Panama Canal has remained on the backburner. The Canal—a key trade enclave—usually accommodates 36 vessel-transits daily (10 Neopanamax and 26 Panamax) but had been forced to reduce that number to 22 as of December (6 Neopanamax and 16 Panamax) due to the abnormally low level of water reservoirs in the artificial lakes supplying the Canal. The situation remains dire, with the water level at lake Gatun at 81 feet, seven feet below its maximum operating level. This is not only creating trouble for Panama from a fiscal perspective— despite increased capacity tariffs, the Canal authority expects a US\$200 million shortfall in revenues this fiscal year (equivalent to 10% of its transfers to the government)—but also risks exacerbating resurfacing supply constraints that could push up global trade costs, particularly in the face of constraints at other major maritime trade routes. Ships being diverted from the Canal are opting for routes that imply at least doubling the shipping time in key routes between the US East Coast and Asia, implying rising costs. Alternatively, firms are reportedly opting to pay millions of dollars in auctions to skip the queues at the Canal, yielding a similar upward push to costs.

That said, the situation has improved on the margin, with the Canal Authority announcing that rainfall had been better than expected recently, allowing it to increase traffic by two vessels to 24. This, however, is still one-third below the Canal<sup>’</sup>s usual capacity, and guidance suggest that important limitations will persist going forward.
