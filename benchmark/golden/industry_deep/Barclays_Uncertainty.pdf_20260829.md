## Equity Research

While current day supply chain and port operations are largely "back-to-normal," with capacity to chase goods and reliable deliveries inbound to the U.S., the recent spike in spot rates suggests the global supply chain continues to be impacted by the Red Sea disruption and its knock-on efects. There is the potential for strain on the system as we enter the retail "high season." Below, we highlight key takeaways:

• Container imports are on pace to increase double digits this year, with recent data suggesting further acceleration could be in store.

• Red Sea disruption

• Supply chain normalized...for now

• Potential restocking cycle could lead to margin pressure in 2025

• No inventory build ahead of potential tarifs

## FICC Research

## US High Yield Retail / Consumer

The consensus from our tour of the Port of Long Beach was that container pricing would find a near-term average of \$3,500-4,000 per 40ft container, despite spot rates being closer to \$5,100, as shippers add lines and capacity. In addition, we expected volumes to increase at a midsingle-digit rate this year, suggesting a more orderly seasonal inventory build than last year. We are incrementally less concerned regarding container pricing going forward, although company from corporate management teams have started to highlight it as a potential pressure point.

## US Investment Grade Retail / Consumer

We are cautiously optimistic on IG retail fundamentals heading into the back half of the year. Color from our supply chain tour suggests that while import volumes are up in the mid-teens YTD (versus a 6-7% growth forecast for the full year), absent issues related to the Red Sea or potential labor concerns at the east coast ports, this figure would be close to flat. While there is some restocking activity supporting this, in terms of the categories, the top shipments are in furniture, apparel, consumer electronics and toys, which is consistent with pre-pandemic trends. This suggests a very “back to normal” feel on the supply chain side, and with most issuers having made strides on the inventory front, it points to execution as the likely diferentiator for performance.

## Earnings - Mixed Q2, high hopes for H2

Emmanuel Cau, CFA, Barclays, UK | Matthew Joyce, CFA, BBI, Paris | Magesh Kumar Chandrasekaran, CFA, Barclays, UK | Emmanuel Makonga, Barclays, UK | Arihanth Bohra Jain, Barclays, UK | Anshul Gupta, Barclays, UK | Tejas Shah, Barclays, UK

Excerpted from European Equity Strategy: Earnings - Mixed Q2, high hopes for H2, published on July 17, 2024

Q2 earnings face a not-so-low hurdle, but should be okay. IBES consensus is for 1% EPS growth in Europe and +10% in US. Global GDP growth near trend so far this year is consistent with positive EPS growth. However, with estimates seeing milder downgrades than usual into reporting season, the bar for positive surprises is arguably higher. This is especially given activity data was slowing over the quarter, and China still looks weak. In Europe margins are expected to contract, led by Cyclicals, whereas they are expected to expand in the US, led by Tech. As Europe FY24 EPS growth is back end loaded into H2, aided by easy comps vs. H2'23, guidance will likely be more important than the Q2 numbers for stock reaction. Expect increased dispersion, and travel & arrive in some cases.

Growth soft patch vs. stimulus hope poses questions for H2 earnings. Positive earnings revisions during H1 were driven by better-than-expected macro and solid Q1 earnings beats, which in turn provided fundamental support to the equity rally. But recent softening in economic surprises could challenge the positive earnings expectations for H2. In fact, we have seen EPS revisions turning negative again in most regions most recently, increasing the importance of company guidance. However, with the rate-cutting cycle getting under way in H2, and burgeoning expectations of a reflationary political scene in the US under a potential Trump administration, we believe investors may look through mixed Q2 earnings, and play the softlanding narrative. But this is likely to help the US more than Europe, in our view. One country seeing upgrades to economic data is the UK – stay OW.

EU Cyclicals missing out on the reflation trade. In contrast to the US, EU Cyclicals' outperformance has lost steam recently. Their relative valuations vs. Defensives remain elevated, and we continue to see EU Cyclicals looking vulnerable to stalling activity momentum and a turn in EPS revisions, while lower rates should benefit bond proxies – we keep a preference for Banks and domestic consumer plays. Semis, Pharma, Comm/Prof Svs and Banks have had some fundamental support with EPS improving, while Software, Energy and Luxury have recorded negative EPS changes. Our sector analysts are most positive on earnings for Utilities, Energy Services, Banks, Aero & Def, Business Services, Pharma and Medtech & Services. Analysts from Mining, Chemicals and Ingredients, Autos and Cap Goods are more cautious. See sector previews from our analysts later in this report.

## H2 24 Market Outlook

Shinichiro Kadota, BSJL, Japan | Joseph Abate, BCI, US | Rohan Khanna, Barclays, UK

Excerpted from Xccy and Money Markets: H2 24 Market Outlook, published on July 18, 2024

Ongoing liquidity drains across G3 markets are unlikely to exert much stress in funding markets and xccy basis with focus on the US elections, the ECB's new operational framework and the BoJ's lagged policy normalization.

USD funding markets: Fundamental liquidity conditions should remain ample through yearend. Unsecured dollar funding rates could come under pressure from reduced demand for CP and CDs following new money fund reform. We think investor uncertainty ahead of the November elections could shift money into bills and gov-only funds. These efects were less obvious in 2016 and 2020 as the opportunity cost of shifting balances was high with short rates near 0%.

EUR funding markets: The ECB formalised its new operational framework in March, formally adopting a floor system and narrowing the MRO-DFR corridor to 15bp. Following this cheapening of liquidity facilities and the continued liquidity drain, it needs to be seen if banks will increase their recourse to ECB’s refinancing operations. We continue to see only a glacial decline in ESTR-DFR spread.

JPY funding markets: JPY money market rates shifting up to a new range after NIRP exit and should rise further with the upcoming BoJ hike. Term rates are likely to remain slightly rich vs OIS due to negative xccy basis and favorable supply-demand dynamics in the short tenor. Money market liquidity has dropped since NIRP exit due to an unwind of arbitrage flows under the tiered reserve system.

Xccy basis markets: Short-tenor xccy basis should remain under gradual tightening pressures with abundant USD supply while funding demand is diferentiating factor between major currencies. Longer-tenor xccy basis should also continue to tighten.

Rising Unwind Risk

Venu Krishna, CFA, BCI, US | Japinder Chawla, CFA, BCI, US | Rex Feng, BCI, US | Madhuresh Rai, CFA, BCI, US | Young Chen, BCI, US

Excerpted from Long & Short of It: Rising Unwind Risk, published on July 16, 2024

Concentrated equity positioning increases unwind risk; systematic positioning is susceptible to outflows; investors appear bullish amid slowing options activity; leveraged ETPs make a comeback; equities still seeing retail inflows but macro worries divert some to bonds/cash.

Concentrated equity positioning increases unwind risk. We see three factors underpinning heightened unwind risk: 1) US equity gains remain extremely narrow and Tech-centric, and the chase for upside has pushed Tech allocations among mutual funds to very high levels; 2) Big Tech valuations are once again increasing, a stark reversal from earlier in 1H when it was mostly better earnings that were driving returns, and; 3) there is more retail money in US equities due to increasing US allocation within global funds. In a sign that the US equity rally may be growing long in the tooth, global macro hedge funds cut their long equity exposure last month amid signs of macro weakness.

Systematic positioning remains elevated, and susceptible to outflows: Strong uptrend in US equities, combined with depressed volatility, led to increased allocation to US equities from Vol Control, CTAs and Risk Parity. Notably, Vol Control equity allocation ramped up from already elevated levels, and now sits above 100%, with limited room to add unless volatility goes back to near extreme lows à la 2017/19. CTAs have trimmed EU equities but their allocation to SPX & NDX remains high. However, they are near neutral on small caps (RTY), and are likely to be buyers under most scenarios, following last week's weak CPI. Overall, systematic positioning seems susceptible to outflows even at the hint of market jitters (pick up in vol), which seems plausible given upcoming FOMC, earnings, elections, and fading seasonality (although vol has historically tended to be seasonally low in July and has only tended to pick up in Aug).

Investors appear bullish amid slowing options activity, while dealers stay long gamma: Mirroring the rise and subsequent fall in put-to-call OI ratio, equity skew steepened throughout the second half of June post CPI release, before marginally flattening in July. Amid declining options activity, investor bullishness, as measured by the call buy-to-open vs call sell-to-open ratio, is near YTD highs. Lastly, intraday price action suggests that dealers by and large remain long gamma.

Focus: Leveraged ETPs make a comeback, and so do concerns around them: Leveraged ETPs (LETPs) have regained interest with their AUM climbing \~70% since the Oct-23 lows in the S&P. Importantly, they have drawn considerable attention in the past as a source of additional market volatility, given the associated rebalancing flows required to maintain their daily leverage (LETP gamma). With them reaching ATH, we analyze key drivers across a welldiversified universe of 281 LETPs. Interestingly, we find that rising AUM in tandem with rallying markets was the main contributor. However, this was not due to flows. Indeed, data point to outflows YTD, the first such instance since 2H20.

Retail money keeps flowing into equities, but bonds and cash see an uptick. Retail flows to equities last month were led by EM, ahead of China's Third Plenum. Among DM equities, US and global developed market exposure were preferred, while Europe and APAC continued to see outflows. Early signs of cooling in the US labor market led retail investors to seek the safety of US Gov't bonds and IG credit last month, compared to muted HY flows. Money market funds also saw an uptick in inflows.

Tech overweight led by semis and hardware. We find that a substantial portion of the YTD increase in Tech allocation is attributable to Semis, in particular NVDA, which are heavy consensus overweights. Long-only funds are now overweight AAPL and the broader Tech Hardware sector, while having significantly reduced their exposure to Software & Services.

## US rotates, Europe struggles

Emmanuel Cau, CFA, Barclays, UK | Matthew Joyce, CFA, BBI, Paris | Magesh Kumar Chandrasekaran, CFA, Barclays, UK | Emmanuel Makonga, Barclays, UK | Arihanth Bohra Jain, Barclays, UK

Excerpted from Equity Market Review: US rotates, Europe struggles, published on July 19, 2024

Expectations of Fed cuts and Trump win have spurred a healthy broadening, but potentially painful unwind in the US. Europe is being left out of the reflation trade, amid concerns about tarifs, weaker China and France gridlock. These are challenges indeed, yet not all is going wrong for the region, and UK is a bright spot.

US rotation brings unwind risk, and hasn't benefited Europe. The main take-away from our US marketing trip last week was that the investors we met were not positioned for the sharp rotation that we saw after the soft CPI print (see US investor feedback: rotation is the pain trade, 12 Jul). Not only did the improved chances of Fed cuts provide a lift to soft landing proxies, like small caps, real estate and Financials, but rising expectations of a potential Trump election win boosted reflation expectations, which led to a broader rebound in US Cyclicals, along with the yield curve steepening. However, Europe has missed out on the reflation trade, which resulted in an even wider performance gap with the US. We closed our tactical OW on Europe last month, mainly due to the risks around French elections (French elections – Fasten your seatbelt, 12 June), and reduced further our sector beta exposure since (On edge, 3 Jul), as we struggle to see Europe competing with the goldilocks narrative of the US. And as we discussed in our earnings preview this week, softening growth momentum raises downside risk to FY earnings expectations, which are back end-loaded into H2 (Earnings – Mixed Q2, high hopes for H2, 17 Jul). But while Europe faces challenge, most recent price action shows that US equities are not immune to downside either. Concentrated positioning and high systematic exposure are increasing unwind risk, as our US strategists discuss in Long & Short of It: Rising Unwind Risk, 16 Jul.

Not all is going wrong for Europe, though. While we are no longer OW Europe, we are happy to hold it at MW, and see some positives to counterbalance the negatives discussed above. Consensus on the region is far less bullish now and positioning has been cut. Fundamentally, we note that household credit conditions across the Eurozone are improving, as per the latest ECB Bank Lending Survey. This helps our positive view on the EU consumer (see Green shoots for the European consumer, 19 Jun), as well as our continued constructive view on Banks (see What next for Banks if rates go down?, 22 May). We acknowledge, however, that the nascent credit recovery is fragile and uneven, given corporate loan demand remains weak, while lending standards continue to tighten, as our economists note (ECB Bank Lending Survey -Corporates lagging, households easing, 16 Jul). Another potential tailwind for Europe, albeit more hypothetical and longer term, would be a truce in the Russia-Ukraine conflict. ## Theorising Trumponomics

Christian Keller, Barclays, UK | Mark Cus Babic, Barclays, UK | Akash Utsav, Barclays, UK

Excerpted from Global Economics Weekly, published on July 19, 2024

With rising odds of a Trump victory, markets are becoming increasingly interested in the macro implications of the policy mix he is proposing. Most point to a US economy with high nominal growth, elevated interest rates, and a strong dollar. Next week, eyes will be on PCE and Q2 GDP in the US and euro area PMIs.

Theorising Trumponomics

Towards higher growth, inflation and interest rates and a strong dollar ...

After last weekend's failed assassination attempt on former President Trump and amid continuing pressure from Democrats on President Biden to drop his candidacy, the odds of a Trump victory seem on the rise. Betting markets now put the probability at about 65%, significantly higher than just a few weeks ago. What is more, the odds that the Republicans also win both the Senate and the House have increased as well in recent weeks. The Presidency combined with a Republican Congress would give Trump — who was oficially confirmed as the Republican nominee this week — significant power to pursue policies without much compromise. In Ohio Senator JD Vance, Trump also chose a running mate with strong policy views himself (eg, the war in Ukraine, dollar strength, tarifs).<sup>2</sup>

No wonder the market is increasingly obsessing about the implications of a potentially unconstrained Trump-Vance policy package, for not only the US, but also the global economy. To position for any 'Trump trade', one has to first understand 'Trumponomics'. The latter center on five areas: trade/tarifs, taxes, migration, regulation, and geopolitics.

We provide a brief outline of the key policy proposals that have transpired thus far and an indication of their macro implications. Often, their immediate, ie, 'first-round', efect could be partly ofset by counter-measures (eg, retaliatory tarifs), exchange rate adjustments and substitution efects, which themselves depend on demand and supply elasticities not fully known in advance. However, it seems fair to say that a combination of higher trade tarifs and less migration are in principle negative supply shocks with inflationary consequences. At the same time, lower taxes without equivalent expenditure reductions would mainly stimulate demand (although with also potentially some positive supply efects).

This would point to a US economy with strong (real) growth but also higher inflation pressures (ie, fast nominal GDP expansion), with higher nominal interest rates and a strong US dollar as a consequence. Deregulation could provide an ofset to the inflationary pressures, to the extent that it translates into a positive supply shock and increased productivity, though such efects traditionally take time.

Paradoxically, Trump and Vance have also highlighted their dislike of a strong dollar, which they believe is detrimental to US manufacturing. However, it remains unclear how the administration could actually achieve a weaker dollar when the rest of their policy package is consistent with a strong currency.<sup>3</sup>

## Higher tarifs

Trump has been vocal about his perception of unfair global trade, with a keen focus on countries with which the US runs large bilateral trade deficits (such as China and the EU). He and his team, centred on former US Trade Representative Robert Lighthizer, have suggested he would implement a 10% tarif on all imports into the US and a 60% tarif on Chinese imports.

If implemented, this would raise the US average tarif to the highest since the 1950s, thus departing sharply from the post-WWII global trading regime. That said, we still think outcomes where the ultimately implemented tarifs are lower are more likely, as our Washington Research analyst notes that the former president sees himself as a deal maker and, as such, tarifs are his negotiating leverage.

## Lower taxes

In his interview with Bloomberg published this week,<sup>4</sup> Trump described his economic doctrine as 'low interest rates and taxes, low taxes'. Thus, it is highly likely that another Trump administration would extend his 2017 tax cuts. He has also pledged to reduce the corporate income tax further, from 21% to 20%, and in this week's interview, he floated the idea of lowering it to 15%, although admitted that 'that'd be hard'.

## Less migration

Trump has promised to reduce immigration, which reached a record high in 2023. Steady immigration flows have been a key source of US exceptionalism over the post-pandemic period, providing a strong tailwind for aggregate supply that has helped sustain disinflation amid a robust, consumer-fueled expansion. In our view, policies to stem the flow of asylum seekers would squeeze labor supply and growth. For instance, our calculations suggest a return to 2017-18 policies, enacted during the Trump administration, would eventually trim monthly payrolls gains by about 125k/m in 2025.

## Less regulation

Finally, the Trump administration would likely take a significantly diferent approach to regulation than President Biden, particularly as it relates to energy and environmental protections. The efects of this could be complicated. For example, expanding oil production might be a positive supply shock, but slower EV adoption would increase oil demand. In any case, our oil analyst expects a limited efect on oil market fundamentals in the near term. Similarly, cutting red tape could make it easier to do business, but repealing some or all of the Inflation Reduction Act could weigh on investment and employment.

## Re-aligned geopolitics

Even though under a 'transactional' approach, the ultimate foreign policy outcomes may be dificult to predict, there are potentially significant changes from current policies, which markets cannot ignore. First, Trump and Vance are outspoken about ending the war in Ukraine by withdrawing support for Ukraine and, thus, forcing an agreement with Russia. Second, while keeping strong anti-China views, Trump has raised doubts about the US commitment to defend Taiwan. Third, in the Middle East, Trump would likely refocus on strong relations with Saudi Arabia and Israel, combined with strong opposition against Iran. The many uncertainties aside, one general theme that seems certain is that under a Trump presidency, the US will expect its allies, be it Europe or Taiwan, to 'pay' for or rely less on the protection the US provides. The implication is likely upside pressure on defence spending across most regions.

## China's Third Plenum brings little change

After this week's hold, we also still expect the ECB to deliver a cut again in September. While the Governing Council (GC) did not pre-commit to this in either the monetary policy statement or the press conference, the president used language similar to that which was employed in April to prepare the market for a rate cut in June. In particular, she indicated that if the data were to evolve in line with current expectations, that would reinforce the GC's confidence in the disinflationary path. Furthermore, she expressed substantial conviction that wage growth and, thus, domestic price pressures, would decelerate in 2025 and 2026.

In the face of this, we retain our call for two more 25bp reductions, in September and December. Skipping one of those cuts is a risk, as indicated by the post-meeting reports citing unnamed ECB sources, but so is the possibility of an October cut should growth remain lacklustre (compared with the ECB's projection of 0.4% q/q in Q2-Q4 24) and underlying inflation indicators continue to edge lower, as they did in this week's June final HICP release. Next week's July flash PMIs will bring more signals on the former, and we expect a slight easing in the composite reading.

## Data to give the BoE just enough comfort to cut in August

Meanwhile, the Bank of England's move in August is a closer call, especially in light of this week's slight upside surprise in June inflation, driven by services. However, the surprise was very narrow, concentrated in the hotels sector, which could be a one-of efect of Taylor Swift's Eras tour, and the Bank has pivoted to put more weight on indicators beyond services inflation. In addition, the labour market report showed a continued softening in conditions as wages eased, including a 0.3pp drop in regular private sector average weekly earnings, to 5.6% 3m/y. There were also signs of continued loosening on the quantities side, as the higher-frequency data pointed to a possible unemployment rate uptick next month and the vacancies-tounemployed ratio fell further.

At the previous Bank of England meeting in June, seven MPC members voted to hold rates, while two voted for a cut. However, among those supporting no change in rates, there were some for whom the decision was already finely balanced. We believe that the overall data flow since the June meeting, including this week's, will give enough comfort to those members to vote for a cut in August.

## Virtuous price-wage cycle supports a BoJ hike in July

Finally, in Japan, June core and BoJ core inflation edged up this week as subsidies to contain energy prices were reduced and services inflation continued to climb. That said, the increase in the former was a little smaller than expected by the consensus. Accounting for these data, we maintain our core CPI inflation forecasts at 2.6% y/y for FY24 and 2.0% y/y for FY25. While policy on energy subsidies might introduce some volatility in the monthly inflation profile, we continue to see underlying inflation ultimately converging to the 2% price stability target, thus proving more persistent than in the past.

In addition to this week's inflation, we think the overall data flow and other information since the June BoJ meeting largely support the scenario of a virtuous cycle between prices and wages that is a prerequisite for another hike. In addition, we continue to see upside risks to the inflation outlook due to JPY weakness and think that if the BoJ does not use 'real ammunition' against it (ie, a hike), it would face the risk of losing the fruits of the recent FX interventions. Moreover, political developments could close the BoJ's window to act after July, potentially postponing any move all the way to December. For all these reasons, we still expect the BoJ to hike at the July meeting in two weeks' time.
