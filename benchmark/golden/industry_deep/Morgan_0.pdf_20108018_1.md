Mayday! Mayday! Dust off your soft landing vs. hard landing debate materials, and remind yourself about residual seasonality in US inflation data. ## Residual Seasonality Fooled You Once

We recently explained that the year-to-date bond market sell-off was driven by the higher inflation prints to start the year. But did the BLS and BEA properly properly adjust those prints for the 1Q seasonal uptick in inflation? We don't think so. Our economists discuss the issue in US Inflation: After the Upswing.

In Exhibit 1 and Exhibit 2 , we display the results of a technique employed by the Federal Reserve Board in Residual Seasonality in Core Consumer Price Inflation: An Update.

• During the decade between the Great Financial Crisis and the Great Covid Recession, core PCE prices rose faster than the yearly average in 1H and fell faster in 2H ( Exhibit 1 ).

• The pattern since the pandemic affected period (2020-2021) seems more extreme than the pre-pandemic period ( Exhibit 2 ).

Exhibit 1: Average annualized seasonally-adjusted quarterly core PCE inflation vs. 4-quarter average: 2010-2019
![](images/3296260b330dae5ad61808a2a4091cbea62d74461d35965762827afadc33fe42.jpg)
Source: Morgan Stanley Research, Federal Reserve, BEA

Exhibit 2: Average annualized seasonally-adjusted quarterly core PCE inflation vs. 4-quarter average: 2022-2023
![](images/2cad0296f460780b10ff446fdbab632e5df5671199ee9a85ca3b3dfd951940de.jpg)
According to the Federal Reserve Board staff:

"The tendency for a time series to manifest a predictable seasonal pattern despite being seasonally adjusted is often referred to as 'residual seasonality'."

Residual seasonality has plagued core PCE inflation data for many years. Our economists think the problem became worse in the post-pandemic years. Unfortunately, the existence of residual seasonality in the past does not guarantee its presence in the future — which makes it difficult to count on.

At the same time, it appears to exist in more than just consumer inflation series. Exhibit 3 and Exhibit 4 show that residual seasonality appears to exist in measures of employment costs as well. And similar to core PCE prices, the issue got worse after the pandemic fully passed in 2021.

Exhibit 3: Average annualized seasonally-adjusted employment cost index (ECI) wage inflation for civilian workers vs. 4-quarter average: 2010-2019

Source: Morgan Stanley Research, Federal Reserve, BLS

Exhibit 4: Average annualized seasonally-adjusted employment cost index (ECI) wage inflation for civilian workers vs. 4-quarter average: 2022-2023
![](images/05279af227444072756ab9489540aafe0b874bdaa940697e5fb61169681e3d00.jpg)

In addition, Exhibit 5 and Exhibit 6 show the same residual seasonality in unit labor costs. That also worsened since the pandemic passed. When so many price metrics display residual seasonality and show a similar pattern of it strengthening, investors would be forgiven for gaining confidence that the pattern will continue in the coming year.

Exhibit 5: Average annualized seasonally-adjusted unit labor costs (ULC) vs. 4-quarter average: 2010- 2019
![](images/ef36862deb0776f2df7fbb924f865d01d30af9b689376da2e9ac25f32bed8dc2.jpg)

Our economists incorporated this residual seasonality effect into their forecast for core PCE inflation. And the results, should they prove accurate, should open investor eyes into year-end. They forecast a rapid deceleration in both 3- and 6-month annualized rates of core PCE inflation (see Exhibit 7 and Exhibit 8 ).

• The 3-month annualized rate should fall to 1.81% for the December 2024 print (data released in January 2025), and

• The 6-month annualized rate should fall to 1.96% for the December 2024 print (data released in January 2025).

We think this type of inflation data should cause US Treasury yields to fall.

• The decline would involve the market pricing in the number of rate cuts our economists project from the Fed this year (3 x 25bp cuts vs. the market pricing only 2).

• It would also involve the market pricing in a much more substantial easing cycle in 2025 than the market, which currently prices in just over 75bp of cuts.

Of course, bond market yields may adjust higher in early 2025, if inflation picks back up in 1Q25, thanks in part to upside from residual seasonality. At this point, however, our economists don't forecast a repeat of 2023 (see Exhibit 7 again).

## Did the MoF in Japan Trade the USD Top?

Did the Ministry of Finance (MoF) in Japan trade the top in the USD? It certainly did in October 2022, when the MoF intervened as USD/JPY broke through 150. Of course, the MoF intervention did not cause the broad USD to correct lower over the subsequent 3m. But there are some parallels with the current FX cycle that are worth highlighting.

From late October 2022 onwards, the USD entered a very sharp 3m correction. This was not driven by the MoF in Japan, but instead by (1) Some softer US inflation data (with headline and core CPI/PCE generally surprisingly to the downside over the following 2- 3m), (2) Growing evidence that China would lift the COVID-19 restrictions on its economy, and (3) A technical set-up that was conducive to a USD rally, considering investor positioning was long USD following a strong bull market for the currency and a strong rise in UST yields.

Today, there is a real and expected parallel but also a difference:

Real parallel: Activity data in both China and the eurozone have been stronger than expected, off of a base of very low expectations. Both our China and eurozone economists have upgraded their growth numbers for 2024 on the back of the stronger-than-expected start to the year.

See here for China and here for the eurozone. This is a real parallel with the USD peak in October 2022 as we were then on the cusp of a strong (but ultimately short-lived) rebound in RoW growth that led to USD weakness.

Expected parallel: Our US economists are expecting to see a significant drop in the monthly rate of inflation over the coming 3 months vs the resurgence in price pressures seen over 1Q. Our economists see core PCE inflation at 0.22% m/m in April and decelerating every month onwards to 0.15% m/m by end-24, versus a monthly average of 0.36% m/m in 1Q. This would likely be a dovish surprise for market and, if realised, would push the USD lower, all else being equal.

Difference: One small difference is the technical set-up. The market is only very moderately long USD. In late October 2022, our positioning tracker for DXY and the broad USD was showing a long USD position of around +50, whereas as of 2nd May, the long USD positioning was +10-20 for DXY and the broad USD, i.e., closer to neutral ( Exhibit 17 ).

Of course, given the right catalysts, the market can move to a short USD position and the dollar can have a meaningful sell off. Yet, we think the risk/reward in terms of positioning is not as compelling as it was in October 2022.

# Interest Rate Strategy

## United States

Residual seasonality in inflation sets up an attractive buying opportunity: As we flagged last week, residual seasonality has been creating a perception of inflation strength, whereas the inflation optics will likely give way to a much cooler inflation profile. In a joint note with our US economics team, we explore the decelerating inflation path ahead, the residual seasonality, and how it has introduced a seasonality in US rates that is likely to reverse. We suggest moving into long 3m10y receivers.

Duration still better than steepeners; switch to long 3m10y receivers: We think some degree of bull-steepening last week makes sense, just that bullish duration remains more appealing than steepeners because we do see a risk of bullflattening – see The Problem with Steepeners. Keeping with these themes, we like receivers in the long end, but we switch long 3m30y receivers into 3m10y receivers given our view that 10s is a better tenor than 30s to represent a change in the market's views on r\*.

High bar for labor market, plus unexpected weakening? Higher labor supply has raised the bar for employment, as seen via the rising Bloomberg median estimate for headline payrolls in the last two months, which helped April's 175k become a downside surprise. The same 175k print would have qualified as an upside surprise in August-September 2023, when the bar was seen lower. In addition, the latest services employment numbers in the ISM and S&P Global surveys show sharp drops for April, which would likely concern investors about the labor markets.

## Euro area

Price action in duration this week saw some relief, despite the front-end remaining relatively anchored. All this happened despite some softer lending data in the US, coupled with a relatively dovish FOMC meeting and with a continuation in the downtick in inflation prints in Europe.

We tend to think that there are limits to the persistence of directionality vs US rates. Thus far, the reflationary theme that has affected market pricing assumes some kind of synchronicity in the next global easing cycle, which might diverge as cuts get delivered. Ultimately, the divergence in economic fundamentals will need to be fed into market pricing at some point. Historically, this reluctancy tends to be normal when markets are close to a turn in the cycle.

Valuation wise, the 10y Bund fair value for May stands at \~2.23%, broadly unchanged vs its June reading. It continues to trade around \~30bp cheap, with the discrepancy adjusting a bit as duration drifted below 2.60% this week, softening mildly the dislocation in std. deviation terms. At the margin the negative net issuance dynamics present in May should help to moderate this cheapening, in our opinion.

## United Kingdom

Next week focus will turn to the May MPC, with the new set of macroeconomics projections, voting split and messaging the main factors to watch. We think that risks are skewed towards a dovish market reaction given soft projections, and a possible change in the vote split. However, the messaging poses a risk to this view.

The upcoming MPC could then offer the opportunity for investors to add long positions in UK duration, and we think that front-end rates could re-invert from fairly steep levels as the market took out curve inversion sharply over the past quarter.

We thus find current valuations particularly compelling to position for front-end rates to re-invert as the market likely underestimates the amount of easing that the BoE will eventually deliver. With these considerations, we enter into a SFIU4- U5 flattener position.

Japan We discuss the detailed analysis listed on the BoJ’s quarterly Outlook for Economic Activity and Prices update. The BoJ seems to be looking for evidence of: (1) this spring’s strong wage hike momentum spilling over to smaller businesses; (2) a recovery in consumer spending as wages rise; (3) pass-through of higher labor costs to output prices, particularly in the services sector; and (4) a continuing uptrend in inflation expectation to assess the underlying inflation trend. This analysis basically points to further improvements for each of these four factors, but we believe the BoJ want to take some time to confirm that things are, indeed, tracking as expected before hiking its policy rate further.

With short-term rates expectations likely to remain anchored until we see some progress in the above data, price action in the JGB market will probably continue to be driven mostly by: (1) movements in UST yields; and (2) supply/demand-related fluctuations in the term premium. We maintain long 5y JGB ASW and long 5y JGB vs pay 7y OIS (DV01 1.75 vs 1) given the BoJ's cautious approach with regards to QT. We also maintain long 20y JGB on 10s20s30s fly. We still see the supply/demand dynamics on the $3 0 \mathsf { y } +$ sector remaining weak, while tailwinds from lower global yields post strong NFP could help the cheap 20y sector.

## Switch 3m30y receivers to 3m10y

With a flurry of economic data and central bank meetings, the rates markets had a lot to digest. With the data on balance being weaker, and a dovish interpretation of Fed-speak, the market eventually rallied over the week, with a bull-steepening trend.

We think some degree of bull-steepening makes sense, just that bullish duration remains more appealing than steepeners because we do see a risk of bull-flattening. As we highlighted and explained in our report, The Problem with Steepeners, markets may reconsider the view of a higher neutral rate, while demand for long-end duration returns amid an "insurance cuts" scenario puts the bull-flattening scenario in play.

We switch long 3m30y receivers into 3m10y receivers. Being long on 10s over 30s maintains our view that duration plays are better than curve plays, and also our view that bull-flattening risks are underappreciated, which favor long-end and the belly over the very short end. We prefer switching to 10s over 30s given that 10s is a better tenor to represent a change in the market's views on r\* than 30s. We prefer receivers over outright duration given that implied volatility is at locally cheap levels.

## Higher labor supply = higher chance of downside surprises?

Labor supply has been a key theme that, in our opinion, has been missed by most investors in the last year or so; now, it is getting a lot of attention – as it should. It has been a vital aspect that has allowed for strong optics for the labor market, and, side by side with cooling inflation, has helped with the possibility of a soft-landing outcome.

Now that we have explored rising labor supply, it raises the bar for economic data – especially headline payrolls ( Exhibit 20 ) as well as GDP growth.

Let's look at today's payrolls, as an example. For the first time in 18 months, the median payroll expectation was \~250k, and the median expectation has been inching up since the CBO immigration numbers were released in February. Today's 175k print would have qualified as an upside surprise in August-September 2023. Our economists see payroll breakeven near 265k for 2024.

This means that, going forward, markets will need to deal with a higher bar for upside surprises for headline payrolls. An additional issue with higher labor supply will be that, as rising labor supply gets better captured in the household survey, the unemployment rate could drift up – which is why our economists see the unemployment rate rising to 4.2% by the end of 2024.

All of these risks depend on markets better understanding the labor supply and its impact. But what is there to see is an organic slowdown in the labor market – the "unexpected weakening" that Powell referred to at the May FOMC meeting (more on this in the FOMC reaction below). Some of the recent data such as ISM employment and the recent S&P employment PMI ( Exhibit 21 ) should bring the same concern into the market, too.

## The residual seasonality risk in US rates

Inflation has been pivotal to the rise in yields in the last three months. In fact, of the last 80bp rise in yields since the start of 2024, nearly 100% has come from the three days around the CPI prints (see Exhibit 22 ). Even the core PCE prints elicited a rise in yields last week, when the 1Q24 number came in higher than consensus. US rates markets have been hypersensitive to upside inflation surprises.

What is notable, in our view, is that markets were not as sensitive to downside surprises in 2H23. Thus, while markets did not react to cooling inflation (see Exhibit 23 ), investors looked at the cooling data and took that for granted, which might explain the current hypersensitive reaction in yields to the inflation surprises. This suggests that Treasury yields might be more vulnerable to cooling inflation than at any time in the last year.

As it turns out, our economists see deceleration ahead, with support from cooling rents, continued cooling in goods, and a reversal of residual seasonality. We think that Treasury yields could fall meaningfully if this forecast materializes.

While many investors expect cooling rents and cool goods prices, we think that the residual seasonality dynamic is the least appreciated among investors.

In the recent sharp reaction to higher inflation prints, the overwhelming narrative adopted by investors has been that inflation is proving "sticky". In essence, markets have extrapolated the recent uptick in inflation strength to the future. But residual seasonality suggests that 1Q strength may remain confined to 1Q, and subsequent inflation readings have a downward slope.

As our US economists have shown, 1Q numbers for core PCE have been stronger than average inflation for the year, with this year's numbers getting a boost of 0.6pp on the SAAR level. This means instead of \~3.7% SAAR, the 1Q number could have been 3.1% SAAR.

And this has been a known phenomenon – and there have been attempts to address this by the Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) in the pre-pandemic years, with the third and last phase of this having been implemented in mid-2018. Removing the 2019 numbers, and the 2020/21 numbers given the pandemic distortions, nine of the last 12 years have seen this residual seasonality trend.

Most importantly, given that many investors prefer looking at recent trends, it is notable that this residual seasonality trend was very visible in 2023 (see Exhibit 24 ). Q/Q Core PCE in 1Q23 was \~5.0% annualized, versus merely \~2.0% annualized in 4Q23. That is some drop in inflation. Also, 1Q24 at \~3.7% is significantly lower than 1Q23 at 5%.

And this residual seasonality in inflation numbers has created some seasonality in the Treasury market itself. Treasuries have been seeing upside surprises in February (when January core CPI is released). Given that nine of the last ten years have seen upside core CPI surprises, Treasury yields have risen in February. And given that 1Q core PCE comes out in April, even April has seen an uptick in seasonal rise in yields.

In the last five years, February and April have a 99% and 93% statistically significant seasonality for higher yields, respectively (see Exhibit 27 ), which lines up well with residual upside seasonality in inflation data for 1Q – released in February through April.

Assuming that there will a downward slope driven by residual seasonality, plus our economics team's expectation for cooler inflation prints, we see a high possibility that markets are going to reassess, and go back to pricing more rate cuts – likely in the mold of insurance cuts.

As we noted in The Problem with Steepeners, April 29, 2024, we think that bull flattening of the curve remains a plausible scenario for Treasuries, and we continue to favor duration trades over steepeners. We recommend long 3m10y receivers.

## May FOMC reaction: Easing asymmetry on display?

Bottom line: Chopping off the hike risks: Powell declined to entertain rate hikes and repeatedly emphasized the need for more time – to get additional confidence on inflation, for policy to be "sufficiently restrictive," and to understand the recent uptick in inflation. He discussed multiple scenarios, but none in which the Fed would need to hike rates.

In reaction to the FOMC meeting, yields endured a round-trip, falling initially but reverting quite a bit by the close of day, ending modestly lower on the day. The yield curve did bullsteepen eventually but showed signs of resistance to steepening for a large part of the day ( Exhibit 29 ). While we agree with bull-steepening pathways, we do see risks of bullflattening, too, which keeps us in favor of duration trades as opposed to curve trades – see The Problem with Steepeners. We recommend long 3m10y receivers.

Data-dependent, not FOMC-dependent markets: Ultimately, tracking intra-day price action, much like the March FOMC it became clear that the May meeting was not going to offer a clear direction to markets – only data will, particularly inflation data. That has been the case through the year, anyway ( Exhibit 28 ).

Powell on employment mandate: "The employment goal now comes back into focus. ...Same is true of quits and hiring rates. Which have essentially normalized."

Steering clear of rate hike paths: Powell was asked in multiple, indirect ways about the possibility of rate hikes, and he counteracted most questions on the subject with the possibility of a longer "on hold" being the most hawkish outcome in his mind (see gray box above). He mentioned three scenarios: two in which the Fed could cut, based on cooling inflation or weakening of labor markets, and one in which the Fed remains on hold for longer if inflation moved sideways.

Speaking of labor market weakness, investors would note that Powell and the FOMC statement have both brought the duality of the Fed's mandate into more focus in recent communications. Powell explicitly discussed falling quits rates and hiring rates, calling them "normalized," though if the 2019 labor market is the ideal (to which Powell has referenced in the past), recent data suggest things are weaker than the ideal ( Exhibit 31 ).

Powell stuck to the bigger-but-not-tighter theme; markets behind the curve: Powell reemphasized the role of the supply side, and how high growth or a strong labor market by itself would not worry him with regard to inflation. This remains a key disconnect in the way long-end yields reacted to tight labor markets ( Exhibit 30 ) and labor surprises through last year – which admittedly were not as tight as the labor data surprises might suggest.

As our economists have noted, the breakeven payrolls in the last year were much higher than previously estimated, which in turn has probably led to low forecasts on payrolls and more upside surprises than there truly were – see Supply Side Curveball.

Taper time: After a signal from the March meeting that the decision to slow the pace of balance sheet run-off would be made "fairly soon," the May statement confirmed the FOMC would begin slowing the decline of its securities holdings in June.

Specifically, the monthly redemption cap on Treasury securities will be lowered from \$60bn to \$25bn, but the monthly redemption cap on agency and agency mortgage-backed securities will remain at \$35bn. The FOMC will also reinvest any principal payments in excess of the MBS cap into Treasury securities.

Securities holdings in the SOMA portfolio have declined on average between \$75bn and \$80bn per month since the runoff caps were raised to current levels in September 2022. MBS paydowns have remained well below the cap at \$15-20bn per month. The Fed lowered the monthly Treasury redemption cap, by a bit more than our base case, in an effort to reduce the overall pace of monthly runoff by "roughly half."

We update our monthly net UST issuance projections ( Exhibit 32 ) and note the QT taper decision reduces the Treasury's additional financing needs in 2024 to \$475bn, from a potential \$720bn in the event QT would not have been tapered – a scenario noted in the New York Fed’s 2023 Open Market Operations report. After holding coupons unchanged at May refunding, we continue to think the Treasury will reduce bill issuance to the private market to accommodate the impact of QT tapering.

This week was very intense on the macro front – let's briefly recap what happened:

• Euro Area GDP came in stronger than expected in 1Q24 at 0.3 Q/Q vs consensus at 0.1%. This has led our economists to revise their growth outlook for 2024 to 0.7% (from 0.5%).

• Euro Area Inflation remained stable in April, despite a 30bp downtick in core inflation y/y figures to 2.7% y/y it still came in around \~10bp higher than consensus. Services inflation started to tick down, printing at 3.7%. The next sizeable tick down on the services front is expected by July, with a likely stall in a couple of prints. All in all, our economists are still forecasting headline at 2.4% y/y in 2024 whilst raising their core estimate by 10bp to 2.7% (more here).

• The Employment Cost Index (ECI), In the US, re-accelerated in 1Q, with broad gains, particularly in services and with a pick-up more evident in benefits as opposed to wages.

• At the May FOMC, Chairman Powell reassured the market that the idea of bringing back hikes remains essentially a tail-risk and the next likely move is a cut, although the timing of the first cut remains unclear given the strong 1Q data (for more see here).

• NFP came in lower than expected for the first time since November 23, with downward beats on every other component of the employment report.

• ISM services printed in contraction territory - the first sub 50 reading since the end of 2022. Despite this, market participants' focus was mainly skewed towards the prices paid beat (59.2 vs consensus at 55), particularly given the previous beats on ECI and the beat on prices paid in ISM manufacturing.

## Which indicators should we closely monitor?

Domestic markets have been relatively quiet over the past few days (with the Golden Week vacation period perhaps a contributing factor), but the April 30 release of the full version of the BoJ’s quarterly Outlook for Economic Activity and Prices update actually offered some very important insights into the central bank’s policy response function.

Governor Kazuo Ueda had previously hinted at the possibility of a further rate hike if the BoJ’s forecasts end up proving reasonably accurate or risks start to be seen as more skewed to the upside. The analysis presented in the latest Outlook Report has helped to clarify just what specifically Ueda and his team will be watching out for.

Boxes 1–6 at the end of the report address a range of topics including “Linkage between Wages and Prices”, “Assessing Financial Conditions”, and “Impact of the BoJ's JGB Purchases on the Yield Curve”.

With regard to the “virtuous cycle between wages and prices”—intensification of which has been characterized as a prerequisite for a further rate hike—Boxes 1–4 suggest that the BoJ will be looking for evidence of: (1) this spring’s strong wage hike momentum spilling over to smaller businesses; (2) a recovery in consumer spending as wages rise; (3) pass-through of higher labor costs to output prices, particularly in the services sector; and (4) a continuing uptrend in inflation expectations.

The Outlook Report’s analysis basically points to further improvements for each of these four factors, but we believe the BoJ wants to take some time to confirm that things are indeed tracking as expected before hiking its policy rate.

With regard to (1), while comments by BoJ speakers will as always warrant attention, much may also depend on the degree to which scheduled cash earnings growth (as measured in the Monthly Labour Survey for continuously sampled establishments) quickens into this summer (see Exhibit 50 ). For (2) the BoJ will probably be focusing mostly on real wage growth and its Consumption Activity Index (see Exhibit 51 ), while for (3) it will likely be monitoring inflation in the comparatively labor-intensive component (see Exhibit 52 ). Finally, for (4) we suggest keeping an eye on those survey-based measures of inflation expectations that are used in constructing the BoJ’s composite index (see Exhibit 53 ).

Our rates colleagues project that US rates will be falling this year and likely for risk-benign reasons (disinflation and Fed cuts), which tend to be USD-negative. Whether that's USD-positive or negative is debatable, though.

No matter how we slice it, rate differentials point to a stronger USD. In absolute level terms, higher US rates offer positive carry, raising USD's attractiveness, while we expect RoW rates to fall faster than US rates in absolute terms and relative to consensus forecasts and market-implied forwards.

Since inflation took hold as a market theme, the absolute change in US rates has effectively driven almost all cross-asset price action as it captured the thème du moment: inflation.

Strong US growth and torpor abroad have been gradually generating more focus on a new theme – policy divergence. Higher for longer in Washington and fasterand-deeper cuts abroad could bring rate differentials back to the fore in explaining FX price action, supporting USD. In his exchange with the shopkeeper, he learns that the doll is cursed ("that's bad"), but that it comes with a free frozen yogurt ("that's good"), that the yogurt is also cursed ("that's bad"), but it comes with his choice of toppings ("that's good"), and that the yogurt contains potassium benzoate ("...that's bad").

His experience is reminiscent of how the global rates outlook appears for USD. Depending on one's perspective for viewing rates, the answer can change. From USD's perspective, US carry is attractive ("that's good") but US rates are likely to fall ("that's bad") but global rates probably fall faster than US rates ("that's good") and so on.

The level of US rates in true isolation is meaningless (what does ‘3%’ or ‘5%’ actually mean without context?) so we have to compare to something – levels compared to history and compared to the estimated neutral rate are good benchmarks.

In the current environment, the level of US rates is elevated. From a very, very long-run perspective, the US 10-year yield is in the 71st percentile and the market-implied terminal rate is elevated relative to investors' expectations for the longer-run neutral rate (see Exhibit 62 and Exhibit 63 ).

One might initially think that elevated US rates are USD-positive. After all, higher US rates amplify its carry (more USD inflows), reduce the incentive for foreigners to borrow in USD (fewer USD outflows) and tighten global financial conditions, weighing on risk assets.

We push back on this axiom, though. As our four-regime USD framework demonstrates, the relationship between USD and changes in US nominal rates depends on why rates are moving and the context.

Unfortunately, at present, the market is in a heated debate about why rates are elevated and the interplay between growth and inflation. Are rates high because of aggressive US fiscal expansion, fueling aggregate demand, stoking inflation and forcing the Fed to hike well in excess of neutral? Are rates high because of a positive supply shock raising shortrun r\*? Are rates higher because the low rate sensitivity of the US economy means that monetary policy is relatively less effective, requiring higher rates than usual?

Reasonable investors can disagree. For the record, our economics team argues forcefully for the positive supply-shock narrative, which suggests that strong growth should not impede disinflation and rate cuts. But depending on how the market ultimately chooses to interpret the level of rates, its impact on USD can vary with the evolution of narratives.

The absolute level of US rate differentials – USD-positive: When we compare the absolute level of US rates to peers, though, the interpretation is USD-positive. This is because USD offers positive carry compared to its G10 peers, as seen in our FX Carry Index for example (see Exhibit 64 ).

Carry on offer can be an important driver of FX moves even if rates themselves aren't changing. Absolute change in US rates – USD-negative: We think it's more likely that US rates over the next 6-12 months are going down than up as evident in our forecasts.

As implied above, typically speaking, investors associate falling US rates with USD negativity as it shifts the incentive to invest and borrow. The global nature of US rates as the global risk-free rate benchmark also means that lower US rates ease financial conditions globally, bolstering risk appetite and RoW growth. Both of these tend to weigh on USD.

Of course, though, context is critical. Why rates are falling matters. Take regime 4 of our four-regime framework, for example. This sees US nominal yields falling as real rates and breakevens both move lower; it's typically associated with risk-off, deflationary shocks and recessions. In regime 4 USD tends to gain versus most currencies despite falling rates.

However, the current environment is much more likely to be benign. If rates are falling, it's likely because disinflation is taking hold and the Fed is able to cut rates. In this particular instance, we'd expect that the benign nature would be risk-supportive and thus more likely to be USD-negative in isolation.

Conclusion – what do rates tell us about USD? It's a mixed picture but leans USDpositive: When we look at rates from six different angles we get six different results: three USD-positive results, two mixed or debatable results and one USD-negative result.

If we equal-weight these angles, this would suggest USD strength at the margin. Whether all angles are created equal is debatable though. For example, markets globally have been linked most heavily to absolute changes in US rates since changes in US rates have captured the market theme most closely – the rise and (eventually, hopefully) fall of inflation.

## MoF intervenes?

It has obviously been a very volatile week for USD/JPY. As we discussed in "Japan | BoJ Behind The Curve?", the April BoJ monetary policy meeting saw the central bank insist that the JPY’s weakness is as yet having only a limited impact on “underlying inflation”, while also tacitly ruling out a monetary policy response to exchange rate movements.

Markets reacted by driving USD/JPY beyond 160 on April 29 (a national holiday in Japan), after which it soon plunged below 155. A subsequent rally to around 158 was then followed by another sharp correction (to around 153) on May 2 (Japan time) in the wake of the FOMC meeting.

With Vice Minister of Finance for International Affairs Masato Kanda having declined to comment, official confirmation as to the size of these apparent MoF interventions will need to wait until the release of data at the end of May.

However, as we discussed in "JPY | Silent MoF Despite Further JPY Weakness", it is possible to estimate the amount of JPY purchased by the MoF by comparing the projections of tanshi money brokers for the BoJ’s daily surplus or shortage of funds—or more specifically, the “Treasury funds and others” component thereof—with the actual figure that ends up being disclosed by the central bank (or its own prior projection) and looking for any major discrepancies.

The differences for May 2 and May 7—the settlement dates for spot transactions on April 29 and May 2—amount to around JPY5.5 trillion and JPY3.5 trillion respectively. This points to total intervention somewhere in the order of JPY9 trillion, which would be on a rough par with that seen in September–October 2022.

These (presumed) interventions appear to have been very well timed. For starters, markets will no doubt have been surprised to see action taken on a Japanese national holiday. Moreover, the lack of any official confirmation will leave markets facing a degree of uncertainty until the relevant figures are published at the end of May.

Had the MoF instead moved on April 26 just after the BoJ meeting (as many had likely been expecting), market participants might have simply opted to “buy the dip”, and the size of the intervention would have become almost immediately apparent with the release of data at the end of April (including the April 30 settlement date for April 26 spot transactions).

So waiting just another (business) day may have ended up making a very big difference. As we discussed in "How Effective Is FX Intervention?", FX market intervention can ultimately do nothing to alter the fundamental drivers of future monetary policy decisions and, as such, is basically only useful as a means of buying additional time. That said, any “damage” suffered by speculators could prove to be a valuable deterrent, particularly given that they seem to have accumulated very large JPY short positioning (see Exhibit 114 ).

In "JPY | What’s Been Driving Movements In USD/JPY?", we presented a factor decomposition of weekly returns that pointed to a buildup of speculative JPY-short positioning driving USD/JPY higher than appeared commensurate with market pricing of US short-term r\*. This “richness” has now been corrected to at least some degree (see Exhibit 114 and Exhibit 115 ).

## When could issuance start?

As noted in TBAC Considers a Green Treasury, we think the creation of a callable issuance program would take several years – if it happens at all. In reality, not all TBAC proposals get implemented (e.g., the 2019 proposal for TIPS linked to CPI subcomponents). Moreover, TBAC members noted the operational investment required before starting a callable bond program (see quote below).

TBAC report to Secretary of the Treasury: "The operational hurdles may constrain Treasury’s ability to monetize the value for the taxpayer. It was noted that further evaluation would be needed to assess the products’ usefulness more fulsomely."

## What structures would be issued?

TBAC recommended issuing callable bonds in structures that have seen common and consistent demand over time. This would allow the Treasury to abide by its "regular and predictable" framework. TBAC cited the "5 noncall 1" (5nc1 hereafter) as a good candidate, arguing that "targeting the investor base of agency callables would make the most sense." It did not make recommendations on option style, but noted that callable bonds "typically have Bermudan calls."

As a refresher, a 5nc1 Bermudan callable bond is a 5-year bond that is first callable after a 1-year "non-call" period from issuance. The Bermudan optionality implies that the issuer has the option to call the bond at regular, discrete intervals following the non-call period. For example, a 5nc1 with 1-year Bermudan optionality means the bond can be called in one year, and every year following the first year.

## How much could be issued?

TBAC did not provide a recommendation on issuance sizes. However, we can use two approaches to estimate potential notional issuance.

The first is to look at history. The US Treasury last issued callable debt during 1975-1991. During this period, callable debt peaked at \~8% of outstanding marketable debt (see Exhibit 130 ). Assuming the Treasury attempted to re-target this\~8% composition over the decade 2025-2035, this would imply \~\$20bn monthly issuance in 2025.

The second is to look at agency callable issuance. TBAC's recommendation to issue callables rests on the premise that if the market is able to absorb agency callables, it should also have the capacity to absorb Treasury callable issuance. Therefore, a Treasury callable program could be approximately of the same order of magnitude as current agency callable issuance.

Per our tracking, US agencies have issued \~\$64bn in callable debt YTD, or \~\$16bn/month. While this notional is not solely concentrated in 5nc1, \~91% of YTD issuance has non-call periods ≤1y and tenors ≤5y, i.e., similar structures (see Exhibit 131 ). Thus, we think the \~ \$20bn/month estimate from our previous exercise provides a reasonable estimate.

## What does this mean for vol markets?

If the US Treasury decided to swap its issuance with counterparties – following Exhibit 132 – callable Treasuries would supply vol to the market (i.e., bearish vol). To put numbers on this, we estimate that \$20bn of 5nc1 issuance with a 6% coupon would leave counterparties with cancellable swaps of \~\$1.5mn/norm vol in vega.

However, we think it is unlikely that the Treasury will swap its callable issuance. TBAC's guidance specified that the "Treasury would need to develop a model to decide when it is optimal to call the bond" (consistent with previous experiences). This suggests call decisions will be made by the US Treasury, something that could not be guaranteed if the Treasury was exposed to a cancellable swap counterparty.

Callable Treasury issuance could impact vol markets even if the Treasury decides not to swap. We think this is the case for two reasons: (1) the substitutability of Treasury callables with agency callables, which could lead to reduced agency swapping activity (i.e.; bullish vol); and (2) increased end-user negative convexity hedging and/or vol hedging (i.e., bullish vol).

## Trading Strategy 1 – "Trade Longs/Fade Shorts"

When the 5-day moving average crosses above the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract (long duration) and hold for a 25-business-day period. When the 5-day moving average crosses below the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract and hold for a 25-business-day period. In short, this strategy buys futures when the Simple Moving Average Crossover (SMAX) generates both a long and a short signal, given the historical outperformance of long signals traded long and underperformance of short signals traded short. Given that the SMAX could generate both a long and a short signal within the predefined holding period, an investor may have a 200% long position since each of the two signals would be traded in separate portfolio sleeves.
