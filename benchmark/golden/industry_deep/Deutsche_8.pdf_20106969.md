Mixed US payrolls; US CPI this week

USA: Employment report (Feb): Up 275k, above mkt, but downward revisions DEU: Industrial production (Jan): Up 1.0%mom, above mkt; best in almost a year

US equities were weaker Friday, S&P down 0.7% following a mixed payrolls report to end the week 0.3% lower. US10yr yields down 1bp Friday to 4.07%, down 10bps over the week.

A beat on US payrolls, up 275k at the headline in February, consensus at 200k. Taking the shine off: 167k in cumulative downward revisions to the two months prior. Private payrolls at 223k, consensus at 165k. Unemployment rose 0.2ppts to 3.9%, consensus at 3.7%, and hourly earnings were softer, up 0.1%mom, consensus at 0.2%mom and prior month revised down 0.1ppt. Participation rate steady at 62.5%.

Brett Ryan with all the US payroll details in his chartbook. Bottom line: latest data should alleviate Fed fears that progress on rebalancing was reversing, but with the participation rate steady in recent months, the improvement in labour supply that Chair Powell has noted may also have run its course. Still, the labour market appears on track to meet the Fed's forecasts outlined in the December SEP.

Fed's Williams: Neutral interest rate is "likely still quite low", "not clear" whether it has risen.

ECB's Simkus: April cut is "possible", but "likelihood is low", "June is the possible month" for a rate cut, "doesn't see" why cuts should be larger than 25bps.

A beat on German industrial production, up 1%mom in January, consensus at 0.6%mom. Eric Heymann notes this is the strongest outcome in almost a year, and asks whether it might mark a turning point. In the details: news for optimists and pessimists, but Eric sticks with this forecast of a 2.5% decline in manufacturing output this year.

FX and the US election, Alan Ruskin notes that long USD/TWD vol and long EUR/ USD vol make most sense (versus short USD/MXN), as both Europe and Taiwan should be deeply impacted by the likely US presidential candidates' different visions of "the (old) world order".

Michael Hsueh argues that the initial stage of the past week's gold rally was idiosyncratic and likely precipitated by China investor demand. But also that it has transitioned into one more clearly fueled by the USD, rates, and firming confidence in eventual Fed easing.

Mark Wall pulls together articles recently published by DB on the ECB's policy implementation review (the review is expected to be finalized this week). Since it isn't clear that expectations were for a particularly hawkish outcome, current valuations seem more consistent with expectations of a generous term tender or a very effective ceiling backstop with a narrower corridor. Mark thinks neither of these seem realistic.

Commercial real estate in Germany: How large a concern for banks? WFH trends and higher interest rates have brought CRE risks sharply into focus. Jan Schildbach argues that while the CRE downturn will remain a headwind for bank profitability, it is very unlikely to cause systemic issues in the German banking sector.

A combination of low FX implied yields, together with the recent decline in FX vols in Asia, presents a unique hedging opportunity for those with local currency exposure in Asia. Bryant Xu notes that buying an ATMF 12M USD call currently yields a positive net hedging PnL for TWD and CNH. The rule of thumb for this to work is that FX vol must be smaller than 2.5x USD/Asia carry.

Australia experienced net portfolio outflows in 2023: foreigners sold Australian equities (high valuations, little tech exposure), and Australian investors bought foreign corporate debt. Tim Baker thinks these trends are likely to persist, but with a healthy trade surplus and tentative recovery in FDI it should not be a problem for AUD.

Macroeconomic fluctuations often account for most of the risks associated with exposures to instruments that promise to pay fixed or variable interest rates. For an investor who is long fixed-rate instruments (thus receiving these cash flows), increases in interest rates naturally lift the discount factors applied to the expected cash flows and thus cause a loss. The longer the maturity of a certain cash flow and the higher the move in the discount factor, the higher will be the loss. Upside surprises in inflation, fiscal funding needs, and FX depreciation rates are among the most common adverse macroeconomic shocks to receivers. But counterparty exposure, issuer creditworthiness, client segmentation, regulation (including changes in assets holding limits for different types of investors), changes in tax policies, currency convertibility (which may constrain currency conversion, repatriation of profits and/or liquidation of positions), and settlement issues related to local clearing houses are also important risk factors. The sensitivity of fixed-income instruments to macroeconomic shocks may be mitigated by indexing the contracted cash flows to inflation, to FX depreciation, or to specified interest rates - these are common in emerging markets. The index fixings may - by construction - lag or mis-measure the actual move in the underlying variables they are intended to track. The choice of the proper fixing (or metric) is particularly important in swaps markets, where floating coupon rates (i.e., coupons indexed to a typically short-dated interest rate reference index) are exchanged for fixed coupons. Funding in a currency that differs from the currency in which coupons are denominated carries FX risk.
