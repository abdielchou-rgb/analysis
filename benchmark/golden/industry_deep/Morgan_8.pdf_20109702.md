# The White House and the Dollar

Policies proposed by the Republican presidential campaign would likely boost USD. A potential Republican administration may attempt to offset USD strength, but in the near term, we expect USD and the likelihood of a Republican White House to be positively correlated.

We expect the US dollar to gain if the Republican Party is increasingly seen as likely to win the US presidency. While former President Trump has spoken against dollar strength, we think his policies would lead to a stronger dollar. And this expectation also reflects the 2016 experience; the DXY rallied around 4% in November and December 2016.

## We think the US dollar would strengthen if a Republican administration

implemented policies espoused by its campaign: Higher US tariffs on the rest of the world could lead foreign authorities to allow a depreciation of their domestic currency or weigh on economic activity abroad, making the US look more attractive in comparison. An expansionary fiscal policy agenda in a Republican sweep outcome could boost expectations about US growth and encourage capital flows into the US. And rising geopolitical uncertainty may send investors into US assets as a safe haven. We discuss each policy area in turn, making the case for why the US dollar is likely to be positively correlated to the likelihood of a Republican administration.

FX intervention, Fed policy: We address common arguments for why a Republican administration might weaken the US dollar, and argue that the likelihood of these policies does not outweigh the USD-positive effects of a Republican administration's fiscal and trade policy agenda. There is significant uncertainty about how a Republican administration would interact with the Federal Reserve, and we do not see any near-term impact on FX movements from this channel.

US dollar headwinds under another Democratic term: The softening of key risks to growth – particularly those involving trade and geopolitics – are likely to reduce demand for safe-haven currencies including the US dollar if the Democratic Party retains control of the White House.

Volatility to come: Independent of the outcome, we expect heightened FX volatility following the election. A delayed result and accompanying policy uncertainty may drive sharp FX movements after Election Day on November 5 – echoing moves seen in November 2016 and November 2020.

# Which Policy Areas Matter for USD?

## Andrew Watrous

A broad range of US government policies are relevant for the US dollar. Fiscal and regulatory policy modulate market expectations for US growth and the outlook for US financial assets. Trade policy impacts investor sentiment and growth at home and abroad. And national security policy has myriad and intertwined effects on commodity prices, US and foreign growth, and broad risk demand.

But how might these policy areas weigh more or less directly on risk sentiment and USD? And therefore how might market expectations of policy action under a Democratic or Republican administration affect market sentiment?

Our analysis suggests that elevated trade policy uncertainty under a Republican presidency may support USD, and elevated regulatory and fiscal policy uncertainty may act as headwinds to USD under a Democratic administration.

Our economists and public policy analysts discussed a range of these issues in Navigating a Noisy Election, June 14, 2024. We delve deeper into the exchange rate implications of key policy areas relevant to exchange rates, and discuss how political expectations may impact USD.

Regulatory policy uncertainty has been more elevated under the Biden administration than under the Trump administration ( Exhibit 14 ); a similar pattern holds regarding the Obama and Bush administrations.

Executive branch regulatory agendas can affect real economic growth, but only with significant lags. Rewriting federal rules often takes several years due to required public comment periods, among other things. Statutory changes may quickly add or remove federal rules, but neither the Democratic or Republican campaigns have put such statutory regulatory changes at the center of their policy agendas. Furthermore, as our US public policy team has pointed out, the recent SCOTUS Chevron ruling has led to questions around agencies' policymaking authorities, potentially moderating the amount of influence a president can have on the regulatory outlook.

That said, the relatively elevated level of regulatory policy uncertainty under the Biden administration has been associated with mild USD weakness. We therefore expect potential continued regulatory uncertainty under a Democratic administration to act as a headwind to USD, while regulatory policy under a Republican administration may be a USD-positive factor.

## Trade/Geopolitics and USD

## Andrew Watrous, Arunima Sinha, Diego Anzoategui

Unlike other policy domains, US presidents have significant discretion over trade policy. Trade policy is a – if not the – central avenue through which the 2024 US election is likely to influence exchange rates. Under the Trump administration, trade policy uncertainty was associated with USD strength.

US yields started falling in 2019 relative to the rest of the world ( Exhibit 15 ). However, USD continued to climb through end-2019. One factor contributing to USD strength was likely trade policy uncertainty, which reached a peak in 3Q19 ( Exhibit 16 ).

USD was likely boosted by a number of factors in late 2018 and 2019, including general trade policy uncertainty's effects on consumption, investment, monetary policy, and risk demand, as well as policy-driven currency depreciation. In this section, we discuss these factors and how they might function differently in the future.

## Transmission mechanisms

To contextualize why USD gained in 2019 (despite yield differentials moving against it) – and may gain again if renewed trade policy confrontation appears likely – we highlight four key aspects by which trade policy may affect USD:

## 1. Impact on US growth expectations and inflation (net USD negative)

Negative effects on US growth expectations: US exports could be affected by US tariffs if other economies impose tariffs in response, but the effects would depend on the scale and scope. A significant response would make American goods more expensive in foreign markets, and weigh down on gross US exports. Tariffs also have the potential to disrupt the ongoing supply chain reorganization, and affect the profitability of producers across the production chain.

Trade uncertainty arising from tariffs could have other effects on the domestic economy. For instance, research has suggested that the prospect of tariffs has the potential to reduce business investment. One potential channel could be the uncertainty about contracts and the policy duration of the tariffs.

Consumption can also be adversely impacted as tariffs rise. The extent of this should depend on factors such as how easily consumers are able substitute the tariffed commodities and to what extent the producers pass on the costs of higher inputs to consumers when intermediate goods are subjected to tariffs (as was the case with the 2018 tariffs).

Positive effects on US growth expectations: These effects (which are all negative for US growth) are offset to some extent by higher tariffs' negative effect on gross US imports. Broad import tariffs could mean that the higher US tariffs encourage domestic consumers to find US suppliers if US goods and services become less expensive relative to their foreign substitutes.

In the previous tariff cycle, in September 2018, US Federal Reserve staff conducted an assessment of US tariffs on China and tariffs on imports of steel and aluminum. Those tariffs together applied to 42% of US non-oil manufacturing imports.

The Fed assessment found that the tariffs lowered both US exports and US imports, and as a result they “have had an almost imperceptible positive effect on our forecast of net exports.” However, the impact on higher prices on consumption and investment led the Fed staff to conclude that the tariffs would have “a small negative drag of only about 0.1 percent on the level of US GDP.”

US inflation: Higher US tariffs raise domestic US prices. The effect on domestic prices may be mitigated by some factors: by producers' decision to reduce profit margins (essentially absorb the cost of the tariff) or by foreign trade partners' decision to weaken their domestic currency to offset higher tariffs' effects on the price to US consumers (see point 3 below).

The September 2018 Fed staff assessment found that US tariffs would add “0.2 percent to the level of core PCE prices by the end of 2019.” However, as our economists wrote here, tariffs imposed in 2018 only boosted US CPI inflation modestly. The tariffs only had a limited effect on US inflation because only part of the goods tariffed were final consumer goods, the CNY depreciated notably, and there was fairly rapid trade diversion.

In the event of a future trade confrontation, we expect markets to price a lower Fed policy rate path. As in 2019, the extent of any Fed policy shift would likely reflect the degree of potential growth spillover from the rest of the world to the US.

## 4. Geopolitical implications (USD positive)

Anticipation of renewed trade confrontation under a Republican administration may also introduce a positive risk premium into USD due to geopolitical considerations. Investors may question whether trade policy objectives may supplant or undermine key US alliances with Australia and the UK (AUKUS), India, Japan, and Australia (the Quad), NATO, and a coalition among the US, Japan, and South Korea. A diminished role for these political and military relationships may heighten market-perceived risk of expanded conflict.

These geopolitical considerations are more elevated at a time when 70% of respondents to a Bank of England survey report that geopolitical risks are among the most challenging they must manage ( Exhibit 26 ). This proportion is far higher than in 2018 and 2019.

## The Trump administration: A USD-trade policy case study

During the Trump presidency, international policy confrontation was associated with USD gains against EM currencies and risk-sensitive currencies like AUD, and USD declines against low-yielding currencies like CHF and JPY.

On average, USD rose by 0.4% against CNH and MXN on days when trade-related actions occurred. Similarly, USD rose slightly against AUD (a highly risk-sensitive G10 currency).

These USD gains aligned with stock market declines and tighter TIPS breakeven yields observed on trade confrontation days. On average, the S&P 500 dropped by 1.1% and TIPS breakeven yields tightened on these days.

## USD under renewed trade confrontation

Would markets pricing in the likelihood of renewed trade confrontation under a Republican administration drive USD down against EUR and JPY, as it did in 2018 and 2019?

The net effect on the broad USD would probably depend on the size and form of any potential tariff increase. For example, the breadth, magnitude, and potential duration of the tariff increase, 10%? Is it universal? Is it policy-conditional or time-limited?

While lower US real yields would likely weigh against USD (all else equal), we see reasons to expect any potential USD weakness to be limited:

1. The stance of ECB and BoJ policy: One factor limiting EUR weakness stemming from broad trade confrontation was the European Central Bank's (ECB) policy stance in 2018- 19. At the time, policy rates were at or close to the effective lower bound, and the ECB was conducting substantial asset purchase programs.

This already-accommodative stance of ECB policy limited further scope for markets to price a lower policy rate outlook in reaction to growth challenges posed by the effects of US tariffs. The limited room for ECB expectations to move reflected the limited policy options available to the ECB to ease policy further.

For example, in September 2019 the ECB cut its deposit rate by 0.1pp from -0.4% to - 0.5%, citing "the ongoing trade tensions" as a key driver of slower growth. This level (- 0.5%) is at or very close to the effective lower bound in the euro area, evinced by the fact that the ECB did not reduce its policy rate in 2020 at the height of the Covid pandemic.

This limited policy flexibility meant that OIS rates in Europe had limited room to fall. Between late 2018 and mid-2019, 5y euro area OIS fell from around 0.2% to -0.8% – around 100bp and around half of the magnitude of the decline in US yields ( Exhibit 30 ).

5y OIS in the euro area is now substantially higher than in 2018-19 (and the ECB has increased policy flexibility compared to 2018-19). As a result, we see significantly more room for ECB policy expectations to move in response to potential trade confrontation ( Exhibit 31 ).

A similar dynamic held regarding the Bank of Japan (BoJ). The BoJ's asset purchases and yield curve control framework limited the extent to which Japanese government bond (JGB) yields fluctuated – moving US-Japan yield differentials sharply in a USD/JPY-negative direction as US yields fell during 2019.

Today, the BoJ has raised interest rates and is no longer setting an upper limit for 10y JGB yields.

Universal tariffs: As illustrated above, most trade actions and statements undertaken during the Trump administration were on products/sectors like aluminum and steel tariffs, or involved imports from individual countries like Mexico, Canada, or China. We expect a possible broader trade confrontation (i.e., the possibility of "universal baseline tariffs") to lead to less EUR and JPY strength than in 2018 and 2019. While countryspecific tariff action in 2018 and 2019 may have led investors to seek safety in European and Japanese assets, a universal tariff may directly influence European and Japanese growth and policy rate expectations to a greater extent than during the previous episode of trade confrontation.

3. Policy space from Chinese policymakers: Our Asia economics team notes that growth effects of the 2018-19 tariffs were substantially larger than the team initially expected – a regional GDP slowdown from a peak of 6%Y in 1Q18 to 3.9%Y in 4Q19.

Our economists wrote that the Chinese economy is now in a weaker state with reduced scope for easing compared to 2018-19. Unlike in that episode, China should not be able to stimulate the property sector to offset weaker external demand, and higher US tariffs would likely add to the deflationary pressures that China is already facing. Corporate confidence may not suffer to the same degree as in 2018-19 (because trade tensions have persisted for some time), but the effect would depend on the intensity of the trade measures taken.

## Conclusion

Trade policy is a key vector by which US politics can affect USD, and likely kept USD supported in 2019 as the Fed cut rates three times. We expect a rising likelihood of trade tensions to again boost USD – because the prospect of tariffs could weigh on growth expectations outside the US and because foreign officials may embrace domestic currency weakness to offset the effect of tariffs on their growth rates.

USD rose against risk-sensitive currencies like AUD, CAD, MXN, and ZAR on days in 2018 and 2019 when trade tensions flared, in line with a drop in the S&P 500 on those days. However, USD fell against EUR, CHF, and JPY during days when trade tensions rose. USD weakness reflected a USD-negative move in yield differentials on those days as there was little move in policy expectations for effective lower bound-constrained European central banks.

Unlike in 2018 and 2019, there is now more room for ECB and BoJ policy expectations to adjust lower in a trade tension scenario. And broadly applied tariffs might cause broader USD strength than in 2018 and 2019.

As a result, we do not expect EUR and JPY to gain against USD if trade tensions resurface. However, the historical precedent suggests that MXN, ZAR, CNH, and BRL may underperform CHF and JPY in a trade confrontation scenario.

Our Asia economists note that Australia and Indonesia would be most exposed to a Chinaspecific growth slowdown in a scenario where the US places 60% tariffs on imports from China, while Korea, Taiwan, Malaysia, and Thailand growth would be most negatively affected if 10% universal tariffs are implemented in addition to 60% tariffs on imports from China.

Broadly, we expect trade and geopolitical considerations to be a tailwind to USD if a Republican White House looks increasingly likely and inclined to pursue trade confrontation. Conversely, we expect trade and geopolitical considerations to be a headwind to USD if the Democratic Party looks increasingly likely to retain control of the White House.

## Immigration and USD

## Andrew Watrous

We do not expect a significant USD reaction to result from changing immigration policy in anticipation of either a Republican or a Democratic presidency. Our economists see little difference in Fed policy resulting from differences in immigration policy. And days during the Trump presidency when significant immigration policy actions were announced were not associated with any clear USD direction.

Our colleagues in economics and public policy research explored five scenarios for immigration policy – in which annual net immigration flows ranged from 0 to 3.3 million people per year. Our colleagues concluded that net immigration flows to the US would be somewhat lower under a Republican White House than under a Democratic White House, with key uncertainties depending on the make-up of Congress.

As our economists discussed here, reduced net immigration flows to the US (which we would expect under a Republican administration) result in lower growth while at the same time putting upward pressure on inflation. Conversely, we would likely see slightly less of a reduction in immigration under a Democratic administration, leading to markets pricing in a relatively cooler inflation outlook paired with firmer growth.

We expect that the growth implications of this change in immigration flows would outweigh the inflation effects for Fed policy. Therefore, reduced net immigration flows to the US would likely put downward pressure on Fed policy expectations and therefore USD, all else equal.

However, our economists and public policy strategists think that immigration flows would be reduced significantly with either a Republican or a Democratic victory. A Republican White House may have a slightly larger dampening effect on immigration flows, but not enough to materially change economic outcomes.

Therefore, we conclude that there are not significant differences in Fed policy in any of the 2024 election outcomes – monetary policy is likely to be easier in all four scenarios listed here. And the 2018-19 experience also suggests that immigration policy had little clear implication for USD.

## Fiscal Policy and USD

## Zoe Strauss, Andrew Watrous

Our economists expect significant fiscal expansion under a Republican administration, which would likely boost USD by raising US growth expectations ( Exhibit 33 and Exhibit 34 ). Our expectation is that the most fiscal expansion would occur if the Republican Party also controls both houses of Congress.

We expect a Democratic White House to lead to less fiscally expansionary outcomes, weighing on USD as a result.

Our economists do not see a significant difference in public spending under a Republican or Democratic White House if there is a divided government outcome (i.e., if the opposing party controls one or both houses of Congress). However, the implied likelihood of a Republican sweep and a Democratic sweep (in which those parties control the White House) is mechanically correlated to the probability of a Republican or Democratic presidency, respectively. Therefore, we expect fiscal considerations to nudge USD higher if the chances of a Republican White House are rising and vice versa.

Concerns about debt sustainability tend to have a negative impact on the domestic exchange rate, but we do not expect the US to have a credit event in isolation driven by debt risks in the near term.

## Policy Options to Reverse USD Strength

Andrew Watrous, Ariana Salvatore, Seth Carpenter, Zoe Strauss

Recent reporting has suggested that public figures associated with the Republican presidential campaign have advocated for a weaker USD. Former President Trump's running mate Senator JD Vance has advocated for "devaluing" the US dollar, noting that “if you want to employ a lot of people in manufacturing, you need to make it easier for us to export and not just import what we need.” And former President Trump has said "we have a big currency problem" due to USD strength.

These developments have given rise to client interest in the possibility that a Republican administration might act to weaken the US dollar through a range of policy avenues. In this section, we provide a high-level overview of some of those options.

To varying degrees, these policy avenues may be effective in weakening USD against one or more trading partners. However, we do not expect the (USD-negative) market expectation of these policy options to fully offset the (USD-positive) effect of other potential Republican policies, including on trade and fiscal policy. This assessment would be different if these USD-weakening proposals become a centerpiece of the Republican campaign or an area of significant policy focus early in a Republican administration.

This expectation is based on legal, logistical, and political challenges associated with many of these options. ## Active intervention

The White House could pursue policy intervention against USD in a number of ways:

## 1) Encouraging foreign unilateral intervention against USD

The administration could encourage foreign monetary authorities to intervene to strengthen their domestic currency. For example, the Japanese Ministry of Finance has intervened multiple times since late 2022 to strengthen JPY ( Exhibit 49 ).

The Biden administration has not publicly encouraged Japan's intervention. Treasury Secretary Yellen remarked in May 2024 that "we would expect these interventions to be rare and consultation to take place." Secretary Yellen previously stated that intervention should aim to "smooth out following undue volatility" rather than "attempt to influence the level of exchange rates."

The Biden administration's statements align with the G7's stated commitment to marketdetermined exchange rates, as well as other currency policy-related commitments from the G20 and IMF.

In contrast, a Republican administration would likely welcome unilateral intervention by foreign monetary authorities to strengthen their domestic currency.

However, the medium-term impact of MoF intervention on USD/JPY is not clear. As the exhibits below illustrate, roughly one year following Japanese intervention in late 2022, USD/JPY had risen to similar levels. And MoF intervention in May 2024 did not prevent USD/JPY from rising above 160 by mid-summer.

## 2) US participation in intervention against USD

A Republican administration might seek to intervene unilaterally, or coordinate with one or more foreign monetary authorities to intervene against USD. For example, it might seek to incentivize international participation in multilateral intervention against USD by offering to exempt participants from US tariffs or to reduce existing US tariffs on participants.

US participation in intervention against USD would likely have more enduring effects on USD than unilateral foreign intervention. Academic literature on the effectiveness of FX intervention finds that it is particularly clear if the intervention is "concerted."

US participation in intervention against USD would likely take the form of US Treasury purchases of foreign assets through the Exchange Stabilization Fund (ESF).

FX reserves make up a small proportion of total US international reserves, the majority of which are SDRs and deposits at the IMF ( Exhibit 50 ). The remaining US\$35 billion in foreign currency reserves are divided between EUR-denominated and JPY-denominated securities and deposits ( Exhibit 52 ), split evenly between holdings at the Treasury and the Fed's System Open Market Account (the SOMA; Exhibit 52 ). EUR-denominated securities holdings are invested in bonds issued by Germany, France, and the Netherlands.

Mechanics: The Treasury Department might aim to weaken USD by seeking congressional authority to issue additional debt. Acting as the agent of the Treasury, the New York Federal Reserve would convert the proceeds of that debt to foreign currency and either purchase additional foreign government bonds or deposit proceeds in foreign currency accounts. We discuss these mechanics in greater detail in this note.

Scale: The scale of potential expansion of the ESF (and presumably SOMA, too) is substantial. US foreign reserves are small in absolute terms ( Exhibit 53 ) and relative to GDP. An expansion in US reserves bringing them to absolute levels comparable with the euro area or Japan would entail over US\$1 trillion in purchases.

The expansion of the ESF is legally constrained only by congressional borrowing authority; the ESF operates broadly at the discretion of the Treasury Secretary and its charter allows the Secretary to purchase “instruments of credit or other securities” in a manner “consistent with the obligations of the Government in the International Monetary Fund on orderly exchange arrangements and a stable system of exchange rates.”

Other avenues: Seeking congressional authority to issue additional debt may introduce a political hurdle to augmenting the ESF. However, there are avenues a Republican administration might pursue to enable intervention which would not require additional congressional borrowing authority.

These intervention options that do not appear to require congressional authority may require alignment with and willingness by Federal Reserve leadership to pursue intervention. As a result, there is a possibility that these options become more politically feasible or otherwise likely after Chair Powell's term ends in May 2026.

For example, the Treasury Department could monetize its holdings of IMF Special Drawing Rights (SDRs), valued at US\$166 billion. This option would involve the Treasury Secretary issuing Special Drawing Rights Certificates to the Federal Reserve, which would lend the Treasury an amount that would not exceed the Treasury's SDR holdings.

The Treasury could also swap foreign currency for USD with the Federal Reserve and use the proceeds for intervention – a practice known as "warehousing" and described in detail here.

Finally, the New York Fed's foreign currency holdings within SOMA have historically matched ESF holdings, and previous episodes of US intervention have been financed equally between the ESF and SOMA. However, while the 1:1 ratio of ESF to SOMA foreign currency assets has held in the past, a future Fed official may determine that a different ratio is appropriate – potentially increasing the US government's intervention capabilities.

## Fed policy

A Republican administration may seek to weaken USD by appointing a Fed Chair inclined to set interest rates at low levels. Such a course of monetary policy could soften USD by diminishing the attractiveness of USD-denominated investments.

However, former President Trump has said he would not seek to shorten current Fed Chair Jerome Powell's current term on the Fed Board of Governors, saying "I’ve had my own disputes with him. But no, I would, I would let him serve it out especially if I thought he was doing the right thing." It was not clear whether Trump was referring to Chair Powell's statutorily-set four-year term which ends on May 15, 2026 or his 14-year term on the Board which ends in 2028.

Former President Trump has said that he would not reappoint current Fed Chair Powell if re-elected. While a Republican administration may seek to appoint a Fed Chair who would pursue a relatively accommodative path of monetary policy during the administration, we expect the Fed's independence to endure under a Republican or Democratic presidency – a topic we discussed at length here.

Note also that former USTR Lighthizer has argued against infringing on Fed independence, noting that “It’s a great accomplishment that America eventually got to an independent Federal Reserve system. The last thing I’d suggest is to do anything to change it.”

## Unconventional policy options

A variety of unconventional policy options aimed at reducing the US trade deficit have been discussed by figures associated with the Republican campaign, including imposing a border adjustment tax, taxing imports to the US at a rate equivalent to the tariff charged by the exporting country on US goods, and taxing capital flows into the US via a "market access charge".

There are significant areas of uncertainty associated with all these policy outcomes, including the degree to which one or more of these proposals might require additional US legislation, whether they would be consistent with US obligations under USMCA and/or WTO agreements, their impact on US growth and inflation, and the degree to which former President Trump supports these proposals. Similar unconventional policy avenues were proposed during the Trump administration

We recommend investors pay close attention to these unconventional policy avenues, and we will follow up with closer analysis of the potential economic and market implications if these avenues appear increasingly likely.

FX Volatility

## Andrew Watrous, Zoe Strauss

We expect FX volatility to follow the US election, regardless of the election outcome.

Our public policy analysts previously noted that it could take days before the election results are publicized. One reason for a potential delay is the sustained increase in the adoption of alternative voting methods across states, including vote by mail, which has been institutionalized in some states post-pandemic.

During such a delay either presidential candidate could appear to be ahead at different points in the vote counting. As a result, we could potentially see competing declarations of victory and widespread confusion about the election result.

A delayed or contested election may drive sharp FX movements following November 5 of this year. Realized FX volatility was elevated following US elections in November 2016 and November 2020.

Realized FX volatility rose between November 3, 2020 (the day the presidential election was held) and November 9 (the day Biden was projected to win the election). During that period, the possibility of a contested election outcome clouded the US political, regulatory, and trade policy outlook ( Exhibit 54 ).

FX markets have been characterized by low volatility over the past year and implied volatility remains low from a historical standpoint ( Exhibit 55 ). However, policy uncertainty and FX volatility may return this fall in case of a contested outcome or dayslong delay in result delivery.
