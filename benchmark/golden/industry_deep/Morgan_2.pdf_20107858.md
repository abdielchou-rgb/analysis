## Anatomy of a Rise

The 2024 year-to-date US Treasury market sell-off may seem similar to the 2023 summertime sell-off. But movements in yields this year exhibit major differences from the rise last year – differences which began last December. For starters, it's much more difficult for investors to blame US Treasury supply for higher yields this time.

We didn't think investors should have attributed higher yields to Treasury supply last year either, but many did nevertheless. Those who blamed supply interpreted the rise in term premiums as a logical concession for the higher-coupon bond sizes announced in August 2023 – increases that investors discussed for months before the announcement itself.

In contrast, we thought the Fed's hawkish pivot and the inverted yield curve explained the rise of term premiums and the bear-steepening of the yield curve. The nature of the hawkish pivot – one focused on keeping rates higher for longer, instead of raising rates further – perfectly explained why short-end rates didn't rise much last summer.

In addition, we reiterated the importance of the inverted yield curve and the Fed's forward guidance on the performance of long-end rates last week. Indeed, for most term premium models – like the New York Fed's ACM model – a yield curve that bear steepens drives term premiums higher by design, complicating the term premium-supply linkage.

This year, investors must lay blame for the recent sell-off elsewhere, as (1) Treasury quarterly refunding announcements did not book-end the rise in yields, and (2) modelbased rate expectations drove the 2024 sell-off so far (see Exhibit 1 and Exhibit 2 ).

To the extent that disinflationary trends return, we think investors should lower their expectations for inflation in the future. Our US economists see faster rent disinflation ahead, which raises the probability of such an outcome. Still, the net effect on Treasury yields depends on more than just investor inflation expectations.

Real yields accompanied higher breakeven inflation rates as well, as shown in Exhibit 9 . This likely reflects investor assessment of the Fed's reaction function. Higher inflation over the medium term may suggest the need for higher policy rates over the medium term, i.e., a higher equilibrium real rate, or r\*.

The recent behavior of real rates and breakeven inflation rates contrasts with last year. Then, real rates rose without much rise in breakevens. At the time, such price action suggested to us that investors thought the more hawkish stance from the Fed would eventually work to suppress inflation.

But the divergence between breakeven inflation rates and real rates may have also reflected the more asyncronous and trendless behavior of the energy complex back then, as we show in Exhibit 8 . With the trending rise in oil and gasoline prices this year, breakeven inflation rates may have been better placed to brush off a more hawkish Fed and higher real rates.

The impulsive rise in energy commodity prices since December last year reflects both more demand and less supply. The changing dynamic prompted our energy commodities research analysts to increase their Brent crude oil price forecasts back in March. They further increased their price forecasts earlier this month to \$94/bbl, based on geopolitical risks, just as oil prices reached their previous forecast of \$90/bbl.

What does all of this mean for Treasury yields? If the disinflationary trend in consumer prices reasserts itself, we think investors should lower their inflation expectations. This may not weigh on breakeven inflation rates much, if crude oil prices continue to rise. But it should weigh on real yields as markets reprice r\* lower.

## USD Outlook into the FOMC

We continue to expect USD to gain over the coming months as it offers attractive carry and US growth remains supported. Our economists' above-market 2024 growth forecasts (2.9% versus 2.4% median Bloomberg forecast) imply that US growth momentum is likely to surprise to the upside over the coming months.

The supply-driven nature of US growth is likely to limit resulting price pressures. Risk demand is likely to remain elevated in line with regime 2 of our real yield FX framework. Regime 2 is a positive environment for trades with a positive carry profile. In particular, we recommend short EUR/USD via options, long NOK/SEK, and short CNH/INR 3m NDF.

## Questions about residual seasonality abound

The Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA), which publishes the GDP and PCE numbers, has documented the issue of residual seasonality whereby inflation data in the first quarter tend to surprise to the upside, and growth data tend to surprise to the downside. The idea of residual seasonality was debated pre pandemic – and while the pandemic caused all sorts of distortions in the data, there has not been an update on whether residual seasonality continues to exist in the data from 2022 to 2024.

A cursory look at average PCE and GDP for 1Q of a given year vs. the rest of the year should be a sign of residual seasonality. The upside surprise in core PCE for 1Q224 and downside surprise in GDP numbers were both signs that residual seasonality may be affecting the numbers here.

Outside 2019-2021, which were affected by the pandemic, or when residual seasonality was actively corrected (2019), in the prior 12 years going to 2008 – 9 of the prior 12 years had core PCE in 1Q above the average for the full year. In both 2022 and 2023, 1Q core PCE was the highest quarterly print of the year. Thus, one reason for the high 1Q PCE results is that residual seasonality caused an upward skew on inflation numbers, which could correct through the year.

Similarly, even for CPI, particularly in the month of January and February, there are questions about whether there is residual seasonality. Nine of the last 10 core CPI prints for January, and 7 of the last 10 February prints, have surprised to the upside, an unusually skewed ratio that also suggests the possibility of residual seasonality.

With the Treasury's finances outlook improved, the Treasury would need to issue less to the market to keep its overall funding profile unchanged. The key aspect to note is that the impact of QT on USTs depends on the Treasury itself – it has to decide whether to reduce issuance in bills or coupons.

In this case, we think the answer might be rather straightforward – the Treasury can reduce bill sizes issued to the market to accommodate the impact of QT tapering.

So as the Fed tapers QT, it reinvests more in maturing coupons ( Exhibit 24 ) by increasing the sizes of add-ons in US Treasury auctions. This means the tapering of QT will improve the funding position Treasury via coupons. In a world where total funding needs don't change and the Treasury is getting more funding via coupons, the Treasury would then have to decide whether to lower coupons sizes or bills.

We think the Treasury will lower bill sizes offered to the market because the Treasury would not want to lower coupon sizes right after they increased them for three quarters (from August refunding up to February refunding) – maintaining the "regular and predictable" issuance strategy for coupons.

The idea of reducing net bill issuance to market also happens to work well because the Treasury's current issuance strategy has pushed the t-bills/total outstanding ratio a bit above TBAC's 15-20% recommended range ( Exhibit 98 ) . Reducing bills in response to QT tapering would help bring bills outstanding toward the recommended range.

## SSA Update – impact of a potential S&P French downgrade

Following a higher-than-expected 2023 deficit in France, the uncertainty around France's rating reviews increased. We think there is greater risk of a downgrade by S&P than by Moody's or Fitch as discussed in Significant Fiscal Risks for 2025 and Fiscal, Where Do We Go From Here?.

Many SSA names' ratings are linked to that of France's. Thus, this week, we investigate the impact on selected SSAs of a French downgrade S&P late May.

The results are summarized in Exhibit 36 for EU, ESM, EFSF, EIB, and Cades. According to our interpretation of recent S&P reports, Cades and EFSF would be downgraded to AAshould France be downgraded by one notch. It would likely be the case as well for all French names whose rating is aligned with that of France.

In such a case, Cades' average rating would decline from AA (Aa2) to AA- (Aa3) (average of the S&P, Fitch and Moody's ratings). For EFSF, the average rating would remain at AA amid a Aaa rating from Moody's.

On the other hand, the EU rating would be probably unaltered. The "anchor" rating, which is based on the nominal GDP-weighted average sovereign foreign currency rating on all member states, should remain at AA-, according to our computation. We note that we find the average rating would be very close to the limit between AA- and AA, but that the outlook is stable. A deterioration in the "anchor" rating juts above the limit could lead to a change of the outlook to negative, in our opinion.

Finally, we think ESM and EIB ratings would also be unchanged at AAA. These names seem less sensitive to France's rating amid, amongst others, significant callable capital buffers.

## Waiting for more constructive price action

The past week's focus was mainly on supply considerations given that we had: 1) The upward revision in this fiscal year gilt remit; and 2) The 30y syndication. On the former, the revision followed the CGNCR release for March, which implied a full FY 2023-24 number higher than OBR projections, leading to an increase of £12.4 billion compared with the forecast published at the Spring Budget 2024 for the current year gilt remit (which is now forecast to be £277.7 billion). While the revision was higher than expected, the market reaction was fairly muted, perhaps given that the increase in supply won't move the needle. In term of bucket split, shorts and mediums were increased the most (by £5.4 billion and £3.9 billion, respectively) while longs and index-linked were increased by £1 billion and £1.1 billion, respectively, similar to the unallocated portion (£1.1 billion). Proportion wise, the split between buckets has been kept broadly unchanged.

The same day we also had the flash April PMIs, which overall pointed to robust growth momentum, led by the services sector. Perhaps the most notable reading was the divergence between input costs accelerating (on the minimum wage uplift) and the prices charged index ticking down, which showed weakening corporate pricing power – that improves the disinflation outlook all other things being equal.

Having said that, the main week's event was probably the BoE's Chief Economist Huw Pill's speech, which was particularly in focus after the speech delivered by Deputy Governor Dave Ramsden. Indeed, the latter supported a fairly dovish market reaction in UK duration given the acknowledgement that inflation risks are now skewed to the downside. Pill's speech was instead more balanced, implying a more cautious tone by reiterating that the BoE likely has to remain restrictive in order to "squeeze" the persistent component of inflation when saying that "we are now seeing signs of a downward shift in the persistent component of inflation dynamics. But we still have a reasonable way to go before I am convinced that the persistent momentum in underlying inflation has stabilised at rates consistent with achievement of the 2% inflation target on a sustainable basis." Market reaction was fairly modest but clearly that phrase made rate cuts pretty unlikely at the upcoming May MPC, in our view.

Indeed, the May MPC will be the next important event in two weeks' time, while next week is fairly light in terms of domestic data releases, with euro area and US data releases likely to drive the price action. The lack of UK data should also prevent investors from reengaging in long duration trades until they get some stability in valuations and more constructive price action. We thus stay neutral on duration into the next week as we wait for more stable valuations, which despite being cheap by several metrics remain highly correlated to the US ones, and we think this might continue without a relevant catalyst. In this respect, the upcoming MPC is the most important one over the short term, and the market reaction will likely depend on the new set of projections, the messaging and perhaps the voting split.

On the new set of projections, as financial conditions have tightened since the February MPR, CPI forecasts should then be lower than in February, where the projections were somewhat mixed, given the relevant wedge between the ones based on market interest rate expectations and the one based on the current Bank Rate (see Exhibit 39 ) This time around the wedge should be narrower and CPI could be forecast at or below target both on a one-year and two-year horizon.

At the press conference, most questions from reporters concentrated on the potential impact of JPY weakness on monetary policy. BoJ governor Ueda answered that a monetary policy response might need to be considered in the event of exchange rate movements (i.e., JPY weakness) having a material impact on underlying inflation.

He said that the FX impact on inflation would essentially be temporary, but if JPY weakness were to be prolonged, it may produce higher inflation and could lead to higher wage growth at the next round of spring wage negotiation (i.e., second force). That said, he added that the majority seemed not to see recent JPY weakness as having a material impact on underlying inflation trends, as shown by the risk balance chart (see Exhibit 48 ). He also added that current import prices have not risen compared to 2022.

Overall, as our economists expected, the BoJ seems to have no intention to respond via monetary policy against recent JPY weakness.

Meanwhile, Ueda indicated in his post-meeting press conference that any further rate hikes would depend on future economic, price, and financial conditions, and he didn’t give any hints regarding near-term rate hikes.

That said, he suggested that if economic development continued to be in line with or higher than the BoJ's outlook, it could adjust monetary policy accordingly. He mentioned that the policy rate may be close to neural later in FY2025 to FY2026 should the economy continue to develop in line with the BoJ's outlook.

Finally, as for the future JGB purchase schedule, Ueda mentioned that he didn't want to use QT as an active policy tool. He said that there is no change to the JGB purchase schedule from March (i.e., roughly JPY 6tn/month), and any material change in purchase size would be discussed at the monetary policy meeting.

As we discussed in "Maintain Carry+Roll-Down Strategies", markets had priced in an almost 70% probability of the BoJ hiking in July and were perhaps hoping for some sort of hint in that direction. And there had also been some expectation (fueled in part by media reports) that the BoJ might opt to cut back its JGB purchases by way of response to the JPY’s continuing slide.

So Friday’s outcome may have been a disappointment in terms of: 1) no hints with regards to the timing of the next rate hike; 2) no serious concern for the recent JPY weakness at this juncture; and 3) leaving its guidelines for bond-buying operations unchanged.

## Enjoy carry + rolldown

With no meaningful hints with regards to the timing of the next rate hike, we think shortterm rate expectations will remain anchored for the time being given that it is likely to take at least a few more months for the BoJ to obtain adequate confirmation of: 1) the overall wage data consistent with spring wage hike results; 2) spring wage hikes feeding through to services inflation; and 3) also boosting real wages sufficiently to support a recovery in private consumption, etc.

Markets are as of yet still only pricing in (at most) 1.2 (25bp) rate hikes per year despite USD/JPY having climbed past 155 (see Exhibit 49 ). Moreover, recent bear-steepening of the JGB yield curve led by the super-long sector can be explained almost entirely by a widening of the term premium as UST yields have moved higher (see Exhibit 50 ). We expect that the curve will basically continue to either bear-steepen or bull-flatten in reaction to US interest rate movements so long as BoJ policy rate expectations stay anchored.

## Staying long 20y JGBs on 10s20s30s fly

Meanwhile, we think the belly of the curve can underperform on the curve. While the BoJ remains dovish despite recent JPY weakness, the market may speculate that the BoJ could be forced to hike rates at a rapid pace eventually should the JPY weakening trend continue (and a second force effect materialize). Hence, the market may require a higher term premium on the belly of the curve.

We meanwhile expect supply/demand headwinds to persist for the super-long (30y+) portion of the JGB yield curve. Stock market performance has deteriorated of late as US interest rates have climbed amid Fed “higher for longer” expectations, with our estimates suggesting that pension funds might accordingly need to rebalance out of domestic bonds into equities.

Moreover, as we discuss below, major life insurers’ FY2024 investment plans indicate that many are envisaging a further BoJ rate hike between July and October, and are forecasting that JGB yields will basically rise through the end of the fiscal year. It thus seems unlikely that lifers will be in any particular hurry to buy up super-long JGBs, although they have for the most part signaled an intention to add to their holdings.

Supply concerns are thus liable to keep weighing on sentiment, and we see a particularly high likelihood of the super-long portion of the curve facing steepening pressure when yields are declining given that lifers will probably see even less of an incentive to buy. Overall, we see ample room for the 20y sector to outperform vs 10y and 30y JGBs for the different reasons, respectively.

## Currency & Foreign Exchange

## G10

G10 | Keep your eye on the vol

Carry demand continues to dominate the FX landscape, but increasing risks of a regime 3 pivot have led investors to take a more defensive posture. The 'liability leg of carry trades has remained, with investors still short JPY, CHF, TWD and CNH, but the 'asset' legs have evolved from high-beta, high-risk currencies to USD.

The key factor driving regime 3 risks is decreasing 'trust' in the disinflation narrative. Multiple CPI surprises, even if for idiosyncratic reasons, have left investors less willing to believe that rate cuts this year are a fait accompli. It'll take at least a couple of CPI prints to convince investors on the inflation path, so as long as US growth holds up, 'regime 2.5' likely holds up at least until June.

Regimes 2 and 3 are similar for low yielders and USD, but very different for risk currencies. Long USD versus low yielders is thus the cleanest expression to hedge for either outcome. We continue to view short EUR/USD via options and long USD/ CHF straddles as attractive expressions of a regime 2.5 world.

Pricing of policy divergence should be inversely related to FX volatility. The more that central banks front-run Fed easing, the more their currencies weaken, which raises imported inflation. But if FX volatility is low, then the magnitude of imported inflation is lower all else equal for a given divergence in policy compared to higher vol.

It's a bold claim, admittedly. The ability of central banks to cut at the pace they'd prefer helps to limit risks to growth and inflation down the line. High FX volatility, hamstringing central banks to follow the Fed at higher-for-longer rates even if not locally warranted, raises risks of market volatility and a global slowdown. The stability of the global business cycle may ultimately depend on which regime we're in and the level of FX vol.

## Japan

JPY | Silent MoF despite further JPY weakness

We discuss why the MoF has not yet intervened in the market, despite further rapid JPY weakness. We suspect that the MoF may be looking for the best time to surprise the market, in order to maximize the impact of any FX intervention. The market seemed to have broadly expected it to happen once JPY weakened post the BoJ MPM, and most clients we talked to seemed to be waiting for the potential intervention as a good opportunity to buy USD/JPY on dips.

If the MoF tries to surprise the market, we should care about the potential risk of intervention even during Golden Week holidays, since the market would not normally be expecting the MoF to intervene in the market during the holiday season. If there is no intervention during the holiday season, however, and if the market assumes that moves by either the MoF or BoJ to support the currency are unlikely anytime soon, we see a risk that USD/JPY could approach as high as 160.00. Given the risk of rapid two-way movement, we remain neutral on JPY and shift our risk skew back to neutral as well.

We also discuss what the MoF will sell if it decides to intervene. We think it would likely fund the intervention via selling short-dated paper (or using redemption money). That said, repo transactions could effectively provide the MoF with a means of intervening first and then securing the necessary funding a little later. Hence, we think that MoF can minimize the impact of its intervention on the US Tbill market.

Divergence from the Fed raises risks of imported inflation via the FX channel: a weaker currency raises import prices, rendering the disinflationary trend harder to achieve. This is a particular concern for the ECB, for example, where a persistent 10% drop in EUR/USD generates a roughly 30bp increase in HICP over a 1-2-year period.

FX volatility and pricing of policy divergence should be negatively correlated: The impact of exchange rate changes on inflation is a function of actual nominal changes in the exchange rate. But the magnitude of exchange rate moves relative to driving forces like rate differentials is a function of FX volatility.

Put simply, if we are in a low FX volatility regime like regime 2, a given change in rate differentials generates a smaller move in FX, impacting imported inflation less. If we're in a high-volatility regime like regime 3, that same move in rate differentials could have a larger impact on FX and thus import prices all else equal.

As a result, if we stay in regime 2 for the next couple of months, there remains more scope for markets to price in policy divergence between the Fed and the ECB. But this also means that, if we shift to a higher-volatility regime like regime 3, FX could become more reactive, 'catching up' to rate moves, though in turn this makes future policy divergence less likely.

This circular positive stabilization makes for interesting amplification. Calm and benign conditions make policy divergence more likely, which allows central bankers to ease policy based on domestic conditions as they warrant. If policy mistakes are excluded, then this makes it less likely that we will see significant risks to growth and inflation as central bankers make the 'right' calls. This makes benign conditions and carry demand more likely.

On the other hand, an increase in market and FX volatility reduces the ability of central bankers to deviate from the Fed without risking imported inflation. Whether strong US growth is elevated because of fiscal policy, a positive supply shock, or perhaps both, the result is the same: US growth remains supported by factors that are unlikely to dissipate anytime soon.

If the Fed is therefore less able to normalize policy, and other central banks are forced to follow, the risk of a global slowdown emerging outside the US rises, rendering the US an even more attractive and safe destination (particularly given the carry). This amplifies the risks of growth and deflation shocks down the line – raising the chances of regime 4 perhaps.

Is FX vol the key to global markets this year? They say to a hammer everything is a nail, and as an FX strategist I'm naturally inclined to think that FX is important (perhaps more important than it really is). But the interplay between FX volatility, policy divergence pricing, and the actual ability of foreign central banks to deviate from the Fed seems important.

Regime 2 keeps us on the 'straight-and-narrow' for now and enables central bankers to begin normalizing perhaps faster than the Fed. But a spike in FX volatility and USD at other currencies' expense could prove an important catalyzing driver for the business and markets cycle overall.

Stay short EUR/USD and long FX vol: EUR/USD and FX vol remain important hedges for the eventual pivot out of regime 2. Policy divergence between the ECB and Fed, particularly in a low-vol FX world, should keep EUR/USD trading (gradually) lower, but risks relating to a convex catch-up keep us long vol via EUR/USD puts and via USD/CHF strangles.

## What will the MoF sell if it decides to intervene?

## How will we know the size of any MoF intervention?

Actual data on MoF interventions will only be made available at the end of each calendar month. However, interventions impact the BoJ’s balance sheet by causing government deposits to fluctuate. It is thus effectively possible to identify intervention by comparing the projections of tanshi money brokers for the BoJ’s daily surplus or shortage of funds— or more specifically, the “Treasury funds and others” component thereof—with the actual figure that ends up being disclosed by the central bank and looking for any major discrepancies. That said, it does need to be recognized that any spot market intervention will only become apparent two business days later following settlement.

With the Treasury's finances outlook improved, the Treasury would need to issue less to the market to keep its overall funding profile unchanged. The key aspect to note is that the impact of QT on USTs depends on the Treasury itself – it has to decide whether to reduce issuance in bills or coupons.

In this case, we think the answer might be rather straightforward – the Treasury can reduce bill sizes issued to the market to accommodate the impact of QT tapering.

So as the Fed tapers QT, it reinvests more in maturing coupons ( Exhibit 100 ), by increasing the sizes of add-ons in US Treasury auctions. This means the tapering of QT will improve the funding position Treasury via coupons. In a world where total funding needs don't change, and the Treasury is getting more funding via coupons, the Treasury would then have to decide whether to lower coupons sizes or bills.

We think the Treasury will lower bill sizes offered to the market, because the Treasury would not want to lower coupon sizes right after they increased them for three quarters (from August refunding up to February refunding) – maintaining the "regular and predictable" issuance strategy for coupons.

The idea of reducing net bill issuance to market also happens to work well because the Treasury's current issuance strategy has pushed the t-bills/total outstanding ratio a bit above TBAC's 15-20% recommended range ( Exhibit 98 ). Reducing bills in response to QT tapering would help bring bills outstanding toward the recommended range.

## Rates vol across macro regimes

This week's growth and inflation data have generated interest in stagflation (see Exhibit 103 ). Sound familiar? It should – Google trend data indicate that stagflation periodically comes to mind this time of year (see Exhibit 104 ).

With uncertainty surrounding the current economic regime, we turn to history to analyze volatility across economic scenarios. We find:

2. Implied vol has not experienced a period of stagflation over our post-2009 dataset. The vol surface shifted higher in both downturn and overheating environments, but expiry and tail structures performed quite differently.

## Defining the regimes

We follow the approach proposed by Bouyé and Teiletche (2024). Namely, we split history into four regimes based on growth and inflation environments. The four regimes are (1) downturn, (2) stagflation, (3) Goldilocks, and (4) overheating (see Exhibit 105 for definitions, and Exhibit 106 for historical occurrences).

We identify growth/inflation environments using Bouyé and Teiletche's approach:

• Growth regimes are defined using the NBER categorizations of "high growth" and "low growth";

• Inflation regimes are defined as high if two conditions are met: (1) the y/y CPI index is above 5%, and (2) the CPI run-rate has come as a surprise (e.g., y/y CPI is above 1y-ahead CPI expectations from the year prior).

## Implied/ex-post realized vol across regimes

Finally, we look at the ratio of implied/ex-post realized vol across regimes. This ratio is a useful approximation of vol positions' profitability, with a >1x (<1x) ratio suggesting that long (short) vol positions made money. Note, however, that this rule of thumb does not guarantee PnL (for more, see here).

We again use our backfilled SOFR vol dataset, and compute ex-post realized vol. We run our analysis on 2y, 10y, and 30y tails on 3m expiries (see Exhibit 117 ). We find:

• During downturns, forward swaps move than less swaptions would suggest. This is most pronounced for policy-sensitive 2y tails (median \~2.15x), and suggests that short-vol positions are profitable.

• During Goldilocks periods, swaptions are again overpriced relative to underlying swaps. Buying 2y tails was frequently profitable for longs, however (avg. 1.39x, but stdev. 0.55x). Selling longer tails proved a better risk-adjusted proposition.

• Overheating is the least profitable environment in which to sell vol. Selling longer, less policy-sensitive tails again proved the best risk-adjusted rewards, but average/ median ratios were very close to parity, and standard deviations were large.

Some investors might be asking whether gap risk is driving the elevated implied/ex-post realized vol during downturns. Indeed, downturns could be associated with rapid monetary policy easing, and thus large d/d moves in swap rates. Recall, for reference, that a d/d swap move of approximately 0.8 x implied vol x sqrt(time to expiry) will likely result in a net loss for a vol seller.

We analyze this by calculating the frequency of rate gaps across our regimes. We identify a rate gap as an occurrence of a d/d swap move at least as large as that implied by the formula above, in the 3m period following the observed implied volatility. Interestingly, we find that downturns do not displays material gap risks. Rather, periods of overheating show an elevated frequency of gaps on short tails.

## Trading Strategy 1 – "Trade Longs/Fade Shorts"

When the 5-day moving average crosses above the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract (long duration) and hold for a 25-business-day period. When the 5-day moving average crosses below the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract and hold for a 25-business-day period. In short, this strategy buys futures when the Simple Moving Average Crossover (SMAX) generates both a long and a short signal, given the historical outperformance of long signals traded long and underperformance of short signals traded short. Given that the SMAX could generate both a long and a short signal within the predefined holding period, an investor may have a 200% long position since each of the two signals would be traded in separate portfolio sleeves.

## Definition of terms

Buy/Long: The analyst expects the total or excess return (depending on the nature of the recommendation) of the instrument or issuer that is the subject of the investment recommendation to be positive over the relevant time period.

Sell/Short: The analyst expects the total or excess return (depending on the nature of the recommendation) of the instrument or issuer that is the subject of the investment recommendation to be negative over the relevant time period.

Selling protection or Buying Risk: The analyst expects that the price of protection against the event occurring will decrease over the relevant time period.

Buying protection or Selling Risk: The analyst expects the price of protection against the event occurring will increase over the relevant time period.

Pay: The analyst expects that over the specified time period the variable rate underlying the swap agreement that is the subject of the investment recommendation will increase.

Receive: The analyst expects that over the specified time period the variable rate underlying the swap agreement that is the subject of the investment recommendation will decrease.

Unless otherwise specified, the time frame for recommendations included in the Morgan Stanley Fixed Income Research reports is 1 - 3 months and the price of financial instruments mentioned in the recommendation is as at the date and time of publication of the recommendation.

When more than one issuer or instrument is included in a recommendation, analyst expects one part of the trade to outperform the other trade or combination of other trades included in the recommendation on a relative basis.
