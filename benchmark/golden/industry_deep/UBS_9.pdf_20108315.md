# China Equity Strategy Capital market with Chinese characteristics II: SOE valuation dilemma and potential solutions

## Why are SOEs undervalued by investors?

The share prices of SOEs have outperformed the broader market since 2023 due to increased defensiveness/dividend yields, elevated commodity prices and some concrete progress on SOE reforms—a 'valuation system with Chinese characteristics'. However, from a long-term perspective there still exists a large valuation gap between SOEs and non-SOEs. There are three main explanations for this, in our view: 1) a majority of SOEs are in commodity-related sectors with business models not appreciated by investors; 2) SOE profitability and operating efficiency lag non-SOE counterparts; 3) a number of ESG considerations have led to a negative perception of SOEs.

## Non-financial SOE valuation discounts not justified under classic theory

Excluding financials, we note non-SOE PE and P/BV multiples have been more than double those of SOEs since 2021. However, non-SOEs' earnings growth averaged 11% pa over 2014-23, only slightly higher than 9% for SOEs. Average annual ROE for non-SOEs over the past decade has been 9.9% versus 8.3% for all SOEs—in the past five years, non-SOEs have had no ROE advantage.

## Multiple ways to boost SOE valuations

We expect higher SOE valuations to support the development of a high-quality A-share market (see "Capital market with Chinese characteristics I: Strategic importance of the A-share market") and create more resources for China's technological advancement and state-owned capital transfer (see "Capital market with Chinese characteristics III: How to support an ageing China?"). We think SOE valuations could be enhanced through: 1) improving operating efficiency and ROE, 2) increasing dividend payouts or share buybacks, 3) restructurings and M&A, 4) aligning the interests of SOE management teams (eg, career goals and incentive schemes) with profitability and improving corporate governance and transparency. From a sector perspective, we group industries into three categories (A, B, C) to help investors generate stock selection ideas based on the different catalysts that exist for different sectors.

Just a day later, the State-owned Assets Supervision and Administration Commission of the State Council (SASAC) announced it would include market cap management results in the assessment of SOE executives. In addition, we note a flurry of key announcements and guidance issued in relation to the high-quality development of the capital market.

But we believe what really attracted investor attention to the SOEs is their recent stellar performance. Market sentiment has remained weak and domestic mutual funds have turned defensive in search of yield enhancement, making the VCC one of the most sought after themes in the market and SOEs the major beneficiaries. YTD, the CSI Central State-owned Enterprises Composite Index and CSI State-owned Enterprises Composite Index registered respective returns of 8.6% and 5.9% while the Wind All Ashare index returned a mere 0.2%. In addition, SOEs usually pay out high dividends. In 2023, the dividend yield of all A-share SOEs reached an average of 3.4%, almost double that of non-SOEs.

In addition, geopolitical tensions have been elevated since 2022, boosting commodity prices. As we discuss in the following section, as upstream sectors are usually dominated by large SOEs which benefit from rising commodity prices, we think it is natural these SOEs gain the most attention from market participants.

## Why investors undervalue SOEs?

From a long-term perspective, investors have substantially undervalued SOEs in both the A-share and Hong Kong equity markets. Current SOE valuations are only half those of non-SOEs in both PE and P/BV terms, and the gap has widened in recent years. In our view, this valuation difference is due to three major reasons, namely sector distribution, operating efficiency and negative investor perception.

We believe there are numerous cyclical and structural issues behind the valuation gap, which has lasted for decades. In our view, there are three main explanations.

## Sector distribution

First, sector distribution explains a significant part of the performance gap. A majority of SOEs are in commodity-related sectors. Coal is the most extreme case with 95% of total market capitalisation in the sector belonging to SOEs. In fact, commodity-related sectors are usually with high entry barriers and are dominated by a few large SOEs. As a result, we think their low valuations are partly due to earnings volatility and investor perception.

As we discuss in our A-share macro equity strategy framework, there is a negative correlation between upstream profitability and overall A-share market valuations. We note overall market valuations could trend down if upstream sectors have a higher percentage of earnings versus total A-share profits. Downstream sectors and companies are usually established on technological innovation and marketing efforts. Their business models are more appreciated by investors, in our view, leading to higher valuation levels. The problems for downstream sectors caused by elevated upstream profitability are more pronounced during years when aggregate demand shrinks.

## Mind the gap 1: PE-earnings growth perspective

From a PE multiple perspective, non-financial SOEs have been trading at an increasingly steep discount to their non-SOE peers to the extent that non-SOE PE multiples have been more than double those of SOEs since 2021. Could this valuation gap be explained by differences in earnings growth?

To some extent, SOE earnings growth is indeed slower. When we compare the earnings results of non-financial A-share SOEs with non-SOEs, we can clearly see that non-SOE annual profit growth outstripped SOEs in six of the last 10 years. However, if we make an aggregate comparison, the lead is not as alarmingly wide with average pa profit growth of 11% for non-SOEs compared with 9% for SOEs over a span of 10 years (2014-23).

In recent years, it has been more difficult for non-SOEs to sustain their lead in earnings growth. Although non-SOEs greatly outperformed SOEs in 2020, the latter grew more than four times as fast as the former in the following year during a much more challenging business environment. However, if we take valuations into consideration, the picture starts to look muddled. SOEs, whose profit has grown much faster since 2021, are trading at a steeper discount to non-SOEs. The discount only narrowed in 2023 as VCC gathered popularity.

## Mind the gap 2: P/BV-ROE perspective

PE valuations aside, we note a big chunk of the difference in P/BV valuations between SOEs and non-SOEs cannot be easily explained by differences in ROE. Hence, we have coined the term 'non-ROE factors' to try to capture that portion of valuations unexplained by ROE differences. Non-ROE factors are the difference between non-SOEs' P/BV premium over SOEs and non-SOEs' ROE advantage over SOEs.

In order to gauge how much of a valuation multiple, P/BV in this case, is determined by sector ROE, we compare the P/BV premium of non-financial non-SOEs over SOEs along with non-financial non-SOEs' ROE advantage over SOEs.

We have found that out of 25 non-financial sectors, only 14 have P/BV ratios that can be explained by their ROE. Although there are some sectors, such as non-ferrous metals and real estate, whose almost entire valuation gaps that can be explained by the difference in ROE, other sectors such as textiles and agriculture suggest operating efficiency is next to irrelevant in explaining the valuation differences. In other words, non-ROE factors for 11 out of 25 sectors are simply too large to ignore.

## Why we need higher SOE valuations?

As discussed, the significant valuation discount of SOEs as a whole against non-SOE peers appears unjustified from the perspective of both PE to earnings growth, and P/BV to ROE. Not only are persistent SOE valuation woes a financial disappointment, they are a pressing issue as both the healthy development and high valuations of SOEs are critical to China for multiple reasons.

## Technological advancement and national security

During the Two Sessions at this year's National People's Congress, "new quality productive forces" became a heatedly discussed phrase and was subsequently written into the government's work report. We believe SOEs are meant to be the champions in China's next-stage pursuit of technological advancement as these fields generally require significant levels of investment and resources. Unlike some of the progress achieved in the technology, media and telecoms (TMT) space in the last decade, where a bottom-up revamp of business models prevailed, industries such as AI, renewables, EVs, high-end manufacturing and modern-day healthcare require deep technological knowhow as well as broad based coordination. Against an ever more complicated geopolitical backdrop, we think SOEs could step up and lead the next phase of China's economic development. With higher valuations, SOEs would be equipped with more resources to support technological advancement.

## SOEs' social service

Although operating results such as sales or profits are the mainstream review metrics of the performance of modern-day companies, SOEs shoulder more social responsibility and occasionally need to provide a 'social service'. Despite comprising less than 10% of China's total number of enterprises, SOEs provide roughly 20% of urban labour employment, 30% of technological innovation achievements, 40% of GDP and 50% of tax revenue. A stronger cohort of SOEs is beneficial not only to economic success but also for social stability.

## SOE and non-SOE symbiosis

SOEs and non-SOEs are not stand-alone entities but frequently interact with each other. Many SOEs are the upstream of non-SOEs, and at the same time they also hold stakes in them. Many non-SOEs have their interests aligned with their SOE counterparts. Sustainable growth of SOE operating metrics could most of the time directly boost the results of the non-SOEs working with them. In this case, we think SOEs and their non-SOE counterparties should not be competing but developing a symbiotic relationship.

## State-owned capital transfer to support an ageing society

In 2017, China began transferring state-owned capital to its social security fund, with operational and investment rules relaxed in March 2024. We estimate Rmb400bn of cash dividends (9% of current annual basic pension income) could be used to supplement pension payments in 2030. Given the widening pension deficit, reinvestment and capital operation are needed to create additional value.

We think SOE reforms are set to accelerate to boost profitability and payout ratios. In fact, 'market value' has recently been added as part of SOE performance reviews. Additionally, we think regulators proposed a valuation system with Chinese characteristics with the goal of reversing investors' negative perception of SOEs (see "Capital market with Chinese characteristics III: How to support an ageing China?").

## Dividend payouts and share buybacks easily tracked

In our view, there are multiple ways to increase SOE valuations. In general, these include: 1) improving operating efficiency and ROE, 2) increasing dividend payouts or share buybacks, 3) restructurings and M&A, 4) aligning the interests of SOE management teams (eg, via career goals and incentive schemes) with their profitability and improving corporate governance and transparency.

We note many fundamental investors are unlikely to react without first seeing concrete evidence of improved SOE operating efficiency and profitability. Two easily followed metrics are dividend payouts and buyback volumes, and we have seen promising developments in both in recent years. The dividend yield of all A-share SOEs picked up materially from 2.3% in 2020 to 3.4% in 2023, while total buyback volumes by listed SOEs rose to Rmb33bn in 2023, a CAGR of more than 17% since 2013. We think continued improvement in dividend yields coupled with growing buyback volumes would convince fundamental investors to consider VCC as a long-term theme and attract investors to explore the investment opportunities among high-quality SOEs.

## Is M&A a viable option?

On 1 October 2023, the Party Committee of the SASAC published a article in Qiushi, a leading Party magazine, saying it was necessary to further ramp up the intensity of market-oriented integration and restructuring of SOEs. It pointed out that SOEs in the integrated circuit, industrial machinery, equipment manufacturing, electronics, IT, healthcare and testing sectors should accelerate the integration and reorganisation process in their industries while SOEs in the renewable energy, mineral resource, pipeline network and ports & terminals sectors should focus on horizontal integration that promotes the rational flows and optimal allocation of state-owned capital.

There are also multiple references to M&A in the recently published 'Nine-point' guidelines ('guidelines')—eg, the third guideline encourages listed companies to focus on their main business, and comprehensively use M&A to improve the quality of development. The fourth point demands further reduction in the value of "shell" resources while strengthening the supervision of M&A; point eight calls for accelerating M&A reforms while taking multiple measures to invigorate the M&A market.

## SOE priority seems to be general industrials

If we break down SOEs' M&A activity by sector over 2020-23, we note some clear preferences and patterns. At the other end of the spectrum are the household & personal products, telecommunication services and bank industries. The telecommunication services sector is already dominated by the big three SOE telecoms giants, which renders further consolidation unlikely, although there could be chances of vertical integration with the aim of controlling the value chain and gaining competitiveness. ## Greater transparency may rewrite story

Company visits, either in terms of analyst meetings or press conference, reflect the transparency and openness of a listed company. On the one hand, monthly company visits to non-SOEs have consistently remained more than 80% of total company visits to listed companies since January 2023, indicating a willingness to communicate with investors and transparent company operations.

SOEs, meanwhile, have long been perceived by the public as a cohort of opaque entities where disclosures and announcements are hard to obtain. This long-lasting perception is backed by the substantially fewer SOE company visits compared to non-SOE peers.

However, we have observed that SOEs are motivated to change that negative perception —company visits to SOEs more than tripled in April versus what they were in February when VCC was introduced. We also note the number of company visits to SOEs as a percentage of total company visits quickly rose in April to 25 right after the CSRC reemphasised the need to "accelerate the development of a modern capital market with Chinese characteristics" and the SASAC added 'market value' to SOE management teams' performance reviews. As SOE management interests have been more aligned with those of the companies, we expect corporate governance to improve from here.

## Bridging the gap—a sector perspective Category A: Enhancing ROE should be sufficient

Looking deeper at the sector level, we can see SOEs from different sectors face different obstacles. We have grouped A-share level-1 industries (excluding conglomerates, banks and non-bank financials) into three categories based on the different measures available to SOEs to bridge the valuation gap with non-SOE peers. Some of the valuation gap is the result of SOEs' low operating efficiency, which could be fixed to some extent through more thorough SOE reforms. Some of the valuation gap cannot be resolved because SOEs in these sectors already appear overpriced by investors as the non-ROE factors are in their favour. The rest are mainly due to negative investor perception, which requires further investor education. In the following section, we take a closer look and offer solutions that SOEs use to catch up with non-SOE peers in terms of valuation.

In category A, we identify sectors where enhancing ROE should be sufficient for SOEs to catch up with non-SOEs in terms of valuation. The five sectors in this first category are steel, machinery, non-ferrous metal, electronics and real estate. They share a trait whereby most of their non-SOE peers' valuation premium can be explained to some extent by their greater operating efficiency. In other words, we believe share prices in these five industries are dictated by ROE. We also believe that if the SOEs in these five industries are able to gradually catch up with their non-SOE peers in terms of operating efficiency, the valuation gap may narrow.

Figure 30: Enhancing ROE should bridge the valuation gap in these sectors
![](images/21789b3927595037bd8cdab5ca3ba4b504ce9cba4ab0f4e0b6fadfabd13c4e2b.jpg)
Source: Wind, UBS-S estimates. Note: P/BV premium & ROE advantage calculated using avg. 2010 -23 numbers

Two SOE weaknesses stood out to us when we talked to UBS-S sector analysts: 1) SOEs usually operate at lower efficiency levels, which typically comes from a bloated workforce and insufficient incentives; 2) SOEs are usually unequivocal leaders in their particular sectors, but the business itself inherently carries a thin margin. We acknowledge the fact SOEs shoulder more social responsibility, which means neither of the two weaknesses mentioned can be addressed overnight, but we think any improvements or changes could be interpreted as a gesture to the investing community.

Using the steel sector as an example, non-SOEs have commanded a valuation premium over SOEs of around 133% since 2010, backed by a similar lead of 161% in ROE over SOE peers. If we break down the ROE, we can see the weaker ROE of SOEs is the result of their much lower profitability and asset turnover. Based on our labour cost calculations and feedback from UBS-S sector analysts, steel SOEs generally keep much larger workforces and offer an overall more generous compensation package to employees. The two combined have resulted in a bloated labour budget.

Another reason is that steel SOEs and non-SOEs are in different businesses—steel SOEs are concentrated in the low-margin carbon steel business while non-SOEs have specialised in the more lucrative special steel and pipeline businesses. This also helps explain why 12 out of 28 steel SOEs registered net losses in 2023, while all of the 17 non-SOEs posted positive earnings.

We believe with higher output per capita and a gradual transformation in business mix, SOEs in the steel sector should be able to partially offset some of their disadvantages and narrow their valuation gap with non-SOE peers.

## Category B: Higher efficiency not fully priced in

In category B, we identify sectors where enhancing ROE would only be able to lift SOE valuations modestly. The five sectors in this second category are home appliances, electrical equipment, textiles, agriculture, and light manufacturing. Like the SOEs in category A, SOEs in the second category lag non-SOEs in terms of ROE—however, the non-SOE valuation premium does not fully reflect their advantage in business operation terms. Although SOEs in this category's five sectors could enhance ROE, their valuations may only catch up to a certain extent as they are already overpriced.

The home appliances sector offers some insight into this phenomenon. Looking at how they have traded, we note valuations of SOEs and non-SOEs in this sector converged over 2010-16 but widened from 2017 onwards. In 2019, when industry leaders were consolidating, SOEs traded at a valuation low that was half that of non-SOEs.

We believe the premium is due to non-SOEs' superior NPM and market leadership. The margin difference between non-SOEs and SOEs using Dupont analysis in the home appliances sector is by far the largest of all 25 sectors. Although these stark profitability differences are partially offset by SOEs' higher asset turnover and leverage, SOE ROEs are still around roughly half of non-SOEs. Based on our conversations with the UBS-S home appliances analyst, non-SOEs usually lead their niche markets where they benefit from economies of scale and thus command outsized pricing power. We believe the pricing heft of SOEs may naturally come with market share gains.

However, we do not think SOE share prices can improve as much as the current ROE differences suggest. Even if SOEs can catch up with non-SOEs in terms of profitability, we think their share prices are unlikely to rise by as much as they are already 'overpriced'.

## Category C: New businesses & investor education

Most sectors fall into category C where the SOEs can use a combination of new business development and focus on growth to boost their valuations. The valuation woes of SOEs in this category are also partly attributable to investors' deeply rooted negative perception of SOEs, as explained by the non-ROE factors in our calculations. In this case, our view is that more investor education may help the investing community form an unbiased, if not more justifiably positive perception, of SOEs.

In addition to traditional chemicals, non-SOEs are spread across subsectors including new materials, nationaldefence-related materials, building materials and cosmetics, all of which have higher profit margins and much better growth prospects.
