## Aluminium

## Will alumina cuts drive aluminium price gains?

## Supply cuts lift spot alumina price from a protracted period in the cost curve

At seaborne (FOB Australia) spot prices of <\$350/t in 2023 alumina producer margins were unsustainably low; but in highly integrated supply chains like bauxite/alumina/ aluminium cash burn can continue for extended periods. In 2024 supply has finally responded driving China alumina futures up \~30% in the last 2 weeks and seaborne spot prices lifting from <\$350/t in Dec-23 to \~\$375/t. Supply developments: (1) Guinea bauxite supply: \~70% of China bauxite imports are from Guinea and an explosion at an oil terminal in Conakry in late Dec triggered concerns on supply of bauxite for China's refineries (\~55% global output). Thus far we are not aware of any disruption to bauxite output/exports from Guinea; but risks of fuel shortages are likely to remain elevated in 1Q24. (2) China cuts: The combination of environmental checks, concerns over bauxite availability and thin/negative refinery margins triggered \~2.5mt of alumina refinery curtailments (Hongqiao 1.2mtpa and Bosai 1.2mtpa) in late Dec/early Jan. (3) Alcoa cut: On 8th Jan Alcoa announced the closure of the 2.2mtpa Kwinana refinery in Australia (WA); the refinery had been operating at \~80% capacity since Jan-23 so the closure will remove \~1.75mt from the Pacific market from 3Q24.

## Lift from 'unsustainably' low level…but alumina well supplied medium-term

Alumina is difficult to transport/store resulting in 'regional markets' in the Pacific & Atlantic; closure of Kwinana will tighten the Pacific market and is likely to drag the seaborne price from deep into the cost curve closer to marginal cost. Near-term bauxite availability from Guinea may be a constraint for Chinese refineries; but in our view the closure of Kwinana (maybe others) is unlikely to create a structural shortage of alumina. China has installed alumina capacity of close to 100mt, if/when China achieves maximum aluminium output (45mtpa @ >95% utilisation) alumina demand would be \~85mt; therefore China has structural overcapacity and is likely to remain the swing alumina producer but adjusting its net trade position (LTM net imports \~600kt). As a result we do not expect sustainable alumina price upside materially above the cost curve.

## Higher alumina prices lift aluminium cost curve...but demand recovery needed

Like alumina, the aluminium price has spent most of the last 15yrs at or into the cost curve. The increase in the alumina price will increase cost pressure for non-integrated smelters at the top of the cost curve; but moderating gas/power prices in Europe (marginal producer) are likely to provide some offset. Profitability for European smelters towards the top of the cost curve is marginal; but in our view the recent lift in alumina is unlikely to trigger another round of smelter cuts (we do not expect any of the \~1.5mt of curtailed capacity to be restarted near-term). Whilst the lift in alumina gives us confidence that LME price downside is limited (prices essentially bouncing off the cost curve in current \$2,100-2,300/t range); in our view, a demand recovery is needed to drive more sustainable price upside. We prefer copper vs aluminium, but when the economic cycle turns (resulting in restocking) and demand improves, supply upside in aluminium is limited and we expect tighter S&D fundamentals to drive higher prices in 2H24/25.

## Equities: S32, NHY, Chalco & Hongqiao have 'some' alumina exposure

NHY: After the sale of a 30% stake in Alunorte NHY net alumina position is balanced; but higher alumina price would drive consensus upgrades (+10% alumina -> \~5% lift to EBITDA). S32 has a \~3mt net long position in Alumina and S32's Worsley refinery in WA (\~200km from Kwinana) should see indirect benefits from lower gas prices & easing

## Q: China: Will capacity be capped at 45mtpa? How much will supply lift in 2024?

Probably. Although China appears to be less focused on its environmental agenda near-term, we have seen no suggestion it will abandon its targeted 45mt cap to capacity. We see recycling as the next supply driver domestically; but scrap supply has been slow to pick up post Covid and the fragmented nature of the industry makes it difficult to have conviction on scrap. Chinese players have suggested they may look to expand in Indonesia and a 500kta smelter appears likely to enter construction FY24; but we think it is unlikely that Indonesia will replicate capacity additions seen in China, or other industries (nickel). With a 45Mt capacity cap, we believe maximum output is likely to be 43-44mtpa; this implies a further \~1.0mtpa growth in China production (vs Oct-23 rate); equivalent to <2% of global supply. Water availability/power issues in Yunnan have constrained the ramp-up of Chinese output towards 45mtpa; after 7mths (Apr-Oct) of consistent increases, output reports suggest power shortages will result in curtailments of \~1mtpa in Yunnan over winter, reducing the probability of China achieving 'maximum output' in 2024. Medium-term we expect growth in Chinese demand and capped primary output to result in a structural decline in net exports.

## Q: RoW: Are aluminium prices/power costs enough to incentivise 'new' supply?

Unlikely. High power costs have driven supply cuts of \~1.5mt in Europe & the US over the last 2yrs. The decline in energy prices has helped ease margin pressure for smelters exposed to power price fluctuations; but this has been largely offset by the decline in the LME aluminium price and premiums. We believe higher aluminium prices/ further declines in power costs are needed to see European smelters restart and we assume capacity remains offline in 2024 but factor in some capacity returns in the next 2-3 years. Russia continues to operate its aluminium smelters close to capacity. Beyond restarts, the ROW smelter project pipeline is thin, with key sources of potential new projects likely to be (1) Indonesia; and (2) Middle East. We estimate an LME price of >\$2,500/t is needed to generate acceptable returns on new smelters in markets with access to renewable power; and (unlike for commodities like copper) companies appear reluctant to commit to new smelters.

## Q: Is the medium-term demand outlook for aluminium robust?

Yes. Like other industrial commodities, aluminium has been exposed to a slowdown in global economic growth in 2022/23 and headwinds from China's property/construction. However, in our view, the structural drivers of Al demand are: (1) vehicle light-weighting (including EVs); (2) exposure to structural growth in renewables capacity (solar); (3) growth in electrical transmission (supported by some copper substitution); and (4) substitution away from plastics in packaging. In our view, this will drive robust demand growth medium-term, albeit growth is expected to moderate vs the \~5% CAGR over the past 15 years. Demand has been weak in 2023 (particularly vs Cu) with Al less geared to the energy transition (renewables/grid) and more exposed to weak manufacturing/construction and a longer supply chain makes it vulnerable to destocking. However, at some point destocking will turn to restocking (2H24?), and we model 2024 demand growth of \~2.5% accelerating to >3% in 2025, but demand for primary is expected to be partly offset by a recovery in scrap supply.

Long-term beneficiary of China emissions tightening: Over the past 10-15yrs, aluminium has suffered from over-supply due to aggressive capacity additions in China; if China caps its capacity at 45mtpa, we believe this will drive lower net exports and this will drive up margins for aluminium producers medium-term. We do not expect a sharp rebound in demand in 1H24 with activity in Europe/US likely to remain subdued and a build in products (solar) inventory in China may weigh on metal demand; but in our view demand downside is limited and the end of destocking/restocking will drive a demand recovery at some point (2H24?). China still has the scope to lift production by \~1mt before hitting the effective 45mt capacity cap; but this looks more likely to happen in 2H24/25, when we expect an improving demand environment. In our view, the probability of material oversupply pushing prices deeper into the cost curve is limited and aluminium is well positioned when the demand/economic cycle turns.

Monthly production run rate: In our view, monthly Chinese & ROW aluminium production rates will be the key indicator of the magnitude of the supply-side response to margins/prices.

Prices close to marginal cost: In our view, the fundamentals for aluminium are better than they have been for most of the past 10-15 years and we expect prices to lift to above the marginal cost.

## Global base metals inventory tracker

Exchange inventories (LME, SHFE and COMEX) are readily available, but, in our view, they do not tell the whole story (particularly in China). We collate most of the global base metal inventory data available to us and believe this provides a clearer picture of the broader supply/demand dynamics; however, we note that significant additional inventory is likely to exist in metal supply chains or unreported financial inventories.

## Weekly inventory moves

Copper: Despite strong refined output (+10% Jan-Nov) seasonally adjusted visible China inventories are at the lowest level in recent years, highlighting robust underlying demand. LME inventories remain at historically low levels, but increased \~135kt (>200%) in Sept ( vs Jul-23 low) and have remained stable in 4Q23/early 2024. In our view generally weak physical market signals (premiums & spreads) are inconsistent with very low visible inventories, suggesting the majority of copper inventory is likely to to be 'unreported', however in our view copper inventories are still likely to be modest.

Aluminium: In 2023, LME inventories lifted risen, driven by inflows of Russian metal into Asian warehouses. We note that \~90% of LME stocks are held in two warehouses in Asia and we therefore question how useful LME stocks are as an indicator of industrial inventory in developed markets. Despite a sustained increase in China smelter output & elevated imports from 2Q23 -> Oct-23, visible inventories in China remain low and have broadly followed seasonal patterns. However, we note that much of the uplift in smelter output is molten metal that is cast into products, so increases in supply in 2023 are likely to have resulted in a build in products rather than ingot inventory.

Zinc: Inventories remained low through most of 2023 despite weak demand and building refined surplus; but in Nov \~105kt of zinc was delivered into warehouses in Asia resulting in a >3x increase in exchange inventory. Some of the metal was reportedly locked into a warehouse financing deal (LINK). In our view, this is another reminder that the majority of metal inventories exist outside of exchange warehouses.

Nickel: Fundamentals for nickel diverged in 2022/23, with oversupply in NPI/FeNi and NiSO4, but bottlenecks in converting NiSO4 into metal kept the refined market tight an inventory low. But LME inventory has been gradually lifting from 4Q23 and the registration of more 'new' LME nickel brands may see inventory build further in the future.

## Aluminium

Visible aluminium inventories declined from a peak of \~7mt in 2014 and settled in a range of 2-3mt over 2017-21. ## Nickel

Tightness in the nickel metal market triggered a consistent drawdown in nickel inventories in 2022, with visible inventories declining >50% over the past 18 months and now close to the lowest level since 2007. However, in 2022/23 the nickel market became increasing bifurcated and bottlenecks preventing arbitrage between class 1 vs class 2 and China vs ROW have resulted in a disconnect in nickel pricing, with persistent tightness in ROW metal markets driving LME drawdown vs oversupply in NPI and NiSO4. In 2H23 this disconnect has started to unwind, resulting in an increase in LME inventories.
