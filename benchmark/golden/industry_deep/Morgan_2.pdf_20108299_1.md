We expect somewhat slower business investment this year than last year, followed by some reacceleration. There are strong crosscurrents: equipment spending eventually turning upward after last year's decline; slower structures investment than last year but at the same time growing more broadly. The basic boost to our forecasts comes from faster employment growth, which puts pressure on the existing capital stock. Housing is in short supply. We expect a continued rise in residential investment through 2025, with a rapid rise in housing starts, solid new home sales, and a bit more turnover in existing home sales as mortgage rates fall. The labor market turns the corner as faster immigration expands the labor supply. The mid-pandemic labor market was characterized by shortage. Now, though, the labor supply has surged, and demand for labor is likely to moderate. Fast immigration has delivered a positive supply shock, allowing rapid employment and income growth, while adding some slack to a tight labor market. Our forecasts for payroll growth are much higher than last November, the unemployment rate is also higher. Payroll gains average 225k this year and 186k next year. The unemployment rate ends this year 0.5 point higher compared to 2023 at 4.2% this year, rising another 0.3 point to 4.5% next year. See Labor Market Outlook .

1Q24 saw substantial reflation, but weaker prints are coming, especially in 2H24. Rents, goods, and other services lead the descent. In recent research we found that residual seasonality also played a role in the recent upswing, suggesting payback in 2H24. We forecast core PCE Q4/Q4 at 2.7% in 2024 and 2.1% in 2025. See Inflation – Disinflation Reboot .

The Fed holds policy at a 5.375% peak for this cycle until September 2024, when we expect rate cuts to start. Faster and volatile 1Q24 inflation data has delayed Fed easing, but after renewed evidence of disinflation, we expect the first rate cut in September. The four additional CPI reports before the September FOMC meeting are enough to give the Fed confidence to lower rates. Thereafter, inflation indicators at or below target on a sequential basis, slower GDP growth, and a rising unemployment rate spur the Fed to continue cutting at every meeting through June 2025. Three 25bp cuts this year and four next year leave the funds rate at 3.625% in mid-2025. The Fed has announced it will begin to phase out QT in June, as anticipated; we expect it to end by March 2025. See Monetary Policy .

We think federal fiscal expansion has peaked for now. Tax revenues have rebounded, and we have moved past the ramp-up in spending from key bills. The post-election deficit is uncertain, though both parties have achieved most of their policy priorities that had a large impact on the deficit. Important post-election policies that can impact the deficit and economic activity will be tariffs, immigration, and taxes. We forecast the government spending contribution to 4Q/4Q real GDP growth to slow from 0.8pp in 2023 to 0.2pp in 2024 and 0.1pp in 2025. See Fiscal Policy .

## Upside/Downside Scenarios

In the upside scenario, a more resilient Chinese economy feeds into higher import prices in the US, keeping the Fed high for longer and weighing on domestic demand. Higher energy prices boost headline CPI through 2025. The pass-through to core goods prices raises core inflation by two tenths this year and next relative to our baseline. There’s demand destruction because of the slower disinflation, and consumption moderates while higher import prices offset a light tailwind to exports. Faster core inflation this year and next pushes out the first Fed cut into late 2024, where we expect one cut at the end of 2024 and 100bp in cuts next year. We forecast GDP growth at 2.0% 4Q/4Q in 2024 and 1.7% in 2025.

In the downside scenario, persistent services inflation in the US leads the Fed to stay higher for even longer, slowing the economy more significantly. Growth is slightly lower in 2024 as financial conditions remain restrictive, but 2025 sees much weaker growth, below 1% as restrictive policy bites deeper. Growth falling below 1% in 1H25 raises recession fears and inflation begins to descend towards 2.5%. This prompts the Fed to start cutting in June 2025 to lower real rates, delivering 150bp in cuts over the back of the year. We forecast GDP growth at 1.9% 4Q/4Q in 2024 and 0.8% in 2025.

## What Gets the Fed to Hike?

What credible scenarios could cause the Fed to alter its path with a hike, either as the next move or as an interruption to a cutting cycle they’d begun?

In the scenario where they are cutting and then hike, there’d likely be a lag — a transition between the cuts and a hike that could last some time.

Supply shocks would have to change the conditions causing disinflation.

• In services, labor supply is important. Policies that slow immigration could have an impact. Although they’d slow both supply and demand, they’d be inflationary at the margin. Trump has raised the possibility of deportations (which he also talked about on the 2016 campaign trail); a swing from rapid immigration to outright outflows would of course have a larger effect.

• Don't look to tariffs. There were very little inflationary effects from tariffs in 2018. Dollar appreciation, falling imports from China, and a macro stumble in the US from the hit to manufacturing. Inflation wobbled briefly, but ultimately these effects mitigated the price shock.

## We could be wrong about current disinflation.

We think that labor market conditions support disinflation: a slowly rising unemployment rate and gradually slowing wage inflation. In prices, the 1Q surge reflected noisy categories along with some pressures on insurance that are likely to slacken. See Inflation – Disinflation Reboot .

If we’re wrong about 1Q exaggeration, the most likely candidates to cause some pressure are:

• In services, healthcare prices remain strong — demand is rising quickly as are prices, and job openings suggest this is one area of continued labor shortage.

• In 1Q, most categories' of goods prices actually fell faster than they had been falling. The pickup in goods inflation was limited to three categories (apparel, videotapes, software). Perhaps the broader 1Q price declines were exaggerated and reverse a bit.

And of course, the further out, the less certainty. Our rent forecasts (and others) for late next year have wide error bands.

In short, the Fed is convinced that they’re restrictive. If progress in disinflation stays stalled, they delay cuts — they’ve told us so, and it’s consistent with this conviction. To hike, conditions have to change enough to persuade them otherwise: a pickup in inflation and evidence from spending and employment that policy was not restrictive.

## Risk to Immigration in 2025

Immigration has significant implications for population growth, labor supply, and GDP. In our baseline, we assume that net immigration remains at 3.3 million in 2024 before falling to 2.6 million in 2025 (population growth 0.9%Y), on the way back to its pre-COVID pace closer to 1 million. Breakeven payrolls are 265k in our baseline for 2024 and 210k for 2025.

However, there's considerable uncertainty as immigration policy could change. We lay out our five 2025 immigration scenarios and their implications for population growth and breakeven payrolls.

The rapid immigration scenario would have flows in 2025 in line with 2023 and 2024 at 3.3 million. This would result in population growth of 1.4%Y, slightly stronger than 2024 due to growth in the native-born population and breakeven payrolls at 265k/month.

In our three slower immigration scenarios, net immigration ranges from 1.4 million, which would be aligned with the Bipartisan Border Agreement (BBA) target, all the way to zero. In the BBA scenario, net immigration slowing to 1.4 million would result in 0.8%Y population growth and breakeven payrolls at 135k/year. In the normalized scenario immigration is in line with the pre-COVID average of 800k, with 0.6%Y population growth and breakevens similar to pre-Covid at 87k/month. In the deportation scenario net immigration is at zero, caused by low immigration and a rise in emigration (likely due to stricter deportation policies). The population growth is entirely driven by the native-born population, at 0.2%Y, and breakeven payrolls at 45k.

In any post-election outcome, we see stricter immigration policy as highly likely but the extent of changes to immigration policies and enforcement is open to question. The five scenarios outlined in Exhibit 10 include details on policy changes that could be expected in a second Biden or Trump administration.

A constriction in labor supply acts as a negative supply shock and would be inflationary. In this scenario, we think the Fed would likely need to keep rates higher for longer, but its reaction would be delayed as negative labor supply slowly begins to feed through into higher inflation.

## Immigration Boosts Potential GDP Growth

One key variable that guides monetary policy is potential GDP, which measures the level of economic activity consistent with a correct supply-demand balance and inflation at target. GDP levels above potential GDP imply that the economy is overheating and inflationary pressures are building.

Like r\*, potential GDP is a counterfactual and hard to measure in real time. It requires estimating structural models, and the estimates can vary depending on the structure of each model. Besides, potential GDP can change at high frequency too; changes in the labor force, productivity, tax policy, or incentives to invest can all affect potential GDP.

The Fed regularly publishes a longer-run GDP growth estimate, which basically represents trend GDP growth. This estimate can be interpreted as an approximation of potential GDP growth once the effect of near-term factors fades — in other words, a level around which true potential GDP growth fluctuates. The median forecast published in the Summary of Economic Projections has been between 1.8% and 2% since 2015.

As we have pointed out in previous research, the US economy has been shocked by an important positive supply shock: immigration. As a result, we updated our population growth estimates meaningfully, moving civilian non-institutionalized population growth from 0.8% to 1.4% in 2023 and 2024, and from 0.7% to 1.1%. And this update has important implications for potential GDP. Larger population due to higher past immigration flows means more production capacity and higher potential GDP levels, which in turn imply a narrower output gap and less inflationary pressures ahead.

Also, higher future immigration flows suggest faster potential GDP growth in the next couple of years. How much higher? It is hard to estimate with precision, but the Fed’s estimate of 1.8%-2% can be a useful starting point. Under our assumptions of no meaningful changes in labor productivity growth and labor force participation rates ahead, the elasticity between population and potential growth is just 1. This suggests that the 60bp and 40bp increase in population growth in 2024 and 2025 might move potential GDP to a range of 2.4%-2.6% in 2024 and 2.2%-2.4% in 2025, much higher than the Fed’s current long-run numbers.

While these estimates naturally entail uncertainty, it's clear that the impact of immigration on potential growth is meaningful over our forecast horizon.

Sector Details

## Consumer Spending

Consumption continues to slow in 2024 and into 2025 as a cooling labor market weighs on real disposable income, elevated rates further pressure debt service costs, and tight lending standards limit availability of credit. By mid-2025, lower interest rates are spurring a pickup in housing demand and goods spending. Following an increase of 2.7% 4Q/4Q (2.2%Y) in 2023, real PCE slows to 2.2% 4Q/4Q (2.5%Y) in 2024 and 2.2% (2.1%Y) in 2025 ( Exhibit 11 ).

## Key drivers of consumer spending:

1. Slowing labor market – Demand for labor is slowing as we complete post-Covid backfilling of the work force. Supply outpacing demand is lowering nominal wage pressures. Real disposable income slows sharply this year and slightly more in 2025 off a softening labor market. Less labor income makes servicing debt more difficult.

2. Drag of monetary policy – While households are better cushioned against restrictive policy in this cycle, the effects of tight monetary policy are continuing to weigh on borrowing costs and access to credit throughout 2024, disproportionately affecting durable goods expenditures and lower-income households. In 2025, lower rates, which should support a pickup in housing activity and access to credit, result in a rebound in goods spending

3. Rising savings rate – The savings rate drifts higher through mid-2025 as high interest rates encourage savings and the desire to spend down excess savings continues to wane. We see a modest drift lower in the saving rate at the end of 2025 off lower interest rates.

4. Population growth – Stronger immigration numbers fuel higher population growth, income, and spending. As a result, real consumption is now growing at a faster pace than previously forecasted in 2024 and 2025, but growth in consumption on a per capita basis is still slowing and should continue to grow at a weaker pace than overall consumption. We dig into details on the characteristics of the new immigration population and implications for consumer spending.

## Slowing Labor Market

A resilient labor market, fiscal stimulus, and aggressive drawdown of savings have fueled consumer spending post Covid, but those peak effects are in the rear-view mirror. In 2023, real disposable income grew by 4.1% 4Q/4Q (4.2%Y) but is on track to slow sharply to 2.5% 4Q/4Q (2.0%Y) this year and 2.4% 4Q/4Q (2.7%Y) in 2025 — reflecting our forecast of slowing job growth and easing wage pressures. Real wage growth (nominal ECI deflated by headline CPI) slows a bit from 0.9% 4Q/4Q in 2023 to 0.7% 4Q/4Q in 2024 and 2025. ## Population Growth

## We raised our estimates for income and consumption last month off stronger

immigration numbers fueling higher population growth. As a result, real consumption in 2024 and 2025 is now projected to grow faster than we previously forecasted, but growth in consumption on a per capita basis is still slowing. We dig into the characteristics of the new immigration population and the implications for consumer spending.

According to new CBO estimates, of the 3.3 million immigrants that entered the US in 2023, \~2 million are classified as "other foreign nationals" (OFN), i.e., people who are undocumented or imperfectly documented.

Undocumented immigrants tend to have less education and are lower-skilled than native-born or other foreign-born workers (lawful permanent residents or temporary workers). A New American Economy report estimates that 90% of undocumented immigrants are of working age (vs. 80% of foreign-born and 62% of native-born) but earn 40-50% less than the median annual wage in the US. In New York, for example, a 2020 report titled “An Economic Profile of Immigrants in New York City in 2017” estimated median annual earnings for undocumented immigrants at \$25,300/year, significantly less than for US-born citizens at \$45,500. Further, because undocumented immigrants aren't eligible for most public benefits, their poverty rate is high relative to other immigrants and non-immigrants — 29% vs. 18% for naturalized and US-born citizens in New York City.

Undocumented workers earn substantially less than others for two key reasons: lower education/skill levels and less bargaining power. Ortega & Hsin (2018) find that skill and education account for roughly 90% of the wage gap, while the rest is a wage penalty for lack of legal status (i.e., less bargaining power). In 1986, the Immigration Reform and Control Act (IRCA) granted 1.7 million long-term unauthorized workers legal status, creating an opportunity to study the law's impact on earnings. Kossoudji & Cobb-Clark (2002) found that the wage benefit of IRCA amnesty was \~6%.

Undocumented immigrants are overrepresented in work that requires less education/ fewer skills, including agriculture, grounds maintenance, food preparation, textile/apparel/ furnishing production, and construction. Edwards & Ortega (2017) found that in 2011–13, unauthorized workers' share of employment was highest in Agriculture (18%), Construction (13%) and Leisure and Hospitality (10%), well above their average labor force share of 4.9%. These are lower-paying industries, with median wages in Agriculture and Leisure & Hospitality \~30% below the national level and Construction, 4% below.

After immigrants enter the US, they move across the country, and different regions have different labor needs. While demand for labor in the US remains solid, it's not clear what kinds of labor market frictions will emerge in matching the new supply of labor with demand. The openings-to-unemployed ratios by industry show that demand remains highest in Health Care & Social Assistance, Wholesale Trade, Mining, Accommodation & Food Services, and Professional & Business Services. Wages for Health Care, Wholesale Trade, and Accommodation & Food Services have all been growing faster than the national average, thanks to labor shortages.

## The final question is how do we expect these new immigrants to spend their

discretionary income?Since their discretionary income is after tax less remittances, the flow of remittances will impact the amount they have left to spend in the US. Data from 2023 on remittance flows into Latin America from the US show that while remittances continue to increase, their growth rate has been declining over the past two years, which does not align with the acceleration in immigration from LatAm to the US over the past year. This divergence could indicate that recent immigrants are spending a larger share of their income in the US than previous cohorts.

Within what they spend, the majority is likely going towards essentials. Living expenses would absorb the lion’s share of additional demand — including housing & utilities, food, and household supplies. Real consumer spending on nondurable goods accelerated in the back half of 2023, which could be explained in part by immigration. Our Hardlines/Broadlines Equity Analyst Simeon Gutman reports that traffic trends at discount and dollar stores have outperformed many other Hardlines retailers since mid-2023. Companies such as Dollar Tree, Walmart, and Five Below experienced positive midsingle-digit traffic in 2023, while others saw traffic decline in the low- to mid-single-digit range. Further, traffic trends among this group accelerated meaningfully in the second half of 2023, with the average 2-year stack increasing by \~190 bps to \~4.7% from \~2.8%.

## Secondary Effects of Second Liens - An Upside Risk to Spending

In April 2024, the FHFA announced a proposal to purchase closed-end second lien mortgages from primary market lenders on properties for which Freddie Mac already holds the first lien mortgage. While many questions remain, this proposal comes at a moment when homeowners have substantial equity in their homes. At the same time, the pace of mortgage equity withdrawal has been slow for several reasons, including (but not limited to) high mortgage rates on second liens, tight lending standards, and perhaps homeowners' reluctance to increase leverage on their home in the aftermath of the global financial crisis. If this proposal were to be approved, we think the implications could be felt far beyond the agency mortgage market and create upside risk to consumer spending in 2025.

The timing of the rollout and take-up of the program carries wide uncertainty bands, but the maximum amount of equity accessible through this program is estimated at \$2tn. We stress that this is an unlikely ceiling, and if anything it is more reflective of the size of the housing market.

Mortgage equity withdrawals typically occur when homeowners need to pay down other debts, fund home repairs, pay for educational expenses, or spend on other big-ticket purchases. During Covid, significant increases in home equity, paired with low interest rates and a rise in homeownership, stimulated increased mortgage equity withdrawals (MEW) through home equity loans (second mortgages) and home equity lines of credit (HELOCs). As interest rates began to climb in 2022, taking out a second mortgage and tapping home equity became too expensive, causing MEW activity to plummet. The new Freddie Mac proposal makes second mortgages more attractive in a high interest rate environment.

How do households typically spend home equity withdrawals? A 2020 report by the Federal Reserve, How Much Does Home Equity Extraction Matter for Spending?, finds that "households spend the bulk of extracted equity to finance home improvements and repairs, though a portion is used on non-housing expenditures or to pay down other debts."

Leading up to Covid (2009-19), mortgage equity withdrawal was very subdued, depressing consumption. The Fed report notes that depressed MEW from 2009 to 2019 resulted in dampened spending: "We estimate that the lower rate of equity extraction lowered annual aggregate spending by about \$45 billion (about \$8 billion of which is non-housing consumption) relative to a counterfactual where equity extraction and spending followed pre-recession patterns." This is because following an equity extraction, which was on average \$40-50k per household over this period, total spending on average increases by just over \$10,000, with much of it going towards home improvements (\~\$4k), home maintenance, higher mortgage servicing costs, and other non-housing expenses (\~\$2k on clothing, furniture, and education).

Another remaining chunk of the equity extraction goes towards savings and paying non-mortgage debts. The study shows that on average, savings increase by \$5k following equity extraction, cash holdings increase by \$1.8k, and non-mortgage debt falls by \$1.6k. We think the non-mortgage debt component could be key here. Given how high interest rates are on other consumer loan products (credit cards, personal loans, student loans, etc.), it could make economic sense for households carrying non-mortgage debt at high rates to take out a second mortgage at a lower rate and pay down their other bills.

Today, as our housing strategists note above, the average homeowner who purchased their home in 2020 or 2021 could tap into \$70-130k of equity in their homes if they take out a second lien. This means tailwinds to spending and nonmortgage debt paydown could be twice as high as the Fed study found over the 2009-19 period on a per-homeowner basis. We see even more upside risk to spending and non-mortgage debt payments on a per-household basis than the Fed study reports, since there are home equity dollars unaccounted for that are likely spent outside the survey period.

## Housing Outlook

Housing is in shortage; we expect a continued rise in residential investment through 2025, with a rapid rise in housing starts, solid new home sales, and a bit more turnover in existing home sales as mortgage rates fall. Homebuilding and increased brokerage commissions (more volume, still-high prices) keep residential investment on the boil: a 5.5% 4Q/4Q (5.6%Y) rise this year and 3.5% 4Q/4Q (3.2%Y) in 2025.

Our residential investment forecasts are a good deal stronger than we expected in the year-ahead outlook last November. Booming 1Q24 growth probably reflected a combination of the warm winter and the temporary downswing in mortgage rates; we don’t expect the same outperformance later in the year ( Exhibit 30 ). At the same time, housing demand is greater than we recognized then, amid faster population growth.

In existing homes, undersupply remains significant. For-sale inventory was at historic lows pre-COVID and has fallen further ( Exhibit 31 ). Current mortgage rates are about 350bps above the rates at which homeowners financed: selling and refinancing is very expensive. With high prices and rates, housing affordability is near record lows, compounding the lock-in effect ( Exhibit 32 ). The result is a minimal supply of existing homes for sale and little turnover. Some noneconomic turnover must occur because of life events; we think recent sales rates, at around a 4.9 million unit annual rate for new plus existing home sales have fallen to that pace of forced turnover.

In new homes, there’s a good deal more supply: multifamily units under construction are at records ( Exhibit 33 ); and single-family building also suggests a lot of units coming on line. However, the new supply doesn’t make up for the earlier shortage. Household formation has been running faster than net new housing ever since the housing crisis during the GFC (the flip side is vacancy rates at or near 40-year lows).

For existing home sales, we expect to see some mild growth because of the recent (slight) improvement in affordability and pent-up need for housing turnover. Prices continue to rise modestly. The new home sales market is more dynamic: our housing strategists expect continued strong growth and, amid higher new home inventories, falling prices. Housing starts, notwithstanding weakness in the multifamily sector, rise at double-digit rates.

How long the strength in building continues depends on new household formation (immigration probably boosts it, but the magnitude is uncertain given most immigrants are low-wage workers that double-up or move in with relatives); and on how serious the posthousing crisis shortage has become. Since the housing crisis, housing completions have been running below the pace of household formation ( Exhibit 34 ) . The extra multifamily supply is small relative to the shortage built up over the past 10 years.

## Labor Market Outlook

The labor market has turned the corner. The mid-pandemic labor market was characterized by shortage. Now, though, the labor supply has surged, and demand for labor is likely to moderate. Fast immigration has been a positive supply shock, allowing rapid employment and income growth, while at the same time adding some slack to a tight labor market. Our forecasts for payroll growth are much higher than last November, and our forecast for the unemployment rate is higher as well. Payroll gains average 225 thousand per month this year and 186 thousand next year, 128 thousand and 110 thousand above our previous forecasts. The unemployment rate rises 0.5 point to 4.2% this year, and another 0.3 point to 4.5% next year. Faster job growth than we had expected supports stronger spending and output growth; more slack in the labor market limits wage pressures. We expect the employment cost index (ECI) slows from 4.2%Y in 1Q24 to 3.6% in 4Q this year and 2.8% in 4Q 2025.

The changes since November 2023 in our labor market (and GDP) forecasts stem from immigration adding labor supply. Our prior forecasts estimated 0.5% population growth; we now estimate 1.2% this year and 0.9% in 2025, reflecting the ongoing surge in immigration. We incorporate updated CBO estimates of 3.3 million in net immigration in 2023, expect the same this year, followed by some slowing toward the pre-pandemic trend of around 800 thousand. (Please see: US Economics: Immigration Headcount)

The immigration estimates are tenuous, however. Changes in domestic policy or foreign conditions that have encouraged migration could change. How do we keep track? Counts of border encounters provide hints<sup>1</sup> . Payrolls also likely pick up the entries to employment: the BLS specifically asks businesses to count employment, wages, and hours regardless of legal status. The household survey is less reliable because levels of employment, unemployment, labor force, and foreign-born workers are based on a population estimate that doesn’t recognize recent immigration. But reported rates of unemployment, labor force participation, and employment-to-population should be more robust because the population undercount affects the numerators and denominators of those ratios similarly. We are tracking other indicators such as court cases, remittances, and the like, but the payrolls data seem the most direct. Remittances seem to have varied as much with price levels and stimulus payments as with immigration. Immigration courts have a long backlog.

The surge in immigration has not yet appeared in Census counts of the population. But we believe it explains last year’s combination of rapid payroll growth and a rising unemployment rate. This year’s flow of immigration thus far looks as strong and possibly stronger than last year's, and the pattern of payrolls, unemployment, and wages also suggest a repeat.

While supply is positive, labor demand is slackening. Job openings have fallen and the vacancy rate (job openings/unemployment) has fallen to near pre-pandemic levels. (In fact, the vacancy rate has probably normalized: it would be slightly lower if not for understated Census population counts.) We expected this soft landing for the labor market — a decline in vacancies without a significant rise in unemployment. Exhibit 35 shows this

return to the pre-pandemic relationship between labor demand (vacancies) and supply (unemployment) — also known as the Beveridge curve.

## Estimating Breakeven

In 2023, payrolls rose 251k per month on average, while the unemployment rate increased by 0.2 percentage point. The payroll breakeven pace — the level of payroll increases that absorbs net new entrants to the labor force and holds the unemployment rate steady — was above 251k per month.

Labor force data tell a different story. They have a different source: the household survey rather than the establishment (payroll) survey. According to the household survey, the labor force increased by only 125k per month after adjusting for the annual population control.

The difference reflects several factors, the most important of which is immigration. The household survey is based on a Census estimate of the population that we do not believe reflects the flood of immigration of the past several years. 3.3 million immigrants in 2023, some large share of whom are working age, imply a higher breakeven than suggested by the household-survey measure of the labor force. ( Exhibit 40 )

Also important to estimating the breakeven pace are the various differences between who's included in payroll- and household-survey counts of employment. Multiple job holders show up as additions to payrolls but not as additional household-survey employment because the payroll survey counts the number of jobs whereas the household survey counts the number of people employed. In 2023, multiple job holders rose 0.3 pct pt as a share of the employed, elevating the payroll breakeven above what labor force alone would suggest. (Other differences between the surveys appear to have been less important.)

The most recent data for payrolls and the unemployment rate suggest a breakeven so far this year no lower than last year's. Payrolls have risen 246k per month on

average this year while the unemployment rate increased by 0.1 percentage point. We are estimating this year's breakeven pace at 265k and next year's at 210k. The lower breakeven next year reflects some slowing in immigration. Throughout our forecasts, payrolls rise at a below-breakeven pace; monetary policy tightness is dragging on activity; and the unemployment rate continues to rise.

If labor supply is interrupted by new immigration policy, the negative supply shock to the labor market lowers the unemployment rate rapidly. We continue to monitor the disparate indicators of immigration flows. Conversely, with such a high breakeven, if demand were to slow, the unemployment rate has upside risk. Job openings ( Exhibit 41 ) and payrolls themselves are probably the earliest indicators; unauthorized immigrants likely do not qualify for unemployment insurance.

## Inflation – Disinflation Reboot

Reflation in 1Q24 was substantial, but our forecasts indicate weaker prints are coming. Rents, goods, and other services will lead the descent. In recent research we showed that residual seasonality also played a role in the recent upswing, suggesting payback in 2H24.

Inflation surprised substantially to the upside in 1Q24... We were early to call for a temporary reacceleration in 1Q, which helped us stay firmly rooted in the view that the Fed would not cut before the middle of the year, even as market pricing piled into rate cuts starting in March. Even so, the strength of the upswing was more than we had anticipated as the 3-month annualized pace of core PCE inflation rate soared from 1.6% in Dec-23 to 4.4% in Mar-24.

...But we expect deceleration ahead, especially in 2H24. We forecast core PCE Q4/Q4 at 2.7% in 2024 and 2.1% in 2025. Easy comparisons in 2H24 mean that 12-month inflation rates will not come down significantly this year, but we do expect meaningful sequential progress ( Exhibit 42 ). ## Residual Seasonality: A Post-Covid Problem

Measuring residual seasonality is tricky because we don't observe the true seasonal factors, only estimates of them. However, we do know this about correct seasonal factors: they should eliminate all seasonal patterns in the data. Hence, one way to assess how BEA's seasonal factors have performed recently is to check if published core PCE SA series still show any seasonal patterns. We do that by double seasonally adjusting core PCE series, i.e., seasonally adjusting the published SA core PCE series and checking whether the series changes materially. Any important modification in the series is a sign of residual seasonality.

Exhibit 49 shows the median difference between published and double-SA series for every quarter (in QoQ SAAR). Three important conclusions emerge:

(1) There is evidence of residual seasonality in 1Q24, with payback especially in the second half of the year.

(2) Residual seasonality has become more important after Covid. Our estimates indicate that residual seasonality increased annualized core PCE inflation (QoQ) in 1Q by 40bp on average in 2021-23.

(3) Of course, noise exists at the end points of a data series, but our results show that residual seasonality might have been even more important in 1Q24 compared to the last few years. Given that seasonals just reshuffle inflation within a given year, residual seasonality this year suggests more and perhaps earlier payback.

What components show residual seasonality? Our analysis identifies it in both core goods and services, with a similar pattern: stronger 1Q residual seasonality post-Covid and payback in the second half of the year.

Monetary Policy

Faster and volatile 1Q24 inflation data is delaying Fed easing, but after renewed evidence of disinflation, we expect the Fed first lowers rates in September. Three 25bp rate cuts this year and four next year leave the funds rate at 3.625% in mid-2025. At that point, the Fed pauses to assess. The Fed has announced it will begin to phase out QT in June, as anticipated; we expect it to end by March 2025.

The Fed has lost some of its confidence that disinflation will persist. Powell still sees the next move as a cut. "We are prepared to maintain the current target range for the federal funds rate for as long as appropriate. We are also prepared to respond to an unexpected weakening in the labor market."

## Policy Restraint, Long- and Near-Term r\*

The debate around whether monetary policy is sufficiently restrictive continues, especially after inflation surprised to the upside in 1Q24. Chair Powell believes the current level of restraint is sufficient to bring inflation down, and we agree. Interest rate–sensitive sectors in the economy point to economic slowdown and provide strong evidence of policy restraint.

Why is it so hard to assess the level of monetary restraint? One reason is that the Fed cannot just look at interest rates and determine whether it has hiked enough. It relies on the concept of near-term r\* or the near-term neutral interest rate, which is the level that neither boosts nor restrains economic activity. A rate above near-term r\* causes the economy to slow down, and how much that rate exceeds near-term r\* determines how fast the economy cools.

But the problem is that near-term r\* is unobservable and hard to estimate with precision, as it is affected by short-run shocks to the economy. The FOMC reports an estimate of the long-run appropriate level of the nominal fed funds rate (currently at 2.6% nominal), but that level should not be interpreted as the relevant r\*. It is just a reference point around which the true near-term r\* fluctuates. And this and next year, near-term r\* might be higher than the 2.6% due to immigration, for example, though it is hard to know by how much. More workers in the economy might boost investment in equipment and tilt the investmentsavings balance towards higher neutral rates.

Looking at historical estimates of near-term r\* is a good way to get a sense of how noisy the estimates can be. The Fed’s staff provides various estimates of short-run r\* to the FOMC leading into policy meetings as a guide, and historical documents are available online. In the January 2010 Bluebook, for example (see page 28), the staff showed near-term r\* based on four different models, and the confidence

intervals ranged from a real interest rate of negative 3.5% to positive 2% (negative 1.5% to 4% nominal using 2% target inflation).

This noise is the reason Chair Powell has been skeptical of neutral rate estimates as a basis for monetary policy decisions. Given the uncertainty, assessing the totality of evidence may be the best way to proceed. Absent severe dislocations in labor markets, the Fed will likely proceed with caution, using inflation as the canary in the coal mine.

Fiscal Policy

We think federal fiscal expansion has peaked for now — tax revenues have rebounded, and we have moved past the ramp-up in spending from key bills. The post-election deficit path is uncertain, though both parties have achieved most of the policy priorities with large impacts on the deficit. Policies on taxes, tariffs, and immigration after the election can move the needle on the deficit and economic activity.

The fiscal deficit is narrowing in 2024 and the government spending contribution to GDP growth further moderates over our forecast horizon. As we move past the post-Covid catch-up for government compensation and investment, we forecast the government spending contribution to 4Q/4Q real GDP growth slows from 0.8pp in 2023 to 0.2pp in 2024 and 0.1pp in 2025. With decelerating government spending in our forecast, monetary policy becomes the primary drag on GDP growth.

The main contribution to government spending in 2023 was re-hiring and the ramp-up of the Bipartisan Infrastructure Bill (BIF) obligations. ## The fiscal impulse

The deceleration in government spending points to a much lower fiscal impulse ahead. We think both government spending and transfers will add just 50bp and 40bp to GDP growth in 2024 and 2025, respectively, vs. a significant fiscal impulse of 130bp in 2023.

We use dynamic economic models to quantify the current and future fiscal impulse of recent government spending measures. We follow Blanchard and Perotti (2002) to estimate the dynamic effect of government spending shocks. The model is a vector

autoregression (VAR), including government spending, transfers, and GDP, with the assumption that government consumption and investment reacts with a delay to changes in GDP.<sup>2</sup> We calculate the effect of current and future fiscal policy by comparing the actual and forecast GDP path with a counterfactual scenario that assumes no active fiscal policy: government spending does not accelerate in 3Q22 and transfers continue at pre-Covid trend.

Exhibit 56 decomposes annualized QoQ GDP growth into five components: (i) longrun potential growth, (ii) the effect of government consumption and investment, (iii) the effect of government transfers, (iv) the impact of monetary tightening<sup>3</sup> and (v) other factors, which are a residual that includes any other forces driving the economic cycle. We think that the key drivers explaining near-term GDP growth other than fiscal spending and monetary policy are: (a) strong investment in equipment as a corollary of strong job creation linked to immigration flows, (b) a more resilient consumer with more fixed rate mortgages and a better balance sheet compared to the past, and (c) nearshoring trends driving continued strength in business investment in structures (see here for data on recent announcements of private sector investment projects).

Our results show that fiscal policy played an important role in explaining growth above potential during 2022 and 2023, but the impulse fades in 2024 as federal, state, and local government spending slow down. We expect no new stimulative fiscal policy legislation through the 2024 election, and our public policy strategists do not see an election outcome that adds more than modestly to the deficit. State and local spending has some upside risk if there is more supplemental investment to the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act from state and local government coffers, but we don't foresee a sharp acceleration given that the hiring catch-up is almost complete. Overall, monetary policy restraint overcomes fiscal impulse to push GDP growth lower through the forecast horizon.

## 2024 Election Cycle

Contributions from our Public Policy Strategists, Michael Zezas & Ariana Salvatore

Our public policy strategists expect that the 2024 election outcome is likely to result in the adoption of policies that will add to the deficit, but for the most part, beyond the FY25 forecast horizon.

In each election outcome configuration (R sweep, D sweep, divided government), we utilize these policies as a starting point, then — accounting for stated positions of key lawmakers — filter down from the estimated level of deficit expansion to what we think can actually pass both chambers of Congress. As we note below, a Democratic or Republican sweep likely has different implications for the deficit in FY25, but both would be very limited. Additionally, it's possible that any effect from legislation post election may have only a limited impact specifically on FY 2025 given that there will be only nine months of a new Congress in FY25.

The key policy areas on the 2024 presidential ballot are: tax policy, immigration, China policy, tariffs, geopolitics (relations with Mexico and EU), and clean tech (IRA). In Exhibit 57 we detail how in each election outcome these policies could change and the possible impact to the deficit.

Current policy has all measures of the Tax Cut and Jobs Act (TCJA) expiring at the end of the 2025 calendar year. If all measures do expire, it will create significant tailwinds to tax revenues (more details here). In all four scenarios we see either some or most of the TCJA measures being extended, which will have a negative effect on the deficit relative to a baseline, where there is a full roll-off of the TCJA.

In a Democratic sweep, we see some TCJA provisions expiring in FY26, but there will be corporate tax extensions (in particular on R&D) and certain individual tax extensions and enhancements. Some of that will be offset by revenue raisers. This should add \$500bn to the deficit over the next ten years. We see negligible deficit implications from other policy changes.

In a Divided Government with Biden or Trump in the White House, we see changes to tax policy starting in FY26, which will add \$800bn to the deficit over the next ten years. In either scenario, we see certain corporate tax extensions (in particular on R&D) and certain individual tax extensions and enhancements, while the remaining provisions of the TCJA expire. There will be no revenue raisers.

In a Trump White House, there is a greater likelihood of deficit impact from tax policy, stricter tariffs, and clawback of IRA funds not yet allocated. A Republican sweep could see \$1tn added to the deficit over the next ten years by rolling forward more TCJA provisions. Clawback of the IRA could lower outlays by \$200-\$300bn in FY25. Tariff impact carries larger uncertainty bands — the direct impact from tariffs is positive for fiscal revenues, but there are offsetting factors, such as weaker import growth.

## Public Debt: An Unsustainable Path

The difference between the average interest rate on public debt and nominal GDP growth is a key element in assessing debt sustainability. Most debt sustainability analysis (DSA) frameworks use this difference because it is important to determine the "debt-stabilizing primary balance," that is, the primary balance that is needed to keep debt-to-GDP ratios constant from one year to the next. A key method to assess whether or not debt ratios are on an unsustainable path uses a simple formula:

$$
\Delta { { d } _ { t } } = { { d } _ { t - 1 } } \left[ \frac { { { r } _ { t } } - { { g } _ { t } } } { 1 + { { g } _ { t } } } \right] - p { { b } _ { t } } + s f { { a } _ { t } }
$$

Here, rt is the average nominal interest rate on government debt, gt is the nominal growth rate of GDP, pbt is the ratio of the primary balance of the government relative to the GDP, and sfat denotes the ratio of the stock flow adjustments to GDP. Note, the primary budget excludes the interest expense on the debt, which is captured in the first term. This formula highlights an important point: if nominal growth is higher than the interest rate, then debt can be sustainable even if the economy has persistent fiscal deficits (pb\* is negative).

The current fiscal forecasts for 2024 and 2025 combined with the CBO projections into the medium term see debt rising to the mid-130%s by 2030 and accelerating thereafter when interest costs finally reset to post-COVID market pricing ( Exhibit 58 , Exhibit 59 ). To stabilize the debt, the primary balance needs to be zero, or ideally positive, which is closer to the norm in the pre-GFC period when inflation last ran consistently near 2%.
