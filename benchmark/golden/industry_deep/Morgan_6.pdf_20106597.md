FX & EM Strategy: Kenya's USD bond issuance is an important positive not only for Kenya but also for the asset class as a whole. It supports two of our key views for the year ahead. First, as external conditions improve, market access will open up to lower-rated issuers, even if it means issuing at around 10%. Second, the number of defaults in the EM sovereign credit index will fall. That said, the focus for most countries is still to consolidate fiscals further, in particular as interest rate costs stay high versus history.

Sovereign Credit Strategy: We focus on stance changes within CEEMEA. In Sub-Saharan Africa, Senegal has been topical and we moved the credit to a like stance following positive developments around election scheduling, which should bring the focus back to the longer-term structural story around oil and gas production. Within the region we also close our Angola like stance given rising positioning and falling spread attractiveness. In northern Africa, we remove our Egypt and Morocco like stances, closing both our Egypt 10s30s steepener trade and our recommendation to buy MOROC 50 versus REPHUN 51. LatAm Macro Strategy: We explore BRL's recent sluggish performance, and we outline three reasons to be long the currency, but not versus USD. Asia Macro Strategy: We tone down our caution on Asia FX and rates by closing all long USD exposure (USD/THB and USD/PHP). We add short CNH/THB and short EUR/KRW. The history of 10% coupons in EM sovereign is decidedly mixed: It's a frequent saying that if a sovereign issues FX bonds above 10% coupon, the intention is not to pay it back.

Looking back through history, there were plenty of EM sovereign bonds with double-digit coupons in the early 2000s, not unsurprisingly given both globally high interest rates and higher EM credit spreads ( Exhibit 1 ). Since 2008, they have been much rarer.

We focus on all the bonds issued with a coupon of at least 9.5% since 2008. Excluding Kenya, there have been 20 from 15 different issuers ( Exhibit 2 ). Three issuers repaid the bonds already (Indonesia, North Macedonia and Mongolia) and another five are still current (Angola, Cameroon, El Salvador, Turkey and Egypt). However, five defaulted on the bonds (Venezuela, Mozambique, Ukraine, Lebanon and Suriname) and another two defaulted on other bonds around the same time (Ecuador and Ghana). So, it does seem that issuing coupons near or at double-digit yields comes with high risks of defaulting in the future.

And we think that the spread should be the starting point for sovereign risk analysis: After all, the credit spread is the assessment of the specific credit risk and default probability. Also, the risk-free rate being high at the moment is nothing Kenya, or other single B issuers for that matter, can change.

Of course, the ideal scenario would be for countries not to issue at all at current levels and instead consolidate fiscals sufficiently to avoid the need to do so. However, this is more easily said than done, especially in a post-Covid and Ukraine conflict world where populations have already been through a lot of hardship.

But the coupon cannot be ignored either: A country issuing its domestic local currency bonds at high coupons is more manageable, as that will tend to be in line with inflation, with the problem for debt sustainability only materialising if real rates are persistently positive. However, as soon as external bonds are issued in a foreign currency there could be an issue. These liabilities are much harder to deal with in the future in the absence of strong real growth, FX accumulation or FX REER appreciation (which can eventually bring other issues). Also, for countries already quite low on FX reserves, it's a particular risk.

In the end, we see it as credit positive for Kenya even if not ideal: This is far from the ideal scenario. Kenya is replacing a bond with a 6.875% coupon with a new one at 9.75% (with an effective yield of 10.375%). Using the US\$1.5 billion issued, that is an increase in annual interest rate payments of US\$50 million, or around 0.05% of GDP. If it simply repays the remaining US\$500 million 2024 bond then it would reduce interest payments by US\$37 million to leave the increase smaller.

However, we add a few caveats. First, and most importantly, it avoids drawing down FX reserves to even more precarious levels, or even worse a missed payment. Second, even issuing a new bond at 9%, if it had been possible, would have seen interest payments rise by US\$32 million a year. Third, in the absence of a significant fiscal consolidation, which is always the best option, Kenya had no other good alternatives, in our view. The IMF programme had been upsized already and multilateral disbursements were also high. And in any case it's hard for either the IMF or multilaterals to justify directly repaying bond holders.

Thus, in the end, we think that the issuance supports our bullish view on Kenya, and the price action leading up to the issuance is in line with this. In the previous three instances of Kenya coming to market, in the days leading up to the issuance bond prices were flat to slightly negative, while the latest issuance saw a 2.3pt increase in prices ( Exhibit 4 ). The fundamental reason is that the issuance bolsters FX reserves and improves Kenya's ability to repay the next bond (2027), thus reducing the risk of a missed payment.

## Does OW high yielders versus UW low yielders and OW LatAm/CEEMEA versus UW Asia generate alpha for GBI-EM investors?

What decides OW/UW? In GBI-EM investors' view, EM local markets is an asset class which offers high carry and high return. Apart from fundamentals, when GBI-EM investors decide to be OW or UW certain countries, they would consider three factors – liquidity, volatility and carry. Hence, it is no surprise that investors would naturally OW CEEMEA or LatAm over Asia because:

• Liquidity in some markets in CEEMEA and LatAm such as Brazil, Mexico, Poland and South Africa is much better than liquidity in Asia, particularly in the swap space.

• Volatility is also higher in those markets. Extremely high volatility is not what they want but they would love to see some decent volatility to generate alpha.

• Carry is the starting point for GBI-EM investors who are naturally OW high yielders versus UW low yielders.

With such factors in mind, the common practice of being OW high yielders versus UW low yielders and OW LatAm/CEEMEA has been a core strategy of GBI-EM investors:

• UW low-yielding bonds (particularly in Asia) is to raise cash so that they could allocate more cash to high yielders in CEEMEA and LatAm.

• The size of OW/UW in the FX space is less so versus bonds as FX is more volatile. But the trend is to UW low yielders versus OW high yielders in EM FX space as well.

Does this have implications for GBI-EM investors and how they plan their investment and business in Asia?

Common practice is to UW Asia: In GBI-EM investors' view, EM local markets is an asset class which offers high carry and high return. As many Asian economies run current account surpluses and keep inflation low, their bond markets normally offer low returns. As a result, they tend to have UW positions in Asia versus OW positions in the other two regions. This has been the case more in bonds, less so in FX:

• UW Asia bonds is to raise cash so that they could allocate more cash to high

yielders in CEEMEA and LatAm.

• They could be either OW/UW Asia FX but the OW/UW size is much less so versus CEEMEA and LatAm.

Apart from fundamentals, when GBI-EM investors decide to be OW or UW certain countries, they would consider three factors – liquidity, volatility and carry. Hence, it is no surprise that investors would naturally OW CEEMEA or LatAm over Asia because:

• Liquidity in some markets in CEEMEA and LatAm such as Brazil, Mexico, Poland and South Africa is much better than liquidity in Asia, particularly in the swap space.

• Volatility is also higher in those markets. Extremely high volatility is not what they want but they would love to see some decent volatility to generate alpha.

• Carry is the starting point for GBI-EM investors who are naturally OW high yielders versus UW low yielders.

With such factors in mind, the common practice of being OW high yielders versus UW low yielders and OW LatAm/CEEMEA has been a core strategy of GBI-EM investors.

The inclusion of India from June 2024 would add interesting dynamics to GBI-EM portfolios. On one hand, GBI-EM investors in general like high real rates markets. In such a sense, Brazil, Mexico and Indonesia have been popular among GBI-EM investors. This makes India less attractive as India's real rates are low or even negative.

However, the stability of INR could be important for investors as they could see G-Secs largely as a carry play. Investors would watch if the RBI would let INR depreciate gradually again in NEER terms as it did before 2020. In addition, India is a high yielder and has a liquid swap market, which allows investors to engage in the derivatives space.

## What is the conclusion?

• Overall, our analysis shows the common practice of OW high yielders versus UW low yielders and OW LatAm/CEEMEA failed to generate consistently positive alpha in the past eight years. We can understand why investors have a preference for LatAm over Asia, mainly due to liquidity, volatility and carry of those markets. But such a strategy doesn't yield the satisfactory result.

• While Asia wont be the major driver of alpha for GBI-EM portfolios, the fact that it would account for almost 50% of the portfolio warrants more attention on this market.

• Investors' approach on some low yielders such as Thailand might need to be reviewed as a consistent UW positions has produced poor results.

• Lastly, India would be an interesting play for GBI-EM investors given its high yield and liquid swap market.

Sell/Short: The analyst expects the total or excess return (depending on the nature of the recommendation) of the instrument or issuer that is the subject of the investment recommendation to be negative over the relevant time period.

Selling Protection or Buying Risk: The analyst expects that the price of protection against the event occurring will decrease over the relevant time period.

Buying Protection or Selling Risk: The analyst expects the price of protection against the event occurring will increase over the relevant time period.

Pay: The analyst expects that over the specified time period the variable rate underlying the swap agreement that is the subject of the investment recommendation will increase.

Receive: The analyst expects that over the specified time period the variable rate underlying the swap agreement that is the subject of the investment recommendation will decrease.

Like: Based on current market conditions as of the date of this report the analyst expects that the relevant securities of the issuer that is subject of the recommendation will perform favorably over the relevant time period as compared to the overall market of comparable securities by other issuers. Dislike: Based on current market conditions as of the date of this report the analyst expects that the relevant securities of the issuer that is subject of the recommendation will perform unfavorably over the relevant time period as compared to the overall market of comparable securities by other issuers. This is not intended to be, nor should it be interpreted as a formal fundamental rating of the issuer or its creditworthiness.
