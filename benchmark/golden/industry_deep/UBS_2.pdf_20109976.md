# China Economic Perspectives China’s changing import structure and implications

## China’s 2023 imports declined along global imports mainly on external factors…

China’s imports weakened along with global trend in 2023, declining by 5.6%, down from +1.4% in 2022. China’s import weakness was largely driven by lower import prices, and softer global demand including a global tech downcycle, while domestic demand also played a role. Overall real import volume only declined slightly by 0.4% in 2023. Price decline had been a notably drag on imports of mineral/fuel, metals, agriculture, chemical, plastic/rubber sectors. Meanwhile, we estimate softer global demand contributed about 60% to the import decline in 2023, which is particularly important for electronics, chemical, plastic & rubber sectors.

## …while China’s import intensity declined in recent years

Import intensity of both processing goods and ordinary imports have come down. The former was due to both the decline of export share in China’s economy and rise of domestic value-added in exports, electronic ICs being a key example. For lower ordinary imports, increased domestic production capability has been a key driver, evident in the auto, cellphone and chemical materials sectors especially. Another factor may be China's changing growth driver, with lower property investment leading to weaker domestic demand for metals and related minerals.

## How does China’s import change matter for key trade partners?

In our 2024 China Export Exposure Chartbook, we showed how China's import pattern evolved. In 2022-23, divergence for tech suppliers and commodity producers dominated the shift in China's import structure. Over the past few years, rising domestic production capability meant that weaker imports from machinery and equipment exporters such as Germany, Korea and Japan, as well as tech suppliers like Korea and Taiwan (also affected by global tech cycle and tech restrictions). Notwithstanding the changes in recent years, EU remained China’s top source of imports in absolute terms. Commodity producers and Vietnam were the top market share gainers in China since 2017. Even though imports from Russia tripled since 2017, it’s still a smaller import origin than Australia.

## Expect low single digit growth in H2 2024 but unlike to rebound notably

Price effect may become a smaller drag in 2024 than last year. Tech cycle had turned upwards since late 2023, which should support electronic demand. Domestically, consumer sentiment remains subdued while property downturn has yet to bottom. Both suggest a rebound in import demand is unlikely. Overall, we expect import growth to record 2.5% in 2024. Downside risks to our forecast mainly include sharper-thanexpected property downturn, slower-than-expected consumption recovery and tougher export restrictions from developed markets. Upside risk mainly come from better exports.

## China’s imports declined in 2023; global factors mattered more than domestic demand

China’s imports declined along global trend, mainly largely driven by lower prices and weaker global demand. Global imports had a 5.7% decline in 2023 after 13% growth in 2022. China’s imports weakened along with the global trend, declining 5.6% in 2023 after +1.4% in 2022. China’s import weakness in 2023 was largely driven by lower import prices and softer global demand, including a global tech downcycle, while domestic demand also played a role. China remained the world’s second-largest export destination in 2023 (as an importer, not counting EU), with its share of global imports largely stable, at 10.7%.

An import price decline explained the majority of China’s headline import weakness in 2023. Overall real import volume only declined a slight 0.4% in 2023, improving from -9% YoY in 2022. In particular, price declines were a notable drag on imports of minerals/fuel, metals, agriculture, chemicals and plastic/rubber, as commodity prices declined after a spike in 2022. Import prices and volume both declined for the electronics sector, reflecting a downward global cycle, although the tech cycle reverted upward in Q423. In contrast, precious metal prices registered solid growth in 2023, more than offsetting a volume decline.

Global demand played a slightly bigger role than China’s domestic demand. In our China Export Exposure Chartbook series, we calculate “imports for domestic use” for products that are consumed domestically. The opposite are imports of components and raw materials, which are processed in China and re-exported at a later stage, catering to global demand. In 2023, we estimate imports used for domestic demand declined 3.6% (from +3.5% in 2022), contributing about 40% of import weakness (- 2.4ppt out of a -5.6ppt decline). Imports for global demand explain the remaining 60%. The global factor was particularly important for electronics sector (driven by the global tech cycle), chemicals and plastic/rubber, while domestic demand was behind the weakness of metal and machinery/vehicle imports.

Trading partners most exposed to China have changed little. In 2023, commodity produces such as Brazil, Russia and Australia gained market share in China, while economies more exposed to the tech supply chain (Japan, Korea and Taiwan) lost share. As of 2023, the EU remained China's top source of imports, accounting for 11% of the total, followed by Asian economies with deep supply chain connections to China, the US and commodity producers. Measured by exports to China as a share of their own GDP, Taiwan remained the most exposed to mainland China imports, followed by South Asian economies and Chile. Among China's major trading partners, Malaysia, Vietnam and Taiwan are the most exposed to China's domestic demand, followed by commodity producers.

## China’s import intensity has fallen notably in recent years

China’s reliance on foreign inputs has declined over time. This has been driven by a declining importance of processing trade and a rise in domestic value added in China’s production. The notable increase before and decline after 2004 in foreign value added in China’s domestic final demand was mainly due to the rise and fall of processing trade, especially in the electronics sector. In the past decade, the import intensity of both processing goods and ordinary imports has come down.

Lower processing import intensity is due to the decline of export share in China’s economy and the rise of domestic value added in exports. China’s headline exports as a percentage of GDP trended down from 34-35% in 2006-07 to less than 20% in recent years. Manufacturing export value added in global final demand as a share of China’s GDP fell from 12% in 2007 to 7% in 2020 (the latest available data), suggesting foreign demand has been a less important a driver of the Chinese economy. Within exports, the share of processing exports among the total has almost halved, from nearly 60% around 2005 to about 30% currently, leading to a declining share of processing imports from 55% in 2007 to about 35% now.

An example is a lower share of imports of electronic ICs (chips) in the past few years. While such imports dropped by 20% in 2022-23, largely due to the downward global tech cycle, the decline of China’s share in global electronic IC imports since 2020 suggests reasons beyond the cycle. COVID lockdowns and economic growth pressure at home may have depressed domestic consumption, reflected by a sharp decline in domestic cellphone shipments in 2022-23. Supply chain reconfiguration may have caused some production to move away from China, leading to lower demand for imports of electronic components, including electronic ICs. Domestically, China gradually increased domestic chip production in light of tech restrictions, which may have helped substitute import demand, to some extent. However, without access to advanced technology, production of high-end chips likely remains limited.

## Changes in China’s tech imports in recent years

China increased chip imports ahead of the Huawei restriction. The US has tightened restrictions on China’s access to advanced technology since 2018, with constrains first imposed on China’s chip imports, then expanded to related machinery. China’s initial response was accelerating chip imports ahead of the Huawei restriction in 2020. Partly reflecting that, China’s share in global electronic ICs edged up modestly, by over 2ppt, during 2017-20 (Figure 8), which was also partly helped by China’s exports of ADP in 2020 (computers) .

As the supply chain shift accelerated, China’s role shifted to being a source of electronic components. Due to US tariff hikes and non-tariff trade barriers, firms have accelerated shifting the supply chain away from China (see CFO survey). After 2020, while China’s share of global imports of electronic ICs dropped, the shares of India and Vietnam rose, although both started from very low bases. At the same time, India and Vietnam became bigger exporters of cellphones, and, in the case of Vietnam, ADP as well. In a previous note (see “US – China – Vietnam trade reconfiguration” on page 6), we found that Vietnam gained the most US market share in electronic products, while its electronic imports from China accelerated the most, which suggests China is becoming a bigger electronic component provider for ASEAN economies.

China accelerated imports of machinery and components related to chip manufacturing. China’s imports of machines used for semiconductor manufacturing had a 12% CAGR between 2017-23, faster than other industrial machinery for domestic use. China’s share in global imports of such machinery rose to nearly 30% in 2023 from 24% in 2017. Similarly, China now accounts for about one-third of global imports of chemical preparations for photographic use, a key component in chip production. Imports of chemical elements for use in electronics have been similar, albeit less notable due to their relatively short shelf life. Japan has been a key source of China’s semiconductor machinery imports, accounting for about one-third of the total, while shipments from the Netherlands more than doubled in 2023 due to rising expectations of tighter export controls (which were eventually implemented in late 2023). However, the strong import momentum of semiconductor machinery may start to wane due to tighter export controls from developed markets.

One reason for the lower import intensity of ordinary imports may be increased domestic production capability. In the auto sector, while China has been a sizeable importer of ICE cars over the past decade, it has recently become a net exporter, on rapid development of the domestic EV industry. The rise of domestic brands is also apparent in the market shift of the cellphone sector. Major domestic brands held strong market positions, while some foreign players, such as Samsung, gradually lost market share over the years (although Apple gained). In the chemical sector, China reduced import reliance on some key materials, including PX and polyethylene, and shifted from a net importer to a net exporter of PTA.

Another factor may be China’s growth driver change. While the import value of minerals and fuel grew 9.5% pa in the past five years, higher prices and rising indirect demand from exports played important roles. In volume terms, iron ore imports for domestic use declined 0.3% pa since 2018 and 3.8% pa since 2021, reflecting the property downturn, while infrastructure FAI offered a partial offset. Import volume of iron ore for domestic use is weaker compared with overall iron ore import volume (+2.1% and +2.4%, respectively) or real ordinary import growth (+2.6% and +1.1%, respectively). Meanwhile, we estimate export-related demand for iron ore imports increased from 6% of the total in 2020 to 19% in 2023, reflecting strong shipments through direct (steel products) and indirect channels (household appliances, auto, machinery, etc.). In the energy sector, coal still accounts for over half of energy sources, even as China has pushed for clean energy. Coal imports expanded 11% pa since 2018 and 21% pa since 2021, as constraints of domestic production were slow to respond to rising global demand in 2021, while key trading partners (eg, Indonesia, Russia and Mongolia) expanded production. More recently, imports from Australia restarted after 1- 2 years of suspension. For crude oil, imports rose 4.1% pa since 2018 in volume terms, although shipments have declined 2% YoY in 2024 YTD after a strong post-COVID rebound in 2023 (when imports jumped 11%).

## Implications for China’s key trading partners

Near-term divergence among tech suppliers and commodity producers. China’s energy demand rebound post-COVID and higher energy prices due to the Russia-Ukraine war meant stronger Chinese imports from countries including Russia, Malaysia, Brazil, the US and Australia in 2022-23. Meanwhile, the global tech downcycle led to weaker Chinese imports from Korea and Taiwan, although the turn of the cycle has led to an import recovery in 2024. A supply chain reshuffle and weak domestic demand in China also contributed to import softness from these economies.

China’s rising domestic production capability has contributed to a changing import structure. In recent years, as China’s own production capability rose in machinery and equipment, imports became less important. As a result, the related exports to China of machinery and equipment exporters such as Germany, Korea and Japan were affected. A more recent example is automobile products, which affect those countries and the US. China’s domestic production capability in electronics has also contributed to changing the import structure in that sector, along with the global tech cycle and US export restrictions on semiconductors, which impact imports from Korea and Taiwan most notably (along with the US).

Despite the changes in recent years, the EU has remained China’s top source of imports in absolute terms, supported by machinery and chemical shipments. Taiwan is next due to a deeply integrated tech supply chain, plus Korea and Japan. US has stayed in the top five, while commodity producers, including Australia, Russia and Brazil. Compared to 2017, Russia, Brazil, India, Malaysia, Australia and Vietnam were among the top market share gainers in China. Korea, Japan, the US and the EU were key market share losers since 2017. Even though China's imports from Russia tripled since 2017, it is still a smaller import origin than Australia. Vietnam, growing over 80% since 2017, made its way to the top 10 of China’s import destinations, following Malaysia.

Looking ahead, imports may continue low-single-digit growth in H224 but are unlikely to rebound notably. The price effect may be a smaller drag in 2024 than last year. Electronic imports could continue to improve due to the global tech cycle and demand related to generative AI, which may benefit economies in the tech supply chain. A continued supply chain shift may partly offset such improvement. Domestically, consumer sentiment remains subdued, while the property downturn has yet to bottom out. Both suggest a significant rebound in import demand is likely missing. Overall, we expect import growth of 2.5% in 2024 (YTD: 2.8%). Downside risks to our forecast mainly include a sharper-than-expected property downturn, a slower-than-expected consumption recovery and tougher export restrictions from developed markets. Upside risk may mainly come from better exports, which could underpin IT component shipments.

## Valuation Method and Risk Statement

We think the risks facing China's economy include a hard landing for the property market, a capital exodus associated with currency depreciation and slow structural reform progress. In our view, any government policies that do not adequately address these risks could result in a shock to the market. For example, an excess of stimulus policies could pose a risk to the transition from an investment-driven to a consumption-driven economy and increase the debt of government and state-owned enterprises.
