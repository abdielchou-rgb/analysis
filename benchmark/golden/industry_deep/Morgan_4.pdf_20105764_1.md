## Wringing Hands Over The Right Reaction Function

Central banks target lagging indicators of the business cycle. The lagging nature of these goals and the lagging nature with which monetary policy operates makes achieving them – consistently over an extended period of time – challenging (the historical record suggests it's nearly impossible).

Some deal with this challenge by implementing a forward-looking approach, i.e., "skate to where the puck is going." Under Fed Chair Yellen, the FOMC attempted this when it began raising rates in December 2015 with core PCE inflation at 1.3% Y/Y.

Per the Summary of Economic Projections, the FOMC would raise rates gradually as inflation approached 2% gradually. While inflation rose in 2016 – coming close to the 1.6% Q4/Q4 median projection – it stopped making progress toward 2 percent in 2017.

The idea of implementing policy today based on forecasts for the economy seems like the right approach in theory. But in practice, it requires accurate forecasts for economic activity – a challenging endeavor. The opposite approach – implementing policy today based on what happened yesterday – carries risks as well.

The pendulum between these two extreme approaches may not swing as freely as it appears to investors. But it does appear to swing nevertheless. For example, in 1H23, the Fed appeared to be laser-focused on realized inflation. It seemed as though nothing but inflation data mattered – a backward-looking approach.

Why might they be reevaluating their view on the link between growth and inflation? Perhaps it's because inflation didn't rebound beyond one month of data as feared. This hints at the possibility that GDP isn't accurately representing activity in the economy.

# Interest Rate Strategy

## United States

Rich duration outweighs weak data: This week in Treasury yields was a tug of war between mixed-to-slowing data and investors wanting to fade the rally in December for having gone "too far too fast" . Discussions of Treasury supply and the upcoming Treasury refunding announcement came up often in investors' conversations. Overall, yields ended higher over the weak despite softer data, suggesting that valuation considerations have outweighed the weaker economic data this week.

Markets focused on the wrong supply, again: After the summer of 2023, the "Treasury supply driving higher yields" narrative is back. We think markets need to focus on labor supply and other supply-side improvements Powell mentioned. Four things to consider: 1) History shows maco matters more than supply, and the rally in November/December proves it, 2) Term premiums, which have risen, might be a misleading signal around Treasury supply, 3) Labor supply matters more to macro and Treasury markets than Treasury supply, and 4) The January refunding is unlikely to provide any supply surprise.

Spotlight on weakening labor market, and the risk of 'abrupt downshift': Powell acknowledged in December that the labor market would grow in importance for the Fed vis-à-vis inflation. To that end, the Kansas City Labor Market index shows the labor market has weakened beyond the 2019 levels that the Fed has sought. Additionally, a weak services ISM employment number highlights the risk of an "abrupt downshift" in labor markets – a risk acknowledged in the FOMC minutes. These risks should keep yields under downward pressure.

Staying neutral for now, but looking to buy dips: As the last week's price action has shown, there is still a sentiment of "too far too fast" among duration investors. Our US economics team expects a strong inflation print for December next week. With investors reluctant to push duration at current levels, and a bumpy disinflation path expected by our US economists, we stay neutral on duration for now. We suggest investors look to buy on dips.

## Euro area

This week, we have started to see some weakening signs on duration's price actions. The release of preliminary Dec euro area inflation continued to show a downward trend. With core still hovering at 3.4% y/y and with a rebound in PMIs plus US data still showing a resilient job market, front-end rates started to reprice higher and, with that, duration followed.

Previously, we had stressed that there were many headwinds for duration ahead, calling for a correction in the near term. Valuations are still pointing to an excessive richening of around \~15/20bp. Moreover, the long positioning bias at a time when heavy January supply is just starting to come to market makes us more confident in calling this near-term correction. All in all, we think that a reconvergence towards the \~2.4% level should be warranted in the near term.

In addition, following the official release of Treasuries funding schedule for 2024, we updated our issuance numbers. All in all, the core support vs. periphery thesis should remain intact, whilst the announced gross issuance schedule from Austria no longer favours our long bias vs. 10y Belgium. As such, we close the trade.

## United Kingdom

UK rates sold off in the last trading sessions as the market readjusted lower its expectations for BoE cuts, while supply is expected to pick up significantly in January. That said, money market valuations still trade with a significant premium versus our expectations and the broader consensus view, hence we investigate historical patterns for, and the relation between, market pricing and consensus.

While market pricing has traded with a significant delta versus consensus over time, the delta seen at the end of December 2023 was significant by historical standards. We also found out that both consensus and market pricing have historically lagged the Bank Rate move, while market pricing has been a better predictor for the effective Bank Rate.

Looking at cross-market valuations the steepening at the back-end of the curve has been particularly pronounced in recent weeks. However, the busy supply schedule alongside the 30y syndication expected at the end of January might prevent any further flattening in the short term, while on a longer time-frame the back-end of the curve should continue to steepen if history is any guide.

Trade-wise we maintain our short 15y ASW position as heavy supply should weigh on gilt valuations, and we maintain our paying 2s5s10s SONIA fly which might cheapen, should the market scale back its expectations for BoE cuts further, while valuations remain rich versus our fair value model.

## Japan

We discuss the background to the recent outperformance of belly of the curve, which we cautioned about in our Top 10 Surprises for 2024 report. Contributing factors would appear to include (1) a supply/demand imbalance seen when comparing the BoJ’s purchase schedule with the MoF’s issuance plan, and (2) a waning of expectations that the BoJ might bring its negative interest rate policy (NIRP) to an end when it next meets on January 22–23.

We believe that positioning premised on underperformance of medium- to longterm JGBs is liable to become increasingly painful in the absence of signs that BoJ policy adjustments might be imminent. JGB-short overseas investors continue to incur carry costs and underweight domestic investors meanwhile suffer opportunity losses (particularly towards the short end of the curve).

We thus see ample scope for the medium- to long-term sector to keep outperforming if domestic investors finally start buying back in, and/or hedge funds continue to unwind their so-called “BoJ trades”. We believe that position adjustments are likely to have been limited through the end of 2023 as liquidity dried up due to the usual seasonal factors.

## Markets focused on the wrong supply...again

The rise in yields in the summer and fall of 2023 – neatly coincident with the August 2023 refunding meeting – convinced many an investor that Treasury supply was a big driver of higher yields. As yields rose in the first week of the year, we have heard a lot of chatter around the Treasury refunding meeting on January 31 as the next catalyst for higher yields. High amount of corporate supply in the last week added fuel to this fire.

The Treasury supply driving higher yields narrative is back – and we disagree with it.

Let's list a few things investors need to unpack to understand why we think Treasury supply is not going to drive higher Treasury yields in the coming weeks. This is not to say that Treasury supply doesn't matter ever – it's just unlikely to matter in the way investors think it will in the coming weeks.

• Macro matters more than supply - a lesson reinforced last 2 months: We argued in our year ahead outlook that macro data and the Fed matters a lot more than supply in driving Treasury yields. History provides many examples of this. The last two months should be the easiest example - supply arguments were nowhere to be heard when Treasuries rallied 100bp on the back of dovish Fed and cooling data. And macro can be seen cooling - especially labor markets.

• Term premiums can misguide investors about the role of Treasury supply: Easily available term premium model estimates allowed investors to conclude that rising term premiums must be a reflection of rising Treasury supply. We showed in our recent publication that term premium model outputs can mislead - as we think they did in 2023, where the rise in yields was more about the Fed and strong growth than supply.

• Labor supply > Treasury supply: The supply that is playing on the Fed's mind has been labor supply and global supply chains, which have ultimately driven the Treasury market much more than the supply investors overfocus on – Treasury supply. Rising labor supply raises the risk of "abrupt downshift" in labor markets.

• The January refunding is unlikely to provide any supply surprise: Specifically, for investors looking ahead to January refunding, we do not see the US Treasury raising coupon sizes more than what they did in November, much in line with their "regular and predictable" style of issuance. Such an outcome will likely dampen the Treasury supply narrative.

## Why markets are likely to turn their focus to the labor market

One of the important takeaways from the December FOMC meeting was the acknowledgement from the Fed that the labor market – the second part of the dual mandate besides inflation – was going to become more important for the Fed (grey box below). With inflation cooling and expected to cool further – as seen from CPI fixings – the focus for investors will likely turn to whether labor market strength can maintain itself. And recent signs suggest the cracks in the labor market are widening.

Powell on the dual mandate, December FOMC meeting: You're getting now back to the point where both mandates are important, and they're more in balance, too. So, I think we'll be very much keeping that in mind as we make policy going forward.

December FOMC minutes: Several participants noted the risk that, if labor demand were to weaken substantially further, the labor market could transition quickly from a gradual easing to a more abrupt downshift in conditions.

A 'gradual easing' or 'abrupt downshift' in labor markets? The FOMC minutes elaborated the signs of weakening observed in the labor market – with a hat tip to the possibility of moving from a "gradual easing" in the labor market to an "abrupt downshift" (see grey box above).

The latest ISM services employment (see Exhibit 15 ) and the latest household survey response will highlight the risk of an "abrupt downshift" in labor markets – something we think markets are not fully appreciating for now.

Even on the gradual easing that we're seeing in labor markets, it is notable that some of the key metrics of the labor market, like the latest private quits rate and hiring rates, are now weaker than they were in 2019 and carry downward momentum.

Similarly, a more comprehensive measure of labor markets, the Kansas City Labor Market Conditions Index, which combines the signal from 24 different labor market indicators also shows that current labor market conditions are on a weaker footing than they were in 2019.

If the 2019-type labor market is seen as the Fed's goal (alongside 2% inflation, of course), then we think markets will feel justified in pricing in significant easing in the coming months as a way of preventing the labor market from moving much below 2019 levels. On top of that, the market needs to price some risk of an "abrupt downshift", which we think is underpriced for now.

Why we aren't back to being long duration yet? So with these downward risks to the labor market in play, the case for being long duration remains. ## The reason for the steeper curve

Liquidity tends to drop away towards the end of December as many market participants go on holiday, but it is typically quite a busy period when it comes to home-grown catalysts.

The final days of 2023 proved to be no exception, with considerable attention focusing on an NHK interview with BoJ Governor Kazuo Ueda, the announcement of the BoJ’s schedule for JGB-buying operations, and the release of the MoF’s JGB issuance plan for FY2024 (April 2024 through March 2025).

Our “2024 surprises” piece is already starting to look quite prescient, with the JGB yield curve having steepened markedly since the December BoJ monetary policy meeting (see Exhibit 49 ).

Contributing factors would appear to include (1) a supply/demand imbalance seen when comparing the BoJ’s purchase schedule with the MoF’s issuance plan, and (2) a waning of expectations that the BoJ might bring its negative interest rate policy (NIRP) to an end when it next meets on January 22–23.

Let us now consider each of these drivers in greater detail.

2024 supply/demand outlook: The MoF not only matched consensus by announcing a reduction of 20y JGB issuance from FY2024, but also signaled sizable cuts to offerings through the 10y sector, made possible by a big drop in total refunding bond issuance. Issuance of 30y and 40y JGBs will meanwhile be left unchanged from FY2023 levels (see this).

On the demand side of the ledger, the BoJ has been dialing back its market intervention right across the curve as declines in overseas interest rates have helped to push JGB yields lower (see Exhibit 50 ). The number of purchase operations beyond the 10y sector will be cut back from January and the lower bounds of offer amount guidance ranges having been lowered for shorter maturities (see this).

## United States

USD | Turning neutral on the USD

Trying is the first step toward failure," said Homer. The USD tried to rally in early December but this proved short-lived. US data deceleration, coupled with the market interpreting a dovish pivot from the Fed, fueled a decline in US rates, in turn bolstering risk appetite. Falling real rates and higher equities is Regime 1 of our USD framework, which sees universal USD weakness.

In our year-ahead outlook we said USD strength would continue, driven by growth outperformance, favorable rate differentials, and investor defensiveness. US data deceleration has compressed growth differentials, US rates have fallen further compared with peers, and investors appear far from defensive based on equity returns.

We turn neutral on the USD as our conviction about USD strength has waned meaningfully. Investors appear to be adopting an 'early cycle' mentality where peak Fed hawkishness is sufficient to 'paper over' other risks. It's not so clear if previous USD-positive catalysts, such as upside surprises to US growth, downside surprises abroad, or an optical uptick in core PCE moving averages will be enough to weigh on risk and bolster the USD.

That said, we note three important (tactical) USD-positive risks. First, positioning has turned meaningfully USD-negative. Second, seasonality has turned from USDnegative in December to positive in the first quarter. Finally, the USD's selloff has overshot what historical relationships imply.

We recommend pivoting from short EUR/USD to short EUR/JPY. The cloudier outlook for the USD doesn't change the fundamentals for the other G3 currencies, in our view. JPY should continue to gain as long as US rates are falling, regardless of the risk outlook. Meanwhile continued downside surprises in Eurozone data, particularly inflation, could lead EUR to re-emerge as a funding currency of choice given its lower carry versus the USD and risks from a dovish ECB pivot down the line.

USD | USD through the lens of foreign currency credit

Changes in US monetary policy have implications for global financial conditions, and in turn foreign currency credit. We discuss the relationship between the US dollar and foreign currency credit.

We previously found evidence that offshore USD lending, one component of foreign currency credit, and the US dollar exhibit a negative relationship. Given that international debt issuance is increasingly the main driver of growth in foreign currency credit we focus on this component as opposed to bank lending.

Impulse response functions suggest that a shock to changes in the amount of international debt securities has a temporary, negative impact on the US dollar. Well-timed Fed rate cuts and a soft-landing could imply an easing in foreign credit conditions, which would be dollar negative.

However, a sharper slowdown in US economic data, combined with weak European growth and depressed sentiment on China's outlook, could weigh on global liquidity growth more than the positive impact of rate cuts.

## SEK | Progress report

In September 2023 the Riksbank began implementing a hedging program of its FX reserves, partially shielding them from mark-to-market swings. Representing roughly 25% of its reserves, the program has seemingly supported SEK in its implementation.

Now the hedging program is almost at completion. Ahead of the originally communicated targeted time frame for USD, and squarely within said time frame for EUR. The relative slow pace of EUR hedging is likely due to the Riksbank's use of Swedish EU membership contributions.

Nevertheless, SEK strength in the closing 2 months of 2023 cannot be entirely attributed to this hedging program. Sweden's economic reliance on manufacturing makes its currency particularly sensitive to global demand and risk appetite. Following a rebound in global optimism towards the turn of the year (as proxied by the equal-weighted S&P 500), this seems to have driven significant strength in SEK.

Investors may opt to employ SEK to express their views on risk given it's high beta. However, risk-bears may prefer the expression through SEK over risk-bulls given the soon-to-be ending hedging program, which will be removing a tailwind for the currency.

When we published our year-ahead outlook in late November, we penciled in continued USD strength with the DXY peaking at 111 in the second quarter, which corresponded with EUR/USD falling to parity.

Since then, things haven't panned out as we expected. The USD tried to rally in early December but was overwhelmed by a shift into Regime 1 - falling real rates, positive risk appetite, and a universally weaker USD, particularly versus higher beta risk currencies like the Nordics and Antipodeans. As Homer Simpson once famously said, trying is the first step toward failure.

With the outlook for the USD getting cloudier, we turn neutral on the USD and close our recommendation to be short EUR/USD, though we still see risks asymmetrically skewed toward a (tactically) higher USD due to seasonality, positioning, and evidence that the USD may have overshot in its weakening trend.

Why did the USD weaken so much? With the benefit of hindsight, the fall in the USD is natural and easily explainable: US rates fell in real terms, in nominal terms, and relative to peers, while risk assets rebounded. A scenario where US real rates are falling and risk appetite is firming corresponds with Regime 1 in our USD framework, which sees widespread and meaningful USD weakness ( Exhibit 62 ).

The drop in US rates was meaningful and it fully eroded the 'term premium' shock seen over the summer ( Exhibit 63 ). With the US rate move outpacing those of its peers, aided by very strong 3Q economic data, the reversal has meant that US rates have fallen in excess of peers in both real and nominal terms ( Exhibit 64 ).

It is impressive how 'risky' the risk asset rally has been amid falling rates. For much of the year, investors have discussed the meaningful divergence between the S&P 500 (a valueweighted index) relative to its equal-weighted peer. The divergence was explained by the sizable outperformance of megacap stocks, while smaller index constituents, which carry much more weight in the equal-weighted index, failed to follow suit.

So the fact that the gains in November and December were largest in US small caps and non-tech stocks suggests that investors really have been taking a more optimistic view about risk, as opposed to simply buying a few securities for idiosyncratic, firm-specific reasons that move the index due to their size ( Exhibit 65 ).

A key element we thought that could catalyze EUR/USD selling was the risk that the ECB would sound more dovish versus market expectations whereas the Fed would push back on market pricing. This doesn't appear to have transpired.

1. US growth would outperform peers, particularly in Europe and China;

2. Rate differentials would remain favorable toward the USD; and

## FX Reserves Hedging? Almost done.

Earlier this year the Riksbank decided to implement an FX reserves hedging program, partially shielding its balance sheet from FX mark-to-market valuation swings. Fortuitous timing as it turns out, with SEK appreciating from near all-time lows since.

Specifically, the Riksbank announced it would hedge 8 billion US dollars and 2 billion euros, representing roughly 25% of its FX reserves. The time frame for the implementation was set at 4 to 6 months.

Despite our initial reservations about the impact, once program implementation began, price action seemed to indicate the hedging program would be supportive to SEK.

Now, we are reaching the end of the program. By our estimations dollar-hedging should be completed by the end of next week ( Exhibit 71 ).

This estimation, however, is predicated on maintaining current pace, which given the lack of liquidity over the festive period may be a week or two early.

During the last weeks of 2023 the US dollar weakened broadly. As discussed in USD | Turning neutral on the USD , the decline in USD was characterized by Regime 1 of our dollar framework, where real rates fall, and risk appetite is robust. Expectations of aggressive rate cuts from the Fed in 2024 played an important part in this dynamic.

FOMC Chair Powell has argued in the past that the role of U.S. monetary policy as a driver of global financial conditions is often overstated, but the importance of the Fed’s policies in the global financial cycle has been well documented.

An important aspect of global liquidity is foreign currency credit to non-bank borrowers (i.e., households, corporations and governments). As BIS research has shown, this international part of credit is rather small compared to total credit, but changes in foreign currency credit can magnify domestic trends and are highly correlated to developments in global financial conditions.

## A brief overview of foreign currency credit

Before moving to the relationship between the USD and foreign currency credit, what are the components of foreign currency credit and how has the composition changed over time?

The BIS splits foreign currency credit into international bank claims and international bonds, where the former can be split into cross-border claims and banks’ local claims in foreign currencies.

Put more concretely, US dollar credit to borrowers in the euro area would be comprised of (1) foreign banks lending US dollar to non-banks in the euro zone, (2) local banks making dollar-denominated loans, and (3) issuance of dollar-denominated bonds.

Bank regulations post-GFC, and interest rates around zero in many advanced economies, have been cited as the main reasons for a shift towards international bonds as a source of foreign currency credit.

## How does foreign currency credit fit into our neutral USD view?

As previously mentioned, we shifted this week from a bullish to a neutral USD stance. Well-timed Fed rate cuts and a soft-landing could imply an easing in foreign credit conditions, which tends to be dollar negative.

However, a sharper slowdown in US economic data, combined with weak European growth and depressed sentiment on the Chinese growth outlook, could weigh on global liquidity growth more than the positive impact of rate cuts.

Moreover, other medium-term developments may hinder the extent to which we could see a meaningful rebound in foreign currency credit and a weaker dollar.

Questions around how long the Fed will keep rates above neutral, whether we have entered an era of structurally higher yields across G10, and geopolitical fragmentation all raise the question of whether the tightening in foreign currency credit conditions could be sustained longer than during previous cycles.

## AUD and NZD fair value estimation approach:

1. Run a LASSO regression on two years of daily changes in potential explanatory variables (independent variables) and daily changes in the currency vs. USD (dependent variable)

2. Select up to five variables that the LASSO regression retains as containing the most explanatory power (to reduce multicollinearity).

3. Run a standard OLS regression using two years of daily changes in the five LASSO-selected variables and two years of daily changes in the currency vs. USD.

4. Retain only those variables whose t-score exceeds 1.96.

5. Calculate the predicted percent move in CCY/USD over the past year using a) the actual change in the variables retained in step 4 over the past year and b) the regressions coefficients on those variables calculated in an OLS regression of those retained variables on changes in CCY/USD.

6. Repeat step 5 for additional time horizons (9 months, 6 months, 3 months, 1 month, 2 weeks, 1 week).

7. Calculate the predicted level of CCY/USD implied by moves in drivers of that currency over the various timeframes

8. Average the predicted levels calculated in step 7.

## We are still moving from abundant into ample reserves

Recent SOFR spikes (see Exhibit 95 ) over the last two month-ends continue to place in focus the Fed's QT plans as repo rates continue to gradually climb given QT and more demand for repo financing given more UST supply.

At the same time, the RRP has declined by \~\$1.5tn since 6/1 as MMFs continue to extend into bills and capture more attractive repo rates in the market (see MMFs Keep Chasing the Extra Bp for more). This has kept reserves \$102bn higher relative to the start of QT ( Exhibit 96 ) and have supported the Fed characterizing reserves as "abundant". We want to emphasize that the distinction between abundant and ample is important when discussing reserves.

One of the goals of QT is to bring reserves back to an ample regime in which the Fed provides a large amount of reserves that allows for the effective implementation of monetary policy (money market rates such as SOFR and EFFR stay within the target range) and allows banks to meet their desired levels of reserve balances while at the same time minimizing the cost of providing those reserves (by controlling the size of interestbearing Fed liabilities – reserves and RRP).

During the pandemic, the Fed engaged in an unprecedented amount of asset purchases (QE) that led reserves to go into an "abundant" or even "super-abundant" regime as the Fed prioritized liquidity over the cost of providing reserves. QT is now allowing the Fed to go back to its intended ample reserves regime.

In November, Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan elaborated on this distinction between abundant and ample reserves and on the importance of "central banks to equate the benefits and costs to society of supplying additional reserves".

In other words, reserves are abundant when the value of reserves (captured by IORB) is too high relative to the cost of liquidity for the market (SOFR) and ample when they are fairly close to each other. In an ample reserves regime, banks are not either rewarded (IORB > SOFR) or penalized for holding reserves (IORB < SOFR).

In simpler terms, abundant reserves occur when there is too much liquidity and money market rates fall materially below IORB. This was evidenced in 2021 and 2022 as SOFR came at or below the RRP. This could be characterized as "super-abundant" as non-bank front-end investors such as money market funds relied on the RRP to obtain a better return vs. market rates given too much liquidity.

As front-end markets continue to come into balance given more UST supply and ongoing QT, SOFR has started to gradually climb above the RRP rate over the past year. Still, SOFR has remained below IORB as the RRP remains at \~\$700bn. This regime would be considered "abundant" by the Fed, particularly when excluding temporary month-end spikes (see Exhibit 97 ).

Exhibit 97: SOFR within the fed funds target range and implied reserves regimes

Source: Bloomberg, Morgan Stanley Research

The eventual depletion of the RRP is what will ultimately allow SOFR to go slightly above IORB, consistent with ample reserves. Hence, SOFR above IORB should serve as a clear indicator that the Fed has achieved an ample reserves regime. This should also inform the decision to taper and end QT in line with the indicators discussed in the December FOMC minutes.

Once SOFR > IORB is achieved, the Fed will then have to be careful to not do "too much" QT to avoid SOFR spiking above the target range and having the Fed be the lender of last resort (via SRF at the FF upper target or FF UT in Exhibit 97 ). This would be consistent with scarce reserves and a repeat of 2019 in which the Fed kept QT ongoing after SOFR started consistently coming above IORB since late 2018.

In addition, dealer intermediation capacity and bank funding needs (more below) also present risks to ongoing QT as they could result in further spikes in SOFR, while the RRP continues to decline. In particular, the willingness and ability of banks to serve as the marginal lenders of cash in repo remains highly uncertain (see here).

We define seven historical easing cycles in the US dating back to 1998. These cycles are presented in Exhibit 114 . We separate these cycles into two categories, namely:

1. Insurance cut cycles: Cycles characterized by either a small number of cuts, or a brief duration. More specifically, Sep98-Nov98, Nov02-Jun03, Jun19-Oct19.

2. Full easing cycles: Cycles which lasted more than 8 months, or which saw the policy rate decrease dramatically, or which saw a lengthy period of accommodative monetary policy following the last cut. More specifically, Jan01- Dec01, Sep07-Apr08, Oct08-Dec08, Mar20.

## Upper left

Insurance cut cycles see short expiry/short tail implied vol spiking after the first cut. However, following the first cut, upper left volatility either stays constant or reprices lower through the rest of the cycle (see Exhibit 115 ).

During full cutting cycles, gamma spikes after the first cut, but unlike insurance cuts, continues to drift upwards (see Exhibit 116 ). The exception to this is cutting cycles that approach the zero lower bound (ZLB) (2008 and 2020).

Intuitively, we interpret this as a sign that insurance cutting cycles are driven by factors that can be anticipated by investors. Forward-looking market pricing adjusts initially, causing near-term vol on policy-sensitive rates to increase. As the new factors are digested, however, there is little need to price additional uncertainty.

Full easing cycles, however, tend to be driven by factors that are harder to anticipate (e.g., asset bubbles, banking stress, pandemics). As such, additional uncertainty is priced in as events evolve.

## Upper left vs. Intermediate left

During insurance cut cycles, the expiry term structure largely follows the trend observed for upper left volatility. There is an initial spike, following by underperformance of shorter expiries, as the term structure steepens.

During full cutting cycles, the initial reaction is the same. However, as the cycle continues, shorter expiries remain bid, preventing the term structure from steepening. This is driven by increased short-term uncertainty, leading to a bid for near-term insurance.

## What does this say about the current cycle?

Our economics team has four cuts pencilled into the year, largely driven by the need to adjust real policy rates as inflation trends lower. Upper left volatility should thus trend lower, given these cuts are not driven by a recession-like macro environment, (our economists expect a soft landing). In turn, the expiry term structure should steepen, while the tail structure could remain range bound given the current distance from the ZLB.

## Trading Strategy 1 – "Trade Longs/Fade Shorts"

When the 5-day moving average crosses above the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract (long duration) and hold for a 25-business-day period. When the 5-day moving average crosses below the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract and hold for a 25-business-day period. In short, this strategy buys futures when the Simple Moving Average Crossover (SMAX) generates both a long and a short signal, given the historical outperformance of long signals traded long and underperformance of short signals traded short. Given that the SMAX could generate both a long and a short signal within the predefined holding period, an investor may have a 200% long position since each of the two signals would be traded in separate portfolio sleeves.

## Definition of terms

Buy/Long: The analyst expects the total or excess return (depending on the nature of the recommendation) of the instrument or issuer that is the subject of the investment recommendation to be positive over the relevant time period.

Sell/Short: The analyst expects the total or excess return (depending on the nature of the recommendation) of the instrument or issuer that is the subject of the investment recommendation to be negative over the relevant time period.

Selling protection or Buying Risk: The analyst expects that the price of protection against the event occurring will decrease over the relevant time period.

Buying protection or Selling Risk: The analyst expects the price of protection against the event occurring will increase over the relevant time period.

Pay: The analyst expects that over the specified time period the variable rate underlying the swap agreement that is the subject of the investment recommendation will increase.

Receive: The analyst expects that over the specified time period the variable rate underlying the swap agreement that is the subject of the investment recommendation will decrease.

Unless otherwise specified, the time frame for recommendations included in the Morgan Stanley Fixed Income Research reports is 1 - 3 months and the price of financial instruments mentioned in the recommendation is as at the date and time of publication of the recommendation.

When more than one issuer or instrument is included in a recommendation, analyst expects one part of the trade to outperform the other trade or combination of other trades included in the recommendation on a relative basis.
