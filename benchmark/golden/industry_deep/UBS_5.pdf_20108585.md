# 15 Essential Charts: What does fading US exceptionalism do to assets?

## US consumer is tiring, but inflation is falling too.

The investment side of the US economy has not been exceptional; it’s the consumer that’s been growing well above trend since Covid. Bottom-up same store sales data suggests that this is quickly changing. We are long the US 2y rate and Gold, are turning from bullish to modestly cautious on credit, and prefer Consumer and Financials equities in Europe vs in US. A slowing-but-not-recessionary US can also weigh on broader equities. High earnings expectations and tight risk premia leave limited room to build in further optimism. The hope is that help arrives from lower rates. Based on our inflation view, lower rates could aid equities before growth headwinds compromise them more.

## Markets running ahead of fundamentals; one of them needs to change pace

Our return attribution model seeks to identify the contribution to returns of 4 market drivers – growth, inflation, risk free rate and risk premia. Unlike H2 ‘23, these fundamental drivers have not changed much in H1 ’24 (Growth factors have in fact weakened lately) but the markets have continued their march higher. ‘Residual’ market optimism is at a level from which it usually reverts. Either fundamentals need to improve or the market needs to correct tactically. If it's fundamentals that improve, this will most likely come through lower rates, which typically help Utilities, Consumer Discretionary, Tech, Materials and Industrials, in that order. Given slowing consumer and broader US growth, we would expect continued leadership from Tech, Utilities, Quality and Growth.

## Will improving growth in the rest of the world compensate for a slowing US?

In GDP terms, yes. It's less clear in market terms. European growth is likely to slowly improve but markets are well priced for this. We see European equities as not far from the upper end of their range, and prefer Consumer and Financials to Industrial and Materials. We are outright long China equities, where authorities get full marks for intent, if not for details, in their plan to address housing inventories. We remain long Japan equities, but are keeping a worried eye on the delayed consumer recovery.

Our return attribution model aims to identify the proportion of market moves that have been caused by a) real growth (UBS’ Regional Growth Indices), b) inflation (breakevens), c) risk free rates (real rates), d) risk premia (credit spreads) and e) a residual (everything else). The model shows how return drivers changed over time. In Q3 ’23, US growth contributed positively to the market but higher real rates, higher inflation and modestly wider credit spreads dragged down overall returns.

In Q4 2023, all four factors – growth (higher), inflation (lower), real rates (lower) and spreads (tighter) helped to lift the market, and explained up to 90% of the returns in most sectors. Technology (positive residual gains) and Energy (negative residual gains) were the main exceptions.

In Q1 2024 US equities continued their move higher, unexplained by these 4 key drivers, which remained fairly stable. Growth, inflation, real rates and credit spreads together explained only 20-30% of the move in the market. The remaining enthusiasm is being captured in the dominant residual in this model.

In Qtd Q2 2024, the US’ Growth factor has begun to contribute negatively to the market, a change from Q3 and Q4, 2023. Markets are still headed higher, in the context of broadly stable (over the quarter) real rates, spreads and breakevens, implying that once again market gains are unexplained by these four factors, and captured in the residual.

The residuals in our return attribution model have been high for two quarters in a row now. This seldom persists. Either the fundamental drivers need to quickly become more supportive to justify current prices or the market needs to correct lower over the tactical horizon. Fundamental factors can become more supportive through a combination of lower inflation, rates, lower credit spreads, improved growth expectations. Of these, lower <sup>)</sup>inflation and rates seem the most likely candidates to help the market. )We are expecting May core CPI at 24bps next week. At the same time, )one needs to keep a close eye on growth, where momentum is weakening from a high level.

We looked at the same store sales growth numbers for the 10 largest retailers in the US (Amazon, which doesn’t report this data, is a big miss here). This data maps very closely into retail sales and therefore into Goods PCE. The prognosis is that US goods consumption is weakening. So far this year, consumer discretionary stocks have clearly underperformed staples, suggesting a worried market. However, the consensus is still pricing in discretionary earnings growth of 15.5%. This has historically been consistent with retail sales growth of 6% y/y, which is very unlikely to be achieved, in our opinion (April’s print was 2.7% y/y).

Based our views on US inflation (core inflation at 0.24% m/m for May and 0.18% m/m for June) we see lower rates over the next two months as a strong possibility. 2y should lead but 10y rates will likely also come lower. Lower rates and lower inflation may help equities before growth worries hurt them. Nasdaq and Russell 2000 have historically shown a greater beta to lower rates than has the S&P500. Among industries, Utilities, Real Estate, Semis and Semicap equipment, Software Services and Commercial and Professional services are the winners amidst declining rates. Consumer Durables and Retailing also do well, but we will avoid these sectors given the slowing US consumer.

Core inflation has shown some inertia in the US, Europe and the UK, but we believe it is likely to continue declining slowly. Wages are declining too. Real wages growth had bottomed first in the US, then in Europe and then in Japan. It is now on its way down in the US (wage growth coming down, bulk of inflation decline largely behind us), is likely around its peak in Europe, and is in the early stages of its rise in Japan (still negative). This has two investment implications: a) a preference for European and Japanese consumer stocks over US consumer stocks, and b) relative to forwards the Fed is likely to be more dovish than the ECB or the BoJ. We see very good risk reward in being long US 2y.

A cut from the ECB today should be in the bag given how clearly it has previously been communicated. Beyond that though, progress in cutting rates will be slow. Labour markets are lagging indicators and the lag between the cycle peaking and employment shifting lower has been growing over time. However, in this cycle, it has been the longest. Europe’s labour supply curve seems to have shifted in (through ageing) while the US’ has shifted out (though immigration). Real wages in Europe may not come off as quickly as in the US. That’s good for Consumer plays, but not for broader company margins. The Fed is much more mispriced than the ECB, we think.

We were bullish Chinese equities before policy makers announced measures to support property and, after its quick rally, the market was not far from our initial target. These new measures keep us interested in staying long. Thus far there has been very limited participation from global investors in China equities despite the market run up. If and when foreign investors buy China, it could add further fuel in this tactical run up, and also help the RMB, which has so far lagged the EUR amidst modest dollar weakness.

The goods news in these latest property stabilisation measures is that a) they come directly from the central government, and b) they seek to address the main issue – excessive developer inventories. The bad news is that size of funds announced for inventory purchases are a fraction of what is likely to be required to clear up inventories in tier 1 and tier 2 cities, let alone, bigger inventories in smaller cities. The PBoC may lend to developers through commercial banks, potentially a form of indirect QE. The PBoC’s balance sheet is much smaller in size relative to that of global central banks, and its lending to commercial banks may yet rise.

Analysts have been getting increasingly less bearish on Chinese equities. After being less negative, earnings revisions have turned positive for selected sectors such as the consumer. If property prices stabilise, we could see decent pent up demand for housing and housing related consumption helping property, consumer and internet stocks, our preferred sectors.

The market where most international investors, including us, are long, and is beginning to throw up signs of worry, is Japan. Here earnings revisions have become progressively less positive, with the Consumer Discretionary sector under pressure. Actual consumption growth and consumer confidence have both been weak in Japan. In contrast to the US, and, more recently, to Europe, real wage growth in Japan has remained negative in the post Covid era. However, inflation is now declining and wages are picking up, so real wage growth should improve, and with it consumer spending. But this is a space we need to watch. Consider us worried longs.

One of our strongest conviction trades for 2024 has been, and remains, long Gold. We have argued that since the Western governments imposed sanctions on the Central Bank of Russia, Gold’s relationship with real rates, its key driver, has become very asymmetric – responding traditionally (a ratio of \~ 1:12 to lower real rates), but broken in higher real rates. This is because of the strong official sector flow into gold. This strength of this flow has weakened since its peak in 2023, but remains well stronger than pre-CBR sanctions, and we expect it to remain strong.

When the market gets very bullish on precious metals, silver tends behave like high beta gold, and therefore outperforms. Over the last 10 years, when the gold to silver ratio drops ztowards the 62-65 range, it typically signals excessive enthusiasm, not just silver, but on the precious metals complex as a whole. This range on the gold : silver ratio being hit has been followed by a 10-15% correction in gold and a 20-30% correction in silver. Silver’s recent outperformance has taken that ratio lower quickly, but the recent consolidation has taken it back to a healthy 79. That enthusiasm in precious metals is not excessive is also evident in very moderate speculative positioning on gold.
