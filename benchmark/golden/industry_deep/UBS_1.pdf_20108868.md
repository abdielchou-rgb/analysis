# China Equity Strategy

# A-share views, feedback from roadshows and the 10 most asked questions

## Downside risk manageable

Given that: 1) the A-share equity risk premium is +1SD above its historical average; 2) the government has YTD repeatedly stated its commitment to safeguard capital markets; and 3) trading volumes for major-index ETFs have spiked again recently, we believe the overall downside risk for the A-share market is manageable. Our discussions with investors show market participants are mainly concerned that it could take time for a number of recently announced supportive policies for the property sector to take effect, while the continued weakness in mutual funds' new issuance, coupled with a recent net outflow of northbound funds, may deprive A-shares of new funds in the near term.

## Overweight value/defensive names, large-caps and SOEs

From a style perspective, we prefer high-yield value stocks as short-term 'defensives' while long-term investors may consider buying low for high-quality growth stocks. Our data shows high-yield stocks as a portion of mutual fund holdings currently remain well below the 2018 level, suggesting no crowding yet for dividend stocks. We expect largecaps to benefit from their high earnings visibility and a potential net inflow of long-term investors, and thus continue to outperform small/medium-caps. Sector-wise, we are overweight electronics, energy, utilities and telecoms. As for themes, we are constructive on 'big picture' high-quality SOEs that relate to a 'valuation system with Chinese characteristics' (VCC).

## Q1: How material is the property sector’s impact on the A-share market?

Although the property sector is a major pillar of China's economy, its significance to the macro economy has been waning in recent years. The UBS China macro team estimates the contribution from real estate and its related sectors to China’s GDP as of end-2022 was 25%, a sharp decline from around one-third in 2001-11.

In stark contrast to its pivotal role in the economy, the real estate sector's market cap as a percentage of all A-shares has been rather limited and in a prolonged decline. If we check the real estate sector's market cap within the category of A-share level-1 industries as a percentage of all A-shares, we note this ratio had been sliding from 5.6% in 2015 to 1.5% in 2023. Admittedly, the sector features longer upstream and downstream value chains. If we total all real estate-related sectors (steel, non-ferrous metals, autos, home appliances, real estate, banks, building materials, construction, machinery and coal) we can still conclude this broader grouping of sectors' market cap as a percentage of all A-shares has been declining overall. This ratio exceeded 46% in 2012 but slipped to 30% as of end-2023.

The rise and fall of property prices also impact related sectors and wider A-share market sentiment through the wealth effect. Residential real estate comprises the largest portion by far of Chinese household wealth, at around 60% in 2020, based on People's Bank of China (PBoC) estimates. The continued downturn in the property sector may result in a negative wealth effect that could further dampen consumer confidence, in our view. While overall property activity remains subdued, high-frequency data shows the decline in new home sales in 30 cities narrowed from -38% YoY in May to -29% YoY by 22 June.

## Q2: How can we interpret declining A-share trading volumes?

The recent decline in A-share trading volumes reflects, to some extent, ongoing weak sentiment, in our view. We note a lack of new funds flowing into the A-share market currently and view the continued weak high-frequency property data YTD as a significant overhang for upstream and downstream sectors. Market participants are generally anticipating challenges to economic growth as the property sector has yet to stabilise meaningfully, dampening investor sentiment.

Meanwhile, under new nine-point guidelines, regulations are set to be tightened further. Investors are proactively shunning companies with poor earnings performance and low shareholder returns (mainly mini/small-caps), further draining liquidity for small/ medium-caps. We note small-caps have significantly underperformed large-caps YTD. The SSE 50 Index has risen 3.3% YTD while the CSI500/1000 indices have fallen by varying degrees. The Special Treatment (ST) sector index is more than 50% below its level at the start of this year.

In addition, during the 2024 Lujiazui Forum on 19 June, chair of the China Securities Regulatory Commission (CSRC) Mr Wu Qing said full consideration would be given to the largest part of the market where small- and medium-sized investors account for the vast majority, monitoring and supervision would be enhanced for trading tools, including those for high-frequency trading (HFT) purposes and OTC derivatives, and regulatory pertinence and adaptability would be improved. With some HFT traders reducing trading frequency, market-trading volumes have consequently declined.

The share prices of these industry leaders have outperformed sector indices by 13% on average YTD. Among the major sectors, electronics, petrochemicals and machinery have outperformed by more than 20%. We think this is mainly because insurance funds for allocations to the equity market are among a handful of new fund inflows amid a deceleration in northbound net inflow and muted mutual fund issuance. Insurers favour large-caps because of their earnings stability, and the 'large-cap' and 'defensive' factors somewhat overlap amid recent subdued A-share market sentiment. After the release of the nine-point guidelines, some investors have shifted to large-caps with higher earnings and more transparent corporate governance. ## Q4: Why is there a lack of net capital inflow into the A-share market?

New mutual fund issuances are subject to challenges. New issuances have been muted YTD (as at 21 June), due to disappointing investor returns in the secondary market, down 37% YoY to Rmb103.5bn—even below the weekly issuance during the peak of 2021. We also note an overall net outflow of northbound funds since June. The monthly net outflow of northbound funds totalled Rmb33.1bn, as of 24 June. Tightening liquidity in the equity market has led to a situation where existing funds are accelerating their rotation of thematic investing.

## Q5: How should we address ongoing A-share volatility—which factors are noteworthy?

The government's resolve to safeguard the capital markets cannot be overlooked despite the Chinese economy's multiple challenges. Since mid-January, a number of government agencies have issued statements on the subject. We also note: 1) RRR and rate cuts by PBoC; 2) Central Huijin's announcement stating its full recognition of the allocation value of the current A-share market; 3) Vice Premier He Lifeng's proposal to increase support for high-quality listed companies; 4) the State-Owned Asset Supervision and Administration Commission's (SASAC) step to incorporate market cap management into the assessment of heads of central SOEs; 5) a government work report highlighting the need to strengthen the intrinsic stability of the capital market; 6) the release of the nine-point guidelines to promote the quality development of the capital market and increase investor returns; 7) a notable increase in the turnover of major stock index ETFs since 23 June after a recent correction.

The A-share market's equity risk premium (ERP) is now at +1SD above its historical average, while the CSI300 Index’s trailing PE is below its five-year average. We therefore think downside risk to the A-share market is manageable overall.

On style allocation, we prefer high dividend yields within 'value'. We expect large-caps to outperform small- to mid-caps due to their higher earnings visibility and the potential inflow from long-term sources of capital. In terms of sector preference, we are overweight electronics, energy, utilities and telecoms. On investment themes, we prefer quality SOEs under the VCC.

We suggest watching property activity as policy tailwinds have begun to play out, which we think may drive stabilisation and an A-share market rally. In addition, the direction of fiscal and tax reforms and the pace of consumption recovery still need further tracking. The third plenary session (held in July) may provide more policy guidelines.

## Q6: Why are investors undervaluing SOEs?

In our view, the significant valuation difference between SOEs and their non-SOE peers is due to three major reasons, namely sector distribution, operating efficiency and investors' negative perceptions.

First, sector distribution explains a significant part of the performance gap. A majority of SOEs are in commodity-related sectors. Coal is the most extreme case as 95% of total market capitalisation in this sector belongs to SOEs. In fact, commodity-related sectors usually have high entry barriers and are dominated by leading SOEs. As a result, we think their low valuations are partly due to earnings volatility and investor perceptions.

Second, SOE profitability lags that of non-SOEs. For years, large-scale SOEs were known for their low efficiency. Though we note the gap between SOEs and non-SOEs in terms of ROE and ROA, this has narrowed notably in recent years. Using DuPont analysis to split the ROE, we find SOEs have significantly underperformed non-SOEs in NPM terms, while leading in terms of leverage ratio.

Third, a few non-operational issues have led to a negative perception of SOEs—eg, weaker corporate governance and their duty as a 'national service' have in our view kept many investors away.

# Q7: SOEs are trading at a large discount to non-SOEs—is this reasonable?

We view this discount as unjustified, whether in PE-earnings growth or P/BV-ROE terms.

From a PE-earnings growth perspective, non-financial SOEs are trading at a significant PE discount to non-SOEs, and this has been expanding over recent years—since 2021, the multiple for non-SOEs has risen to 2x that of SOEs. Is this big valuation gap explainable by the earnings growth difference between them?

Figure 18: Within non-financials, non-SOEs have a valuation premium over SOEs
![](images/ee59384c6536fb9cbdf7190f403c252068a62f88518813055b9a2727623a3baf.jpg)
Source: Wind, UBS-S

It is true that in some ways SOEs lag non-SOEs in terms of earnings growth. A comparison of earnings between non-financial SOEs and non-SOEs in the A-share market shows clearly the latter did better in terms of profit growth in six of the past 10 years. On a compounded basis, however, the non-SOE lead is not as big as envisaged— over the past decade (2014-23), their NP delivered an 11% CAGR versus SOEs' 9%.

Most recently, non-SOEs' superior earnings have been increasingly less significant. Though their profit outpaced that of SOEs in 2020, SOE profit growth was more than 4x faster in 2021 when the business environment was more challenging. If we take valuations into account, the outcome is less clear—SOE profit growth had risen substantially since 2021 while the valuation gap with non-SOEs had widened further; it was not until 2023, when the VCC was introduced and became sought-after, that the valuation gap narrowed.

Figure 19: Within non-financials, non-SOE profit growth outpaced SOEs' in six out of the past 10 years
![](images/a24d0d52637753a0d166d9f769aa5cb53134602f726933230786f36c83b2f5be.jpg)

Apart from PE, most of the SOE/non-SOE P/BV gap cannot be explained by the corresponding ROE difference, in our view. To identify this unexplainable part of the ROE difference, we use 'non-SOE factors' that we define as the difference between the: 1) non-SOE P/BV premium over SOEs, 2) non-SOE ROE that is greater than for SOEs.

To gauge the portion of a P/BV multiple as determined by ROE for each sector, we examine the P/BV premium of non-financial non-SOEs over SOEs along with nonfinancial non-SOEs' ROE advantage over SOEs.

We note that out of the 25 non-financial sectors, only 14 have P/BV gaps between non-SOEs and SOEs explainable by the corresponding ROE differences. While it is almost 100% explainable for sectors such as non-ferrous metals and real estate, the explainability is nearly zero for those such as textiles and agriculture. In other words, in our view 'non-ROE factors' for 11 out of 25 sectors are simply too large to ignore.

In our "Capital market with Chinese characteristics … " series of reports, we discussed what might help bridge the valuation gap between SOEs and non-SOEs in different sectors:

Category-A sectors: Improving ROE should suffice to boost SOE valuations, including steel, machinery, non-ferrous metals, electronics and property.

Category-B sectors: Non-SOEs' higher operating efficiency is not fully priced in, including home appliances, electrical equipment, textiles, agriculture/forestry/ husbandry/fishery and light-industry manufacturing.

Category-C sectors: Higher SOE valuations call for new business exploration and investor education, including building & design, chemicals, mining, communications, transportation, automobiles, media, utilities, defense & military industry, medical care, leisure, commercial trade and food & beverage.

## Q8: How crowded is the 'dividend strategy' now?

A-share investors have gravitated towards a high-dividend strategy as property activity is yet to stabilise and consumption recovery has so far been modest. This dividend strategy also has significant overlap with the large-cap, defensiveness and SOE factors. The SSE Dividend Index has YTD significantly outperformed the broader market (as represented by Wind All A-share and CSI300 indices). YTD (up to 24 June), the SSE Dividend Index is up 11%, the CSI300 is up 1% and the Wind All A-share Index is down 8%.

We note mutual funds have kept increasing their positions in high-dividend stocks since 2021. We define as 'high dividend' those A-share stocks with a dividend yield equal to or greater than 4%; we then estimate their proportion in the aggregate stock holdings of mutual funds. We estimate after falling to a 10-year low of 8.9% in 2021, the proportion rose in 2022-23. It rose swiftly to 15.0% as of Q124, still well below the peak of 23.1% in 2018.

In addition, mutual funds have continued to increase their positions in SOEs on the tailwinds of the VCC theme. We think recent SOE stocks’ performance could be mainly due to investors chasing defensiveness. SOE stocks usually have high dividend yields, which aligns with the broader policy context of the nine-point guidelines' emphasis on shareholder returns.

## Q9: What should we make of the 'people’s nature of the capital market'?

We think capital appreciation aids common prosperity. According to the CSRC, there are more than 220m retail investors in China's stock market and an even larger number of mutual fund investors in China. We think it is reasonable to assume the rise and fall of share prices affects the majority of Chinese households' wealth. If we total the stakes of the state, mass market retail investors, mutual funds, insurance funds and the national social securities fund and label them 'the stake of ordinary people' in the A-share market, the total amount reaches Rmb49trn, more than 62% of total A-share market capitalisation (see our report Capital market with Chinese characteristics I: Strategic importance of the A-share market.)

This year's nine-point guidelines call this the "people's nature of the capital market". We therefore think the A-share market could serve as a new household wealth reservoir, especially during the property downturn, and that the market now has a higher level of strategic importance for the economy. We believe 'new quality productive forces' and self-reliance cannot be fully developed without stronger financing support from the equity market. A re-rating of SOEs (see our report Capital market with Chinese characteristics II: SOE valuation dilemma and potential solutions) could also help alleviate the pension shortfall via state-owned capital transfers (see our report Capital market with Chinese characteristics III: How to support an ageing China?).

## 10: How is SOEs' market cap management progressing?

On 24 January 2024, the SASAC of the State Council announced for the first time that it would include market cap management results in the performance appraisal of central SOEs’ management teams. Based on the “profit + five ratios” system of metrics (total profit, ROE, overall employee productivity, OCF/cash ratio, R&D/sales ratio and assetliability ratio) already in place, the SASAC plans to comprehensively roll out the market cap management assessment of listed companies where central SOES hold a controlling stake in 2024, thereby evaluating the measures and effectiveness of corporate market cap management and drawing more attention to the intrinsic value and market performance of listed companies. The ultimate goal is to help enterprises enhance their sustainable development capabilities and drive forces for value creation so as to enhance market confidence and investor returns.

Shortly afterwards, the CSRC also issued laws and regulations to encourage companies to pay more attention to market cap management. On 15 March, the CSRC issued its "Opinions on Strengthening the Supervision of Listed Companies (Trial)", which states listed companies should always prioritise investor interest, respond in a timely manner to investor concerns and enhance investors‘ 'sense of gain'. In particular, it mentions it is imperative to “push listed companies to strengthen market cap management and enhance investment value”.

On 17 June, the SASAC issued a document requiring SOEs to "focus on primary business and upgrades, and seek better development". Going forward, we expect SOEs to develop new quality productive forces and immediately increase efforts in horizontal integration. In recent years both central and non-central SOEs have been focusing their attention on sustainable development and corporate governance. China has this year accelerated the roll out of policies related to sustainability information disclosure; the trend of A-share companies enhancing ESG information disclosure is increasing. Central SOE-controlled listed companies have set an example by releasing ESG/sustainability special reports. Compared with previous years, the content highlights of these reports have turned to the development of new quality productive forces, the implementation of green and low-carbon transformation, and the promotion of value creation and discovery of central SOEs.

## Valuation Method and Risk Statement

For various stocks across the industries we cover in the Hong Kong and mainland China stock markets, we use a variety of valuation approaches including DCF models, Gordon growth model analysis and relative valuation analysis using various multiples such as PE, EV/EBITDA and P/BV.

We think the risks facing China's equities include a hard landing for the property market, a capital exodus associated with currency depreciation and slow structural reform progress. In our view, any government policies that do not adequately address these risks could result in a shock to the market. For example, an excess of stimulus policies could pose a risk to the transition from an investment-driven to a consumption-driven economy and increase the debt of government and state-owned enterprises.
