## China: Growth and the labor force gender disparity

Female labor force participation ratio has fallen to 60%, amid faster shrinkage of working-age population

Females spend more time on unpaid family work, especially childcare due to less government support

Better education doesn’t grant females higher wages, who are severely underrepresented in leadership roles

• Closing labor force gender gap likely could lift China<sup>’</sup>s annual growth by more than 0.23%

More efforts are needed to secure equal job access, empower equal voice and improve social welfare

China has risen as a global economic power in the past few decades, but one key challenge the economy now faces is potential growth deceleration. We estimate that China<sup>’</sup>s potential growth has slowed from above 10% on average between 1990-2010, to around 6% in the five years prior to the pandemic, and will decelerate further to 2-3% in 2030s. The major drag comes from shrinkage of working-age population with low birth rate and aging population.

However, one factor which has often been ignored in the standard production function model for potential growth estimation is gender disparity. Lower female labor force participation, occupational segregation, and leadership underrepresentation usually lead to inefficient human capital allocation, and weigh on economic growth. In this note, we review the state of labor force gender disparity in China, and assess the room to improving gender equality and slow the pace of potential growth deceleration.

## Starting with a biased sex ratio

In 1980, China was among the first batch of economies to ratify the United Nations Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women. Then gender equality was integrated into Chinese law, with the Law on the Protection of Rights and Interests of Women and the Law on Maternal and Infant Healthcare being signed into practice in early 1990s.

However, China's birth sex ratio (the number of males per 100 female births) picked up substantially from the mid-1980s, reaching nearly 117 around 2005 (Figure 1). Note that the "natural" sex ratio at birth should be around 106 males per 100 females (ranging between 103-107). The significant spike of sex ratio at birth is mainly due to a son-preference culture and the strict "one-child" policy back then, leading to possible under-reporting of females and selective sex abortion. The latter likely increased amid greater availability of B-scan

ultrasonography machines (even though the practice is illegal).

The elevated sex ratio partly explains the high saving rate in China. For instance, Shangjin Wei and Xiaobo Zhang (2011) found that Chinese families with a son tend to save more to increase their son's advantage in the marriage market, though families with a daughter may not reduce their saving to avoid an erosion of their daughter's bargaining power after marriage. The sex ratio normalization is halfway, and the saving tradition has continued. In 2023, Chinese households<sup>’</sup> saving rate stood at above 30%, while that for the US fell to 3.7% by year-end.

## Lower labor participation, faster aging

With a gradual normalization in sex ratio at birth, the total female population ratio has been on a very modest upward trend. Chinese females<sup>’</sup> participation in economic activity is relatively high compared to elsewhere in Asia. However, the female labor force participation ratio has fallen by early 10%pts since early 1990s to around 60% lately, and the gap with their male peers has remained elevated.

This labor force participation gender disparity also exists among the youth, and the gap has widened recently. Labor force participation rates for both youth males and females stood at nearly 70% in the early 2000s, partly due to low college enrollment rates back then. Over the past decade, the government made significant progress in boosting education attainment. The higher-education college enrollment ratio increased from 15% in 2002 to 59.6% in 2022, among which the ratio of females increased from 44% to 50% over the same period. Meanwhile, female youth labor force participation rate continued trending down to 45% as of 2022, and its gap from their male peers has become larger. It points to less friendly labor market conditions for youth females (Figure 2).

## More unpaid family care responsibility

One globally common factor holding females back from more active participation in the labor market is a heavier burden from unpaid family care work. On average, females perform

76% of all unpaid care work, and no country in the world has achieved equality in the distribution of unpaid care work. On recent trends, it may take until year 2228 to close the gender gap in unpaid care work. Females globally average 4.4 hours of unpaid work per day versus 1.4 hours for males.

This disparity is present in China. The NBS carried out the national time use survey twice, one in 2008, and the other in 2018. Over the decade, both females and males spent more time on unpaid family work, though the burden was still significantly heavier on females. In 2018, unpaid family work cost females 3.8 hours (vs. 3.6 hours in 2008), compared to 1.5 hours for males (vs. 1.3 hours in 2008). Less time was spent on housework such as food preparation, cleaning, laundry etc in 2018 (2.1 hours for females vs. 45 mins for males), but more time was spent on childcare (53 mins for females vs. 17 mins for males in 2018), sending kids to school and caring for adult family members etc (Figure 5).

Some literature, e.g. Liu, Zhang and Li (2009), attributes the heavier childcare burden partly to China<sup>’</sup>s economic transition and SOE reforms. SOEs used to have childcare facilities, but they are becoming less usual now. There has also been a decline in government support for childcare and elderly-care, with the responsibilities being shifted toward households. For the generation under the one-child policy, there was also an increase in family demand for childcare, including sending kids to extracurricular classes, etc. Usually, females are more likely to spend more time on unpaid family work, making them less favored in the labor market, and tend to earn less than males.

## More educated, but less represented

For females participating in the labor market, the gender disparity issue also exists. While the gender gap in education attainment has largely closed in China, better education does not grant females more job opportunities or higher salary payment. In recent years, females have even outpaced their male peers at higher levels of education attainment. For example, the number of female postgraduates reached 1.87 million,

51.2% of total postgraduates (Figure 6). However, according to the CFPS (China Family Panel Studies) conducted by Peking University, Chinese females<sup>’</sup> wages were about 75% of males in 2010s regardless of education level. This percentage dropped to below 70% by 2020 (vs. 80% in the US). For graduates, females<sup>’</sup> wages were about 75% of males.

![](images/caf028094cd7b2b87a1aa95a898b227fe585ec019d3439a38dd9791a5dcc178e.jpg)

Moreover, females are severely underrepresented in leadership roles. For political positions, the proportion of seats held by females in national parliaments was 24.9% in 2022 (vs. 21.8% in 2000). The ratio stands at 26.5% for the 14<sup>th</sup> National People<sup>’</sup>s Congress deputies, and 22.5% for the 14<sup>th</sup> Chinese People's Political Consultative Conference members (Figure 7). No female has ever been a member of the Politburo Standing Committee of the Communist Party.

According to MSCI<sup>’</sup>s annual Women on Boards report, the average female directorship for Chinese companies was 13.8% in 2021, compared to 22.6% globally and 14.5% for developing economies. In 2023, the proportion of companies with no women on boards stood elevated at 21.8%, significantly higher than 2.1% in Japan and 0.2% in the US. Out of the 601 CEOs under the MSCI<sup>’</sup>s tracking, only 6.8% of them are females. For listed banks, there were 526 senior executives by end of 2022. Only 91 of them or 17.3% are female. By ownership, females are much less represented in large and share-holding banks (Figure 8).

The gender disparity for retirement age may partly explains the lower female force participation rate, and has inhibited experienced Chinese females professionals from assuming senior leadership positions. China<sup>’</sup>s statutory retirement age is set at 50 for female workers in general and 55 for women holding management positions, but at 60 for men in both cases. It has been unchanged since 1951, despite the fact that life expectancy already increased from 35 in 1949 to 78.2 in 2021.

## A glimpse of migrant worker

A glance at female migrant workers (FMWs) is also helpful to gauge the state of labor force gender disparity in China. FMWs are among the most vulnerable groups in the labor market as they are usually less educated and with a rural household registration. Some of them work in a town or city close to their hometown (local or short-distance FMW), while others work in cities further away (goes-out or long-distance FMW) where pay is typically around 30% higher. FMWs were hit more during the pandemic as they usually take on more family care (kids/elderly) responsibilities, especially amidst COVID waves and lockdowns. Hence,they have lower flexibility to travel away for jobs. Instead, more FMWs have worked locally at the cost of lower wages (Figure 9). As China reopened, more FMWs became capable to travel to cities further away and seek better job opportunities.

## Efforts on the way, but more needed

In response to the labor force gender disparity in China, the government has made some efforts on protecting female rights and promoting female development.

China amended the Law on Women<sup>’</sup>s Rights and Interests Protection on October 30, 2022 (effective from January 1, 2023), aiming for stronger female protection against gender discrimination and sexual harassment. One major change is the stipulation of behaviors that employers must not engage in during the recruitment process, including restricting/prioritizing a job offer to males, marital and maternal situation investigation or requiring pregnancy tests as an entry physical examination item for females<sup>’</sup> job application, etc. However, according to a survey conducted by Zhaopin, one of China<sup>’</sup>s largest online recruitment platforms, about 61% of women in China have been asked about their marriage and childbirth plans during job interviews.

Meanwhile, China has largely removed restrictions on family planning, and several local governments have increased maternity and paternity leave and introduced new employerpaid childcare leave entitlements. However, the impact on lifting the birth rate has been relatively limited. There has also been a lot of China onshore discussion, especially among the NPC deputies during the annual Two Sessions, to raise the retirement age, likely to 65 for both males and females. But progress on this has been quite slow.

The latest 2021-2030 Outline for Women Development aims for further progress in promoting equal employment treatment, targeting female employment ratio at around 45% (vs. 40% in the 2011-2020 outline). In the 2022 Chinese female development monitoring report, female employment recorded 0.32bn persons, accounting for 43.2% of total employment, or 67.66mn persons (40.5%) for the urban non-private sector. Note that the female employment ratio has been hovering around 43.5 with a moderate downward trend since 2017 (Figure 10), partly attributable to pandemic related distortions. The gap from the 45% target may require additional efforts amid the soft labor market recovery.

Figure 10: China female employment % of total employment
![](images/7d049c087482eb6d42d0d4f9ad7e9f9317fb61485518106e3265154fd026a42b.jpg)

To release the growth potential supported by better labor force gender equality, more efforts are required in China. Aside from eliminating the remaining gender disparity in education attainment, Chinese policymakers need to prioritize their efforts on increasing females<sup>’</sup> access to economic and political opportunities, empowering females an equal voice in households and societies and improving social welfare, especially childcare and elderly-care facilities and benefits, etc. Removing gender-based discriminatory practices in the workforce would increase the efficiency of China<sup>’</sup>s labor market and slow down the pace of potential growth deceleration. The OECD has estimated that closing the gender gaps in labor force participation and working hours has the potential to provide an average 9.2% boost to GDP across OECD countries by 2060, adding about 0.23% to average annual growth. While we do not have a concrete estimate for China, with labor force gender disparity similar or more severe in some cases compared to the OECD average, we think the lift to China<sup>’</sup>s economic growth by closing labor force gender gaps could be even larger.
