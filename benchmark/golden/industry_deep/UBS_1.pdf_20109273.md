# European Equity Strategy Europe's China challenge

## A headwind of rising importance

Alongside the energy crisis and covid disruptions, China looms large as a headwind to Europe's economic recovery and companies. China's domestic weakness has been a multi-year drag but was overshadowed by strength in the US. Now that both regions appear to be slowing, the nascent European recovery is vulnerable, again, to external forces. Our Narrative Radar has recently picked up Europe's China exposures as being the dominant driver of SXXP returns. China has been holding the market back having eclipsed the positives from lower 10y yields and soft data improvements. With the 3rd Plenum underway, we look closer at Europe's China challenge and ask our analysts about the demand and competitive environment of their sectors' exposure to China.

## Eastern exposures a drag on the market

China is Europe's second largest export market (13%) with Germany accounting for half. Industrial goods/autos account for 54% of exports. Luxury, semis and chemicals products account for most of the rest. China is also the buyer of half the global supply of commodities. Weakness in demand in these sectors remains a key theme.

Five of the six major European industries with China exposure have forecasts for ongoing earnings stagnation or weakness. Cap goods buck the trend with broader and more positive drivers elsewhere. Demand weakness in China is the primary concern currently. However, China is becoming a competitive threat too - most obviously in the auto sector but soon too in the semiconductor and chemicals sectors.

## Sector and stock greenshoots but no sign of thematic outperformance

The New Order PMIs in China-exposed sectors have been among the weakest - averaging just 45. At least this is off a low point of around 40. The improvement lends some hope that a recovery may be underway but 'less negative' is still quite different from the positivity that sectors generally need for outperformance. Additionally, the PMI improvement has been no stronger than in other European sectors and so leaves China exposures languishing on a relative basis. Stronger domestic demand as well as property and infrastructure investment would help spur more optimism but as Andrew Garthwaite points out, China is still digesting a decade of excesses in real estate investment and credit creation.

While the average stock-level REVS score for China-exposures is barely above zero (neutral), our analysts are finding some room for optimism. About half the stocks on our exposure list are Buy-rated and some do have higher REVS scores. Stocks like Adidas, SKF, Schneider Electric and Antofagasta all score positively, have Buy ratings and average around 20% expected upside. Sell-rated stocks with China exposure include Volkswagen, Volvo, Schaeffler and Burberry.

Key themes to watch from China are rising competition in the semis, autos, robotics/ automation and chemicals sectors, luxury demand trends in China, supply/demand balances favouring copper over iron ore and EV demand/policies in Western markets.

This report includes analysis of China's economy and its impact on European companies and economy. There are six sector reviews by our analysts. We include a China-exposures list of stocks, recommendations and the latest REVS scoring.

## European equity exposure to China

In our Narrative Radar, we use a variable that we hope gives us insight into the activity trends in China that the Stoxx 600 is sensitive to. We do this by looking at the performance of China exposed stocks relative to non-China exposed stocks and making this relative performance a factor in our machine learning model for the SXXP. This variable seemed to capture the importance of the changes in demand from China that European companies benefited from at times during Covid and China's reopening in early 2023.

Recently, this "China activity" variable has been rising in importance to the SXXP again. It is unclear why, because the data in China has not moved significantly and generally remains weak. However it might well be this persistent weakness that is the cause of the rise in China's importance and something to take note of. The most China-exposed industries in Europe include luxury, semis, capital goods, autos, chemicals and mining. Several of those continue to struggle for earnings momentum and aside from capital goods, all have 12 month forward EPS estimates that are below those forecast a year ago.

As the third plenum approaches, the competitive threat to European autos in EV production looms large. So too does the weakness in the property market that constrains some of the capital goods and mining stocks. Displays of wealth (luxury/ autos) is increasingly discouraged and a huge surge in spending on semiconductor equipment may fade, lead to more semis supply and also be under threat from further US/China tensions/restrictions. Considering those factors, perhaps recent weakness we have seen in Europe is not all down to election risk premia and may just reflect that some of Europe's biggest and historically best companies are having a tough time growing earnings right now.

That's not to say opportunities don't exist. Many companies with China exposure are Buy-rated by our analysts and score highly on our more tactical REVS framework. They have global operations and, particularly for cap goods and semis, benefit from strength in demand from policies like the IRA and CHIPS Acts in the US.

The New Orders PMIs for sectors in Europe that have exposure to China (tech hardware, autos, industrials goods, mining, consumer goods and chemicals) have been weak since China's slowdown began in 2021. This has correlated with underperformance of Europe's China-exposed companies. There was a brief period at the beginning of 2023 when optimism around China's re-opening from covid restrictions saw orders recover slightly and stocks outperform. This proved short-lived however. The recent improvement in PMIs in these sectors gives some hope that there is a recovery happening but we caution that less pessimism is not the same as optimism. Additionally, the recovery in these sector PMIs has been no faster than other sectors in Europe - leaving these China-exposed European companies still weak relative to the broader market.

China exposures seem to have become the dominant driver of SXXP returns recently.

The threat is not just on Chinese demand but also rising competition in multiple sectors like autos, chemicals and semis.

Opportunities are stock-specific and rely on exposure to stronger end markets like the US reshoring and industrialisation theme.

So how important is China really for the EU? A shortcoming of Figures 7-13 is that they are based on final exports without taking into account the origin of imported subcomponents that were needed to produce the export. For example, a car shipped from Germany to China might be manufactured with components that were produced in Slovakia or China itself. To gain a better understanding of the "true" importance of China as an EU export destination for the EU in aggregate as well as individual EU members, we need to disentangle and net out the trade in inputs and indirect trade via other countries. This can be done with data from the OECD's TiVA data base, which we use to compile Figure 14. Importantly, intra-EU exports are excluded here. Figure 14

China's demand for EU imports represents around 1.9% of EU GDP

(above) shows that, within the EU's exports to the rest of the world, China had a share of 13% (2018), thus coming second after the US (24%). For Germany, France, Italy, Spain, the Netherlands, Switzerland and the UK, exports to the US were more important than exports to China. For Germany, however, China is the biggest trading partner, with exports and imports combined.

How important is Chinese final demand for economic activity in the EU and its individual member states? Figure 15 shows that Chinese demand for imports from the EU represents around 1.9% of EU/Eurozone GDP, with higher shares in Ireland (4.5%), Germany and Switzerland (2.5%), Finland (2.0%) and somewhat lower shares in France (1.5%), Italy (1.4%), the UK (1.3%) and Spain (1.1%). In comparison, the respective shares are a lot higher for Australia (7.8%), South Korea (6.8%), Brazil (3.9%) and Japan (2.9%), in a similar range for Canada (1.4%) and somewhat smaller for the US (0.9%).

Based on this data, we would estimate, as a rough approximation, that a net decline in Chinese imports by 10% would reduce EU GDP by around 0.2pp - a bit more in Ireland, Germany, Switzerland and Finland and a bit less in France, Italy, the UK or Spain.

## China after The Third Plenum

The much awaited 3rd Plenum is being held July 15-18, with key topics possibly including: adjustment of central-local government fiscal relationship and debt structure; development of high quality growth to boost innovation, productivity growth, and breakthroughs in emerging sectors and bottleneck areas; pushing for new urbanization and improving social safety net with reforms in hukou and land systems; further opening up and measures to improve SOE efficiency and support non-SOE development.

The 3rd Plenum could reinforce the new industrial policy of China toward higher value-chain exports.

Despite the sometimes significant announcements at 3rd Plenums of the past, this one could reinforce existing trends or announced ambitions. We take this as an opportunity to reflect on the importance of China as both a competitor and a customer.

China has been increasingly competitive with Europe - especially in the auto sector - while also slowing their demand for European consumer and industrial products. The competitive threats loom large in chemical and semis too.

# Q: How has the competition and demand environment in China changed for European SEMICONDUCTOR companies?

## UBS VIEW

Semis: China is somewhat of a paradox for the European semiconductor sector. On one side, it presents itself as a high growth region, with specific end-markets such as automotive materially outgrowing Western markets. In 2024 for example, we estimate EV sales in China to increase 31% y-o-y vs. 11% and 8% in the US and Europe respectively. European power semiconductor suppliers such as a Infineon and STMicro currently have strong footholds at Chinese OEMs and are set to benefit from this growth. On the other side, the market poses a mid-term threat, with rocketing investment in domestic manufacturing capacity and government pressure to localise supply chains. This raises the chance that European players lose share within China but also presents a greater, arguably existential threat of Chinese semiconductors flooding the global market.

SemiCap: For semiconductor equipment manufacturers like ASML and ASMI, the threat is less existential but poses a potential downside surprise if and when China demand slows. Tool demand from China has spiked due to the above investment in domestic manufacturing capacity and likely also due to the prospect of further tool export restrictions on Chinese foundries and IDMs. We estimate domestic China demand for ASML has risen from 10% of group revenue in 2022 to c45% in Q1 of this year, with a similar spike seen at ASMI. We estimate that the market is valuing earnings in the region at a premium to group earnings, likely overstating the sustainability of these sales.

For now, China is mostly a strong demand driver for European semi makers. In the near future, they could be a powerful competitor posing existential risk.

Very strong demand stems from political tensions that continue. There is a risk that demand slows or more onerous restrictions are imposed.

## EVIDENCE

1) Our bottom-up model breaking out auto semis revenues by region suggests incumbents will still benefit from growth in China despite share losses; 2) Our cohort model shows the buffer from the installed base of EV models where incumbents are present; 3) Our teardown of a BYD Seal suggests there is still a quality and performance gap between incumbent and Chinese products; 4) China customs import data for semiconductor production equipment, which shows continued growth in imports.

## WHAT´S PRICED IN?

Semis: We estimate high China exposed semi names are trading at a c20% discount to low exposure names, on a 12m forward P/E basis. This compares to no persistent premium/discount pre-2023 when the China theme was less prominent. SemiCap: In contrast, for SemiCaps, we estimate China earnings across the sector are being valued at a premium to group averages.

# Q: How has the competition and demand environment in China changed for European INDUSTRIAL companies?

UBS VIEW

China has been a key end-market for the European Capital Goods sector with >10% sales exposure to the country in terms of group sales for the sector on average and with a number of companies at c15%. China has also been a very competitive market domestically and in a number of cases has developed international competition for European Capital Goods players.

In the Industrial segment, domestic demand enjoyed a period of strong growth post COVID reopening through to end of 2023, somewhat boosted by the automotive EV OEM and supply chain investments, which was followed by a sharp correction. Together with some commentators on the corporates' side, we anticipate a stabilisation and a degree of recovery in China Industrial demand in H2 2024.

In the Construction space, demand saw a quick burst of a recovery over 2020-2021 but has been in a severe decline since then driven first by government liquidity curbing measures to the property sector and subsequently by consumer confidence crisis. China Elevators orders for instance saw a c30% decline over 2022-2023 and we expect a further 10% decline in 2024 followed by stable 2025.

From a competition point of view, domestically, the latest meaningful segment that has been impacted by local competition is Rail Equipment with Knorr-Bremse market share coming down from very high levels over the past 3-5 years. The area that we see as subject to most severe local competition currently is Industrial Automation. The Robotics market has become highly competitive in the lower-end segment while Factory Automation (Programmable Logic Controllers, Drives, Motors, Servos) is gradually becoming tougher with local players such as Inovance and Estun clearly taking share.

In terms of China as a competitive threat in Western markets, we saw this as a substantial threat in some segments over 2012-2019 (for instance, in Bearings, Rail, Power Equipment). However, we believe it has now diminished significantly because of recent geopolitical tensions that curtail the appetite for purchasing of Chinese equipment into critical infrastructure applications such as Rail, Power Distribution, Telecom Equipment or Industrial Automation. In Industrial Automation, we have seen Inovance launching a small PLC offering in Europe two years ago but with limited traction so far.

## EVIDENCE

In Industrial, Siemens Digital Industries China orders rose nearly 100% cumulative over 2020-2022 and have since declined to land c7% below 2019 level in Q2 FY2024. Siemens DI sales reached a c80% growth peak and now declined to c30% above. In Construction, starts in China are now down 70% vs 2019 level while Real Estate Investment is c20% below. From a competition point of view, Chinese players have tripled their share in Small PLC market over 2019 to 2023.

## WHAT´S PRICED IN?

We believe the investors currently discount a degree of recovery in China Industrial segment within their expectations for European players for H2 2024 and early 2025 while we strong believe there are virtually no expectations of a recovery in the Construction space. From a competition point of view, we see a degree of awareness of China competition threat locally in China for Industrial Automation & Robotics.

A stabilisation of China's industrial demand at weak levels is forecast.

Competition in the local market is ramping up in rail equipment and industrial automation.

Political tensions may actually ease competitive threats in the rest of the world.

UBS VIEW

Divergence set to continue (note): In 2024 there has been an unusually high level of divergence in performance between different commodities and different stocks with iron ore -25% vs copper/gold +15%. We maintain detailed supply-demand models for the key commodities and financial models for the stocks. Spot metal vs iron ore prices are expected to diverge with copper soon undersupplied but iron ore above cost support levels.

# Q: How has the competition and demand environment in China changed for European CHEMICALS companies?

## UBS VIEW

For the global chemical industry, China is one of the key markets both from the size of the market and the leading in terms of growth. Our more pro-cyclical stance at the beginning of the year was partially predicated on Chinese chemical demand improving and supply-demand tightening. While we seen Chinese exports to Europe declining over the last 12months as European energy prices have fallen, the lack of domestic demand improvement in China remains a headwind for the global industry. While we believe that the destocking across the chemical sector is now behind us, the recovery back to volume levels of 2021, is taking longer to realise, with the exception of non-discretionary consumer volumes. We continue to favour the Consumer Chemical sub-sector, which is delivering organic growth and trading at 8% discount to its average EV/EBITDA multiple over the last five years We least favour the Agricultural sub-sector due to lower farmer incomes and crop prices. We outlined the key themes for the EU chemical sector in a recent report - 2Q24 - What are the key themes?

Consumer chemicals exposures appear more resilient than agricultural. Chinese demand remains soft generally.

## EVIDENCE

Our detailed supply-demand models show that upstream chemical capacity will increase by an average of \~4% pa from 2024 - 27. Approximately \~60% of new capacity is expected to be added in China, growing at an average of \~7% pa, which is ahead of Chinese GDP growth, of below 5% pa in 2024 and 2025. We believe that this raise the risk of exports of commodity chemicals from China to Europe (and other parts of the world). Our analysis in the recent report Go East – How does the Middle East China relationship change the global energy sector? outlines in details the risk of exports from China.

## WHAT´S PRICED IN?

Over the last 3 months the share prices in every sub-sector except Consumer Chemicals have declined, as investors have become more sceptical of a cyclical recovery. On an EV/EBITDA basis, all sub-sectors are trading below the 5-year averages. Specialty Chems are cheapest and Gases most expensive. However, as the Diversified Chems earnings volatility is (on the whole) significantly higher than in other sub-sectors, this means that at trough earnings these companies will appear relatively more expensive. Gases have benefitted from better earnings resilience than the other sub-sectors, strong pricing and the medium term benefit from new clean energy project announcements.

In our outlook for 2024 we highlighted Chinese growth as one of the themes for this year (see here). China is the largest chemical market in the world. We estimate that China will provide \~60% of incremental global petrochemical supply over the next five years. It is also one of the fastest-growing industrial gas markets especially in chemicals and electronics. China is a major player in the energy transition, with a significant slice of the global polysilicon and solar markets as well as being the leader in battery materials and we believe it will continue to be the country with the largest EV market on the planet this decade according to UBS Autos research.

China's incremental chemical supply will dominate globally. This is a major risk for European producers that at current energy prices are competitive but Europe's limited energy resilience makes that outlook cloudy.
