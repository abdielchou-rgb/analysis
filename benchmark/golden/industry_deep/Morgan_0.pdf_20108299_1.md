# Asia Economics Mid-Year Outlook | Asia Pacific

## Syncing Up

The lift from trade is helping trade-dependent economies to sync up with the strong domestic demand stories. The growth cycle is underpinned by more drivers and more economies will be posting a healthy growth trend. The key risk is rise in trade tensions.

A more constructive growth outlook: Until a couple of months ago, Asia’s growth cycle was asynchronous and largely supported by domestic demand in select economies with structural stories. But a better-than-expected recovery in trade has meant that external demand will now complement domestic demand and will lift the trade-dependent economies too. Asia's growth will be on a stronger footing,underpinned by more drivers and more geographies. This strength in exports is sustained by a recovery in the DM capex cycle, which will further support Asia's own capex cycle and help ward off the drag from real rates persisting higher for longer.

Inflation moving towards target range: Inflation has been stabilising close to pre-Covid averages and 82% of the region on a PPP-weighted basis already has inflation within their respective central banks’ comfort zones. We expect further progress on the disinflation front and project that 93% of the region will have inflation back in comfort zone in 2025. When it comes to inflation, China remains the exception in the region. We remain concerned about the persistence of deflationary pressures, driven by the policy approach of stabilising growth via investment instead of consumption.

First the Fed, then Asian central banks: Even though inflation is within comfort zones for the majority of the region, central banks have been held back from cutting rates. Aggregate policy rates – both on a nominal and real basis – remain lower than in the US. A strong US dollar backdrop has meant that Asian central banks have been worried that weaker currencies will lead to imported price pressures and move inflation away from target again. With this in mind, we still see central banks in Asia ex Japan having to follow the Fed and that the rate cut cycle will be shallower than in the US. As our US economics team expects the first cut in September the first cuts in Asia will happen from October 2024 onwards. For Japan, we still see a 15bps rate hike in July 2024 and a 25bps rate hike in January 2025.

Trade tensions are a key risk: Recent developments suggest that trade actions are broadening out across geographies. This reflects concerns over the persistence of China’s excess capacities – something that we have been highlighting in previous research. If trade tensions do rise meaningfully from here, we will be watchful of the indirect effects on financial conditions, corporate confidence, and capex. Shifting investment flows from supply-chain diversification efforts may provide a partial offset, but the lower demand would still be a net negative.

## Syncing Up

# Asia’s growth cycle is on a better footing now

A better and broader base: Compared with a couple of months ago, Asia’s growth is now broader across geographies and drivers. Previously, we had confidence in a positive growth outlook for India, Japan, and Indonesia. The commonality across these economies was that they had the support of structural factors – keeping domestic demand strong in India and Indonesia and in the case of Japan, stronger nominal GDP growth. Now, the expansion cycle in these economies is still intact and more economies are joining in supporting Asia’s growth cycle.

An upturn in trade provides a lift for broader Asia: The trade cycle can be a significant driver of Asia’s growth outlook, considering that Asia accounts for 47% of global manufacturing and 34% of global exports. Asia’s real exports growth has accelerated to 6.6%Y year-to-date from 2%Y in 2H23. On an indexed level basis, real exports have accelerated in the past three months to reach a new high post-2021. As external demand is emerging to be in much healthier shape, this is helping to underpin growth in some of the more trade-dependent economies – like Korea, Taiwan, Malaysia, Singapore, and Hong Kong.

Rising exports help ward off the effects of modestly higher real rates: The rise in exports is occurring at a time when real interest rates are staying higher for longer. Both these phenomena can be traced back to the US, where over the last few months, the upside in GDP growth as well as a slower pace of moderation in inflation led to the market pricing in fewer Fed rate cuts as compared to the start of the year. The resulting weakness in Asian currencies has led central banks here to delay policy rate cuts even as inflation has continued to moderate. The further rise in real interest rates across the region has constrained domestic demand growth to some extent, but this has been offset by the rise in real exports.

## More constructive on Asia’s growth outlook

Our forecasts for 2024 are now higher than what we had in the year-ahead outlook: We now forecast real GDP growth of \~4.5% for Asia over 2024-25. Our new growth forecasts are about 30-40bps higher as compared to our 2024 year-ahead outlook. This principally reflects better incoming data, stronger external demand, and our expectations of the positive spillovers that better exports will generate for capex growth. Overall, we see better growth dynamics across Asia – private final domestic demand is picking up but it will be offset by higher real imports and slower government spending as policymakers take up fiscal consolidation.

External demand recovery should be sustained with a better DM capex trend: Asia’s trade cycle has turned and some of the forward-looking indicators suggest that the recovery in exports should be sustained. Our favourite indicator to assess the near-term outlook for the trade cycle is the ratio of global PMI new orders to inventory. While the initial improvement was driven by inventory, the upturn in the past few months has been driven by a stronger trend in new orders.

Fundamentally, Asia’s trade cycle has been highly correlated to the global investment cycle, particularly the US capex cycle. Over the next 18 months, we expect Asia’s exports to be on a gradual recovery path – our global economics team is projecting a modest improvement in the investment cycle for DM, particularly the US. Considering that 30% of Asia’s goods exports are tech, the US IT capex cycle has direct implications for the region’s tech goods and Indian IT services exports. Our US tech hardware team is expecting tech capex to continue to accelerate, driven by the decline in cost of computing. ## Trade tensions do pose risks to this outlook

Just as trade fundamentals are looking up, mind the rise of trade tensions: Improving fundamentals of the trade cycle have unfortunately been met by the rising risk of trade tensions. Starting from late January this year, we have been highlighting how the persistent rise in excess capacities in China’s manufacturing sector could result in an increase in trade tensions in a series of reports:

• The Viewpoint: How Big a Deflationary Force Will China Be?, January 29,

• The Viewpoint: The Risk of Trade Tensions Returning, February 5, and

• The Viewpoint: Trade Tensions, Supply Chain Diversification, and China, March 19).

This is because China has continued to expand manufacturing capacity at a time when domestic demand is weakening and now that global supply-chain disruptions have receded, trade tensions are also returning.

## Trade policy actions have picked up in recent weeks:

• As it is, the US administration has announced tariffs on several products where China has excess capacities.

• This is adding to the investigations that the European Commission had already launched on China’s EVs, steel and medical devices.

• Just this past weekend, there have also media reports that Canada may be exploring the imposition of higher tariffs on China’s EVs, possibly intended to align them with the US level of tariffs.

What’s next – measures to check rerouting? There are also media reports suggesting that the US may look to address the rules of origin provisions in the USMCA to prevent China from using Mexico as a production base to circumvent tariffs. The US Secretary of Commerce, Gina Raimondo, stated that “The purpose of the USMCA was not to help China, it was to help the signatories of that agreement...We will do whatever we need to make sure China does not use Mexico to get around the new tariffs.” Recent developments are indeed suggesting that the trade actions are broadening out across geographies, reflecting the concerns over the persistence of China’s excess capacities.

Indirect effects, supply chain diversification efforts matter: We have highlighted before that the indirect effects – i.e., via the channels of financial conditions, corporate confidence, and capex – are likely to affect the growth outlook more than the direct effect of tariffs. The uncertainty around trade policy and the effects of tariffs are likely to weigh on corporate confidence and animal spirits, reducing the incentive for the corporate sector to invest.

Supply-chain diversification efforts will only provide a partial offset. For instance, in the event that the US imposes tariffs on all imports from all economies, the effects would likely be more adverse – it would likely skew the supply-chain diversification toward onshoring. However, if the US imposes tariffs on just China, then we think that it will accelerate the "friend-shoring" trend, which would help provide a benefit for other economies in the region excluding China. But, ultimately, the lower demand that would result in this scenario would still weigh on overall growth and be a net negative. One potential mitigating factor could be that this round of trade tensions may have a smaller effect on corporate confidence than in 2018.

## Outlook by economy

Structural stories – India, Japan, and Indonesia – will still be the best: With US rates staying higher for longer, Asian central banks will have to keep rates somewhat high. Against this backdrop, economies which can generate growth with tailwinds from structural factors will be best positioned. Within Asia, this means that we remain constructive on the outlook for India, Japan and Indonesia.

• India: Investment to GDP has been on a decisive rise since 2020, after a decade of decline, and we expect capex to stay strong and drive the growth cycle. This backdrop helps ensure the longevity of the expansion cycle as well as keeping macro stability risks at bay.

• Japan: We see a paradigm shift towards a stronger equilibrium. Recent shifts in wage-price dynamics are helping the vibrancy and dynamism of the corporate sector, which in turn will sustain stronger nominal GDP growth over the medium term (see Blue Paper: A Revitalised Japan).

• Indonesia: The capex cycle is on the rise too and we expect the investment to GDP ratio to continue rising towards pre-Covid levels, which will create a self-sustaining virtuous growth cycle.

Trade-dependent economies syncing up: With the backdrop of a strong US economy, trade-dependent economies will benefit, and this means that their growth outlook will sync up with the stronger domestic demand stories in the region.

• Korea and Taiwan: Over the last few months, these two economies have been the biggest beneficiaries of the rise in external demand, particularly for tech exports. Our Chief Economist for Korea and Taiwan, Kathleen Oh, is expecting both to grow at an above-trend rate over the forecast horizon.

• Malaysia, Singapore, and Hong Kong: We have also lifted the growth forecasts for other such trade-dependent economies like – in effect broadening the list of economies in the region with heathy growth trends.

China – stabilising cyclically but still structurally challenged: Cyclically, the economy is stabilizing, also thanks to the improvement in external demand, as well as robust levels of manufacturing and infrastructure investment, helped by stimulus. Over the past few days, there have also been policy efforts to stabilise the property market. We believe that the combination of these factors will help to stabilise real growth but it will not be enough to sustain moderate levels of inflation.

Indeed, the structural challenges are still overwhelming. The current policy approach of defending real GDP growth with more investment in manufacturing is resulting in persistent deflationary pressures (weak corporate margins), weak incremental return on capital employed, and softer trends in nominal GDP growth. Policymakers have now swapped the allocation of credit from the property sector to industrial investment. This is exacerbating the excess capacity issue in the manufacturing sector.

The structural challenges are likely continue to overwhelm the cyclical improvements. Indeed, even with real growth stabilizing in 1Q24, nominal GDP growth has remained weak and it is well below broad credit growth. This suggests that debt ratios – which were already elevated at 303% of GDP in 2023 (vs. 292% in 2022 and 281% in 2021), likely moved even higher in 1Q24.

## Inflation – stabilizing near pre-Covid level

Back to central banks’ target ranges: Asia’s aggregate headline inflation has been stabilizing close to pre-Covid averages over the past quarter, averaging just 1.8% in 1Q24. Core inflation too has been trending lower and has been below 2% in five out of the past six months. On a PPP-weighted basis, 82% of the region currently has inflation within their respective central banks’ comfort zones, a tad higher than the 78% near the end of last year. Excluding China, where deflationary pressures remain persistent, the rest of the region’s aggregate headline inflation is near pre-Covid averages at 3.5%Y in March 2024, supported by a 31-month low in core inflation.

Delving into the components, much of this improvement in Asia ex China inflation has been led by continued easing in fuel and core goods inflation. Indeed, the fuel segment has been in deflation for the past seven months, while core goods inflation has softened to a post-Covid low of 2.1%Y, more than halved from the 5.4%Y peak in February 23. Core services inflation has also gradually moderated to a two-year low of 2.4%Y amid cooling wage growth. Food inflation has remained sticky in a broad-based manner across the region ex-China. In China, food inflation has been a key drag on headline inflation, while core inflation has been trending below 1%.

## China’s inflation to remain below comfort zone, moving towards comfort zone for Asia ex China

China – on course for longest deflation episode since 1998-99: As we highlighted above, the current policy focus of stabilizing real GDP growth via manufacturing and infrastructure capex risks entrenching deflationary pressures in the economy (see the Viewpoint: China's Exit From Deflation – It's More Challenging This Time, March 25). As such, we expect China’s GDP deflator to average -0.3% in 2024 and remain weak at 0.4% in 2025. This would signal the longest deflation episode since the 1998-99 cycle. PPI inflation would also likely remain in deflation until at least 2Q25 given the excess capacity situation in key sectors. Meanwhile, both headline and core CPI inflation could remain below 1% for 2024 and 2025, far below the 2-3% we deem as moderate levels of inflation conducive for the overall macro environment and managing debt-deflation loop risks.

## We see Asian central banks in four groups

## 1) BOK, BOT, RBA, BSP, BI, and CBC

We see these central banks embarking on shallow rate hike cycles over our forecast horizon. In terms of sequencing:

• BOK and BOT: Policy stance has been restrictive and domestic demand faces more headwinds. We expect them to cut first from October. We see 75bps and 100bps rate cuts from these central banks through 2025, respectively.

• RBA and BSP: Elevated inflation – from sticky wage growth and higher food inflation, respectively – should keep both central banks on hold until 1Q25, when inflation largely returns to target. We expect RBA to cut rates by 75bps and for BSP to cut by 100bps in 2025.

• BI; While inflation has already returned durably to target, but FX weakness and uncertainty around the Fed path mean policymakers have guided that rate cuts would not happen in 2H24. As such, we expect rate cuts to begin from 1Q25, with a total quantum of 100bps for the cycle.

• CBC: With headline and core inflation expected to remain relatively range-bound at 2-2.5%, we see CBC staying on hold through 1H25, then cutting from 3Q25 for a total of 25bps in 2025.

## 3) RBI and BNM

• RBI: With credit growth tracking at 16%Y, growth momentum remaining robust and inflation still higher than the midpoint of its target range, we expect RBI to remain on hold through 2025.

## Fiscal policy – moving towards gradual consolidation

Asia's aggregate fiscal deficit is expected to be relatively stable in 2025 after what would be a mildly expansionary year in 2024. We highlight the following idiosyncratic stories within the region.

China – fiscal easing to remain insufficient: In China, the fiscal easing announced year-todate has been modest, with the NPC budget announcing just 0.5ppt expansion in the augmented fiscal deficit this year, to 12.9% of GDP. As we have been highlighting, the supply-side focus of the easing – in the form of manufacturing and infrastructure capex instead of consumption rebalancing efforts – exacerbates the deflationary pressures, in our view.

We expect the augmented fiscal deficit expansion to remain small at 0.5ppt for 2025. To be sure, we remain watchful of upside risks in the deficit expansion, given uncertainty about how recently announced measures to support housing inventory digestion could be funded.

• Our property analyst for China, Stephen Cheung, estimates that it would take RMB 3-4trn in funding to bring housing inventory back to a more reasonable level (i.e., 12 months). See China Property: Optimism on Policy Impact Priced in While Fundamentals Remain Lukewarm (16 May 2024).

• Our economics team for China believes an actionable national inventory digestion plan (with modest central funding) is plausible in 6-12 months. See China Economics: Housing Policy Positives Accelerating (17 May 2024) for more details.

Asia ex China – highlighting idiosyncratic drivers: For Asia ex China, we expect aggregate fiscal deficit to remain stable at 4.8% of GDP in 2024, before further fiscal consolidation brings the aggregate deficit to 4.2% in 2025. Digging deeper, we highlight specific developments in several economies:

• Australia: Our economist, Chris Read, notes that the recently announced FY25 budget projected wider deficits in the coming years to support broad-based spending, including cost of living measures and industrial policies. The former will support a 2H24 sequential growth recovery. In addition, the incoming income tax cut due mid-year should also boost consumer spending. See Australia Macro+ FY25 Budget: Expansion Continues (14 May 2024).

• Indonesia: Investors remain focused on the future conduct of fiscal policy in Indonesia following the presidential elections in February 2024. The incoming administration’s pledge to provide free lunches to school children could signal a large fiscal cost. However, we expect more clarity over fiscal policy to emerge and our base case is that the incoming administration will continue to prioritize a prudent approach in the conduct of fiscal policy. In this regard, we would be watching for announcements on cabinet appointments, which are likely to be unveiled before the new administration takes office in October 2024.

• Korea: Our Chief Economist for Korea, Kathleen Oh, highlights that recent election results mean that a shift towards fiscal expansion in the 2025 budget would be likely, with focus on welfare spending. Moreover, the government is expected to announce tax incentives to encourage companies to participate in Korea’s corporate value-up programme.

• Thailand: Policymakers are still pushing for implementation of the digital wallet program, which will provide eligible citizens with THB 10,000 for spending within six months. The cost of the program is estimated at THB 500 billion (\~2.5% of GDP), with policymakers indicating that the disbursement will happen from October 2024. While we do see this as helpful in boosting consumer spending, we are mindful that the rollout has been pushed out several times, which means that the delayed implementation could pose downside risks to growth.

## Risks to the outlook

China and the US – the anchors to the global upside and downside scenarios: Our global economics team has framed the upside scenario as a fundamentally better Chinese economy and the downside as persistent services inflation in the US. In the upside scenario, more effective policy support leads to a faster and more sustainable reflation. A stronger recovery in nominal GDP growth helps industry profit margins and wage growth, sustaining overall growth. In the downside scenario, the immigration surge ends sooner than expected and leads to more persistent services inflation in the US. In turn, the Fed would have to stay higher for longer.

For Asia, trade tensions are the key risk: Given the importance that trade has in Asia’s cycle, the key risk to the outlook would be a meaningful rise in trade tensions that would curtail the capex cycle in both DMs and Asia. Moreover, a weaker exports outlook would also increase the risks that China’s debt-deflation loop becomes even more challenging to manage, which would transmit to the rest of the region.

Aside from trade tensions, another downside risk we are watching is if the Fed stays on hold for longer. That in turn would mean that rates in Asia would also have to stay higher for longer, which would weaken domestic demand.

One potential source of upside risk is from China. We have seen a recent round of policy initiatives and just this past week, measures were introduced to help stabilise the property market. If these measures provide a durable floor of support to property market activity, this could help lift China’s growth.
