# Further cooling unwelcome

Continued supply and classical risk-of behaviour have framed credit markets ahead of a pivotal non-farm payrolls report. Unemployment is likely to dictate the Fed's policy decision in a few weeks and with that the direction of markets in the near term.

Rarely do financial markets hang on a single data point, and even more rarely one that is scheduled and highly anticipated, but this appears to be one of those occasions. Softening inflation has shifted the Fed's focus to employment, as emphasised by Powell's comments that the FOMC do not " seek or welcome further cooling in labor market conditions". As such, September non-farm payrolls have taken on elevated importance, ahead of the 17-18 September FOMC where the Fed is widely expected to start its cutting cycle. A stronger print is likely to see the Fed cut 25bp, in line with our economists' forecast, while a weaker print has the potential to both raise concerns of a cyclical slowdown and push the Fed towards a 50bp cut.

Into this highly anticipated event, credit has been generally steady. The €-IG index, having traded in a 1bp range since 16 August, slipped only 2bp wider under the cumulative weight of issuance (in both EUR and USD) and the Wednesday rates-rally that took €-IG yields back to their year-to-date low. Cash priced bonds struggled slightly with the move lower in rates ( Figure 1), but in general credit has been resilient to the swings in NVidia's market cap and the pricing of Fed Funds. Indeed, there have been positive signs on the demand front (muted new issue concessions and continued demand from fixed-maturity-funds), suggesting that support for credit is still healthy even with yields in the 3.5% context. Nonetheless, it is clear that credit wil not be immune to what is likely to be broad-based, risk-of price action if payrolls surprise materially to the downside.

Our economists forecast non-farm payrolls will report a net gain of 175k, keeping the US economy on track for a soft landing. A significantly lower number is likely to upend many of the assumptions that our H2 outlook is based on. Putting NFP to one side as a known-unknown, in this week's Alpha we highlight that underperformance of IG Retailers has been a Chinese rather than a cyclical issue, look at HY bond holdings of CLOs, and publish the second half of our European high yield default study, looking at loss rates. Our data pushes back against several popular narratives regarding cov-lite securities and reinforces our preference for IG over HY, risk adjusted.

# Default study, Part 2: HY loss rates in the context of IG vs HY pricing

Loss-on-default data for the Pan European HY index compliments our history of defaults, and appears to contradict key narratives around 'cov-lite' securities. Combining our datasets, we show that HY spreads structurally overcompensate for credit losses, while relative value versus IG is nuanced and cyclical.

This report was previously published on 5 September 2024.

While there is considerable subjectivity in the pricing of high yield, not least because it incorporates various elements such as mark-to-market volatility and general uncertainty, the debate is better informed by having reliable historical data, especially on losses given defaults.

In part one ( see here) we constructed a historical default rate series for the Pan-European HY bond index and showed there was a substantial gap between this and the default rates reported by credit rating agencies. In this report (part two), we collate historical losses for investors that held defaulting securities and use the combined data as an input to frame the pricing of HY versus IG.

Keeping with the trend in default rates, we find that post-GFC credit losses have been very low for European investors. We estimate that the average credit loss over a rolling 12-month period was less than one percent since 2010. Whilst the common narrative has been that cov-lite securities would lead to lower defaults and lower recoveries, we find no evidence that this has been the case thus far. Instead, we find that credit losses are highly idiosyncratic, ie, they do not tend to follow a pattern.

Our second conclusion is that, whilst spreads overcompensate for default losses, most of the excess carry over IG is eroded after accounting for market-to-market volatility and drawdowns. There have been numerous periods when IG has ofered better volatility-adjusted returns, including today. We argue that the best time to buy HY over IG is when HY spreads ofer a premium over a high backward-looking default rate: periods when excess carry (returns volatility adjusted) is above the 10y average and ex ante (backward-looking) losses are above their 35th percentile.

## Default losses average less than 1% through the cycle

At an aggregate level, arguably the more straight forward component of any default loss estimation is the default rate, given the relatively clear cut definition of a default (we include distressed exchanges in our definition). Recovery rates on the other hand can be computed in a variety of ways – often using ultimate recovery or the post-30-days trading price – and, for Europe specifically, data is sparse.

Given the available data, we focus on the realised loss for investors at the point at which bonds drop out of the index due to default, using the index price on the last day that the bonds are in the index. Figure 1 shows the rolling 12-month default losses for PE HY through history, utilising our index default rate outlined in part one of this series. As the chart shows, outside of the GFC, 12m credit losses have rarely exceeded more than 200bp. In fact, the most recent trailing 12m credit losses (187bp ex fins and 270bp with fins) are the highest since 2010 while the average post-GFC is just 80bp.

A major issue with analysing trends in credit losses over time in Europe is the relative lack of defaults. Unlike the US, where there has been a far higher absolute number of defaults, the lack of default cycles in Europe makes it almost impossible to infer any trend in losses over a multicycle period. One could argue that, at the margin, losses are higher in the higher-default periods, as seen in Figure 2, but even so, in the long period of 2010-19 when the default rate was very low, losses were almost on a par with the GFC. And Figure 3, which shows the distribution of credit losses since 2004, is a reminder that losses can be very widespread even if the highest concentration of losses is above 90%.

While there is no denying that covenant protection has been in structural decline for the last decade, there is little in our data to support the idea that cov-lite deals have brought European high yield into a period of lower default rates and lower recoveries. Our data shows no clear change in losses in the post-2016, cov-lite era. Indeed, if anything, the average loss on default has been lower, albeit not in a statistically significant way ( Figure 6) – and we note that this is not skewed by GFC-period data. Likewise, defaults are currently at a multi-year peak and so, while this is in the context of higher rates, it's far from evident in our data that cov-lite issuance has generated fewer defaults or lower recoveries.

A rise in redefaults in Europe would further underline that weak covenants are not depressing the number of defaults.

## Default risks are covered, other risks less so

The first and rather trivial observation is that, with 12m default losses averaging c.80bp through the cycle and only moving above 300bp in the GFC, High Yield credit has ofered spreads in excess of realised default losses for the entire post-GFC period ( Figure 11). This is true both ex ante and ex post, and underscores the issue of referring to market-implied break-even default rates as 'market expectation of HY defaults'. Hence our prior work to quantify the 'excess spread' ofered by European high yield credit at various points in the cycle (and through it).

FIGURE 11. High yield spreads have over-compensated for forward loss rates by a large margin since the GFC
![](images/74025e8c8729e984f44f9e9a1c3a726d6eb396d644f603c989efb43c8544214d.jpg)

One step further, it is also the case that the excess spread on high yield credit over investment grade credit has historically been higher than the realised default losses on the HY index. However, it stands out to us that this is clearer when comparing spot-spreads with trailing 12m losses (ex ante) than when comparing spot spreads with forward 12m losses (ex post), Figure 12. For ex post losses, there are several periods when defaults consumed >75% of the excess carry paid to investors in HY at the beginning of the period. In 2008, 2015, 2018-19 and H1 23, more

than three-quarters of the excess carry ofered by holding HY over IG was consumed by default losses ( Figure 13). Hence, the benefits of high yield as a 'carry trade' have often been far smaller than might have been expected.

Periods when ex ante losses are a high percentage of the IG/HY spread are interesting, as they are periods in which investors were compensated for past defaults, but usually during a recovery: eg, in 2010, 2017 and 2021 when spreads were tightening from wide levels. Arguably, they represent periods in which HY investors were being compensated for past defaults. In our view, the current period does not fit this pattern, as default losses are creeping up towards spreads rather than spreads falling towards trailing default losses.

## Volatility explains most of the spread-gap between HY and IG

At first glance it might appear that the excess carry ofered by High Yield over default losses, even if it is sometimes largely eroded, implies that HY is a superior investment versus IG in the long run. However, to repeat ourselves, HY spreads at not only a compensation for default losses: other factors such as liquidity and mark-to-market volatility need to be taken into account.

Assuming investors desire roughly the same credit spread per unit of spread volatility, then we find that HY is generally a poor way to express a long view in European credit relative to its spread beta. However, if we consider returns volatility instead of spread volatility, and monthly returns versus daily spread moves, then we find that high yield generally ofers more than enough carry to compensate investors for the increased volatility ( Figure 14). The longer returns period (monthly vs daily) dampens volatility favourably for high yield, making it more attractive than IG for investors targetting a set level of volatility. This excess carry, above what would be required to compensate for returns volatility, has averaged c.130bp since 2011.

If we now compare this excess carry to realised losses, we find that quite often the entire benefit of HY over IG, weighted for volatility, is wiped out by realised losses ( Figure 15). This happened (ex. post) in 2015, 2019, and (remarkably) H1 23. Hence, the risk premium on European HY relative to IG is small – c.50bp through the cycle and often zero ex. post – once we demand compensation for both higher returns volatility and default losses.

In less academic terms, we find it reasonable to intuit that, despite being ofered an additional 250bp of carry, the risk of >75% of this being taken away by defaults makes HY far from a ' slam dunk' for asset allocators when considering an asset with c.2x the returns volatility. This explains our ongoing preference for IG over HY at current valuations and at this point in the cycle. At current levels, investors are receiving a compressed level of 'excess carry' (vol adjusted) in a period when we expect defaults to continue running at a more normal level versus the past decade.

## The best of times, the worst of times

Of course, it is also untrue that high yield is never the superior asset class. Instead, we think it is most true to say that a preference for high yield over IG requires either a view that the market will continue to rally, such that leveraging a long-position by holding HY over IG will generate superior returns (conditional on being correct), or a willingness to hold HY through the full cycle and thereby generate superior, unleveraged excess returns by collecting the spread premium ofered by HY in excess of its default rates, even on a volatility-adjusted basis. It is notable that the second strategy becomes more and more reliable the further that we extend the holding period ( Figure 17) which makes it particularly suited to 'sticky' capital such as private credit and pension funds.

Leaning into these general observations, we note a weak negative correlation between trailing 12m losses and the ex post destruction of excess carry by default losses. Put another way, buying HY over IG when spreads are tight due to low trailing default rates is generally a poor strategy ( Figure 18). In contrast, but intuitively, the best time to take HY risk is when the trailing loss rate is high and spreads reflect this. Keeping with the idea that the HY market has nearly always paid a premium over default losses, buying HY when default losses are elevated in some sense compounds the risk premiums that investors receive.

FIGURE 18. Ex-post, compensation for default losses is poor when the IG-HY spread diferential is tight
![](images/166863163a9dd40eec58d20758b09770f99e8fb01d8d4dc1f8964d43acca557c.jpg)
Source: Barclays Research, Bloomberg

For example, one simple strategy would be to buy HY over IG when excess carry (returns volatility adjusted) is above the 10y average and ex ante losses are above the lowest 35% of their range. We find that buying in these periods, albeit occurring only for three to six months each cycle, has generated vastly superior returns to a simple overweight in European HY – even when held over the long term ( Figure 20). Notably, this strategy would have pointed to overweighting HY last summer.

Currently, on the basis of these simple rules, the excess carry in Pan European High Yield is too low, and so this simple strategy agrees with our view that IG credit remains the place to be for now.

Retailers have been lagging, with the sector one of the worst performers in €-IG year-to-date. Spreads are flat vs end-23, compared to the 19bp tightening of the overall index. Indeed, this underperformance has been ongoing for even longer, with the Retail sector and the index gradually converging over the last 18 months ( Figure 1), but it has changed in nature in the last six months. In 2023 underperformance was driven by a rally in the overall index, while the past six months have seen outright widening of the Retail sector from the April lows. The sector now trades only 9bp inside of the index, compared to more than 80bp early last year. Notably, this widening in retailer spreads began before the recent concerns of an economic slowdown in the US.

High Yield and European CLOs

# CLO share of the bond market tapers

CLOs ownership of the €HY bond market is high in a historical context but has tapered of this year. This comes as bond prices have started to converge to loan prices and the loan market has, this year, outgrown the CLO market slightly.

## CLOs own less of the €HY bond market

Since our last update, CLO holdings of €HY bonds have decreased. Admittedly, it has been a modest decline, nevertheless we estimate that their ownership of the €HY market including FRNs fell to 10.8% at the end of August, from a peak of 11.5% at the end of 2023 ( Figure 1). For fixed rate bonds only, ownership is 7.0%, which is two-tenths of the recent highs.

It is worth noting that while we have seen a slight decline, CLO ownership of the bond market is still high in an historical context. Indeed, relative to even end-2021 levels, CLO ownership has roughly doubled and relative to 2018, it is nearly three times the level today, while it was only a decade ago that CLOs owned virtually no bonds at all.

## Part of that is a supply story and part is a cash price story

In our view, the arguments that drove the increase in bond holdings for CLOs over the past few years were mostly focussed on two factors; a lack of loan supply and low bond cash prices. The fact that holdings appear to have tapered of therefore should not be a massive surprise in the sense that the loan market has more recently been growing and cash prices between bonds and loans are converging.

At the end of August, the average HY price of 96.4 was the the highest since February 2022 and up 2.5pt year-to-date. While there is still a pull-to-par argument for CLOs, this attractiveness is waning in the context of where prices are for HY versus loans now.

One pushback to this could be the distribution of prices, but interestingly we find that while 38% of the loan market trades above par, an almost identical proportion of the HY market (35%) does also. Where these numbers start to diverge is the proportion trading above 98.0, which for the loan market is 80% but for the HY market it is comparatively much lower at 53%.

## A more speculative point is the divergence in CCCs

Another interesting observation, albeit more speculative, is that the decreased ownership of the €HY market has come at a time when the percentage of CCC bonds has risen to 7.2% today from 6.7% a year ago, but for loans it has fallen to a five-year low of 2.4% ( Figure 8).

Some of the CCC bond downgrades this year have been particularly high profile, including the ALTICE complex and INTRUM, and one could argue that these downgrades may have made CLO investors more reluctant to increase bond exposure as a result (especially as the loan market has seen a decline in CCCs).

In any case, the net efect on Caa buckets for CLOs is that they are higher due to downgrades ( Figure 9, albeit not the case for CCC), but still below the 7.5% threshold (although not required to sell, breaching the threshold has implications for CLOs).

## Largest bond holdings in CLOs

While CLOs have decreased their share of the bond market, we still estimate there to be 10 bonds where more than 65% of the outstanding bond amount (notional) is held in CLOs, albeit all but one is an FRN ( Figure 10). If we look at fixed rate bonds only, there are 15 bonds with more than 40% of the bond outstanding held in CLOs ( Figure 11). However, within the CLO universe, they are widely held with some 100-200 CLOs holding small amounts of each of these bonds, limiting any concentration risks ( Global CLOs: Limited risk from single issuers in CLO assets, 5 April 2024). This was particularly notable with the ALTICE/SFR debt stack, where 85% of active CLOs had more than 1% exposure ahead of the downgrades (CLO and Leveraged Loans: Risk from Altice/SFR, 25 March 2024).

There is an argument to be made that we have seen the peaks in CLO ownership of the €HY market for now. We would expect the convergence in cash prices to continue, not least as rate cuts will help support bond prices in particular. Additionally, momentum appears to be behind a resurgence of M&A/LBO supply with a healthy near-term pipeline. And our expectation is that this will continue to be biased toward the loan market, not only because of the structural benefits for issuers but also pricing given the number of rate cuts currently priced in through 2025.
