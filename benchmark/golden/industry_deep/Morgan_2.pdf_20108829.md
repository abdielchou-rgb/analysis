## Japan

• Ueda leaves door open for July rate hike

• Activity and inflation reports mixed through 2Q...

… but incoming data unlikely to materially sway BoJ’s immediate policy path

• Next week: Retail sales up, IP rebounds in May

The past week’s data flow was punctuated by Ueda’s remarks on the possibility of a rate hike in July, largely repeating comments he made at the prior week’s monetary policy meeting. By again stating that a hike is possible, we think Ueda aims to leave all options on the table next month, particularly in the face of a renewed yen slide.

Against this backdrop, market views for the July meeting appear to be converging around two scenarios. The first is for a relatively small shift in balance sheet policy that would be coupled with a 15bp rate hike. In other words, a minor reduction in near-term bond purchases would be announced at the same time as a hike to 25bp, albeit the BoJ could also signal a later, more significant, reduction in purchases over a 1-2 year horizon. The second scenario is for an immediate material reduction in JGB buying; i.e., a near-term inflection in liquidity conditions via QT, but no policy rate hike in July.

We noted last week that a “substantial” cut in JGB purchases announced in July could raise the hurdle for a rate hike at the same meeting. While acknowledging the risk that a July policy hike gets pushed back, we maintain our baseline forecast for a 15bp hike next meeting at this point. Taking a step back, either scenario above would represent an important move toward a reduction in policy accommodation. Indeed, the BoJ has already reduced its footprint in the JGB market relative to its large-scale net bond buying in the peak days of QQE and during its aggressive defense of the 10Y YCC ceiling more recently (Figure 1). We suspect that the BoJ’s intent is to desensitize the market somewhat to what will be an important policy meeting in July.

Between now and the July meeting, the BoJ’s 2Q Tankan survey, released July 1, will be closely watched, as will incoming inflation and consumption data. But we still think it would be a mistake to put too much weight on the high-frequency data flow in judging the BoJ’s policy path. We remain of the view that the BoJ is on something of a pre-determined course that will not be materially swayed by the economy’s short-term cyclical stance. By first removing YCC and negative rates, the BoJ has set the stage for a “substantial” reduction in bond purchases and Ueda has signaled that hikes above the zero bound are coming. If we are correct, then the BoJ’s dragging its feet in June was less of a reaction to incoming data weakness, and more an attempt to lay the groundwork for the next important shift in policy that is now in the cards.

It is in this context that the mixed bag of incoming data should be interpreted. Activity indicators through 2Q are painting a varied picture of demand, with a surprise fall in the services PMI implying activity stalled this month. We look for monthly rises in next week’s May retail sales and production reports, but the risk is that a sustained auto sector drag— owing to temporary output suspension amid an ongoing safety testing scandal—weighs on output, exports and sentiment into early 3Q. ## Underlying inflation muted in May

The May nationwide official core (ex. fresh food) CPI inflation jumped, as expected, while underlying inflation remained rather muted reflecting weak domestic demand. The May official core (ex. fresh food) inflation jumped 0.5%m/m, sa, pushing up oya inflation to 2.5% from 2.2% in April (Figure 3). The May jump was attributable to a rise in the renewable energy charge, that had been temporarily reduced for a year until April as a part of the government measures to curb energy inflation. With the removal of the temporary measure, energy inflation rose 2.7%m/m, sa, in May. We expect further rises in energy inflation in June and July, as the government will remove electricity and gas subsidies over the two months.

Figure 3: Core CPI inflation measures
![](images/c039457be432f0bca1c43c0371780e3b6d010a1d7fb3b65d21b7a7d6e4dc45ee.jpg)

However, the global standard measure of core inflation (ex. food and energy) remained rather muted, with a second consecutive monthly decline in services prices, down 0.1%m/m, sa. Reflecting receding cost pressures over the past months, services inflation has moderated in recent months, particularly for administered prices such as public transportation (1.5%oya), education (-1.1%) , and medical care (1.1%). Other services price inflation remained elevated, and core goods prices rose 0.2%m/m,sa, to 2.1%oya. While core goods inflation had trended down since the middle of last year, we now start seeing some signs of a reversal.

We continue to think the moderation in underlying inflation will be transitory. Energy inflation likely will continue to rise sharply for the next two months, which likely will feed into underlying inflation with a lag. We expect this dynamic to give the BoJ confidence in achieving its 2% inflation target.

## June PMI disappoints on services drop...

Japan’s all-industry flash PMI fell 2.5 points to 50.0 in June, reflecting a unexpectedly large 4-pt plunge in the services PMI (Figure 4). The drop left the services index at 49.8, its first dip below the expansionary 50-threshold since mid-2022. By contrast, the manufacturing index slipped marginally by 0.2 points to 50.1, largely as we had forecast, and underlying details were relatively upbeat. The output index moved above 50 for the first time in 13 months, consistent with our forecast for a recovery in production momentum this quarter. That said, we suspect the drag from auto plant shutdowns will become more apparent in early 3Q.

While demand for services slowed, commentary released alongside the PMI data also highlighted labor constraints that are limiting services providers’ ability to operate at full capacity—even as they attempt to hire additional staff. We think this dynamic will lead to sustained pressure for higher wage growth, eventually supporting consumption. But at this juncture, the June PMI is pointing to a stall in activity in late 2Q.

In the details, services activity fell 3.9 points, its largest decline since the pandemic, with a 3-pt fall in the new business index and a 4.1-pt drop in new orders from overseas. There was a 1.3-pt drop in the employment index, its sharpest fall in 9 months, likely reflecting difficulties in securing workers. The internals of the manufacturing PMI were more upbeat. While the headline slipped 0.2 points, there was a large 1.9-pt rise in the future output index, albeit new orders (-0.8-pt) and new export orders (-0.4-pt) softened.

## … adding to mixed 2Q demand signals

The data through 2Q are painting a mixed picture of demand. Capex appears to be recovering after stalling in 1Q, but a sustained auto sector drag is weighing on exports and sentiment. Following a slip in small business sentiment through May, large manufacturers reported a decline in confidence through June, and exports slipped in mid 2Q. In the details:

April core machinery orders declined by 2.9%m/m sa, broadly in line with our expectations. This decrease follows significant gains over the previous two months, resulting in a run rate of 36.9%3m/3m saar, suggesting some upside risk to our forecast for a 3%q/q saar gain in capex this quarter (Figure 5).

May export volumes fell 1.4%m/m sa, weaker than our expectation for a moderate rise, largely reflecting a drop in auto shipments. The fall last month left overall exports down 2.1%3m/3m saar, and while the pace of contraction has moderated, the trade data are coming in weaker than our forecast for a 10%q/q saar rise in GDP exports this quarter. Import momentum is running stronger, and the data are suggesting a modestly negative contribution to 2Q GDP. We will revisit our forecast with the June data (Figure 6).

While our baseline remains for external demand to recover moderately through midyear, the risk is rising that further halts in auto production and shipments weigh on export growth. May auto export volumes stalled after a rise in April, falling 0.4%m/m sa and were down 7.5%3m/3m saar. The drop in auto shipments to the EU has been particularly sharp: volumes fell 23.5%m/m sa and in level terms are at their lowest since the pandemic (Figure 7). Tech export volumes were stronger, up 2.6%m/m sa, albeit not quite reversing a 2.8% fall in April.

The June Reuters Tankan survey suggested a deterioration in sentiment among large manufacturers, with the index falling 3 points to a level of 6. The decline was primarily driven by negative sentiment in the steel/nonferrous metals sector and the auto/transport equipment sector. By contrast, sentiment among non-manufacturers improved 5 points to 31, nearly reaching its highest level in the current cycle. We continue to note a sharp rise in sentiment in the construction/real estate sector, which rose almost 20 points to its highest since December 2019, potentially on sustained rises in property prices (Figure 8).
