## Too Far, Too Fast? Hardly

On January 5, the day after the December FOMC meeting minutes published, the New York Fed released the results of its December Survey of Market Participants (SMP). The median market participant, as of early December, thought the Fed would most likely cut rates by 125bp in 2024, with the first cut delivered in June.

On the deadline of the survey submissions (December 4), the market priced in the same number of cuts the median market participant expected. Put differently, the pricing of Fed policy perfectly aligned with investor expectations – a rarity.

When we evaluated market pricing against the expectations of our economists, we saw a negative risk premium worth 25bp. In December, our economists expected the Fed to deliver 100bp of cuts in 2024, not the 125bp priced by the market.

We evaluate market pricing against the median expectations of market participants, i.e, investors. After all, they move the markets we try to forecast. Exhibit 1 and Exhibit 2 show the disconnect between the market pricing of Fed policy against the expectations of the median investors participating in the SMP.

Over various horizons, market prices usually disconnect from investor expectations. In fact, market prices rarely align with them perfectly. We label this disconnect a "risk premium" or a "term premium". We think of this disconnect as insurance investors take out for the chance that their expectations prove incorrect.

• Over the past decade, the pricing of Fed policy in the next 12 months oscillated between 75bp (3 x 25bp) more dovish and 50bp (2 x 25bp) more hawkish than the median SMP expectation (see Exhibit 1 again).

• The pricing of Fed policy in the next 24 months oscillated between 100bp more dovish and 100bp more hawkish than the median SMP expectation (see Exhibit 2 again).

After the US CPI and PPI data this week, markets now price 167bp of Fed easing in 2024. We disagree with the notion that markets moved "too far, too fast", a common refrain these days. If investors now expect more easing than they did in early December, then market prices may seem just as reasonable as they did then.

Consider the possibility that investors now expect 150bp of easing in 2024. In that case, the market price at 167bp implies a 17bp negative term premium.

• Before the pandemic, the negative term premium over a 1-year horizon averaged 25bp.

• Between the end of the 2015-2018 hiking cycle and beginning of the 2019 easing cycle, the negative term premium averaged 47bp.

Given this context, the 17bp of negative term premium seems too low, especially if the previous tightening cycle ended last July and the next easing cycle will begin by June. A negative term premium of 50bp should not surprise anyone. In the extreme, the negative term premium could rise to 75bp even.

We find market pricing even more interesting beyond a 1-year horizon. According to the December SMP, the median market participant expected 250bp of cuts through 2025. That compares to market pricing of 229bp today – suggesting a positive term premium of 21bp.

• Before the pandemic, the term premium over a 2-year horizon averaged negative 44bp.

• Between the end of the 2015-2018 hiking cycle and beginning of the 2019 easing cycle, the term premium averaged negative 44bp.

As you can see, term premiums over a 1y or 2y horizon could fall much more without market pricing looking egregious, relative to the pre-pandemic period.

Is the pre-pandemic period the right gauge of fair value? Market prices for future inflation rates argue for a more cautious approach to easing policy, even if only slightly, than the Fed employed before the pandemic.

• Markets price the 1y headline CPI inflation rate to be about 40bp higher than before the pandemic (see Exhibit 3 ), despite...

• … also pricing in a 1y real rate about 150bp higher than before the pandemic (see Exhibit 20 ).

In a sense, market prices suggest that keeping inflation low – even if higher than before the pandemic – requires much higher real rates than before the pandemic.

• The recent CPI and PPI data lowered our economists' forecasts for core PCE by 8bp: from 25bp M/M to 17bp M/M.

• That 17bp M/M annualizes to 2.1% – which would still be lower than most of the recent underlying PCE inflation averages (see Exhibit 5 ).

• Now, they forecast a move from 1.9% to 2.65%.

In summary, we don't see market pricing of Fed policy as particularly egregious or having moved too far, too fast. Instead, with downside risks abound, we could see much more negative risk premium/less positive risk premium enter the market over the next few months.

As a result, we continue to suggest investors look for opportunities to buy US duration when yields rise - something we would expect to occur if our economists' forecasts for inflation prove prescient.

However, if inflation continues to surprise to the downside, then both investor expectations and market pricing of Fed policy will take rates even lower than today – and "too far, too fast" will join "transitory" and "sticky" in the "this time is different" graveyard.

## Fed pricing now more real-istic

Markets have worried about whether Treasury supply that originated in August 2023 has had a persistent impact on Treasury yields.

As an additional step, we normalize M1UT so that the index reflects the number of months from January 2023 when the first cut is expected. The results, in Exhibit 15 , indicate that a 1- month delay in the first cut is worth 6.6bp, while each additional cut in the first 6 months of cutting is worth -15.2bp.

The residual of the model, shown in Exhibit 16 , represents 10-year yield less the portion of yield that can be inferred from short-term rate expectations (M1UT and P1UT). This residual could be interpreted as a "term premium", but it could also reflect factors such as long-term rate expectations. Currently, the residual is 8.9bp, just above one standard deviation (8.7bp) from zero, suggesting that there is no lasting impact from supply concerns on yields.

## Europe

## NOK/SEK | NOK, Stokk and barrel

Both the Riksbank and Norges Bank have been active in FX markets, though for different reasons and in different directions – the Riksbank buying SEK and Norges Bank selling NOK. Both flows are set to slow meaningfully, removing a significant NOK/SEK headwind.

We estimate that on-market transactions from the Riksbank's FX-hedging program, where it buys SEK versus USD and EUR, should end in the next week or two. The recent rally in SEK has reduced the chances that the Riksbank will extend the hedging program at its February 1 meeting.

Meanwhile, Norges Bank has slowed its daily average pace of NOK sales from NOK 1,400 million/day to NOK 350 million. This is twice as fast a decline as we were expecting and we think that the much slower pace of sales will continue.

The absence of central bank flows suggests that NOK/SEK can trade more on fundamentals – which point to a higher NOK versus SEK. Norwegian data have held up while Swedish data have disappointed the most in the G10. We estimate that Norges Bank-Riksbank policy divergence implies NOK/SEK at 1.06, compared to sub-parity today.

NOK/SEK's correlation to risk assets has fallen to roughly zero. Continued lack of correlation suggests that macro factors like global risk demand won't impact the pair much, rendering it a better reflection of the local stories suggesting NOK/SEK topside.

CHF | SNB FX reserves: Has the SNB already started selling CHF? The month of December saw the first increase in the SNB's foreign currency reserves since May. This has caused certain market participants to speculate that the SNB net sold CHF in December.

However, given the available information, it is difficult to conclude with certainty that the SNB indeed net sold CHF in December. We have built a proxy portfolio using broad SNB allocation data to better understand how the returns of these reserves may have developed in December, and found that an increase in FX reserves in December could be explained by market returns.

While the proxy portfolio doesn't guarantee that market returns are responsible for said increase (particularly given that the proxy portfolio increased less than the published numbers), it does support the thesis that it is too soon to tell.

The turn of the month will see additional data released about the SNB's balance sheet, which can be used to estimate the SNB's FX transactions and give us a clearer picture. We will get 4Q SNB FX transaction numbers at the end of March.

That said, whether or not the SNB explicitly net sold CHF in December isn't exceedingly significant. It began its pivot to CHF selling in December with changes to its FX intervention language. Hence, selling in December would simply be confirmation of something it has already announced that it is willing to do.

Ultimately, however, the SNB remains at the mercy of inflation. Until domestic inflation comes down meaningfully, it cannot weaken the currency excessively without overshooting its 2% target. Hence, we maintain our focus on how inflation progresses to identify when CHF weakness will come.

## Maybe.

In this second week of January, the SNB released data (as part of its regular reporting schedule), showing an increase in the SNB's foreign currency reserves for the first time since May 2023.

As a result, there has been speculation in markets as to whether this is evidence of the SNB's pivot to CHF selling.

When evaluating this claim, one needs to consider mark-to-market valuation adjustments of the FX reserves portfolio. According to the SNB, FX and gold are marked to market at the end of every month, while fixed income and equity investments are marked quarterly.

Given that the release was an end-of-quarter print, we attempt to build a proxy portfolio of the SNB foreign currency reserves and calculate the returns for December.

Exhibit 92: The SNB's FX reserves are largely concentrated in USD and EUR, making up 70%+ of the portfolio

![](images/2df6683238e1b0616555f0d2dfdf0c71e0e13754ea625dae8c33641bd2e52d4f.jpg)

Although the SNB does not publish the constituents of its FX reserves investments on a line-by-line basis, it does provide allocation data on a currency (see Exhibit 92 ) and asset class (see Exhibit 93 ) basis.

This allocation data can be filtered further, distinguishing fixed income investments by issuer type (see Exhibit 94 ) and issuer rating (see Exhibit 95 ), represented in the exhibits as percentages of the total reserves portfolio.

Exhibit 94: Within fixed income investments, the SNB favors government bonds over corporates...
![](images/3c839e3bdb59043717bad522baad3c88f8e8f20a11d09e1b68984d0f846cfd7a.jpg)
Source: SNB, Morgan Stanley Research

![](images/228c49ace090df48d699930044e94699c11832bfd50f118043dda685b583217c.jpg)

Despite the allocation data, there are still a large combination of possible investments, with large variations in returns, that can make up the portfolio.

Thus, we look for further data, identifying the weighted duration of the portfolio at 4.8, and additional qualitative observations in the SNB's 2022 annual report that give us guidance. Notably:

• The SNB does not hedge currency risk;

• Investments within given markets are diversified broadly across maturities;

• Equities are managed passively without over- or under-weighting specific stocks sectors;

• Some of the equity investments are in emerging market economies.

With all these factors in mind, we build a basic proxy portfolio (allocation specified in the aforementioned exhibits) and find a marginal positive return in December.

As many market participants alluded to, fixed income and equities rallied significantly, from which the proxy portfolio made a gain of 2.60%.

However, what certain speculators may have failed to consider is the equally significant CHF rally. Given the currency allocation employed, the proxy portfolio incurs a loss of 2.51%.

On net, we therefore find that the SNB's FX reserves, proxied by our portfolio, will have gained roughly 0.09% in the month of December.

That said, the reported FX reserves number increased by 1.02%. Therefore, in a world where our proxy portfolio is a perfectly accurate representation of the SNB's FX reserve investments, the SNB still conducted CHF sales in December.

However, we have shown that is possible to generate positive returns in December while reflecting the SNB's published allocation data quite closely.

In addition, we find in Exhibit 96 that the proxy portfolio tends to under-estimate the FX reserves of the SNB, further suggesting that the December increase may be the result of market returns.

Exhibit 96: Our simple proxy portfolio underestimates the SNB's FX reserves portfolio

Source: SNB, Morgan Stanley Research
Exhibit 97: A crude estimator of FX interventions can be constructed using FX reserves and "provisions and equity capital" from the SNB's balance sheet

![](images/2fa67fe430b335bf0fb0aa50c53064cf265144ae7c384e244d634e5e3b1e9ba5.jpg)
Source: SNB, Morgan Stanley Research

Hence, it is not unreasonable to assume that the actual FX reserves portfolio managed by the SNB, which is likely more optimally allocated, could have produced the 1.02% gain reported in FX reserves.

Either way, the data currently available are inconclusive.

## When will we know for sure?

We will receive more data at the end of the month regarding the SNB's balance sheet.

As part of this release, the SNB reports "provisions and equity capital" under liabilities of its balance sheet. A large part of this are provisions to act as a buffer against losses of the FX reserves portfolio.

While other aspects contribute to "provisions and equity capital", the size (and allocation) of the FX reserves investments means that fluctuations in this value represent changes in the PnL of the portfolio with some accuracy.

At the very least, the direction of intervention can be deduced quite reliably using these data (as found in Exhibit 97 ). Hence, we will be able to build a better picture of what happened in December once we have these numbers.

There are other balance sheet items that can be used in an attempt to identify intervention. However, they bring their own issues.

Arguably we will never know for sure what happened in December, as official FX transactions data are aggregated on a quarterly basis.

## Does it matter? Not really.

The SNB already adapted tits communication in December, removing "the focus is on selling" from its press release when discussing FX intervention.

Doing so gives it the flexibility to adapt its FX market operations as needed. This is a valuable option given the relatively long time between meetings, and a fast-evolving macro environment.

As such, CHF sales in December would simply be confirmation of something the SNB has already implied that it is willing to do.

## If it did sell in December, have the floodgates opened? No.

Despite gaining optionality, the SNB is still bound by its inflation target. While domestic inflation remains stubborn, the SNB cannot excessively sell CHF without driving up imported inflation and overshooting its target.

In conclusion, the SNB may or may not have sold CHF in December. However, whether or not it sold in December, progress on inflation is in the driving seat and the SNB doesn't seem ready for excess CHF selling.

## BTFP expiration confirmed

This week, comments from Fed officials confirmed that the BTFP is set to expire this upcoming March. Given that usage has grown by almost \$40bn in the past several weeks ( Exhibit 139 ), we received questions from investors on its potential impact for funding conditions. In our view, the BTFP expiration will have a limited impact on funding in the near term. In addition, banks are not currently acting as lenders in the repo market, with the RRP at \~\$600bn. This reduces any near-term shocks to SOFR.

First, the fact that a little over 30% of all Fed loans mature within 90-days has been referenced as a potential risk ( Exhibit 140 ). Given that the BTFP currently makes 96% of all Fed loans, this serves as a good proxy for the maturity profile of all BTFP loans outstanding. Considering that banks have until March to refinance existing BTFP loans with no penalty, we expect banks that have a truly liquidity need to extend out their current loans. This should remove any risks of an immediate shock post expiration.

Second, the impact to reserves will be gradual as banks are able to pay down existing BTFP loans until 1Q25. As a Fed asset, a decrease in the BTFP translates into a 1:1 decrease in reserves, all else equal. As we discussed in our last weekly, we see banks being able to rely on other sources of funding like FHLB advances, Fed discount window, and CP/CD/ time deposits to meet future liquidity needs.

Third, the recent increase in Fed loans does not seem concentrated. Using the H.4.1 releases since 12/6 until 1/10, we estimate the change in total loans by the Federal Reserve Bank (FRB) shown in Exhibit 141 . We observe that the biggest increase amounts to \~\$6bn at the FRB of San Francisco, reducing concerns that individual banks might be facing liquidity stress.

As shown in Exhibit 142 , total borrowings for all US banks have remained largely unchanged over the past months (down \$21bn from 11/1 to 1/3). At the same time, the BTFP has increased by \$32bn during this same time period. This continues to suggests that the BTFP is serving as an attractive source of funding to replace other forms of nondeposit liabilities such as FHLB advances (see here for more), making arbitrage (BTFP rate < IORB) and bank funding stress unlikely drivers for the recent BTFP surge.

Over the coming weeks, we would expect to see BTFP usage gradually increasing further as we near expiration. Without a surge in FHLB issuance, tightness in funding markets, and a shift in total borrowings in the H.8 release, this trend will continue to reflect bank funding preferences towards an attractive source of funding vs. stress or near-term risks to funding.

Enhancements to a G4 10y government bond futures momentum strategy have produced higher Sharpe ratios and stronger returns, relative to total return government bond indices for the G4, US, Germany, Japan, and the UK since 2000. ## Trading Strategy 1 – "Trade Longs/Fade Shorts"

When the 5-day moving average crosses above the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract (long duration) and hold for a 25-business-day period. When the 5-day moving average crosses below the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract and hold for a 25-business-day period. In short, this strategy buys futures when the Simple Moving Average Crossover (SMAX) generates both a long and a short signal, given the historical outperformance of long signals traded long and underperformance of short signals traded short. Given that the SMAX could generate both a long and a short signal within the predefined holding period, an investor may have a 200% long position since each of the two signals would be traded in separate portfolio sleeves.

## Definition of terms

Buy/Long: The analyst expects the total or excess return (depending on the nature of the recommendation) of the instrument or issuer that is the subject of the investment recommendation to be positive over the relevant time period.

Sell/Short: The analyst expects the total or excess return (depending on the nature of the recommendation) of the instrument or issuer that is the subject of the investment recommendation to be negative over the relevant time period.

Selling protection or Buying Risk: The analyst expects that the price of protection against the event occurring will decrease over the relevant time period.

Buying protection or Selling Risk: The analyst expects the price of protection against the event occurring will increase over the relevant time period.

Pay: The analyst expects that over the specified time period the variable rate underlying the swap agreement that is the subject of the investment recommendation will increase.

Receive: The analyst expects that over the specified time period the variable rate underlying the swap agreement that is the subject of the investment recommendation will decrease.

Unless otherwise specified, the time frame for recommendations included in the Morgan Stanley Fixed Income Research reports is 1 - 3 months and the price of financial instruments mentioned in the recommendation is as at the date and time of publication of the recommendation.

When more than one issuer or instrument is included in a recommendation, analyst expects one part of the trade to outperform the other trade or combination of other trades included in the recommendation on a relative basis.
