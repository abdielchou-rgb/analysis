## United States

• All signs still point to a first Fed ease at the September FOMC meeting

Retail sales picked up in June, easing earlier concerns about consumer spending

• A slow rise in the number on unemployment benefits points to a cooling job market

• Next week’s 2Q GDP report expected to show trendlike 2.0% growth

The soft landing story has been gaining increasing credibility in recent weeks. If anything, some of the growth data had been suggesting activity was slowing more than would be desirable. This had spurred speculation that the Fed could cut rates as soon as the late July meeting. Two things happened this week to check that chatter. First, Chair Powell spoke on Monday and gave no hint that the FOMC is in any rush to cut rates. While obviously pleased by the recent inflation data, the Fed chief indicated that the Committee was taking a considered approach to the first cut, which is more consistent with a September ease. Second, the economic data this week generally came in better than expected, suggesting activity retained good momentum heading into 2Q.

Among that activity data the most notable upside surprise was the June retail sales report. Before this report, 2Q consumer spending looked to be growing at an annual rate similar to the 1.5% outturn registered in 1Q. Instead, June retail sales increased a peppy 0.8% (taking out the volatile auto and gas components) and estimates for the prior two months were revised higher. With these data in hand, real consumption growth last quarter now looks to have expanded at around a 2.0% pace, which is pretty close to a Goldilocks outcome.

The latest housing and manufacturing data also offered some better news. The only cautionary signal in this week’s data came from jobless claims. Not only did initial claims for unemployment insurance jump in the latest week, but ongoing claims for jobless benefits continued climbing to the highest since late ’21. As we discuss in this week’s research note, a slowing in gross hiring can push up claims even when layoff activity is limited (as is likely the case now).

Well, we’ve managed to make it all the way to the fourth paragraph of this essay before talking about the presidential election. But that topic continues to dominate discussions about the medium-term economic outlook. In a wide-ranging interview about economic policy published this week, Republican nominee Trump was asked if he would let Fed chair Powell serve the remainder of his term, to which he answered “I would let him serve it out, especially if I thought he was

doing the right thing.” When asked about Fed rate cuts before the election, Trump stated “It’s something they know they shouldn’t be doing.” In a webinar this week with Sarah Binder, a noted scholar of Fed politics, we discussed various ways the White House can exert influence on the central bank.

The Fed will be quiet next week ahead of the FOMC meeting the following week. But the data flow will not be quiet. Much of the remaining June data will be released, including consumer spending, durable goods orders, and new and existing home sales. Thursday brings the first look at 2Q real GDP; we expect a modest acceleration from the 1.5% growth pace in 1Q to 2.0% last quarter. Friday’s June core PCE inflation report is expected to show a 0.2%m/m increase in prices last month, leaving the year-ago increase unchanged at 2.6%.

## Consumers get their mojo back

Retail sales were stronger than expected in June, and we see 2Q real consumer spending tracking 2.0%q/q saar, a bit stronger than our prior assumption before the report. Total nominal retail sales and food services spending was unchanged in the month, dragged down by declines for sales of motor vehicles and at gas stations, but excluding those categories spending rose a solid 0.8%m/m. The control category (ex auto, gasoline, building materials, and food services) also increased 0.9%m/m compared to expectations for a 0.2-0.3% gain, and there were upward revisions to May and April (Figure 1).

Based on this report we look for real consumer spending to have risen 0.2%m/m last month. The 2.0%q/q pace for the whole quarter is slower than the 3%-plus pace in 2H23, but an acceleration from the 1.5% growth in 1Q. The latest number should thus ease concerns about an abrupt loss of momentum in consumer spending .

Looking across some of the other categories of spending, food services rose 0.3%m/m and the prior month’s 0.4% drop was revised to a 0.4% increase. Spending at restaurants had been quite soft from December through March but has risen in each of the last three months. Sales at building material

stores, which have been hampered by the slowdown in the housing market, increased a decent 1.4%m/m. Sales were also up for most categories of spending within the control group.

## Homebuilding still trending down

Housing starts and permits rebounded in June, led by the vol atile multifamily segment, while single-family activity continued to moderate. Single-family construction is likely pull ing back temporarily in response to the recent rise in inventories, while multifamily construction was coming off a low base. Builders had already sharply cut starts in prior months as they contend with an elevated pace of new deliveries (Figure 2).

Both starts and permits are down substantially in the second quarter overall (total starts fell 16%q/q saar in 2Q on top of a 19% decline in the prior quarter, while permits dropped 22% in 2Q after a 1% drop in 1Q), pointing to a near-term drag on residential investment. Mortgage rates have drifted slightly lower in recent weeks, which will be supportive of housing, although the decline is still relatively small. Weekly mortgage purchase applications are largely moving sideways, while the NAHB housing market index for July continued to point to softness in the single-family space.

## A good run for manufacturing

The June industrial production report was solid, with total IP rising 0.6%m/m and the manufacturing group rising 0.4%m/ m (0.3%m/m ex motor vehicles) to the highest level since late 2022, inclusive of some upward revisions to the prior two months. For the quarter, manufacturing rose 3.6%q/q saar, the best gain in just over three years. Motor vehicle and parts industry output was up 13%q/q saar, but even excluding motor vehicles the 2.6% gain was the largest increase since the start of 2022 (Figure 3). As we discuss in this week’s Focus, the rebound in motor vehicle output is helping keep on lid on core goods inflation.

## Claims not sending the “all clear”

Initial jobless claims in the week ending July 13 spiked up to 243k from 223k the prior week, matching the 2024 high from the week of June 8. The four-week average of claims edged up to 235k from 234k, which is still down a bit from the recent high of 239k at the end of June, but is up materially from the roughly 210-215k range that was seen for much of the year through April. One item that may temper the negative interpretation of last week’s jump in initial claims is the effect of Hurricane Beryl, which made landfall in Texas on July 8 as a Category 1 hurricane, after which it was downgraded to lesser storm designations as it moved up across the Midwest and Northeast.

Perhaps more notable, continuing claims in the week ending July 6 rose to 1.867mm from 1.847mm the prior week, and are more convincingly breaking out of their range during the past year: prior to the rise over the last month they had not been higher than 1.829mm. The increase should be at least partly related to the larger inflow of new claimants, though changes in initial and continuing claims don’t always align. The further leg up in continuing claims bears watching as it may point to greater cooling in the labor market.

In addition to GDP and core PCE, next week we will get the flash PMIs for July, along with June data on home sales, durable goods, inventories, and trade. The flash PMIs will provide a timely reading on business activity in the manufacturing and services sectors. Existing home sales should remain depressed given the signal from pending home sales, while new home sales could recover some of their big drop in May.

We look for real GDP to have increased 2.0%q/q, saar in 2Q. While a step down from the very rapid 4.2% pace in 2H23, this is an improvement on the 1.4% gain in 1Q, driven by stronger growth in consumption, equipment investment, inventory building, and government spending. With more inventory accumulation the acceleration in domestic final sales is not as apparent as for overall GDP: domestic final sales should increase 2.5% in 2Q vs. 2.4% in 1Q.

Based on the monthly PCE data and other available information for June we think that real consumer spending growth picked up, increasing 2.0% in the quarter after growing 1.5% the prior quarter, with solid momentum going into the end of the quarter. Consumption was supported by gains in real disposable income, which we estimate rose 1.8%.

On the investment front, there should be a split between declines in structures spending and more rapid growth in equipment/IPP. We expect that both nonresidential and residential structures investment declined in the quarter after growing in 1Q, and for residential the drag is likely to get worse in the coming quarters given the recent trajectory of housing starts and declining number of units under construction. For equipment spending, we expect reasonably strong growth stemming from vehicles, aircraft, and computers, but with roughly no contribution from other capital goods.

After subtracting from growth in 1Q, more rapid inventory building should make a positive contribution to GDP in 2Q, though this will be partially offset by a widening trade deficit. Finally, we look for stronger growth in government spending relative to 1Q, though the pace should be slower than in 2H23.
