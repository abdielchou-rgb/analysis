## It’s not easy being (priced for) perfect

While the EMBIGD has backed up a bit in part on record January issuance, the overall narrative of tight valuations and little margin for error coming from the top-down story leave us MW. While January<sup>’</sup>s record gross issuance and some volatility in UST levels since the start of the year led to negative returns in January, the widening observed in spreads is relatively modest. Moreover, while the top-down narrative remains broadly favorable, enough for the market to largely shrug off the pricing out of a Fed cut already in March, overall a US soft landing remains priced almost to perfection, leaving room for volatility if new data presents a challenge. January payrolls data is case in point. While short-term recession fears may be increasingly banished, the market could be forced to quickly pivot back to considering “boiled frog<sup>”</sup> on the dinner menu of scenarios. Any return to the rates/risk dynamic of September-October would be unwelcome and throw cold water on the January narrative of a wide-open primary market supporting lower-rated sovereigns facing a gradually rising maturity wall. Certainly January payrolls raises the stakes around upcoming inflation readings, where our economists have maintained concerns over the reemerging stickiness (Not so fast: Global core CPI to rise 3% in 1H24 Jan 4; B. Kasman).

The EMBIGD had a poor start to the year if we consider the month of January in a vacuum. But context presents a more benign story. The index widened 18bp on the month to 402, delivering a -1.02% return. But the performance largely reflected some payback from the low liquidity Fed pivot rally of December, when spreads tightened 21bp and the EMBIGD returned 4.7% (following up a -31bp/+5.6% November). The cumulative Nov-Dec spread return was 3.9%, followed by a -1% January; the UST return was +7.4% and nearly flat, respectively. (Note the mtd Treasury return was -1.7% as of January 24, before the rally at the end of January. ) From a spread perspective, January saw the EMBIGD retrace the prior two months gains by about one-third, with IG the driving the retracement (Figure 2), The overall picture of historically tight spreads in the performing space remains intact: the EMBIGD ex-CCC at 235 has bounced off the December lows and has now broken through the three-month moving average; it remains well tight of the 12-month moving average and around the lows of the last half decade (Figure 3).

## Refreshing our recommendations

We are MW in EM credit, where already tight spreads have led to a range-bound market, with recommendations in EM sovereigns skewed to single-B and CCCrated countries, where recent idiosyncratic developments have focused on unlocking much needed financing. We remain OW in Angola, Côte d'Ivoire, Ecuador, El Salvador, Honduras, and Guatemala and UW in Peru and Uzbekistan. We have refreshed other recommendations in this report, taking off some long-standing trades and adding an UW in Hungary. On the EMBIGD Model Portfolio side, we recommend moving UW Hungary given risks around fiscal slippage and recently simmering EUrelated tensions, while we close our UW recommendations (move MW ) in Azerbaijan and Iraq given expectations are for oil prices to remain steady at supportive levels this year (see Global Commodities, 22 January 2024). We also take profits in our OW Mozambique as current valuations seem fair in our view. On the outright and RV trades side, we recommend 10s30s steepener in Dominican Republic to add on to our existing steepeners in Oman, Panama, and Uruguay while we close our 5s30s flattener recommendation in Egypt and recommend an outright long in Egypt 7.625% 32s. Furthermore, we close our long Zambia 27s recommendation due to lack of clarity on the restructuring talks after the November deal with the bondholder creditor committee got derailed after objections from the official creditors with resolution no where in sight. ## Supply Technicals: Another record January again raising hopes for a higher annual supply

January continues to be the favorite month for primary markets with the aggregate issuance so far already surpassing 2023<sup>’</sup>s record start. Similar to 2023, the turn of the year saw EM sovereigns rushing to primary markets to meet their financing needs with record \$46.8bn of new issuance. Historically January has been among the busiest months for primary markets; however, the aggregate issuance sizes in 2023 (\$44.4bn) and 2024 (\$46.8bn) have been well above the 2016-2021 average of \$23.4bn. Although 2023 saw a record start, the pace of issuance declined over the course of the year, ending at \$133.8bn, much lower than the average issuance seen between 2016 and 2021 (Figure 6), given borrowing costs remained high with the US Fed continuing with rate hikes. However, similar to 2023, improving risk conditions and material decline in rates over the fourth quarter of the previous year led to the front loaded borrowing by EM sovereigns (Figure 7). While we expect the momentum to slow down a bit in February in line with the historical levels, the pace of issuance over the course of the year could likely return back to the 2016-2021 average if the financing conditions ease as per market expectations, i.e., there could be upside risks to our 2024 forecasts. This would be in contrast to 2023, where we witnessed a slowdown given we were still in the midst of further tightening.

EM IG’s dominance continues, but lower borrowing costs could likely see the return of lower rated sovereigns. EM sovereign issuance again was largely driven by EM IG, which comprised \$39.7bn of \$46.8bn of issuance so far. As such, higher-rated (IG) EM sovereigns have not experienced a material difference in primary market volumes over the past two years and have issued on pace with the historical pace (see EM Fixed Income Focus, 25 January 24). That said, the return of first SSA sovereign to primary markets in two years (Cote d<sup>’</sup>Ivoire: \$2.6bn) provides a ray of hope for the lower rated sovereigns still eyeing a return to primary markets. Furthermore, the overall borrowing costs have declined as well, with 2024 issuance for most countries coming at a relatively lower cost than 2023 (Figure 8). The bulk of our issuance forecast falls in the IG space, with BB credits seen issuing relatively lower sums and only three single-Bs forecast to come to market, but a less severe global growth backdrop and relatively easier financial conditions could likely bring back the lower rated sovereigns back into the issuance mix (see EM Sovereign Credit Strategy, 19 January 24).

## Hungary: Move UW (from MW) as fiscal slippage risks and the EU-related tensions could keep bonds under pressure

The ongoing tensions between Hungary and the EU will continue to unnerve markets. Hungary<sup>’</sup>s government has been embroiled in a long-standing dispute with the EU, which has partly frozen its funds as part of the 2021-2027 financial framework intended for the country over concerns about human rights and the rule of law. Although the European Commission did agree to unlock close to €10bn in cohesion funds around mid December on the back of the progress on the judicial reform side, nearly €11-12bn of funding from the EU remains suspended. The size of future funding as well as the timing remains uncertain, which increases the risk of higher Eurobond supply from Hungary. A €50bn aid package planned for Ukraine was the current bone of contention between the two, with Hungary the only EU country to have not backed the package in the December summit. That said, the deal was finally reached during the recent January summit (link).

High risks of slippage around fiscal targets as had been the case in 2023. The government had revised its 2023 planned deficit from 3.9% to 5.2% (JPM: 5.8%) around October last year without announcing any new measures to improve the fiscal performance. For 2024, the deficit target has been set to 2.9% which seems difficult given the government<sup>’</sup>s vocal desire for better growth. Our economists forecast 4.6% GDP fiscal deficit this year with risks skewed to the upside. Given this backdrop and issues around EU funding, the risk of Hungary tapping Eurobond markets for more than our current expectation of \$6bn remains high with them already issuing close to \$4bn so far.

## Egypt: Switch from 27s51s flattener to outright long 32s

We close our 5s30s flattener recommendation on the back of positive developments around the possible restart of the IMF program. Back in September we recommended long Egypt 51s vs Egypt 27s at the same notional levels, expecting further curve inversion amid rising repayment concerns. The delay in completion of the first and second review of the \$3bn IMF program had raised concerns on Egypt<sup>’</sup>s ability to secure significant financing for its medium-term large external financing needs. The concerns got exacerbated as the year progressed, with the curve inverting further as per our expectations until end-October 2023 (Figure 15). Furthermore, the country was downgraded by all three rating agencies (S&P and Fitch to B-, Moody<sup>’</sup>s to Caa1) between October and November given mounting macroeconomic concerns. However, sovereign bond spreads started tightening materially later in the year as talks around a possible restart of the stalled IMF program started to make the rounds along with improvement in the global risk tone. Furthermore, as highlighted as one of the risks to our recommendations, positive developments around the possible restart of the IMF

program led to the re-steepening of the curve. With the current IMF mission team<sup>’</sup>s visit suggesting increased chances that the country might be closer to restarting the program, we close out of our current flattener recommendation, i.e., sell Egypt 8.75% 51s (63.8 bid, z +1034bp) and buy Egypt 5.8% 27s (76.5 offer, z +1052bp). The price loss due to the steepening of the curve was partially offset by the carry gain due the same notional trade recommendation (Figure 16The curvstpnig ld o fmy ba  ).

The risks to our view include the IMF program getting stalled again, low or no reforms undertaken by the authorities, and a shift in global risk tone.

Nonetheless, given the already seen limited underperformance and remaining prudent, we move MW Iraq in the EMBIGD Model Portfolio at 492bp as on 1 February 2023, having entered at 431bp.

Valuation & Methodology: For J.P. Morgan's Emerging Markets Sovereign Research, we assign a rating to each sovereign issuer (Overweight, Marketweight or Underweight) based on our view of whether the combination of the issuer<sup>’</sup>s fundamentals, market technicals, and the relative value of its securities will cause it to outperform, perform in line with, or underperform the credit returns of the EMBIGD index over the next three months. Our view of an issuer<sup>’</sup>s fundamentals includes our opinion of whether the issuer is becoming more or less able to service its debt obligations when they become due and payable, as well as whether its willingness to service debt obligations is increasing or decreasing.
