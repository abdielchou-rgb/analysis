# European Economic Perspectives Eurozone: How is bank lending holding up?

## Financial conditions remain tight, with signs of modest/uneven easing

Monetary and credit dynamics have been followed closely over the past two years to scrutinise the impact of ECB monetary policy tightening. In this report, we analyse the newly released Eurozone monetary and credit data for May. The overall message remains that Eurozone financial conditions are tight, but improving very slowly and unevenly. While overall private sector credit growth was down 0.1pp to 0.8% y/y in May, growth in loans to non-financial corporates and in loans to households were both up 0.1pp to 0.3% - hence upholding the impression that "peak tightness" in monetary conditions lies behind. Growth in monetary aggregates has continued its slow recovery as well (from low levels), but the decline in corporate borrowing rates proceeds only slowly.

## Private sector loan growth: momentum remains weak

Growth in total credit from Monetary and Financial Institutions (MFI) increased by 0.1pp to 0.0% y/y in May. Public sector credit (previously dominated by QE) was up 0.5pp to -1.4% y/y. Private sector lending (adjusted for loan sales and securitisation) was down 0.1pp to 0.8% y/y, due to weaker dynamics in credit to insurance corporations and pension funds (-2.4pp to -4.7% y/y) and non-monetary financial firms (-1.8pp to 8.1% y/y). However, the growth in loans to non-financial corporates (NFC) and to households, which are more important from a business cycle perspective, were both up by 0.1pp to 0.3% y/y (with mortgages -0.1pp to +0.3% y/y and consumer loans +0.4pp to +2.8% y/ y). Sequential momentum in credit growth remains weak, however: The 3-month moving average of m/m private sector credit growth was unchanged at 0.1% m/m, with NFC loans and HH loans both unchanged at zero (Fig. 17-20).

## Bank lending rates broadly stable, official NPLs low and stable

EZ bank lending rates for new corporate loans most recently published by the ECB (April) were unchanged m/m at 5.18%, down 9bp from the peak in October 2023, but up 382bp from the low in December 2021. Corporate lending rates remain highest in the Baltic nations (6.27-6.70%), Ireland (6.39%), Slovakia (6.19%) and Greece (6.04%); they were lowest in the Netherlands (4.36%) and Luxembourg (4.03%). The spread between small loans (<€1m, typically requested by SMEs) and larger loans (>€1m) narrowed to 0.23%, well below the 10-year average of 0.75%. Eurozone (volumeweighted) mortgage rates were unchanged at 3.80%, but down 22bp from the peak in November 2023. ECB data on NPLs is released with a substantial delay; in Q4-23, Eurozone NPL stood at 1.70%, 8bps up from the low in Q1-23. National data for Italy and Spain is more up to date; in Italy, NPLs stood at 1.93% in March, in Spain, NPLs stood at 3.60% in April - both broadly stable during Q1-24 (Fig. 87-89).

Loan-to-deposit ratio slightly down to to 94.4%, but well below the 2020 high Private sector deposit growth was up 0.4pp to 1.6% y/y in May. The EZ loan-to-deposit ratio fell by 0.3pp to 94.4%, and down from 104.5% in early 2020 (Fig. 77-82).

## Growth of monetary aggregates has bottomed out and is slowly improving

Further signs that monetary growth has bottomed out: In May, M3 growth picked up by 0.3pp to +1.6% y/y, while growth in M1 (which includes the most liquid components of M3, including currency in circulation & overnight deposits) rose 1pp to -4.9% y/y. (Fig. 94-98). For the ECB's latest thinking on monetary analysis, see Philip Lane: "Modern monetary analysis" (June 2024) and Isabel Schnabel: "Money and inflation" (Sept. 2023). The two ECB Executive Board members appear to differ on the question what role strong growth in broad money played in the inflation surge of 2021-23. -- See annex for data, charts and concepts.

## Methodological Annex

## 1. Consolidated balance sheet of monetary financial institutions (MFIs)

The basis of monetary and credit analysis in the Eurozone are the balance sheets of monetary and financial institutions (MFIs), which include the balance sheets of credit institutions, the Eurosystem (ECB and national central banks), money market funds and other MFIs located in the Eurozone. In its consolidated form, this excludes the interrelations between these entities. The consolidated MFI balance sheet shows the assets and liabilities of the MFI sector vis-à-vis (a) Eurozone non-MFI resident bodies (i.e. general government and other Eurozone residents) and (b) non-Eurozone resident bodies (including banks and non-banks). The ECB releases data on the MFI balance sheet on a monthly basis, usually towards the end of the following month.

## 2. Monetary aggregates

Monetary aggregates comprise the monetary liabilities of Eurozone MFIs (including the Eurosystem) vis-à-vis non-MFI euro-area residents (households, corporates) excluding central governments.

•M1 is the sum of currency in circulation and overnight deposits;

•M2 is the sum of M1, deposits with an agreed maturity of up to two years and deposits redeemable at notice of up to three months;

•M3 is the sum of M2, repurchase agreements, money market fund shares/units and debt securities with a maturity of up to two years.

## 3. Counterparts of M3

Based on the consolidated MFI balance sheet, the monetary aggregate M3 can be presented as:

M3 = credit to euro-area residents (including governments) + net external assets held by MFIs – longer-term financial liabilities (i.e. deposits/securities with original maturities over 2 years) + other counterparts

## 4. How have QE and TLTROs affected monetary aggregates?

On 25 September 2023, ECB Executive Board member Isabel Schnabel gave a speech on "Money and inflation" in which she touched upon the link between QE, TLTROs and broad money growth in the Eurozone. In our view, her remarks were highly instructive, so we paste the relevant section of Ms Schnabel's speech below:

## Asset purchases and broad money growth

Asset purchases, together with targeted longer-term refinancing operations (TLTROs), were our primary instruments for responding to the outbreak of the pandemic in 2020, as our key policy rate was already in negative territory, and hence close to the effective lower bound.

Asset purchases were highly effective in addressing illiquidity in financial markets in an environment in which traditional intermediaries were either unwilling, or unable, to provide liquidity to the market. And they were instrumental in reversing the sharp tightening in borrowing costs that resulted from the uncertain effects of the pandemic on the balance sheets of firms, households and governments.

Asset purchases affect monetary aggregates in different ways. When central banks purchase securities, they create new central bank reserves to pay for these transactions. Reserves can only be held by commercial banks, which use them to settle payments among themselves.

These reserves are part of the monetary base, or M0. Because of QE, the monetary base increased in a mechanical way one-for-one after 2015, when the ECB launched its asset purchase programme (APP), and again during the pandemic, when we created the pandemic emergency purchase programme (PEPP) and conducted further purchases under the APP.

The TLTROs further added to the large increase in base money. In these lending operations, banks could pledge collateral against central bank reserves and obtained funding at highly beneficial rates if they fulfilled certain lending criteria.

By contrast, the impact of these measures on broad money growth, M3, is much less mechanical. TLTROs, for example, have no direct impact on M3. They only contribute to broad money growth to the extent that banks responded to the incentives provided for in the design of these operations.

For asset purchases, the effects on M3 are more convoluted. Specifically, the purchase of a bond by the central bank will result in a one-for-one increase in M3 if the ultimate seller of a security is a euro area household, non-financial firm or nonbank financial firm. In these cases, the proceeds from the sale are credited to the

seller’s deposit account, raising broad money. Other cases, especially transactions with non-residents and banks, which are often the main counterparties, will leave M3 unchanged at the time of settlement. Over time, however, QE, much like the TLTROs, can have important indirect effects on broad money growth. Above all, QE compresses the yields on long-term debt securities, stimulating loan demand and supply as well as economic activity. Lower interest rates also make money holdings relatively more attractive by reducing the opportunity cost of holding money. These effects tend to raise M3. On the other hand, if euro area residents use the proceeds from the sale of a bond to repay loans, to acquire foreign assets or to purchase financial instruments not included in M3, such as other long-term bonds, from non-money-holding institutions, the initial positive effect on broad money is reversed. In net terms, the impact of QE on M3 fundamentally depends on the strength of these indirect effects, which may vary over time. This can be seen when considering the money multiplier, which is the ratio of broad money to base money. The multiplier was broadly constant until the outbreak of the global financial crisis in 2008. This was mainly because the injection of central bank reserves was demand-driven – that is, banks’ recourse to our operations depended on currency in circulation and banks’ reserve requirements, which are a function of banks’ short-term liabilities. For that reason, M0 and M3 were expanding at a broadly similar pace. But after the start of the APP, the multiplier fell measurably and persistently. It declined further when we launched the PEPP. This reflects the fact that, with QE, the quantity of reserves is, by and large, determined by the Eurosystem, resulting in a large amount of reserves in excess of banks’ liquidity needs. The banking system as a whole cannot escape the addition of new reserves, as purchases are always settled through banks, regardless of who the ultimate seller is. Importantly, banks do not draw on excess reserves to create new loans, as is sometimes suggested, with base money “multiplying” into broad money. So, if there is no demand for credit, or if banks do not want to lend, because of risk considerations or capital requirements, asset purchases will not, on their own, affect broad money growth beyond their more limited direct mechanical impact. Footnote 39: The end of reinvestment under the APP portfolio mechanically reduces the amount of money in the economy as the Eurosystem receives the repayments of the principal maturing amounts. Repayments of TLTROs have likely contributed to the slowdown in M3 growth to the extent that the repayments have induced banks to issue long-term bonds that are not included in M3. If these bonds are purchased by euro area residents, such transactions weigh on money growth. Excerpt from: Isabel Schnabel: Money and inflation, 25 September 2023

## 5. Types of bank credit

Total credit (from Monetary and Financial Institutions, MFI) can be divided into credit to the public sector (often via purchases of government bonds) and the private sector. Private sector credit can be divided into credit to the corporate sector (including purchases of corporate bonds) and the household sector. In some statistical series, there is a separate (private sector) category for non-profit institutions serving households (NPISHs), such as charities and religious organisations, universities, professional societies, trade unions, sports clubs, or political parties. Credit to households can be divided into mortgage loans, consumer loans, and “other loans”, i.e. loans for special purposes, such as education or the purchase of securities.
