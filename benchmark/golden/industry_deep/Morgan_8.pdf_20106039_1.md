All right stop, collaborate and listen, winter is back with a brand new intention. December warmth helped data improve nicely. But now snow and polar vortex, daily and nightly. Will it ever stop? We hope so. Prepare for downside risk? We think so. To the extreme, we buy the dip like a vandal.

## Groundhog Day for Data? Not So Fast, Punxsutawney Phil

To some, 2024 has started in the same vein as 2023. Better data to start the year sends bond yields and equity prices higher. Hawkish Fed speak (Waller) pushes back on marketimplied rate cuts. And it all happens in the context of Groundhog Day occurring at the beginning of every year (on February 2).

However, a more precise accounting of the start of 2024 shows important differences from the start of 2023. In particular, the weather in the US. The data we received for December 2023 in January 2024 generally beat expectations.

That data, in combination with slightly more hawkish Fedspeak and the upcoming US Treasury refunding, sent yields higher from local lows. This is "the dip" we have been looking to buy. Does weather play a factor in our decision to buy bonds on this dip? Yes.

We think that an unusually warm December 2023 probably helped the US economy. It certainly stands to reason that when the weather is nice, activity picks up relative to when weather is not so nice. In Exhibit 1 , we show "heating degree days" in the US in December. The low reading in 2023 suggests the weather was warmer than average, and much warmer than December 2022.

Through the 15th of January 2024, the cash flow deficit was 28% lower than through the same period in 2023 (see Exhibit 8 ).

With less fiscal support and much colder weather, we see downside risks to US activity data delivered in February. In the end, fortune favors the brave. And in the spirit of Vanilla Ice, we buy this dip "like a vandal", light up a fire, and sip hot chocolate by a candle.

Important US data, including 4Q GDP, and January NFP and core PCE, present important risks. GDP tracking estimates differ meaningfully from consensus forecasts for 4Q GDP (see Exhibit 9 ), which is a recipe for post-print volatility.

# Interest Rate Strategy

## United States

Hard landing hardly priced: In the very front-end, markets lowered the probability of a March cut and also decreased the total amount of cuts priced into the next twelve months. So much so that according to our binomial pricing model, markets are now pricing almost no chance of 8 cuts or more – something many investors associate with a hard-landing type scenario. Risk of a hard landing is hardly priced at all.

Markets missing inflation progress? We believe markets have not taken notice of very encouraging data on inflation, especially the decline in New Tenant Repeat Rent (NTRR) Index, which is at a lower level than the trough we saw in the GFC. The NTRR can be a key data point to refute the inflation holdouts who think 1) inflation will be "sticky", 2) the "last mile" of inflation will be hard, or 3) those who directly want to see more progress on rent inflation like Chicago Fed President Goolsbee.

Markets not paying attention to fixings: We think the fact that the fed funds rate is falling slower than the fixing-market-implied core CPI path could be a sign that investors' inflation forecasts are higher than CPI fixings, which in turn imply core CPI possibly going below 2% in 2024. If investors were indexing the fed funds path to CPI fixings, the fixings-implied real rate would likely not have been going up, as most investors tend to agree that real fed funds rate should be going down or flat, but not up.

Limited impact of QT on Treasuries: We see the implications of a tapered QT for US rates markets as limited. The shift in supply of Treasuries relative to expectations will likely not be substantial, and the effects will be most noticeable in T-bills and swap spreads, rather than longer-dated yields.

January Treasury refunding preview: We expect Treasury to increase coupon sizes for the 2y and 5y +\$3bn per month, 3y +\$2bn per month, and 7y +\$1bn per month. We expect Treasury to increase both new issue and reopening auction sizes for the 10y +\$2bn and 30y +\$1bn. We expect Treasury to maintain the new issue and reopening auction size for the 20y. All these changes are the same as the increases in November, and we do not see the Treasury "opportunistically" increasing coupon sizes.

## Euro area

On duration, we continue to think that the risks of further sell-off are more balanced. The valuation gap versus our fair value has been closed in the sell-off as positioning on longs got reduced substantially, as the market turned short during the correction observed. The sell-off observed was predominantly driven by (i) a stronger than-expected UK inflation print coupled with (ii) an upside surprise in US retail sales and (iii) a barrage of ECB speakers pushing against market pricing.

We highlight that in the absence of a meaningful shock in underlying dynamics, a sustained continuation of the sell-off looks unsustainable considering that market pricing brought back the terminal rate to around 2% (and it will likely remain

anchored at this point for the near term) and nominal yields decoupled meaningfully versus 5y5y inflation expectations.

On the curve, we think that the macro is still supportive of a steeper curve, and we wish to opportunistically take advantage of the recent flattening at the long end to pay the 10s30s segment of the swap curve. As a severe risk-off remains a tail event for now, the higher probability of entering into an easing cycle should be supportive of the steepener expression, particularly given the current inversion of the 10s30s curve versus previous tightening cycles and given how little the steepening is priced into near-term forwards.

Additionally, we estimate the current dislocation on EUR 10s30s at around \~18bp (more than 2 standard deviations) and, as we believe that both duration and vol will stay range-bound, we like playing the mean-reverting angle versus our model. Screening for forward structures that eliminate the slight negative carry on spot 10s30s while retaining its directionality versus the spot curve, we identified paying EUR 20y5y versus receiving EUR 5y5y as the best candidate for the steepening expression.

## Japan

We see the risk of the BoJ's future rate hike path repricing higher at the upcoming January MPM, if the central bank does express greater confidence in the achievability of its price stability target and this is seen as laying the groundwork for a NIRP exit in March or April.

On the other hand, even if no such hint ends up being offered, it is possible that USD/JPY could keep rallying towards 150 and thereby fuel expectations that the BoJ might need to shift onto a more hawkish footing in order to curb JPY’s slide. We thus see a non-negligible possibility of markets starting to price in a faster and/ or steeper BoJ rate hike trajectory irrespective of what actually ends up being said on January 23.

Hence, we are reluctant to maintain a long bias around the belly zone given the aforementioned potential for a hawkish repricing of the BoJ’s rate hike trajectory. Meanwhile, we expect super-long JGBs to keep struggling for at least the time being given poor supply/demand dynamics.

We have previously recommended 10y OIS receiver vs. 30y JGB short (DV01 1.5 vs. 1) positions, but now suggest shifting to DV01-neutral weights, which can benefit from a sharper BoJ rate-hiking cycle being priced in while remaining positioned to benefit from supply/demand weakness in super-long JGBs. Meanwhile, we continue to recommend TONA OIS 2s5s steepeners (DV01 1.5 vs. 1) and 30y ASW shorts.

## Are markets missing the inflation progress?

The key market-moving events in the last week include comments from Fed Governor Waller and President Goolsbee, as well as strong retail sales data. Waller's remarks brought focus on the upcoming CPI annual revisions on February 9 – our economists do not see a major change in the inflation trajectory. They see the upside revisions in early 2023 as an exception than a rule.

Amid all of the data, we believe markets have not taken notice of very encouraging data on inflation, especially the decline in New Tenant Repeat Rent (NTRR) Index, a significant influence on the trajectory for rent inflation (see Exhibit 17 ). The New Tenant Repeat Rent Index is released every month after CPI, and we think the timing of the release means that it wasn't noticed by investors this week.

The last time we saw the NTRR get near these levels, rent inflation ultimately bottomed around close to 0% y/y nearly three quarters later.

This release highlights a major downside risk to the path of rental inflation, but most importantly, it is a key data point to refute the inflation holdouts who think 1) inflation will be "sticky", 2) the "last mile" of inflation will be hard, or 3) those who directly want to see more progress on rent inflation, like Chicago Fed President Goolsbee.

## Goolsbee, January 19: Need to see more progress on rental inflation

However, the latest reading on NTRR should inspire confidence that rental inflation cooling should be showing up in the coming quarters. Given that rent inflation is often the median component of CPI (see Exhibit 19 ), as rent inflation cools, the path of core CPI will generally reset lower, all else equal.

Is the market missing the CPI fixings signal? One more thing that markets have not noticed is how weak is the CPI path implied by CPI fixings. Core CPI fixings derived from headline CPI prices suggest the possibility that core CPI could move lower than 2% in the second half of 2024, which could even raise a scenario where inflation is seen as "too low". While not the base case of our US economics team, we think that the market has not paid attention to this more downbeat forecast implied by the CPI fixings market.

We think the fact that fed funds rate is falling slower than the fixing-market-implied core CPI path could be a sign that investors' inflation forecasts are higher than CPI fixings. If investors were indexing the fed funds path to CPI fixings, the fixings-implied real rate would likely not have been going up, as most investors tend to agree that real fed funds rate should be going down or flat, but not up.

Given that CPI fixings have in the past had a good track record on CPI instead of consensus economists, we think markets should pay more attention to the CPI path implied by CPI fixings.

Given our view that the market is missing a lot of inflation progress, not pricing the risk of a hard landing, and not pricing the risk of an inflation level that's too low, we see scope for duration to rally from here. ## Limited impact rom QT changes to duration

Our US economics team now expects the Fed to announce a taper earlier than previously expected – around May 2024, with an end to QT in 1Q25. The timing of the start of tapering would be a little on the sooner end vs. what the December survey of primary dealers (3Q24 tapering) and survey of market participants (2Q24 tapering) imply. The bigger surprise in our call on QT tapering would be the Fed keeping QT running longer as an offset to starting tapering earlier. Many market participants associate an earlier start to tapering with an earlier end to QT.

With the Treasury's finances outlook improved by \$110 billion, the Treasury would need to issue \$110 billion less to the market to keep its overall funding profile unchanged. While the \$110bn change in our supply estimates may or may not match the market's expectation, the key thing to note is that the impact of QT on USTs depends on the Treasury itself – it has to decide whether to reduce issuance in bills or coupons.

In this case, the answer might be rather straightforward – the Treasury can reduce bill sizes issued to the market to accommodate the impact of QT tapering.

So as the Fed tapers QT, it reinvests more in maturing coupons, by increasing the sizes of add-ons in US Treasury auctions. This means the tapering of QT will increase the funding of the Treasury via coupons. In a world where total funding needs don't change, and the Treasury is getting more funding via coupons, the Treasury would then have to decide whether to lower coupons sizes or bills.

We think the Treasury will lower bill sizes offered to the market, because the Treasury would not want to lower coupon sizes right after they increased them for three quarters (from August refunding up to the upcoming January refunding) – keeping with the "regular and predictable" issuance strategy for coupons.

The idea of reducing net bill issuance to market also happens to work well because the Treasury's current issuance strategy has pushed t-bills/total outstanding ratio to 21.5% in 2023, above their generally preferred 15-20% range. Reducing bills in response to QT tapering would help keep the bills outstanding within the preferred range.

Ultimately, the supply channel will be a lot more influential in affecting T-bills than long-dated yields.

2) The monetary policy signal from QT: Our economics team believes the Fed will frame any changes to QT as linked to the liquidity available in money markets and not as an active tool being used to adjust monetary policy. The perception of QT not being an active monetary policy tool is bolstered by the fact that the ultimate duration impact of changes to QT does in fact depend on changes by the Treasury – as we have explained above.

This perception is bolstered by the fact that QT will continue while rate cuts could start later this year – clearly highlighting the focus on rate cuts as the primary monetary policy tool. And thus, we do not think that investors need to treat QT as a monetary policy signal. However, perception is often reality in markets, and it is possible that investors see changes to QT as a dovish signal and Treasury yields could react lower based on that perception.

Overall, we expect changes to QT in line with our house view to have a limited impact on duration. We expect swap spreads to be much more affected by discussions on QT.

## Into front-end MPCs receivers and long-end steepeners

Macro data release took center stage over the past week with wage growth data followed by the December Inflation Report and the December Retails Sales print. The main market mover has been the stronger-than-expected December inflation print despite being followed by very weak Retail Sales, which plummeted in December, marking the biggest drop since January 2021. As pointed out in the UK Inflation Watch: Fasten Your Seatbelts, the inflation beat versus market fixing was only modest, as the RPI print came at 5.16%Y versus consensus at 5.1%Y, while the fixing ahead of the print traded around 5.08%Y. The CPI print was stronger than expected as headline came at 4%Y versus consensus at 3.8%Y and core came at 5.1%Y versus consensus at 4.9%Y.

While the print was a beat versus consensus, we think two main points dampen the relevance of the upside surprise. First, the most significant driver of the beat was air fares, which came at 57%M and should reverse in January, despite the next print being impacted by the basket re-weighting. Second, CPI headline and core came at \~60bp and \~45pb lower respectively than the November BoE's projections – which should support a meaningful revision of the CPI projections at the upcoming MPC. Looking ahead, we expect disinflation continuing and headline CPI possibly slipping below 2%Y in April 2024. Should inflation settle below the BoE's target, the importance of core and service inflation remaining elevated decreases in our view, and should support a dovish pivot from the BoE.

The price action following the inflation print was somewhat unexpected given the magnitude of the sell-off; however, stronger-than-expected US Retail Sales and market positioning have likely contributed to the sell-off. Looking at current valuations, the market still prices a more dovish BoE for the current year compared to our modal view with \~110bp of cuts being priced in 2024 at the time of writing as shown in Exhibit 43 . In addition, the upcoming budget poses a risk to the evolution of the Bank Rate; hence, we remain overall neutral on duration, despite very front-end money market valuations and, in particular, reds looking attractive after the recent sell-off as they offer a compelling risk-reward profile in our view.

The market is currently pricing \~50% probability of a rate cut in May (see Exhibit 44 ), which sits below our projections and we see limited scope for further repricing in lower easing expectations in front-end MPCs. ## Steeper curve beyond the 10y tenor

JGB market participants currently appear to be focusing mostly on overseas developments along with auctions and other supply/demand drivers now that expectations for some sort of policy adjustment at the January 22-23 BoJ meeting have been dialed right back.

Overseas bond markets had been priced for rapid rate cuts as of late last year, but have since seen somewhat of a correction amid a pushback from central bank speakers as well as US retail data indicative of consumer spending having remained strong despite such significant Fed tightening.

The resultant rises in overseas interest rates appear to have helped to drive up JGB yields via two channels, namely a widening of the term premium and higher short-to-mediumterm rates expectations via a renewed rally in USD/JPY. Meanwhile, JGB auction results since the start of this year have been disappointing, suggesting that domestic investors may have been reluctant to join in the rally seen through late December.

As we discussed in Japan | Lower BoJ Expectations Meet Positive Carry Trades, while many do appear to have pushed out their envisaged timing for an exit from the BoJ’s negative interest rate policy (NIRP), the general consensus still seems to be that a commencement of monetary policy “normalization” will indeed be possible in the not-too-distant future.

Our suspicion is that domestic investors as a whole have a wait-and-see stance in expectation of facing better dip-buying opportunities at some point prior to the April meeting.

As a whole, the rises in overseas interest rates and weak supply/demand pushed both term premium and rate expectations higher, with the curve steepening amid the rates selloff (see Exhibit 47 ).

The steepening pressure particularly became pronounced beyond the 10y+ sector, while less than 10y tenors had traded relatively anchored despite the recent rapid rally in USD/ JPY.

Correlation does appear to have picked back up more recently as USD/JPY has rallied to around 148. Meanwhile, the curve has bear-steepened as any repricing of expectations for BoJ rate hikes looks to have been comparatively modest.

## Could we see higher rates expectation once again at the January MPM?

The BoJ’s messaging is likely to be the main focus of the January meeting. With the central bank yet to say anything whatsoever about what might happen to its policy rate following a NIRP exit, market participants have basically been left to make their own guesses based on overseas conditions, economic data and any other available information.

The period from November 2023 through early 2024 saw market participants dial back their expectations for both the timing of a NIRP exit and the subsequent trajectory of the BoJ’s policy rate (see Exhibit 51 ). Contributing factors are likely to have included (1) declines in USD/JPY, (2) a deceleration in domestic inflation, (3) a rethink of BoJ rate hike timing, and (4) additional uncertainty generated by the Noto Peninsula earthquake.

The BoJ will probably want to avoid making any direct mention of exchange rates, but what might it have to say about the other three points?

With regard to (2), the updated inflation projections in the quarterly Outlook for Economic Activity and Prices report offer some insight into how the recent slowdown in price growth is being viewed at the central bank. Our economists expect the BoJ to lower its FY2024 forecast to reflect recent declines in oil prices but are meanwhile looking for the median FY2025 forecast to rise by 0.1pp to +1.8%Y.

Such a move could help to convince market participants that BoJ normalization is not all that far away if viewed as an expression of increasing confidence that inflation can be sustained over the longer term.

With regard to (3), we thus see potential for envisaged rate hike trajectories to be repriced if the BoJ ends up sending some sort of signal that might hint at a higher probability of the short-term policy rate being hiked to zero at the March meeting (see Exhibit 51 ).

Markets are likely to be listening especially closely to what Ueda has to say about whether the “likelihood” of achieving the +2% price stability target might have increased.

As has been pointed out by our economists, the BoJ itself appears to be paying particularly close attention to the outlook for spring 2024 wage hikes as well as evidence of spillovers from wages to prices. In this regard, our economists are also mindful of the possibility that Ueda might start to use at least slightly more upbeat language when discussing such matters.

With regard to (4), our economists currently expect the earthquake to have only a limited macroeconomic impact, and see potential for a more hawkish repricing if Ueda were to express a similar view.

Taking all the above into consideration, we see a risk of markets starting to price a BoJ rate hike trajectory back in if (as envisaged by our economists) the central bank does express greater confidence in the achievability of its price stability target and this is seen as laying the groundwork for a NIRP exit in March or April.

And even if no such hint ends up being offered, it is possible that USD/JPY could keep rallying towards 150 and thereby fuel expectations that the BoJ might need to shift onto a more hawkish footing in order to curb JPY’s slide.

We thus see a non-negligible possibility of markets starting to price in a faster and/or steeper BoJ rate hike trajectory irrespective of what actually ends up being said on January 23.

Both are potential catalysts for the long end of the US yield curve and thus USD as well. An earlier-than-expected announcement that QT is ending, and/or a lower-than-expected increase in coupon sizes, could weigh on long-end rates and USD as well.

Treasury refunding: Lower-than-expected coupon issuance could weigh on long-end rates and USD: Treasury refunding, or when the US Treasury guides the market on expected note and bond issuance over the coming quarter, isn't normally something on FX investors' radar. But given the sizeable shift in US rates last summer which some investors partly attributed to supply concerns, these factors are quite important.

As our colleagues note here, the Treasury has already guided the market that the total size of coupon issuance is likely to rise as early as the February meeting. But what happens if the supply of coupon issuance is less than expected, either because total borrowing estimates are lower, or because the distribution of issuance suggests less longend bond selling than expected?

FOMC meeting: Talking about talking about QT? Comments from Dallas Fed President Logan suggesting that the Fed should slow its balance sheet shrinkage as overnight RRP usage approaches a "low level" have kicked off investor discussions of when the Fed might end quantitative tightening.

Given that ON RRP usage, currently around US\$700 billion, is relatively close to the roughly US\$550 billion our economics team estimates to be consistent with a "low level" of take-up , the time for 'talking about talking about tapering' QT appears to be approaching (see Exhibit 67 ).

The January meeting could thus provide an opportunity for the Fed to guide market expectations on the balance sheet through a combination of the statement, Chair Powell's press conference and the statement on longer-run goals (typically reaffirmed at the January meeting).

An earlier-than-expected slowdown in tapering would mean that the Fed would be reinvesting incoming cash flows in larger and faster size, which could weigh on longer-end yields and perhaps USD too.

## Who will matter more for USD – Bill Murray or Julius Caesar? Perhaps both, but unlikely neither

Concentrated catalysts around Groundhog Day and the Ides of March may prove important turning points for USD. Both famous days have a mixed bag of risks. While US data could be a bit USD-positive, falling long-end yields should we see less supply (refunding) and more demand (an earlier end to QT) could counterbalance this.

On Wednesday, Thomas Jordan, Chairman of the Governing Board of the SNB, gave an interview to Bloomberg about monetary policy in Switzerland.

In this interview Mr Jordan alluded to, among other things, an appreciation of the Swiss franc in real terms at the end of 2023.

He stated that the real appreciation "makes the situation for some of our firms more difficult" in contrast to the nominal appreciation seen throughout most of the year, which had shielded Switzerland from foreign inflationary pressures.

The market interpreted this comment as an indication that the Swiss franc needed to weaken materially; but is this truly the case?

## Does this mean anything for monetary policy going forward?

Other than his remarks about real appreciation, Mr. Jordan also stated that "monetary conditions in Switzerland is on the one hand given by the exchange rate level and on the other hand by the interest rate level".

By this definition, monetary conditions tightened further post the SNB's December meeting, despite already being sufficiently tight for the SNB's entire conditional forecast horizon to be below the 2%Y target.

Hence, in the SNB's own eyes we may be seeing overtightening in Switzerland. Given that intervening in FX markets is a more nimble monetary policy tool than changes to policy rate, this may be exactly what the SNB is doing – ensuring that the Swiss franc weakens sufficiently, bringing it back to a level in real terms it finds preferable, while allowing nominal appreciation going forward if inflation abroad remains greater than locally.

However, Mr. Jordan also said that "at the moment it looks like we have adequate monetary conditions". He caveated that inflationary risks remain both to the upside and downside, meaning that the adequacy of Switzerland's current monetary conditions "may change".

## What could result in more material CHF weakness?

We continue to believe that a drop in inflation of the domestic basket is necessary for the Swiss franc to weaken materially without overshooting the SNB's 2%Y target.

Until then, the Swiss franc will be only be able to strengthen or weaken marginally depending on relative inflation dynamics locally and abroad before the SNB walks it back to the monetary conditions it deems adequate.

## Destination: Ample reserves

As discussed in Talking about Talking about QT, January 6, 2024, we characterize reserves as abundant when the opportunity cost of reserves (captured by IORB) is above the cost of liquidity for the market (SOFR) and ample when they are fairly close to each other. In an ample reserves regime, banks are neither rewarded (IORB > SOFR) nor penalized for holding reserves (IORB < SOFR).

In other worlds, "abundant" reserves occur when there is too much liquidity and money market rates fall materially below IORB (SOFR < IORB). In the extreme case of too much liquidity, SOFR tends to print at or below the RRP rate, which would be consistent with a "super abundant" reserves regime. At the other extreme, SOFR being materially above IORB shows that banks are being penalized for holding reserves even when it is economically better not to do so (SOFR > IORB).

As front-end markets continue to come into balance, SOFR has started to gradually climb above the RRP rate over the past year. In our view, the eventual depletion of the RRP is what will ultimately allow SOFR to come in consistently at or slightly above IORB as banks become the marginal lenders of cash (right now, dealers can meet surges in demand for repo financing by bidding up MMF cash in the RRP, earning the fixed rate of 5.3%). Current conditions remain consistent with abundant reserves ( Exhibit 93 ).

Into 2H24, SOFR consistently at or above IORB should serve as an indicator that the Fed has achieved an ample reserves regime. By the same token, stopping QT soon (particularly before the RRP is depleted) would leave the Fed implementing monetary policy under an abundant reserves regime and using the RRP as a ceiling for money market rates instead of a floor.

Once SOFR > IORB is achieved, the question then turns to how low reserves can go before reaching banks' minimum comfortable level and causing disruptions in the repo market. This would be against the effective implementation of monetary policy (money market rates such as SOFR and EFFR spike above the target range) and the Fed's goal of maintaining financial stability while achieving its dual mandate.

The Fed would then have to be careful to not do "too much" QT to avoid SOFR consistently spiking above the target range and having the Fed effectively become the lender of last resort (via the Standing Repo Facility or SRF, which currently sits at the fed funds upper target of 5.5%).

Another consideration is that the "ample" reserves corridor is currently at 10bp (spread between SRF and IORB). This could create the incentive for the Fed to align its taper plans with technical adjustments that lower IORB and RRP by up to 10bp, widening the ample reserve corridor to up to 20bp. This would create an even greater incentive for banks to lend cash in a post-RRP world (more below).

## SOFR - IORB spread

A positive and wider SOFR - IORB spread over time should serve as a clear indicator that banks are not comfortable holding lower reserves balances even when it is profitable to do so by lending in the repo market (SOFR > IORB). In addition, paying close attention to market signals is likely to be a preferred avenue for Fed officials this time around. In 2019, expectations on when to taper and end QT were shaped around estimates of reserves that proved to be too low.

Today, the SOFR - IORB spread currently sits at -8bp with the 20-day moving average also \~6-7bp below IORB while starting to increase gradually ( Exhibit 94 ). This compares to 2018/19, in which the SOFR - IORB spread became increasingly positive since late 2018, reaching a peak of 315bp on September 17, 2019 and forcing the Fed to purchase bills and conduct repo operations to restore order to funding markets.

## Usage of the Standing Repo Facility (SRF)

As the RRP continues to be depleted with MMFs extending into bills and capturing attractive repo rates in the market (see Exhibit 98 and here for more), banks should eventually start lending excess reserves to opportunistically capture returns above IORB. The uncertainty comes from not knowing the level of reserves that banks will be willing to lend in a post-RRP world before disruption in funding markets occurs.

Still, in 2H24 we should observe a pick-up in reverse repo and fed funds sold by all banks (assets) in the weekly H.8 release once the RRP is depleted ( Exhibit 99 ). This same dynamic occurred in 2018 and into 2019 as demand for repo financing (reflected via higher SOFR volumes) continued to grow and banks acted as the marginal lenders of cash.

Where the SRF comes into play is when banks no longer become willing and/or able to lend excess reserves while demand for repo financing continues to increase. This backdrop into 2H19 is what contributed to the eventual dysfunction in repo as demand for funding (SOFR volume) continued to climb while reserves deployed in repo plateaued ( Exhibit 99 ).

We estimate that SRF usage above \$50bn would trigger the Fed to end QT, particularly if this occurs consistently and outside monthend.

At the same time, we emphasize that the SRF is not a perfect ceiling and SOFR is likely to print above the SRF rate and the fed funds target range when conditions for its use arise. This is mainly due to the fact that the SRF is only available to primary dealers and 25 banks, which then have to distribute this funding to those in need (leveraged investors in need of repo financing).

This is further complicated by the fact that the Fed's repo operations are not centrally cleared, meaning that dealers and banks would need to have balance sheet capacity to make effective use of the SRF. Regardless, the existence of the SRF as a standing facility will likely remove sharp upwards tail risks for funding and more importantly provide the Fed with a valuable signal that reserves are becoming scarce.

## Distribution of reserves

Dallas Fed President Logan named the risk that "individual banks can approach scarcity before the system as a whole" as a key reason to taper QT once "ON RRP balances approach a low level". A slower pace of QT should allow the markets to "redistribute liquidity" as reserves continue to shrink and demand for funding continues to grow. Hence, the distribution of reserves and how that informs banks' ability to lend excess reserves and its borrowing needs will also serve as an important indicator for discussions around QT.

In Exhibit 101 , we show reserves as a percentage of assets for large, small and foreign banks (as classified by the H.8 release) over recent years. This analysis helps us to assess how comfortable individual bank types might be with current reserve levels.

As shown in Exhibit 102 , all bank types currently have reserve levels above those seen during the March bank stresses, late 2018 (when SOFR - IORB turned positive) and the September 2019 repo spike. We recognize that this analysis is limited as the Fed is able to see reserve balances for individual banks on a daily basis. This also does not account for the likely shift higher in desired reserve levels.

However, elevated cash holdings for foreign banks give us comfort that some reserves out of the current \$3.5 trillion will be deployed into funding markets once rates become attractive. Given that foreign banks are not subject to the same regulatory constraints, these banks tend to be more responsive to swings in funding conditions. For instance, as shown in Exhibit 103 , foreign bank reserves as a percentage of assets declined by over 15pp during QT1 as money market rates (SOFR and EFFR) cheapened.

In our base case forecast, we estimate that the bulk of the decline in reserves to \$3.2 trillion will be provided by foreign banks as US banks decide to hold on to cash in line with the recent trend of sticky reserves (see here for more). This conservative assumption will leave foreign banks with \~30% reserves to assets while US banks maintain current levels.

As reserves eventually decline, trends in reserves relative to total assets across different bank types should help to inform bank willingness and ability to lend excess cash and overall funding needs. In particular, cash over assets becoming "sticky" suggests that banks are no longer comfortable holding lower reserves relative to their total holdings of liquid assets.

This would then lead banks to seek other sources of funding to offset reserves and limit the ability of reserves to maintain functioning repo markets as demand for funding continues to increase. As we observed in past episodes of tighter funding conditions, we expect small banks to exhibit this behavior first (for instance, as shown in Exhibit 101 , reserves/assets for small banks became sticky around \~6% in 2018), leaving foreign and large US banks as those willing or able to shed excess reserves as QT continues.

## Fed to remain attentive to money market conditions

Even with a robust framework to appreciate and analyze the impact of QT over time, the timing and size of the eventual taper this year remains highly uncertain as Fed discussions are still in early stages. As these discussions take shape and the Fed formalizes its views around its future balance sheet reduction plans, the accuracy of our views is likely to improve over time.

At the same time, an earlier taper versus our previous base estimate (see here) of September reduces tail risks to funding conditions in the coming months and gives us further comfort that reserve scarcity remains some time away (1H25). We believe that this also significantly reduces the likelihood that the Fed will rush into tapering and ending QT assuming our economists' base case for a soft landing plays out for the rest of the year.

## Remain neutral on SOFR/FF

In 2019? Not Yet, December 8, 2023, we noted that market expectations for funding conditions moved too fast in late 2023. As shown in Exhibit 104 , 1m SOFR/FF spreads as measured by 1m SOFR (SER) and fed funds (FF) futures contracts (negative means SOFR expected to be above EFFR) have reversed some of this overly pessimistic pricing that saw SOFR realizing 5-6bp above EFFR in 2H24.

In particular, this move into year-end appeared excessive when considering that realized SOFR/FF averaged 2bp in 2018 and 4bp in 2019 ( Exhibit 105 ) and contributed to our decision to exit our short 2y SOFR swap spread trade. This continues to present the risk of SOFR/FF realizing above current pricing, particularly in 1H24.

So far, realized SOFR prints continue to show that temporary month-end SOFR spikes are driven by typical dynamics such as dealer intermediation constraints and large net settlements. This is in line with our expectation for repo markets to remain largely well behaved as long as there are still ample balances in the RRP (see here for more).

In addition, recent talk about an eventual taper has likely contributed to the recent widening in SOFR/FF and front-end swap spreads. At the same time, we think that potential market expectations for a quick start to taper and end to QT in the next 1-3 quarters might be overly optimistic when considering current funding conditions, and that recent Fed discussions have only revolved around tapering versus ending QT.

As discussed above, our current read of funding indicators and our updated forecast for the Fed's balance sheet continue to suggest that clearer signs of scarcity that will motivate the Fed to fully end QT are still some time away. This leaves the door open for potential tighter funding conditions, particularly in 2H24 and into 2025. For now, we remain neutral on SOFR/FF and 2y SOFR swap spreads.

This week, we look at the statistical structure of short expiry vol, via principal component analysis (PCA). We find that PCA provides a meaningful signal on future changes in implied volatilities. We start our analysis by running PCA on weekly changes of implied volatilities for 2y/5y/10y tails on 3m/6m/1y/2y expiry SOFR swaptions. We next study whether PCA provides a useful signal on future implied vol changes. More specifically, we again run a rolling PCA analysis over a grid of lookback windows. For each day in our backtest, we reconstruct the vol surface using the first two principal components. We interpret the reconstructed PCA estimates as historical "fair value," and compute the "residuals" of actual pricing relative to this fair value. We then analyze the correlation between the residuals, and ex-post realized changes in implied vol, over a grid of holding periods.

The meaning of the residual is worth spelling out. Consider, hypothetically, that the 3m2y point is trading at 100bp on January 8, but PCA fair value is 90bp. This would correspond to a 100bp-90bp = +10bp residual, signalling 3m2y richness. Were PCA analysis statistically useful in understanding future implied vol changes, we would expect 3m2y vols over a future holding period, say 2w, to decrease (at least relative to the rest of the surface).

In practice, we find evidence of a negative correlation between PCA residuals and subsequent 4w implied vol changes. This result is most pronounced for 3m expiries, but less so for larger expiries (see Exhibit 124 ).

We think our results can be explained by interpreting the PCA signal as containing pricing information relative to the vol surface, rather than in absolute terms. Shorter expiry points are more volatile, and thus their relative price moves will be largely determined by absolute price moves. Less volatile >3m expiry points, however, must be analyzed in relative terms.

In future work, we aim to evaluate our above hypothesis to better gauge whether PCA could be the source of a systematic strategy. We do so by extending our analysis from absolute to relative price changes, but also aim to integrate (1) transaction cost analysis, (2) roll-down of holdings, and (3) delta hedging PnL.

## Trading Strategy 1 – "Trade Longs/Fade Shorts"

When the 5-day moving average crosses above the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract (long duration) and hold for a 25-business-day period. When the 5-day moving average crosses below the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract and hold for a 25-business-day period. In short, this strategy buys futures when the Simple Moving Average Crossover (SMAX) generates both a long and a short signal, given the historical outperformance of long signals traded long and underperformance of short signals traded short. Given that the SMAX could generate both a long and a short signal within the predefined holding period, an investor may have a 200% long position since each of the two signals would be traded in separate portfolio sleeves.

Not-Rated (NR) - Currently the analyst does not have adequate conviction about the stock's total return relative to the relevant country MSCI Index or the average total return of the analyst's industry (or industry team's) coverage universe, on a risk-adjusted basis, over the next 12-18 months.
