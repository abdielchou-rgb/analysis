## Raiders of the Lost FCI

It seems like just yesterday that investors and policymakers worried that easy financial conditions would make the Fed's mission to bring inflation back to 2 percent impossible. In fact, investor concern regarding increasingly easy financial conditions faded alongside worries about sticky inflation. The last two US inflation reports had slain two dragons simultaneously.

But why? Was the financial conditions framework in which investors believed faulty? If not, shouldn't investors be growing even more concerned? Equity prices continue to rise with only minor drawdowns, like the one seen earlier this past week (see Exhibit 1 ). Even equal-weighted equity indexes popped higher after a recent consolidation (see Exhibit 2 ).

Even FOMC participants – including Fed Chair Powell – hardly bring financial conditions into their speeches recently. Indeed, on a popular financial conditions index (FCI), conditions continue to ease in trend-line fashion. What gives?

Earlier this year, we discussed the Federal Reserve Board staff's US Financial Conditions Impulse on Growth (FCI-G). The index translates quarterly movements in seven financial variables, using weights implied by the FRB/US model and other models in use at the Federal Reserve Board, into a real GDP growth impact over the next year (see Could Easier Financial Conditions Mean Fewer Rate Cuts?).

As of June 2024, the FCI-G implies that quarterly changes in financial conditions over the past year would add 0.36pp to real GDP growth over the next year. At the same time, changes in financial conditions over the past three years would subtract 0.11pp to real GDP growth (see Exhibit 3 and Exhibit 4 ).

• Neither of these estimates should raise concerns about upside or downside risks to growth - which may explain the lack of public comments about financial conditions from FOMC participants.

## Trade, Tariffs, and the US Dollar

Trade policy is likely to be a primary avenue by which a new administration has implications for macro markets. We think the USD would likely experience tailwinds if it looks increasingly likely that the next presidential administration will raise tariffs substantially on imports, but might decline if the next presidential administration appears inclined to pursue a relatively status-quo trade policy.

In 2018-2019, the rise in US tariffs were associated with a decline in US growth expectations. However, they were also associated with a larger reduction in growth expectations outside the US ( Exhibit 7 ).

Exhibit 7: Growth expectations fell outside the US in late 2018 and 2019 as trade tensions rose
![](images/716c1a35594e00ca01358ebacdccf3a7f5b588190a864c2d45485520104a743e.jpg)
Source: World Bank, Macrobond, Morgan Stanley Research

Exhibit 8: Exports are a relatively small portion of the US economy
![](images/c6bada47cac5e274e3d170def75308955912bbf58c15200eb2cef1e524906aaf.jpg)
Source: Bloomberg, Macrobond, Morgan Stanley Research

The limited effect on US growth expectations (compared to growth abroad) could be related to the relatively small ratio of goods and services exports relative to the size of the US economy (around 12%). Compare that small US share to the euro area share, where gross exports of goods and services equal roughly 54% of GDP ( Exhibit 8 ).

An overall reduction in trade volumes could slow growth expectations outside the US more than within the US – making the US relatively more attractive as an investment destination. The overall effect would depend on which tariffs are applied and on which trading partners. Services are included in Exhibit 7 because non-tariff barriers were an important but relatively little-discussed part of the 2018-19 trade conflict.

As an example of how this dynamic played out previously, growth expectations declined during the 2018-2019 trade war outside the US more than they declined in the US ( Exhibit 8 ).

Exhibit 9: Policy rates fell in a number of economies relative to the US during the 2018-9 trade tensions

Slower growth outside the US resulted in policy rates declining more in several economies outside the US than policy rates fell in the US ( Exhibit 9 ). In other words, policy rate differentials moved in the US’s favor against a number of central banks - particularly those of emerging market economies (which had room to ease policy). This decline in growth expectations and policy rates outside the US likely contributed to the USD's resilience in 2018-2019 despite a drop in 2y Treasury yields ( Exhibit 10 ).

There are significant differences between today and 2018-9 regarding the degree to which trade confrontation is already priced in, which currencies might underperform or outperform against the USD, and how a future trade confrontation scenario might generally differ from 2018/2019. We plan to explore these themes in upcoming notes.

## Can US Policymakers Actually Weaken the USD?

If the US government wanted a weaker USD, could they make it happen? A weaker USD after all probably would help boost US exports, all else equal, considering more than 95% of US exports are invoiced in USD. But the USD’s outlook is driven by domestic policy and forces abroad and the policies investors are discussing as potentially most likely, threaten to bolster, not weaken, the USD.

As noted in Trade, Tariffs, and the US Dollar , rising US tariffs raise the risk of a higher USD by creating asymmetric downside risks to foreign growth. USD weakness tends to be most sustainable when foreign growth is experiencing a cyclical upswing.

Policies that bolster real US interest rates likely enhance the USD’s attractiveness. Higher real rates could come from a few channels: additional fiscal expansion; a higher neutral rate (driven by regulatory changes enhancing productivity); and/or an inflationary impulse prompting greater relative restrictiveness from the Fed. The precise impact on the USD from each of these varies, but the common theme is that it’s a lot harder for the USD to fall.

This isn’t to say a weaker USD is impossible. But arguably the ripest environment for a weaker USD would see the interrelated forces of US rates falling faster than those of its peers, a broad-based risk asset rally, and strong, synchronized growth abroad. Domestic policies which make these harder make USD weakness harder too.

But we shouldn’t underestimate attempts to generate a weaker USD either. We’ve written previously that we’re skeptical that USD-weakening intervention, particularly if unilateral, is likely to be successful in a sustained manner. But that doesn’t mean it can’t happen and, as price action this week reflects, even expressing concern about the USD’s strength can have a negative effect on the greenback as investors price in more risk premia.

We shouldn’t rule out coordinated efforts to weaken the USD either; some foreign trading partners, most notably in Japan but perhaps others in Asia as well, for example South Korea, might welcome USD weakness and local FX strength.

Finally, some US policies continue to raise concerns about the USD. As we detail here, concerns that the US is using its reserve currency status to pursue foreign policy aims through sanctions and other tools have pushed some investors to search more actively for a US dollar-alternative.

In sum, a weaker USD might be policymakers’ preference, and we think investors shouldn’t be too complacent to the risk that they’ll try, but if other policies make the greenback more attractive, it’ll be difficult to see USD weakness taking place.

## Shades of August seasonality support steepeners

The intraday price action in the last two days reminds us of last summer. In the last two days, with no data or Fedspeak or election rated information, Treasury yields started to rise. This was common last summer, where the default price action seemed to be higher yields – amid a stronger data profile, however.

We also noted last summer that investors tend to front-run seasonality in late July, even though August is the true seasonality month given its lower trading volumes. This is why yields rose starting late July last year, and continued into August and even September. ## Inflation confidence continues to increase

Last week we noted that the CPI print for June was conducive to increased confidence because (1) inflation has broadly followed the contours of residual seasonality and (2) a monthly rent inflation step-down in June could shave off \~6bp a month of core CPI and \~3bp a month of core PCE. This week, we got even more confidence-inspiring numbers around rent inflation: the BLS's own micro-data-driven New Tenant Rent (NTR) Index.

The BLS produces an NTR update every quarter to show how new tenant leases have moved. The NTR tends to influence the overall rent inflation basket, with a lag of about four quarters according to the BLS's own study. In other words, the NTR today can suggest where rent inflation will be in four quarters from now. So far, rent inflation has tracked the NTR well in the last few months ( Exhibit 28 ).

## Impact of JGB purchase tapering

A wide range of views on the pace and extent of BoJ JGB purchase tapering appear to have been expressed at the July 9–10 “Bond Market Group” meetings, but our overall impression is that many investors are now looking for the central bank to roughly halve its monthly purchases (to around JPY3 trillion) over the next two years in quarterly or perhaps half-yearly increments.

Governor Ueda indicated in his June post-meeting press conference that he wants to offer the market a degree of “predictability” so as to avoid a situation whereby the tapering process creates instability in long-term interest rates.

We thus believe the BoJ will likely respect the apparent market consensus when it comes to both the size and the speed of reductions in its JGB purchases. A reduction to JPY3 trillion per month over the space of two years should come as little surprise and have only a limited impact on the interest rate delta. That just leaves the question of how the reductions might end up being spread across the yield curve.

The most natural assumption—and that which is likely being made by market participants —is that the reductions will be biggest in the sub-10y sector where the BoJ’s ownership share is largest (see Exhibit 37 ).

Opinion is more divided when it comes to super-long JGBs, with most at the aforementioned “Bond Market Group” meetings having called for tapering to focus mostly on the short- to medium-term sector. We ourselves envisage something along the lines of offer amounts being reduced by JPY50 billion, JPY100 billion, and JPY100 billion for each operation in the 1y–3y, 3y–5y, and 5y–10y sectors, respectively, if the BoJ opts to cut back its monthly purchases by JPY1 trillion.

More reduction in sub-10y sector should already have been mostly priced in and is accordingly unlikely to have more than a limited impact on ASW spreads through the 10y sector. That said, unchanged offer amounts further out the curve would likely be seen as positive for the super-long sector and might thus cause recent cheapening of long-end JGB ASW to be at least partially reversed.

## Positioning ahead of the BoJ meeting

The BoJ’s rate hike trajectory is liable to be repriced upwards depending on media speculation between now and the end-July meeting as well as what actually ends up being announced on July 31. In particular, the aforementioned comments by Taro Kono—coupled with what appears very likely to have been JPY-supportive intervention from the MoF— are likely to have given many market participants a sense that the central bank will now be working hand in hand with the government to counter the JPY’s slide.

We accordingly see scope for the curve as a whole to face further flattening pressure due to the combination of: (1) a rise in the short-term rates expectation; and (2) a narrowing of the term premium—particularly in the super-long sector—as markets react to the recent weakness in US economic data, resultant declines in UST yields, and also the correction in USD/JPY that has been seen over the past several days.

## G10 | Don't take my breadth away

It works until it doesn't. You hear this phrase a lot in FX and the correlation between USD (and currencies in general) to the equity market sometimes works and sometimes doesn't. The carry regime (regime 2) of our four-regime framework is a clear example of when the DXY historically rises alongside the stock market.

We find that equity market breadth (the amount of stocks moving higher or lower together) plays a big role. When rallies or drawdowns are 'broad', we find that the distribution of currency returns tends to be more intuitive, the moves tend to be larger, and the relationship is more statistically significant. Broad rallies see the largest gains for Nordics and Antipodeans while JPY tends to weaken the most, while broad-based declines see the inverse.

Traditionally risk-sensitive currencies gain (fall) when narrow rallies (declines) take place, but in smaller, less consistent, and less statistically significant magnitudes. European currencies, most noticeably EUR but also CHF and even GBP, tend to lag on both narrow declines and narrow gains, suggesting that European FX should be watched closely and may be an important funder when stock breadth isn't particularly broad.

In theory, a larger equity move should have a larger impact on correlated asset classes. After all, a larger move would suggest a greater change in available information for the market: the more that prices change, the more that life has changed, suggesting that other prices should adjust too.

But there's more than one way to assess the 'information content' in equity moves. Breadth is an important signal too. In this note we explore how the relationship between USD and US equities evolves when we incorporate stock breadth into the picture.

Breadth reflects the amount of stocks in an index that are moving higher or lower. A 'broad' stock index rally (sell-off) means a large portion of stocks are rising (falling), whereas a narrow rally (sell-off) means only a few stocks are rising (falling). Narrow rallies or sell-offs can take place when the small subset of stocks have a large weight in the index, move meaningfully, or both.

The significant variation between index performance and breadth is important. Exhibit 43 shows that only about 70% of S&P 500 performance is explained by stock market breadth.

To examine how stock market breadth impacts the USD relationship to equities, we look at the performance of USD/CCY in the G9 using four subsets of data: periods where the S&P 500 is rising and falling conditioned on periods where stock breadth is broad or narrow.

Broad stock breadth is defined as when the majority of stocks are moving in the same direction as the index relative to the median advance-decline index (of +25). For example, a 'broad' S&P 500 rally would mean at least 275 stocks rose on that day. A 'narrow' rally would mean fewer than 275 stocks rose. For declining days, a 'broad' decline sees fewer than 275 rising.

'Narrow' breadth equity moves see more mixed results, though: In many cases, currency movements are still directionally consistent with what one would expect given the directionality of the equity move, just with more muted magnitudes, such as AUD and SEK.

Similarly, NZD tends to weaken by a similar amount regardless of the breadth during an equity market sell-off. In the case of CAD, we actually see marginal strength even when equities are declining in a narrow breadth environment.

Perhaps most interesting though is Europe. EUR and CHF weaken the most when equity market breadth is narrow regardless of whether it is rising or falling. This pattern is particularly noticeable in periods of narrow equity gains, though. Unlike a broad equity rally which suggests funding in JPY, narrow equity rallies suggest that the clearest funding currency is EUR, followed by CHF and GBP.

The number one question we’ve gotten this week has been around US currency policy. If the US government wanted a weaker USD, could they make it happen? A weaker USD after all probably would help boost US exports all else equal, considering more than 95% of US exports are invoiced in USD.

But the USD’s outlook is driven by domestic policy and forces abroad and the policies investors are discussing as potentially most likely, threaten to bolster, not weaken, the USD.

As noted in Trade, Tariffs, and the US Dollar , rising US tariffs raise the risk of a higher USD by creating asymmetric downside risks to foreign growth. USD weakness tends to be most sustainable when foreign growth is experiencing a cyclical upswing.

Policies which bolster real US interest rates likely enhance the USD’s attractiveness. Higher real rates could come from a few channels: additional fiscal expansion; a higher neutral rate (driven by regulatory changes enhancing productivity); and/or an inflationary impulse prompting greater relative restrictiveness from the Fed. The precise impact on the USD from each of these varies, but the common theme is that it’s a lot harder for the USD to fall.

This isn’t to say a weaker USD is impossible. But arguably the ripest environment for a weaker USD would see the interrelated forces of US rates falling faster than those of its peers, a broad-based risk asset rally, and strong, synchronized growth abroad. Domestic policies which make these harder make USD weakness harder too.

But we shouldn’t underestimate attempts to generate a weaker USD either. We’ve written previously that we’re skeptical that USD-weakening intervention, particularly if unilateral, is likely to be successful in a sustained manner. But that doesn’t mean it can’t happen and, as price action this week reflects, even expressing concern about the USD’s strength can have a negative effect on the greenback as investors price in more risk premia.

We shouldn’t rule out coordinated efforts to weaken the USD either; some foreign trading partners, most notably in Japan but perhaps others in Asia, for example South Korea, might welcome USD weakness and local FX strength.

Finally, some US policies continue to raise concerns about the USD. As we detail here, concerns that the US is using its reserve currency status to pursue foreign policy aims through sanctions and other tools have pushed some investors to search more actively for a US dollar-alternative.

In sum, a weaker USD might be policymakers’ preference, and we think investors shouldn’t be too complacent to the risk that they’ll try, but if other policies make the greenback more attractive, it’ll be difficult to see USD weakness taking place

Until now we have been arguing that BoJ policy actions should have only a limited FX market impact, citing our analysis that shows the market-implied BoJ rate hike trajectory lacking statistically significant explanatory power for fluctuations in the USD/JPY other than for quite limited periods (see Exhibit 73 , "JPY | What’s Been Driving Movements In USD/JPY?"). As we argued in "JPY | Strong Demand For Carry?", this “anomaly” appears primarily attributable to the continuing strength of demand for JPY carry trades despite recent signs of weakness in US economic data, with market participants seemingly placing considerable weight on the Fed’s apparent reluctance to cut and also pricing in increased fiscal stimulus under a “triple red” scenario for November’s US elections.

Demand for JPY carry trades does look to remain strong, but may have been shaken to some modest degree by the recent plunge in USD/JPY as Japanese authorities seemingly sought to take advantage of a dip in the greenback after US CPI inflation came in soft for a second straight month on 11 July.

A similarly sharp fall in USD/JPY was also seen the next day, and subsequent analysis of the difference between tanshi money market brokers’ forecasts for the portion of the change in BoJ current account balances attributable to “Treasury funds and others” and the BoJ’s own projections for the same suggests that the MoF may have spent over JPY 5 trillion shoring up the JPY over the course of 11–12 July.

This action appeared to take market participants by surprise (in contrast to previous interventions) as Japan seemingly looked to amplify existing downward pressure on the greenback rather than preventing a sharp rise in USD/JPY.

USD/JPY then fell further this week in apparent reaction to a Bloomberg interview in which former President Donald Trump expressed concern about the weakness of the JPY as well as the Chinese yuan. As our FX strategist colleagues discussed in "USD: Republican Sweep Would Boost USD", the general consensus has been that a Republican sweep scenario in November would boost the US dollar by fueling expectations of inflation being rekindled by a combination of increased fiscal stimulus, a crackdown on undocumented immigrants, and higher tariffs on trading partners.

And there were also factors on the Japan side, with Minister for Digital Transformation Taro Kono suggesting that he and Trump were basically on the same page with regard to concern about the JPY’s excessive weakness. Pressure to unwind JPY carry trades may thus have been exacerbated by hints that Japan and the US could potentially end up working together to arrest the currency’s slide.

Kono went on to call for a BoJ rate hike aimed at supporting the JPY. Kono is considered to be one of the potential candidates contesting the ruling Liberal Democratic Party’s leadership race in September and is thus likely to have the ear of many market participants.

We thus believe that a July rate hike might very well end up being construed as “evidence” of the MoF and the central bank acting in alliance (irrespective of the BoJ’s actual intentions) and accordingly cause markets to start pricing in a steeper rate hike trajectory going forward.

## A Special Proposal for Repo

The New York Fed released a request for public comment by September 16 on proposed modifications to the calculation methodology of SOFR. The two proposed adjustments to the calculation methodology are to ensure SOFR continues to reflect its underlying interest. Implementation is proposed by 1Q25.

Both proposed changes involve treatment of transactions within the centrally-cleared DVP (bilateral) segment of the repo market. This segment is only used in the SOFR calculation and thus the proposed changes will have no impact on the TGCR or BGCR.

The proposed changes aim to:

• Remove transactions between affiliated entities in centrally-cleared DVP

• Remove a consistent 20% of the lowest-rate transactions from the centrallycleared DVP distribution

Since January 2020, the New York Fed said the indicative SOFR rate with the proposed changes would have been the same “approximately 93 percent over the time”, while volumes would have been similar (but slightly lower) than current volumes. Ultimately, the impact from proposed changes on SOFR depends on the environment for specials. In an environment with high demand for specials, the change would bias SOFR lower, whereas low demand for specials would bias SOFR higher.

Exhibit 94: Spread to Published Rate
![](images/67988b5e762f15ebbbe89ee652d7a79471fc93b1e224b63d218cf8c14f53a987.jpg)
Source: BNY, Federal Reserve, OFR

Exhibit 95: Published Volume vs. New Methodology Volume (30dma)
![](images/3e32f93da615f327b8f9b43cb582d784534b965c02f2f592ab273a732672d908.jpg)
Source: BNY, Federal Reserve, OFR

The rationale for the first change comes from greater transactional detail now available that offers the ability to identify transactions between affiliated entities in centrallycleared DVP repo. There has been limited affiliate trading in central-cleared DVP repo to date and eliminating these trades does not have a notable impact on SOFR when applied to historical data.

Currently, all DVP repo transactions with rates below the 25th volume-weighted percentile rate in the centrally-cleared DVP segment are trimmed from the SOFR calculation. The expansion of central clearing has changed the distribution of DVP activity, with large volumes of transactions occurring at the same or similar rates at specific areas of the broader distribution.

The change in distribution has impacted where the 25th volume-weighted percentile lands in the overall DVP distribution and how much volume occurs at that rate, which impact the SOFR print on occasion. This has led to greater variations in the share of overall DVP activity trimmed from day-to-day: a significant portion of volume at the 25th volumeweighted percentile would not be trimmed but a significant portion of volume below the 25th percentile would be trimmed.

A consistent trim, calibrated to 20% should:

• Eliminate day-to-day variability in the share of DVP activity removed from the calculation

• Result in SOFR published rates, on average, that are in line with the published SOFR rates under the current mechanism

• Effectively balance risks of potential including “specials” activity and remove activity occurring at rates similar to GC repo

• Ensure SOFR remains a robust benchmark in anticipation of more centrally-cleared repo ahead of the June 2026 deadline to comply with the SEC’s rule on central clearing

## Trading Strategy 1 – "Trade Longs/Fade Shorts"

When the 5-day moving average crosses above the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract (long duration) and hold for a 25-business-day period. When the 5-day moving average crosses below the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract and hold for a 25-business-day period. In short, this strategy buys futures when the Simple Moving Average Crossover (SMAX) generates both a long and a short signal, given the historical outperformance of long signals traded long and underperformance of short signals traded short. Given that the SMAX could generate both a long and a short signal within the predefined holding period, an investor may have a 200% long position since each of the two signals would be traded in separate portfolio sleeves.

## Definition of terms

Buy/Long: The analyst expects the total or excess return (depending on the nature of the recommendation) of the instrument or issuer that is the subject of the investment recommendation to be positive over the relevant time period.

Sell/Short: The analyst expects the total or excess return (depending on the nature of the recommendation) of the instrument or issuer that is the subject of the investment recommendation to be negative over the relevant time period.

Selling protection or Buying Risk: The analyst expects that the price of protection against the event occurring will decrease over the relevant time period.

Buying protection or Selling Risk: The analyst expects the price of protection against the event occurring will increase over the relevant time period.

Pay: The analyst expects that over the specified time period the variable rate underlying the swap agreement that is the subject of the investment recommendation will increase.

Receive: The analyst expects that over the specified time period the variable rate underlying the swap agreement that is the subject of the investment recommendation will decrease.

Unless otherwise specified, the time frame for recommendations included in the Morgan

Stanley Fixed Income Research reports is 1 - 3 months and the price of financial instruments mentioned in the recommendation is as at the date and time of publication of the recommendation.

When more than one issuer or instrument is included in a recommendation, analyst expects one part of the trade to outperform the other trade or combination of other trades included in the recommendation on a relative basis.
