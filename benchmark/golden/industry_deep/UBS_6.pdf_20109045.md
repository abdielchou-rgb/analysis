# Global Equity Strategy The UK election: Midcaps, Consumer, Homebuilders

There was no surprise in the result but there are some implications we think.

1) Our economists' view is here. In light of these results, there is an increased risk that fiscal policy could be looser (with lower tax and more public spending), potentially helping GDP growth. Also Labour policies appear much more pro EU, which at the margin is supportive for sterling.

2) Buy small/mid cap. We believe they are not discounting a recovery (that is clearly happening, especially on the FTSE 250 mid-cap PMI UBS has constructed), they are abnormally cheap and benefit if sterling appreciates. Models show double-digit EPS growth for the FTSE 250 ex trusts.

3) Homebuilding. Labour has set a target of 1.5m homes over five years (up from 1.05m in the past five years). Already, lead indicators of housing imply a c5% increase in prices, which homebuilders are discounting with P/B just below normal levels. Our team has Buy ratings on Taylor Wimpey and Bellway. Clearly the brick companies may also benefit from more housing supply (eg, Ibstock).

4) Buy UK retailing. Real wage growth could accelerate even faster under a Labour government (and Sterling could be stronger). Both help support what already looks a compelling story. Housing-related retailer are Kingfisher and low-end retailing B&M if Labour were to raise minimum wages sharply and help gig economy workers.

5) Have to reform public spending. With the tax take at a 70-year high and little room on the fiscal side, the solution is more efficient government spending. This might help the outsourcers Serco and Capita.

6) How to raise more revenue – capital gains tax, less pension subsidies, windfall tax on companies that can’t move, possibly change the accounting of QT to a Fed model.

7) The FTSE 100 remains cheap. The sector-adjusted P/E is on a c20% discount to global markets. Historically the FTSE has been a very defensive market.

## Macro. Risk of stronger growth and sterling.

## Macro. Risk of stronger growth and sterling.

The economists have a note here. In light of these results, there is a risk that fiscal policy becomes easier and the deficit larger, pushing up both GDP growth and gilt yields.

The Institute of Fiscal Studies highlights a black hole of c£20bn if Labour do not cut ‘unprotected’ areas of public spending such as prisons and local government. Moreover, the tax take from closing tax loopholes (which alone was costed to be £5bn) and taxing non-doms and public schools could easily be lower than expected.

A Labour government may also mean establishing closer links and ties to Europe, and thus helping Sterling.

Labour has indicated that it would potentially align itself to European standards for veterinary, chemicals and even recognition of professional qualifications, for example.

## 3) The consumer, especially the low-end consumer.

It is quite likely that the minimum wage continues to rise faster than consumption, and Labour has said that within 100 days it will announce enhanced protection for gig economy workers. There is a scenario that a Labour government might pay compensation for both the Post Office Workers and victims of the NHS Blood Scandal quite quickly (where payments could amount to £20bn, or 1.5% of consumption). We wrote about the UK consumer in our recent note, see here.

Figure 17: UK real wage growth
![](images/3b40f720f888bd5b67874df0fce6f0bec08a174e9048294fa871fcf50acd43f0.jpg)
Source: UBS, Refinitiv Datastream

Real wage growth is rising very sharply (it is currently 4%). This is driven by strong nominal wage growth (6% against headline inflation of 2%), while utility bills have fallen around 40% from the previously capped level. Disposable income is even higher when also factoring in 4% in National Insurance tax cuts for workers earnings under £50,000 (most). If we also get interest rates coming down in the near future, as we expect, borrowers should find their disposable income rising further. The Bank of England highlights that 1% off rates adds c0.5% to consumption).

A Labour government might also be more generous to public sector workers who continue to strike for more pay in various large sectors including the NHS and education.

## 4) Carbon prices.

Potentially, the Labour Party could align itself with the EU Carbon Adjustment Mechanism. The UK CABM scheme is due to start a year later than that of Europe (and hence the UK would risk becoming a dumping ground for carbon-intensive products). The price of carbon is below that of Europe. A higher price of carbon would help the renewable generators. A higher carbon price might also help the ‘green’ credentials of Labour, which have come under pressure as the party sharply revised down green spending from £28bn to c£5bn. This, according to our utilities team, helps SSE, Orsted and Iberdrola, in that order.

## 5) Transmission and distribution

Labour has said it would speed up the approval and transmission and distribution, and update the NSIP (Nationally Important Infrastructure Plans), originally brought in by Labour in 2008. This would help National Grid's RAB growth.

## 6) What might fill the tax black hole?

The Institute of Fiscal Studies highlights that there is likely to be a black hole (that could be c£20bn, see here) if the Labour manifesto is implemented. Labour has indeed committed not to increase income, corporate, VAT or national insurance (which account for nearly 80% of total tax). There is also a commitment to reduce government debt as a share of GDP by year 5, which may be hard to keep, given its public spending commitments (especially with commitments to spend more on childcare and schools, and £5bn on green investments).

## Potential windfall taxes?

i) The tax on North Sea oil has been well flagged (see this FT article) but one could not rule out the chance of a windfall tax on banks in some disguise.

ii) A sharp rise in the capital gains tax to be closer aligned to income tax.

iii) A sharp reduction in pension tax credit hurting the fund management and life industry.

iv) A form of wealth tax by, for example, rebanding council tax bands.

One possible solution would be to account for the losses imposed by QT on the Treasury in the same way as the Fed. This could raise significant sums (see this FT article).

## 7) Outsourcing

To quote the words of likely incoming Chancellor Rachel Reeves, "borrowing more is not an alternative because debt as a share of GDP is the highest it has been….[taxing more was also] not an alternative because the tax is already at a 70-year high". The solution in part has to be to have more efficient public spending. This could potentially involve more outsourcing. This might help the likes of Capita and Serco.
