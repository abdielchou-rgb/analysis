# European Economic Comment Switzerland: PMI recovery stalls, FX sales stop

## PMIs and KOF indicator diverge in January, SNB FX estimates for December out

As Eurozone growth has slightly surprised to the downside and CHF strength is turning into a renewed headwind, manufacturing activity in Switzerland remains depressed. Meanwhile, the more domestic-oriented services sector is holding up relatively well. Below, we review the January readings of the KOF indicator and the PMIs in more detail and discuss implications. We also look at the SNB's balance sheet data and conclude that FX sales stopped in December. For an overview of the latest data, see our Switzerland heatmap at the end of the document (Figure 16Switzerland hmp).

## Swiss Manufacturing PMI unchanged in the face of CHF strength

The Swiss Manufacturing PMI stayed practically unchanged at 43.1 points in January (after 43.0), below consensus of an increase to 44.5 points (Figure 1). That's despite longer supplier delivery times related to the Red Sea disruptions pushing the PMI higher by 1.2 points; with constant delivery times, the PMI would have been lower at 41.9 points. The output component fell by 4.5 points, back to its August 2023 level of 39.1, whereas the backlog of orders component saw its third consecutive increase (40.8, +1.0pts). Supplier delivery times rose by 8.2 points to 45.9, which is elevated but still significantly below the levels observed during the pandemic when they had reached 88.7 points at the peak. The purchase price component rose by 5 points to 47, which might reflect rising transportation costs but could also be a reversal of last month's fall. With the January print below 50, the Swiss Manufacturing PMI has now been in contractionary territory for more than a year, and the overall reading does not point to an imminent recovery of manufacturing activity.

The Swiss export industry is closely linked to German manufacturing, such that developments in Germany somewhat lead Swiss manufacturing momentum (Figure 2). Although the overall ifo index and the German Composite PMI fell in January, the respective manufacturing components showed signs of improvement. The German Manufacturing PMI increased in January beyond the effect of supplier delivery times, and manufacturing sentiment in the ifo index was also up. This has not translated into an improvement in the Swiss Manufacturing PMI thus far. One reason could be the recent CHF strength. The real effective exchange rate index has appreciated by c.5% from mid-November to the end of 2023. While much of the c15% nominal appreciation since the start of the SNB's tightening cycle had been largely offset by higher inflation abroad and did not harm the competitiveness of Swiss companies as much, the recent real appreciation is increasingly turning into an additional challenge. FX headwinds were mentioned in almost half of the Q4 earnings reports of Swiss companies under UBS coverage published thus far.

## Service resilience continues

Contrasting the ongoing weakness in manufacturing, the more domestic-oriented Services PMI continues to signal solid momentum above the growth threshold, albeit slower than in December. The index fell by 2.4 points to 54.6 and partially reversed last month's jump. The new order component fell by 7.8 points (to 53.8), which might indicate a deterioration in business activity over the next months. That said, the overall reading still remains at a relatively high level last seen at the beginning of 2023 and points to ongoing service resilience.

## SNB stopped FX sales in December

Following substantial FX sales through November (at an average pace of c.11bn per month) to strengthen the CHF and counter inflation, the SNB announced in its December meeting that it would no longer focus on FX sales, although it still signalled a willingness "to be active in the foreign exchange market as necessary" (Figure 13).

Yesterday's release of monthly SNB balance sheet data indicates that FX sales indeed stopped in December. Our estimates (see Figure 11 and footnote for details) even show a marginal uptick for December, which could theoretically imply that the SNB might have purchased FX in modest amounts (CHF 1-2.5 bn, depending on the estimation model) to counter CHF strength towards the end of last year. While this may fuel the debate about whether the SNB would respond to a stronger CHF first with rate cuts or with interventions, it should be noted that the correlation between our early balance sheet data estimates and the official intervention data (published with a lag) was not perfect in the past (see Figure 12).

Going forward, we would expect FX interventions to become less one-sided than in the past 1.5 years and would not exclude that the SNB also purchases FX, at least temporarily, should it be concerned about CHF strength. This would be in line with its December communication that it remains willing to intervene if necessary. In recent comments, Chairman Jordan noted that the CHF strength at the start of the year was dampening inflation and also seemed concerned that the increase in the real effective CHF was becoming a headwind for Swiss companies.

## What to watch: inflation, labour market, SNB balance sheet data

Inflation numbers for January published on 13 February (we expect 1.5% y/y) should give more clarity regarding price pressures. We will also watch how PMI employment trends will translate into short-time work and unemployment and if our expectation of a moderate softening holds; the labour market report for January will be published on 7 February. Q4 GDP data released on 29 February will show if hard data confirms the momentum improvement that leading indicators showed at the end of last year. And SNB balance sheet data for January (published on 29 February) might give more indication about SNB FX interventions in light of CHF strength.
