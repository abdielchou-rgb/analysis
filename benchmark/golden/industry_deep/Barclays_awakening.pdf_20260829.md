## US Credit Alpha

## Overview

Following several months of calm, spreads have been sharply wider over the past week, albeit well of the worst levels, amid slowdown concerns and positioning unwinds. We see few signs of true credit stress, but a further decline in yields could pressure spreads further.

## A rude awakening

Volatility returned with a vengeance. Markets had been fairly orderly for much of the past few months, with credit spreads staying in a narrow range (IG and HY cash spreads were in a 10bp and 40bp range, respectively, from mid-March to July) and the S&P drifting higher with limited draw-downs. That changed as the calendar turned to August, starting with the weaker-thanexpected ISM manufacturing employment data on the 1<sup>st</sup> and continuing the next day with disappointing nonfarm payrolls data. Equities and Treasury yields were down sharply at the end of last week, and IG and HY cash spreads sufered their worst weekly widening since March 2023. We think that the decline in Treasury yields in particular helped stoke slowdown and recession concerns, as the 40bp weekly decline in 10y yields was the largest since December 2008.

While the disappointing data were likely a factor in the sell-of, we think other developments and stretched positioning in some asset classes (but not in corporate cash) contributed as well, especially the sharp appreciation of the yen, which may have led to an unwind of some carry trades. In addition, the decline in risk assets and expansion of volatility likely drove risk reduction by some systematic investors (CTAs and vol control, in particular), which may have increased the severity of the moves.

This week’s price action has been characterized by large intraday ranges, with the major equity indices still down for the week as of midday Thursday, while credit has fared better, with the CDX indices unchanged and cash spreads tighter. Macro data may have ofset some of the prior week's concerns, as the ISM services employment gauge showed expansion and initial jobless claims were lower than expected. At the same time, dovishness from the BOJ in the middle of the week may have allayed fears of further yen appreciation, at least in the near term. Treasury yields have also retraced part of the prior week's decline, with 10y yields more than 20bp higher and back near 4%.

Amid the increased volatility, rate cut expectations have been shifting quickly. On July 31, following the FOMC rate decision, markets were pricing in fewer than three 25bp cuts in the fed funds rate by year-end. This jumped to 5.5 cuts early Monday morning, as the price action felt somewhat panicky (S&P futures were down 250 handles and CDX.IG was 7bp wider), but it has since declined to about four cuts. Barclays economists now expect three cuts, versus two previously (see Change in Fed call: Three 25bp cuts this year). With rate cuts on the horizon, we discuss in this week's high yield and loans section why we think it will take at least 3-4 Fed cuts in the next 12 months for HY bonds to outperform loans.

Despite the large moves, credit markets are displaying resilience. The widening in cash spreads from July 31-August 5 was certainly significant - IG spreads were 18bp wider and HY spreads 67bp wider - but both markets had retraced a third to a half of this as of Wednesday's close, and both were sharply tighter as of midday Thursday. Anecdotally, when we spoke with a number of credit investors early in the week, most were cautious, but their main consideration was not whether to step in to buy, but when and how.

Beyond spread moves and conversations with investors, we think that the ability of issuers to come to market is a clear indication of whether there is stress or apprehension on the part of investors. Through Wednesday's close, investment grade supply was more than \$38bn, with nearly \$32bn of that coming on Wednesday alone, the highest one-day total of the year. Deals on Wednesday were nearly 5x oversubscribed, and new issue concessions were less than 10bp, which we view as a healthy demand backdrop.

The recent price action has been constructive, but historically, shocks such as this have typically led to a weaker market in the near term if the issue is not quickly circumscribed, which is something we look at more closely in the investment grade section. The risk is that the US economy is slowing too fast and spreads are pricing in only about a 10% chance of recession. In that respect, the CPI and retail sales numbers next week are likely to be key for short-term market direction.

The retracement in spreads over the past few days and the strong level of demand has certainly been encouraging, but our main concern is the level of yields. Much of the demand for investment grade has come from yield-focused buyers, and all-in yields dipped below the psychologically important 5% level this week for the first time since February. They have rebounded since, but lower yields could pose a risk to spreads, from a demand standpoint and in terms of the potential for increased supply.

Shocks like this are rare and typically lead to a weaker market in the near term. Since the GFC, this is only the sixth instance of a widening of at least 17bp in three days after a sustained period of range-bound spreads. In the following week, spreads tend to remain flat, so long as the event that sparked the sell-of is not sustained (eg, the European sovereign crisis/COVID).

Credit signals do not point to a recession. Investment grade spreads remain tight relative to their long-term history, even after the recent widening, and are pricing in only about a 10% probability of a recession (Figure 3). Earnings season has been fine, but mixed. Revenue beats have been on the low side of the long-term range at 48%. However, 81% of companies have beaten on EPS, the second best figure in the past ten quarters. Furthermore, Barclays' fundamental analysts' earnings scores do not signal any call for concern.

However, lower yields present a problem for IG. Much of the year-to-date strength in investment grade spreads has been derived from yield buyers, with spreads an afterthought. Demand could weaken with all-in yields dipping below the psychologically important 5% level, as they did this week for the first time since February. Lower yields may also encourage an influx of supply, creating another technical headwind.

We highlight relative value opportunities given the recent moves. We prefer single-As to BBBs given the historical compression in their ratio and increased uncertainty on the economic outlook. In industrials, we prefer non-cyclicals. Note that the largest underperformers over the past week have been cyclical sectors such as airlines, gaming, and autos. In financials, we prefer the Big 6 to Yankees, as the Yankee-Big 6 spread ratio has reached multi-year tights. In hybrids, we continue to favor corporate hybrids over bank preferreds, and if volatility stays elevated, longer-duration, higher-reset securities should outperform.

In fact, this is only the sixth time since the GFC (Figure 1). In those cases, the median spread move was +5bp one week forward and +33bp one month forward. However, spreads were essentially flat the following week in three of the five instances, and they were tighter in the last two episodes.

The diference between these outcomes seems to be what caused the sudden sell-of and whether it was quickly circumscribed. The times spreads continued to widen were driven by situations that took time to be resolved. For instance, the 2010 sell-of was driven by the European sovereign crisis, and the 2011 widening was sparked by the US sovereign downgrade, combined with another bout of European sovereign crisis. Finally, 2020 was driven by COVID (Figure 2).

By contrast, the situation did not meaningfully degenerate in the 2022 and 2023 episodes. In the first case, the start of the Ukraine war surprised the market and pushed spreads wider. But the market quickly decided that this did not represent a massive threat to the US economy. Furthermore, the market quickly turned its attention to the Fed liftof that took place only a couple of weeks after the sudden widening. The 2023 episode was linked to regional bank concerns and Credit Suisse. In that case, central banks intervened quickly to contain the problem, which readily reassured the markets.

The drivers of the current situation appear to be the BoJ's surprised hike, stretched positioning in some asset classes (but not credit), and weak data suggesting that the US economy may be slowing too quickly. On the positive side, the BoJ tried to reassure the markets this week, and positioning seems to have at least partially corrected itself in the sell-of. However, the situation with the US economy is not yet clear, and there are diverging views about recession risk. Barclays Economics believes that a recession is unlikely, but the market is likely to wait for more data before taking a stance. CPI and retail sales next week will be important.

## But lower yields present a problem

US investment grade yields dipped below 5% this week, the lowest level since February 2023. Even after the rebound in Treasury yields, US IG yields are almost 50bp lower since July 1. With support for the IG market coming from many types of yield-focused buyers, lower all-in yields likely contributed to the backup in spreads even before the payrolls print. The four-week correlation of weekly IG spread changes and 10y UST yields is -0.98 (Figure 7). Anecdotally, client conversations indicate that 4.75-5.0% is where flows might become more of a drag on the market.

Supply might stay elevated given the relatively low yields, which likely prevent spreads from tightening meaningfully in the near term. Investment grade issuers tapped the primary market for \$35bn between Tuesday and Wednesday after the market turned more constructive. The new issues were well received, with concessions hovering around 8bp and

oversubscriptions around 5-6x, and they have continued to trade well in the secondary market. These are encouraging signs that demand is still strong and buyers have not been scared away by recent fluctuations.

With yields near 5%, we think more issuers might pull issuance forward to next week and the first weeks of September. Wednesday was the largest single day of primary activity so far this year, and weekly supply through Thursday met dealer estimates despite a blank session on Monday (Figure 8).

High Yield and Leveraged Loans

# Cuts are coming – Now what?

We think the time is right to rotate back into duration, since Fed cuts appear imminent and loans typically underperform high yield in cutting cycles. Loan funds are already experiencing large outflows, but we do not see a strong relationship between fund flows and forward returns.

For our latest US high yield company rating changes, click here. For our sector ratings, click here.

With the market expecting the Fed to start cutting in September, we think this is a good time to add duration. Many investors have been overweight loans because of their superior carry, but loans usually underperform high yield in cutting cycles.

Loan underperformance is not drastic, however. In most cases, the bulk of the underperformance occurs in the three months after the first cut, then loan returns "catch up" over the longer term.

We think it will take three to four Fed cuts in the next 12 months for high yield to outperform loans. Given that Barclays' economists forecast six between now and September 2025, high yield outperformance seems likely.

Loan retail funds tend to see large outflows during cutting cycles (this week had a record \$3.1bn of outflows). However, fund flows are fairly uncorrelated with performance in loans, likely because retail is a fairly small fraction of the loan ownership base compared with CLOs.

## We think it will take 3-4 cuts in the next 12 months for loans to underperform

Although high yield almost always outperforms loans in a cutting cycle, the record-low index duration (Figure 5) may be a headwind. A shorter index duration means that, all else being equal, a reduction in base rates will result in smaller price appreciation than in prior cycles. On top of that, though defaults have been more contained in high yield than in loans this cycle, any relief from lower base rates will naturally come to issuers of floating-rate paper before issuers of fixed-rate paper. Both factors are headwinds for high yield going into the upcoming round of cuts.

Having said that, we can estimate the number of rate cuts needed for duration to outperform. Using the methodology outlined in The Fed's efect on bond-loan relative value (June 7, 2024), we model bond and loan forward 12-month returns under various cutting scenarios. For each move in the Fed funds rate, we shock the Treasury curve using historical betas observed in previous cutting cycles<sup>2</sup> and measure the efect of that rate change on forward returns. We also shock the high yield index spread and loan dollar price by various amounts to model changes in the credit risk premium. The result is a matrix of potential forward returns for each Fed cut scenario. For examples illustrating both a low number of cuts and a high number of cuts, Figure 6 and Figure 7 show the expected forward returns under various high yield spread and loan dollar price scenarios under two and six Fed cuts in the next year, respectively. If inflation prints get hotter and expectations for rate cuts go down, we would expect loans to continue to outpace bonds. If we get several Fed cuts, however, duration should, of course, outperform.

## Credit Derivatives and Macro Macro credit refresh

In light of the recent volatility, we update our macro credit views. We still like CDX HY/IG decompression, think Main-IG should tighten as volatility subsides, prefer credit ETFs over CDX for long-risk, and think CDX.IG options are attractive for expressing long-risk views or to generate carry.

Risk assets remained volatile this week, oscillating between gains and losses amid economic concerns, potential carry trade unwinds, de-risking by systematic investors, perceived BoJ dovishness, and a weak 10-year Treasury auction (among other factors).

Following the recent moves, we take a fresh look at the macro credit landscape, with our thoughts summarized below:

• CDX HY versus IG: we still favor decompression for the longer term, while near-term moves will be driven by positioning.

• Main versus IG: a reduction in volatility should drive the relationship tighter.

• CDX versus credit ETFs: we prefer longs in cash/ETFs over CDX, but a further decline in rates could cause cash to underperform.

• CDX.IG implied volatility: for those that are bullish, elevated volatility and steep skew make structures such as bullish risk reversals an attractive way to position for a rally, while straddles appear attractive from a carry perspective.

## CDX versus credit ETFs: prefer longs in ETFs

Credit ETFs underperformed CDX significantly in the initial part of the sell-of. The dislocation was especially pronounced in the high yield market, with HYG spreads closing more than five standard deviations wide versus their one-year relationship with CDX.HY spreads on August 5 (Figure 3 and Figure 4). Since then however, ETF spreads (and the broader cash market) have outperformed, but spreads still appear wide on a one-year lookback.

We think that part of the initial underperformance can be explained by the large move lower in rates last week, as cash spreads and rates have historically been negatively correlated. In terms of the relative value implications of lower rates, looking at the past three years of weekly changes in the CDX.HY-HYG spread basis and 5y Treasury yields, we find that there has been a tendency for the basis to decrease (CDX.HY outperforming) in weeks when 5y yields move significantly lower, and for the basis to increase (HYG outperforming) when 5y yields move significantly higher (Figure 5).

With ETF spreads still appearing wide, we have a preference for expressing longs in ETFs (consistent with our view published on July 12 in Long HYG versus short CDX.HY), and think that stable or higher rates should support ETF and cash spread outperformance. However, a large decline in rates would pose a risk to this trade, in our view.

## CDX.IG implied volatility: elevated vols make bullish risk reversals and straddles attractive

Amid the sell-of, CDX.IG three-month ATM implied vol has spiked to a new one-year high (Figure 6) and three-month skew has steepened to the highest level of the past year (Figure 7). For investors that are bullish, we think that this presents an opportunity to add risk via bullish risk reversals. For example, buying the CDX.IG October 55bp-70bp bullish risk reversal (ref 58bp) is costless and has an attractive risk-reward profile as the trade is selling a payer that is 12bp outof-the-money to buy a receiver that is only 3bp out-of-the-money.

Investors that are looking for ways to generate carry can also consider selling straddles or strangles to take advantage of the high level of implied vol. We monitor three-month straddle breakevens in the weekly CDS index option dashboard, and as of Wednesday's (7 August) close, the breakeven range for a three-month CDX.IG straddle was nearly 27bp (Figure 8), which puts it at the 91st percentile of the past year.
