## China

China’s growth slowed sequentially in Q4, with prices continuing to face downward pressure from the property sector downturn and insufficient recovery in aggregate demand. Consumer sentiment suffered a slight setback in Q4, but consumption was still the main growth driver in 2023.

n Judging from the mixed message in December data, we think China's economy may have passed another near-term trough and it is on track to improve in the next few quarters on the back of incremental policy support.

n The fiscal stance is key with the budgetary deficit being 1.5% to 2% of GDP more expansionary this year and many investment projects likely to be implemented in the first half. Total fiscal spending growth will likely rise to 8% in 2024. Monetary policy is expected to accommodate fiscal expansion; a RRR cut will likely come soon. We continue to expect a total of 45 bps MLF rate cuts in 2024 but now expect the first rate cut to happen in March.

We forecast that China’s sequential growth will likely rebound in the first 2 quarters of 2024. We reiterate our growth forecast of 4.7% in 2024, with quarterly growth forecasts of 1.3%, 1.3%, 1.2%, and 1.1%, respectively, for Q1-Q4 2024.

## Passing another near-term trough?

China’s growth slowed sequentially in Q4. Real GDP growth grew by 5.2% YoY in Q4, in line with our expectations and slightly lower than the market consensus forecast of 5.3%. Sequential growth slowed again to 1% QoQ in Q4 from 1.3% QoQ in Q3, albeit still better than the 0.5% QoQ growth in Q2. Deflationary pressures remained, as the GDP deflator stayed negative for a third consecutive quarter at -1.4%, as prices continued to face downward pressure from the property sector downturn and insufficient recovery in aggregate demand. Hence, nominal GDP growth slowed to only 3.7% in Q4 2023 and 4.2% for 2023 full year. In fact, nominal GDP annual growth in 2023 was even lower than its 4.8% growth in 2022, despite a rebound in real GDP growth from 3% in 2022 to 5.2% in 2023. The drop in nominal GDP growth is bad news for top-line and bottom-line growth of Chinese companies, eroding their profit margins and willingness to invest and expand.

Consumer sentiment suffered a slight setback in Q4, but consumption was still the main growth driver in 2023. Household spending growth dropped by almost 2ppts to 9.1% YoY in Q4 from 10.9% in Q3. This is despite a small improvement in income growth, to 6.4% YoY in Q4 from 5.9% YoY in Q3. The household savings rate, on a seasonally adjusted basis, has suffered a setback, rising again to 33% in Q4 from 30% in Q3. For the full year 2023, the household savings rate is at 31.7%, coming down from 33.5% in 2022 and just passing half way in its return to the pre-Covid savings rate of 30% in 2019. However, consumption's contribution to GDP growth was 4.3ppt (out of 5.2) in 2023. Net exports have been a big drag on growth (-0.6ppt) in 2023, mostly in Q2 and Q3 with some improvement observed in Q4. Investment was disappointing in 2023, especially in the second half of the year, as property investment continued to shrink amid a prolonged housing downturn.

## The property sector has not recovered despite further easing in major cities.

Shenzhen has intensified its easing policies in November, followed by Beijing in December and then Shanghai in January, in the form of relaxing purchasing eligibility, reducing down-payment requirements and transaction taxes. However, housing prices of both new homes and second-hand homes have been falling and saw their largest monthly drop since 2015 in December. Additionally, various indicators related to the property sector renewed their historical lows in December. Real estate investment, property sales value and housing construction starts declined to 70%, 56% and 37% respectively compared to their levels in 2019.

Other economic activities showed uneven recoveries in December. Retail sales, services output and fixed asset investment improved marginally, while industrial production deteriorated. The labor market also gave mixed messages. The headline urban unemployment rate rose by 0.1ppt to 5.1%. Average weekly working hours also rose by another 0.1 hours, reaching a historical high of 49 hours/week. But active migrant workers increased by another 0.7 million in Q4, and their wages have also increased by 1.9% QoQ. This suggests the economy continues to create more jobs for lower-income groups.

We think China's economy may have passed another near-term trough… Monetary and property policy changes led to the improvement Q3 2023; this time the expected improvement will be largely owing to a fiscal stimulus through infrastructure investment and social housing projects. Some signs of improvement were observed in December from a pick-up in non-property investment. Export growth also turned positive again in December, which suggests that external demand may also become less of a drag in the near term.

… and it is on track to improve in the next few quarters on the back of incremental policy support. Given that the economy is still lacking endogenous momentum, a positive shock through forceful and coordinated policy responses will be necessary to jump-start the economy.

We think the starting point will be a more expansionary fiscal stance. On the budgetary deficit, it is likely be 1.5% to 2% of GDP more expansionary in 2024. We had thought that the government's 2024 budgetary deficit would be set at 3.8% of GDP; instead, it seems the government will reduce its budgetary deficit to 3% of GDP, and issue another CNY 1tr of ultra-long special bonds. The 2 items combined would still be 3.8% of GDP. In addition, we expect extra fiscal stimulus from discretionary items: (1) the carryover of CNY 1tr additional CGB issued in 2023; and (2) the utilization of a total of CNY 1tr in funding from the PBOC's PSL facility for public housing and urban redevelopment projects. If the above assumptions are correct, total fiscal spending growth will surge to 8% in 2024, from average annual growth of 0.2% in the past 3 years. This will have significant implications for growth. We estimate that China's fiscal multiplier has been around 1.3 in recent years. We thus expect that China's economy will likely strengthen in 2024.

Fiscal expansion also has important implications for monetary policy. China's money market and interbank borrowing rates have tightened since October, on the back of increased government bond issuances. The PBOC injected almost CNY 2tr of liquidity through its MLF operations, but the gap between short-term rates and the PBOC's OMO rate remains wide. The PBOC will need to provide more long-term, stable funding into the financial system to accommodate additional government financing in 2024. In this context, one possible explanation of why the PBOC did not cut rates earlier this week is that the PBOC may want to bring market rates down closer to its policy rates before cutting rates further. If this interpretation is correct, (1) we should expect the PBOC to cut the RRR soon, potentially before the Chinese New Year, to ease bank liquidity conditions. (2) In addition, the PBOC may also need to think how to accommodate the potential issuance of CNY1tr ultra-long term bonds, given that banks might not have the appetite to significantly increase their holding of assets with such long maturities. Potentially, the PBOC may need to find ways to provide liquidity to long-term asset managers, such as insurance companies. (3) PBOC's rate cuts will likely be delayed to later this year. We continue to expect a total of 45 bps MLF rate cuts in 2024 but now expect the first rate cut to likely happen in March, after the government's budget is announced at the National People's Congress, followed by 2 more rate cuts in Q2 and Q3, respectively.

We forecast China’s sequential growth will likely rebound in the first 2 quarters of 2024. We reiterate our growth forecast of 4.7% in 2024, with quarterly growth forecasts at 1.3%, 1.3%, 1.2%, and 1.1%, respectively, for Q1-Q4 2024.

## India

Kaushik Das, Chief Economist, India

n We expect the central government to meet the FY24 fiscal deficit target of 5.9% of GDP. Once the general elections are behind us in May, we expect fiscal consolidation to pick up pace, with the central government’s fiscal deficit likely to narrow to 5.3% of GDP in FY25 and finally to 4.5% of GDP in FY26, as per the medium-term macro framework.

n CPI inflation surprised to the downside in Nov and Dec and is likely to moderate to about 5.0% average in 1H24. Core inflation has fallen below 4% in Dec and is likely to average around 3.7% 1H24.

n We stick with our view that the RBI will likely embark on a rate cut cycle starting from June 2024, converging with the Fed rate cut cycle. We expect 75bps of rate cuts in 2024, starting from June 2024, and another 25bps in 1Q25, resulting in 100bps of total rate cuts through FY25.

## Political and fiscal outlook

India’s all-important general elections are likely to be held in April/May 2024. Latest Opinion Polls (ABP-Cvoter Survey, 25 Dec 2023) and the results of five recentlyconducted state elections (in which BJP won three states) suggest that the incumbent Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP) and its alliance partners (National Democratic Alliance: NDA) under the leadership of PM Modi are likely to gain a majority in the Lok Sabha (Lower House) once again and form the government at the centre for the third term. Our forecasts build in political stability over the shortto medium-term, which is positive for the ongoing reforms agenda and the structural outlook of the Indian economy.

## FY25 Union Budget preview

India’s FY25 Union Budget, which will be announced on 1st February, will attract a lot of attention from different stakeholders in the economy, even though this will be an interim budget before the April/May general elections in 2024. The fiscal authorities are not likely to make any major policy announcement in the interim budget; we will have to wait for such an announcement in the fully-fledged budget, which will be presented post elections. As is always the case, the formulation of the FY25 Union Budget will need to be a fine balancing act. On one hand, growth momentum will need to get continued support from fiscal levers, particularly with a global recession looming on the horizon, but at the same time, there will be expectations for the government to aim for continued fiscal consolidation, so as to not crowd out private investments and to be able to bring down the centre’s fiscal deficit to 4.5% of GDP by FY26, as per the proposed glide path.

Despite elections in April/May 2024, we expect the central government to meet the current year's fiscal deficit target of 5.9% of GDP. Once the general elections are behind us, we expect fiscal consolidation to pick up pace, with the centre’s fiscal deficit likely to narrow to 5.3% of GDP in FY25 and finally to 4.5% of GDP in FY26, as per the medium-term macro framework (see this report for details).

The recent news, of the JP Morgan Global Bond Index team including India in the GBI-EM Global Series starting June 2024, is indeed positive, as this will be a welcome source of incremental demand for local currency government bonds, which should bring about USD25bn inflows in a staggered manner through FY25. During this period, there will be incremental increases of % in India's index weighting, ultimately reaching the maximum allocation of 10% by March 2025. This will lead to a fundamental change in India’s fixed Income market, with demand-supply dynamics of bonds improving in FY25.

The offshore holding of Indian bonds (which remains below 2% of total outstanding local currency bonds) should increase, because of the index inclusion, which will reduce the pressure on banks (currently holding 38.0% of outstanding bonds) and RBI (RBI currently holds about 13.1% of local currency bonds) on the margin to buy bonds. Overall, India's inclusion in the global bond index will help reduce the cost of capital in the economy with a lag.

## Key areas of the FY25 budget arithmetic to focus on:

1. Nominal GDP growth: As per Deutsche Bank (DB) estimates, India’s real GDP is likely to grow 6.3%yoy in FY25, with nominal GDP growth likely to rise to 11.0%yoy, from about 9%yoy in FY24. In FY25, the GDP deflator will normalize, as WPI and CPI inflation will converge and average between 4.5-5.0%. We expect the authorities to use an 11.0%yoy nominal GDP growth assumption for calculating the FY25 fiscal ratios. In FY24, nominal GDP was estimated to grow by 10.5%yoy for budget calculation purposes, but recently the CSO has estimated nominal GDP growth to be lower at 8.9%yoy for FY24.

2. Revenue and expenditure growth assumption for FY25: On the back of a strong revenue growth outturn in FY24, even after accommodating for a lower nominal GDP growth, overall revenue collection will likely moderate to about 11.2%yoy, in line with the expected nominal GDP growth assumption. This, in our view, will be a realistic target, and will be seen as a credible estimate. With the expenditure base already having risen significantly in the last few years to support growth and accommodate higher subsidies, we think total expenditure growth will be estimated around 6.8%yoy in FY25.

3. Fiscal deficit likely to be targeted at 5.3% of GDP in FY25, 60bps below the likely 5.9% of GDP outturn in FY24: Our revenue and expenditure assumptions yield a fiscal deficit target of 5.3% of GDP for FY25, with total revenue/GDP rising to 9.4% of GDP (from 9.3% of GDP in FY24) and expenditure/GDP moderating to about 14.7% (from 15.2% of GDP in FY24). Once elections get out of the way, the pace of fiscal consolidation will likely increase, to achieve the objective of pushing the centre’s fiscal deficit down to 4.5% by FY26. The consolidated fiscal deficit is likely to come down to 8.1% of GDP in FY25 (from 8.7% of GDP in FY23), assuming a state fiscal deficit at 2.8% of GDP in FY24 and FY25.

4. Capital expenditure: The strategy of boosting on-budget capital expenditure is likely to be continued (we expect INR11 trn allocation for FY25 vs. FY24 at INR10 trn), which will in turn help the private sector investment cycle to gain traction, with capacity utilization on the path of recovery.

5. Quality of fiscal spending: An indicator to track in this regard is the revenue deficit/fiscal deficit ratio (lower the ratio, the better). Since FY17 (which recorded a low of 59.1%), this ratio moved up to a peak of nearly 80% in FY21, but it is encouraging to note that it has improved once again to 49% by FY24 and is likely to fall further to 41.5% by FY25, a record low. We expect the authorities to aim at keeping this ratio as low as possible, by continuing to boost capital expenditure while reducing revenue expenditure, particularly subsidies, as soon as the global geopolitical situation improves.

6. Small savings: In the last few years, India's dependence on funding its fiscal deficit through small savings has increased significantly, with a commensurate fall in net market borrowing. For FY23 and FY24, small savings have helped finance 25-26% of fiscal deficit. For FY24 also, we expect the ratio to remain the same.

## Inflation, growth and monetary policy outlook

Dec CPI inflation came in at 5.7%yoy, lower than the Bloomberg consensus estimate of 5.9%yoy. Even in Nov, CPI inflation had surprised to the downside, with the actual outturn being lower than the Bloomberg consensus estimate by about 23bps (5.5%yoy actual vs. expectations of 5.8%yoy). The Dec data constituted a -0.3%mom decline in headline CPI vs. +0.5%mom in Nov. Despite the drop in momentum, the Dec year-on-year inflation rate inched up due to an unfavorable base. The downside surprise was led by softer-than-expected momentum in the case of both food (-0.7%mom; 8.7%yoy) and core CPI (+0.03%mom; 3.76%yoy) prices. We note that CPI ex vegetables moderated to 4.5%yoy in Dec, compared to 4.8%yoy in Nov, 5.0%yoy in Oct and 5.1%yoy in Sep.

With the Dec print, CPI averaged 5.37% in Oct-Dec'23 vs. RBI's latest forecast of 5.6%. With the base effect becoming more favorable from Jan and as vegetable prices continue to fall during the winter months aided by the arrival of new crops, CPI inflation will likely moderate to 4.9-5.0% average in Jan-March'24, as per our current forecast (RBI's forecast is slightly higher at 5.2%).

December core CPI inflation (RBI definition: CPI ex food and fuel) moderated further to 3.76%yoy (+0.03%mom), lower than the Nov outturn of 4.1%yoy (+0.25%mom), with the month-on-month momentum being weaker than our expectations (+0.3%mom), leading to a lower headline CPI print as well. In Dec, as is the usual trend, housing prices declined -0.6%mom, which pulled core inflation momentum lower. While this was along expected lines, services inflation only rose by +0.22%mom, which was lower than our expectation of +0.5%mom, leading to the overall downside surprise in core CPI. On a quarterly basis, core CPI inflation is likely to average around 3.7% 1H'24, compared to a 4.0% average in Oct-Dec'23, a 4.8% average in July-Sep'23, 5.1% in April-June'23 and 6%+ in FY23. In FY24, we are forecasting core inflation to average about 4.4% and moderate further to a 4.0% average in FY25, lower than our CPI estimate of 4.6%.

Post the December CPI print, we have revised down our FY24 CPI inflation forecast to 5.3%, from 5.4% earlier. Currently the RBI's forecast is at 5.4%. We expect CPI inflation to moderate to about 5.1% average in 2HFY24 vs. 5.5% in 1HFY24 under our base case scenario. As per our current forecasts, we see CPI inflation averaging around 5.0% in 1H'2024. In FY25, we are forecasting CPI inflation to moderate further to 4.6% average, assuming a normal monsoon. As CPI inflation has averaged 6.4% in July-Sep'23, it can potentially fall below 4% average in July-Sep'24 (DB forecast: 3.7%) due to the exceptionally favorable base effect, absent significant food price shocks. As of now, we are forecasting July'24 CPI inflation at 2.8%yoy and August'24 CPI inflation at 3.5%yoy.

## Nov IP growth moderated to 2.4%yoy, from 11.6%yoy in Oct

Nov IP grew 2.4%yoy vs. 11.6%yoy in Oct. This was lower than the Bloomberg consensus estimate of 3.5%yoy. Oct IP growth came at 11.6%yoy, higher than our and the Bloomberg consensus estimate of 10.5%yoy. The volatility in Oct-Nov IP growth can be explained by the "Diwali holiday effect", in our view. We note that the average IP growth in Oct-Nov 2023 is 7.0%yoy, which is significantly stronger than the average growth of 1.8%yoy over Oct-Nov 2022. The upside and downside surprises in Oct and Nov IP data respectively in 2023 offset each other and the average growth in these two months is consistent with our GDP forecast for Oct-Dec'23 of 6.3%yoy at this stage. We are forecasting real GDP growth at 6.8%yoy for FY24, thereafter moderating to 6.3%yoy in FY25.

## Extended pause for now; easing may commence from the middle of the year

We stick with our view that the RBI will maintain an extended pause for now and likely embark on a rate cut cycle starting from June 2024, converging with the Fed rate cut cycle. Analyzing the various monetary policy cycles in the last two decades, we note that the maximum that the RBI has paused between the end of a rate hike cycle and start of a rate cut cycle is 11 months (in two episodes). Given that the last rate hike was in Feb'23, a potential rate cut cycle commencing from June'24 would be a 16-month gap, the highest gestation period between the end of a rate hike cycle and start of a rate cut cycle.

We expect 75bps of rate cuts in 2024, starting from June '24, and another 25bps in 1Q'25, resulting in 100bps of total rate cuts through FY25. We are projecting headline and CPI core inflation for FY25 at 4.6% and 4.0% respectively, which would result in a 100bps real interest rate even if the RBI were to cut rates by 100bps in the next fiscal year. The biggest hurdle will likely be the Fed's reaction function, if it were to delay its rate hikes significantly or not cut rates at all in 2024; in such a scenario, RBI's rate cut cycle could get delayed as well.

## S Korea and Taiwan: Domestic politics not a game changer

n Taiwan's election results do not change our view on the economy, although the legislative process may become more complicated, with no party winning a majority. We continue to see CBC on hold through 2024.

Juliana Lee, Chief Economist, APAC

South Korea: The Bank of Korea (BoK) tone has turned neutral, as per our expectation, with the January MPC statement no longer noting that "the Board will judge whether the Base Rate needs to be raised further". Although the Board aims "to maintain a restrictive policy stance for a sufficiently long period of time until the Board is confident that inflation will converge on the target level," it temporarily raised "the amount of financial support to regional small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs), utilizing 9 trillion won worth of reserves in the ceiling of the Bank Intermediated Lending Support Facility." as "the negative impacts from high interest rates could disproportionately affect vulnerable sectors and regional SMEs." The BoK sees risks to inflation tilted to the upside - the central bank's alternative scenarios point to CPI inflation of 2.8%, above its baseline forecast of 2.6% - and it remains concerned that premature rate cuts "could stimulate inflation expectations and cause inflation to rise again." That said, inflation has surprised to the downside of late, prompting us to lower or own inflation forecast, amid softness in oil prices and weakness in domestic demand. Despite the latter, however, we expect stronger exports to guide South Korea's GDP growth higher, to +2.2%yoy (+0.6%QoQ sa) in Q4 from +1.4% (+0.6%) in Q3. While our baseline view remains that the BoK starts its easing cycle in July - a total of 50bps in 2024 - risks to our rates view have tilted to the downside amid renewed pressure on the housing market and rising concerns over PF loans.

## Indonesia: Easing bias

Bank Indonesia (BI) kept its policy rate steady at 6%, exercising “caution” with monetary policy easing and waiting for “more convincing signs of rupiah stability”, as per our earlier expectation. BI seeks “to continue strengthening pro-stability monetary policy and building policy synergy with the Government to ensure inflation in 2024 remains within the 2.5%±1% target range”. In December, headline inflation fell further to 2.6%, leaving the annual average at 3.7%, well within the 2023 target of 3.0%±1%. Core inflation also fell, sharply, to 1.8% in December from 2.5% in 2023. BI assessed that “the economic recovery in Indonesia remains intact, supported by domestic demand…primarily in the form of ongoing consumption growth, including the positive impact of the general election and increasing investment, particularly building investment, in line with current PSN development, encompassing the new capital city (IKN)”. While Indonesia will hold its election on 14 February, developments thus far do not suggest a need to alter our expectation for the economy. We see Indonesia’s inflation remaining well within the target, giving sufficient room for the central bank to cut rates by 100bps in total, starting in June, as the rupiah achieves a more durable appreciation against the US dollar, amid the Fed’s pivot. BI may move earlier than expected, if the rupiah strengthens faster than expected.

## Malaysia: BNM to hold, but strike a more dovish tone on growth

Malaysia's GDP surprised sharply to the downside in Q4, posting +3.4%yoy growth in Q4, vs. the market expectation of 4.1% growth, moving largely sideways from +3.3% in Q4. On a sequential basis, the economy contracted by 1.7% QoQ sa in Q4, after three consecutive quarters of rapid expansion, with the Q3 rate at +2.6%. The Q4 GDP result (advance estimate) left Malaysia's annual growth lower at 3.8% in 2023, vs. 8.7% in 2022, dragged down by manufacturing. On the demand front, we suspect that net trade and destocking likely weighed heavily on growth. More importantly, its annual growth stood below BNM's forecast range of 4-5%. Meanwhile, CPI inflation fell further, to 1.5% in November from 1.8% in October, with a similar print likely in December, leaving the annual inflation at 2.5% in 2023, vs. 3.4% in 2022. While CPI inflation is likely to rebound, on the back of fading base effects, targeted subsidy cuts, and "resilient" consumer spending (amid sustained payrolls growth of 2% in Oct/Nov), its average is likely to be lower than our earlier forecast, at 2.6%. On investment, BNM expects support from "continued progress of multi-year infrastructure projects and implementation of catalytic initiatives under the national master plans”, with “additional impetus” from the 2024 budget. In contrast, E&E exports' performance may disappoint against BNM's expectation – Malaysia's goods exports fell 5.2% in Oct/Nov, vs. a 15.2% fall in Q3, lagging behind its northern neighbours as semi supported their gains – while a further pickup in tourist spending may be limited, given China's weak demand. By September, tourist arrivals stood about 11% below the pre-Covid levels, while those from China stood about 33% below. Although it is likely to keep its policy rate unchanged at the upcoming meeting and reiterate that the “policy stance remaining supportive of the economy", we see BNM striking a more dovish tone on growth, given downside surprises.

## Philippines: Waiting for the right moment to cut

Bangko Sentral Ng Pilipinas (BSP) saw CPI inflation falling to 3.9%, finally within its target range of 2%-4%, with the annual average at 6% in 2023, slightly below our forecast of 6.1%. The downside surprise in December has pushed this year’s inflation lower by 0.1ppts to 3.9%. As per BSP's view, Diokno (recently appointed as a Monetary Board member) noted that “inflation might increase slightly in the second quarter because of base effects”, but will remain “within the target band of two to four percent“. More importantly, he went on to add that while BSP would remain "data-dependent", “a 75bps cut by the Fed this year could actually be matched by the central bank or even by 100bps”. Note that our prevailing forecast has BSP cutting rates by 150bps in total, starting in June.

Singapore: MAS to wait, as growth surprises to the upside

Singapore’s GDP growth surprised sharply to the upside, accelerating to 2.8%yoy (7% saar), about a percentage higher than the consensus and faster than the +1.1% (+5.3%) growth in Q3, leaving its 2023 growth at 1.2%. While manufacturing rose +3.2%yoy (+41% saar), up from -4.7% (+1%) in Q3, as domestic non-oil export (headline) growth rebounded to -1.4% (+0.2%) from -19.4% (-15.6%) in the same period. Construction growth also rose significantly, to +9.1% (+18.5%) in Q4, from +6.2% (+3.3%) in Q3, services growth stood steady at +2.5% (0%) vs. +2.3% (+4.3%) in the same period. On the domestic front, retail sales (volume) growth fell to -0.3% in Oct/Nov, from +1.5% in Q3, while headline inflation (core) fell to 3.6% (3.2%) in November from 4.1% (3%) in September. Housing (purchase) prices rose 6.7%yoy in Q4, up further from the 4.4% rise in Q3 – about 35% above the pre-Covid (2019) levels, while rental inflation stood at 19.3% in Q3, about 56% higher than their pre-Covid levels. Positive surprises in growth support our prevailing view that the MAS would keep its policy unchanged through mid-year, waiting until July to begin its monetary easing. That said, underwhelming growth performance by its neighbours may raise additional concerns over its own growth prospects.

## Thailand: BoT resists pressure to ease

The Bank of Thailand (BoT) held firm against increasing pressure to ease, as expected, noting that the current level of its policy rate does not impede economic growth. It also raised concerns over potential risks to financial stability in the event of lower rates, especially in the context of already-high household debt. The BoT saw disinflationary pressure as temporary and policy- (eg temporary fuel subsidy programs) and base effect-driven. The BoT sees headline inflation returning to positive territory after Q1 this year, to average within the target range of 1%-3%. As for growth, its comments hint at downside risk to its growth outlook. Ytd in November, the number of tourists stood at 24.8mn, suggesting that last year’s number may fall a bit short of the BoT’s assumption. What is more important is this year’s projection, which we think may be at a greater risk, given its increasing reliance on Chinese tourists. The BoT has assumed that the number of visitors rises to 34.5mn in 2024, from 28.3mn in 2023, led by Chinese tourists, which they assumed to rise to 6.2mn from 3.5mnm in the same period. As of last November, the number of tourists ex China stood at around 69% of pre-Covid levels, while those from China stood at around 39% of the pre-Covid levels. Although the recovery in tourism may fall short of its expectation, data on consumption remained robust. Retail sales continued to post strong growth of 7.4%yoy in Oct/Nov, vs.7.3% in Q3, as consumer sentiment rose amid stronger employment growth (2.1% vs. 1.2%) and low unemployment rate (below the pre-Covid levels now). Meanwhile, goods exports rose 6.4% in Oct/Nov, vs. -0.5% in Q3. The BoT provided two scenarios, with and without a cash handout, which would see 2024 GDP growth at 3.2% and 3.8%, respectively, followed by a modest slowdown to 3.1% and 2.8% in 2025, with the headline (core) inflation forecast at 2% (1.2%) and 2.2%.(1.5%) in 2024 and 1.9% (1.3%) and 2% (1.4%) in 2025. (Our own projections for Thailand did not take into account the cash handout programme, given elevated uncertainties.)

## Vietnam: Strong finish to 2024

Vietnam finished 2023 on a strong note, as both external and domestic demand improved. Despite ongoing pressure on the housing market, retail sales growth accelerated in Q4, to 8.8% (5.3% discounted by inflation) from 7.4% (4.5%) in Q3, led by services, while export growth rose to +6.9% from -2.2% in the same period, led by computer, electrical product, spare parts and components thereof, slightly faster than the +6% growth in imports. While public investment growth stood strong at 19.4% in Q4, albeit slower than the 27% growth reported in Q3, FDI (implemented) growth slowed to 4% from 11.3% in the same period. Despite the weakness in the latter, registered FDI surged 88.6% in Q4 to reach new heights, USD36.6bn in 2023, as Vietnam remained an attractive place for investment by MNCs, as an integral part of their supply chain, including the high value-added segment. As a result of a strong finish to 2023, Vietnam achieved GDP growth of 5% in 2023 and pushed our 2024 growth forecast higher by 0.2ppts to 6%. Other than export recovery, an end to a number of domestic uncertainties (with reforms) may provide an additional boost to Vietnam’s headline growth in the medium term, relative to our forecasts, despite elevated external uncertainties. For example, the revised land law seeks to update land use rights that improve transparency in price setting and facilitate (rather than impede) real estate development processes, although political conditions may not be conducive for the market to perform better in the near term. The amended law on credit institutions aims to strengthen the financial system, while prompting banks to diversify their loan base, as it seeks (for example) to reduce single borrower exposure and maximum stakes in financial institutions to 10% from 15%, while allowing the SBV to step in to mitigate any insolvency risks.

Macroeconomic fluctuations often account for most of the risks associated with exposures to instruments that promise to pay fixed or variable interest rates. For an investor who is long fixed-rate instruments (thus receiving these cash flows), increases in interest rates naturally lift the discount factors applied to the expected cash flows and thus cause a loss. The longer the maturity of a certain cash flow and the higher the move in the discount factor, the higher will be the loss. Upside surprises in inflation, fiscal funding needs, and FX depreciation rates are among the most common adverse macroeconomic shocks to receivers. But counterparty exposure, issuer creditworthiness, client segmentation, regulation (including changes in assets holding limits for different types of investors), changes in tax policies, currency convertibility (which may constrain currency conversion, repatriation of profits and/or liquidation of positions), and settlement issues related to local clearing houses are also important risk factors. The sensitivity of fixed-income instruments to macroeconomic shocks may be mitigated by indexing the contracted cash flows to inflation, to FX depreciation, or to specified interest rates - these are common in emerging markets. The index fixings may - by construction - lag or mis-measure the actual move in the underlying variables they are intended to track. The choice of the proper fixing (or metric) is particularly important in swaps markets, where floating coupon rates (i.e., coupons indexed to a typically short-dated interest rate reference index) are exchanged for fixed coupons. Funding in a currency that differs from the currency in which coupons are denominated carries FX risk.
