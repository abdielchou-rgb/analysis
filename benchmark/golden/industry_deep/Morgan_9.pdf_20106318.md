## When it rains, it pours

Its hard to imagine that its only been a week since our last publication, given the number of new developments that markets have had to absorb in a short time. Coming into this week, there was much to anticipate with the Treasury refunding announcement, the FOMC meeting and month-end all occurring on Wednesday. But the re-emergence of regional banking concerns earlier in the week, and an absolutely stellar employment report helped make this into a truly action-packed week.

The refunding announcement was largely in line with our expectations and not particularly market-moving (see US Treasury Market Daily: The Fed closes the door on March but opens the door to May, J. Barry et al., 1/31/2024). The return of regional bank concerns this week, however, was a surprise development, and came on Wednesday as New York Community Bank announced that it was boosting loan loss reserves and cutting its dividend. Regional bank stocks have fallen by \~10% in the aftermath. But thus far, there are little evidence of broader stress. As seen in Figure 1, week-on-week changes in BTFP usage and other Fed balance sheet trends, as well as funding levels (SOFR and the effective funds rate) show no signs of stress. And while cross currency bases turned more negative this week, these changes remain well within the range of normal variation and basis levels remain significantly better than their typical levels in stressed environments. Thus, we do not see this as a significant factor that impacts our views and themes in markets. Rather, the potential for escalation is a tail risk we need to consider.

Regarding the FOMC meeting, perhaps the main takeaway was that while the Fed dropped its pre-existing tightening bias, it also clearly indicated the need to see more accumulation of evidence that inflation is on a downward trajectory. To paraphrase Chair Powell's message, the Fed is looking not for better data but for more confirmatory evidence on inflation before deeming it appropriate to cut rates. In summary, we see rate cuts as unlikely to occur at the March meeting, and keep our call for the first rate cut in June while acknowledging a reasonable chance of a cut at the May meeting (Maybe May, but March less likely, M. Feroli, 1/31/2024).

Despite the message of patience from the Fed with respect to easing, and a refunding announcement in line with expectations, the market nevertheless managed to rally significantly into Thursday, thanks to strong month-end buying as well as the above-mentioned lingering regional bank concerns. That changed, however, on Friday on the back of Payrolls, which surprised to the upside in virtually all respects. Nonfarm employment increased 353,000 beating expectations, job growth in the prior two months was revised up, bucking the recent trend of downward revisions, and average hourly earnings surprised to the upside (see Groundhog Day for January job growth, M. Feroli, 2/2/2024). In response, Fed expectations have been repriced higher and yields rose sharply led by the front end (Figure 2).

This week's developments are already impacting markets in several ways. Perhaps most important implication is that policy uncertainty is elevated, a point we were already making even before this week. One technique we have often used to illustrate and gauge the extent of policy uncertainty is to look at the implied probability distribution inferred from the premia of calls and puts at various strikes on (say) Dec '24 SOFR futures, and then decompose that distribution into a weighted combination of scenario-specific conditional Normal distributions. While a glance at a raw implied probability distribution is not very illuminative, such a decomposition tells us the extent to which significantly different scenarios are being incorporated into the distribution. This is shown in Figure 3 - as can be seen, although none of these scenarios that comprise the decomposition involve rate hikes, the options market is assigning significant weights to rate cuts of magnitudes ranging from 50bp to 300bp, in addition to a non-trivial weighting on a still-on-hold scenario.

This is quite illustrative of the policy uncertainty that characterizes the current macro environment. But what does this mean for markets? As we have noted often in the recent past, such a wide dispersion of possible future outcomes is supportive of high delivered volatility. As new information causes market participants to recalibrate the weights on these scenarios, front end yields can move by significant amounts given the significant differences between the policy rate cuts that describe each scenario. Thus, the rise in delivered volatility in short tails is unsurprising, and is likely here to stay for some time. In addition, from a valuation standpoint, implied volatility on 2-year tails remains the cheapest relative to our fair value estimates (Figure 4). Lastly, lingering / worsening regional banking concerns remain a tail risk even if not our baseline expectation. Should this occur, this too would support a bullish stance on volatility, especially on shorter tails. As the experience of March 2023 tells us, implied volatility-versus-rate correlations would likely turn sharply negative for some period of time under such a scenario (Figure 5), and long volatility positions would likely outperform even as yields drop on the back of more aggressive rate cut expectations. Given these reasons, we maintain our bullish view on volatility with a preference for expressing it on short tails.

We also continue to recommend underweighting volatility in 5- and 7-year tails versus 2-year tails. The rationale for this view can be explained by looking at Figure 6, which presents the first and second empirical as well as implied principal components. (For a discussion of how we infer implied principal components from swaptions and yield curve spread options prices, see i-PCA: Implied Principal Component Analysis, 12/6/2022). We include only the first two principal components because they suffice to explain the bulk of the variation in yields. As a reminder, the numbers in the "PC1" column tell us how a 1-sigma move in the so called "level" factor would impact yields in various tenors, while the PC2 column tells us the effect of a 1-sigma move in the second (and uncorrelated) "curve" factor. Implied principal components tell us what the options market is collectively pricing in for the principal components on a forward looking basis, while the historical principal components reflect actual empirical behavior over the last six months. Two things are evident from this table. First, the implied principal components are strikingly similar to empirical estimates using the past six months of historical data. This tells us that the forward-looking options markets are mostly priced to a regime similar to the past six months or so. Second, it is worth noting that 5- and 7-year yields are mostly impacted by only the level factor, and moves in the second factor tend to produce pivots around the belly and contribute little to the volatility of 5- and 7-year yields because of the relatively small loadings of the second factor in those tenors. This is significant, because historical experience tells us that moves in the second factor tend to get larger going into the onset of Fed easing. Moreover, should regional banking fears continue to weigh on markets, that too would cause larger moves in the second factor. All this suggests that volatility on either long or short tails (which benefit from larger moves in the second principal component) should outperform volatility on 5- or 7-year tails (which do not). In accordance with this theme, we continue to recommend overweighting volatility on 2-year tails versus 5-year tails (see Polar vortex duration extension, 1/19/2024).

## Swap yield curve

Thus far, our yield curve trading strategy has revolved around carry trades, with the basic premise being that forwards were pricing in too much easing in the face of the Fed's (likely) patient stance. Given this week's developments, we take another look at this broad theme.

After Friday's selloff, forwards are now much closer to our own projections for the funds rate as seen in Figure 2, although there is still some room for near term forward OIS to rise. However, given that our economists now believe risks of a rate cut in May have risen, it would appear that forwards have less room to drift upwards, especially if regional banking fears were to escalate in the near term. Thus, on the one hand, the risk-reward characteristics of carry and roll-up trades are now likely worse than before.

At the same time, Friday's jobs report is a timely reminder that labor markets remain tight, bringing with it the risk of a Fed that is slower to ease, which in turn argues for carry trades on the curve. But given the near term risks mentioned above, it is important to seek carry trades that are hedged against the potential risks of aggressive Fed easing. One way to do this is by using payer swaptions. Consider, for instance, a weighted composite flattener that involves longs in 5s and 30s (24% and 100% risk respectively), versus a 100% risk weighted short in 2s. Changes in the weighted yield spread corresponding to this trade have exhibited almost zero correlation with changes in 5Y swap yields over the past 6 to 9 months, and a modest negative correlation with front end yields. In addition, this composite flattener also offers \~20bp of carry over three months. Of course, one key risk to such a composite flattener is a sharp pullforward of Fed easing, which would cause the curve to steepen in a rally. One way of mitigating this risk is to implement the composite flattener conditionally in a selloff using payer swaptions - i.e., buying 3Mx2Y payer swaptions versus selling 24% and 100% risk in 3Mx5Y and 3Mx30Y payer swaptions, respectively. This trade can currently be done close to premium neutral. At expiry, such a trade simply becomes a composite flattener if yields are higher. If however yields decline at expiry, the swaptions are likely to expire worthless, erasing flattening exposure. Thus, such a conditional composite flattener offers a way to position for attractive carry, while guarding against the risk of a near term pull-forward of Fed easing expectations. Therefore, we recommend this trade (see Trade recommendations). One risk to this trade is a steepening of the yield curve in a selloff.

In the belly, we have maintained a widening bias on swap spreads due to several supportive factors including an anticipated taper-down of QT and increased bank demand for UST asset swaps (see Polar votex duration extension, 1/19/2024). Now that swap spreads have widened by \~7-8bp over the past month, it is worth reassessing whether the case for further spread widening still holds. As we can see in Figure 8, there is still some room for further widening, to the tune of 3-4bp. But, at the same time, headwinds are emerging as well, even if only mild ones. In particular, our expectations of higher implied volatility as well as our FX strategists' outlook for a strengthening in the dollar are both sources of narrowing pressure on swap spreads. All in all, as we incorporate the likely evolution of the drivers themselves, we find that swap spreads in the 5-year sector continue to have about 2.5bp of widening potential. Therefore, we remain mildly bullish on 5Y swap spreads and acknowledge that spread widening in this sector is now in its end game.

In the front end, we maintain our narrowing bias and continue to recommend either outright 2-year spread narrowers or weighted 2s/5s spread curve steepeners. In this sector, 2-year spreads continue to appear wide to fair value, and the expectation of continued declines in RRP balances as well as our bias towards higher implied volatility both point to narrower spreads from here.

In the 30-year sector of the curve, although swap spreads have not moved by much, the fair value for long end swap spreads has moved significantly and swap spreads in this sector no longer appear compellingly wide to fair value (Figure 9). Although the residual remains a touch above 2bp, we turn cautious on narrowing exposure in this sector, as residual convergence appears to be occurring mainly on the back of increases in fair value. Therefore, we now turn neutral on 30-year maturity matched swap spreads and recommend unwinding narrowing exposure (see Trade recommendations).
