# The Viewpoint: All About the US

Asia is now more reliant on trade and the US. In the coming months, US growth, Fed policy and tariffs will be the key factors driving Asia’s macro-outlook. Our base case is the US soft-lands; Asia ex China steady recovery continues while China muddles through.

## Key Takeaways

■ Asia has become more reliant on the US – as evident in a rising trade surplus with the US and the US being the key driver of end global demand.

■ Upcoming events in the US could shift the economic trajectory and potentially generate significant spillover to Asia.

We see a US soft-landing; Fed cutting rates by 175bps in total and given uncertainty over the US election outcome, our base case does not incorporate tariffs.

This backdrop indicates Asia ex China’s recovery should continue to broaden, but won’t be enough to lift China out of its debt-deflation loop.

Risks to Asia’s outlook would arise from a deeper slowdown / onset of recession and if the US implements tariffs.

# Why does the US exert an outsized influence on Asia’s macro-outlook?

Developments in the US will matter more: In this report, we provide a refreshed overview of the Asia macro-outlook post the summer break. We highlight that the US will likely play an outsized role in driving Asia’s macro-outlook, perhaps even overwhelming domestic idiosyncratic developments in each economy. The reason why? Asia’s reliance on trade has increased in this cycle and the US has been the key marginal source of lifting global demand. Moreover, the US faces a busy calendar of macro events in the coming months that could alter the trajectory of the economy and potentially generate significant spillover to Asia. Three things we are watching – (1) the US growth outlook and its impact on Asia’s exports, (2) the Fed policy path and its implications on financial conditions, in particular the USD, and (3) tariff risks.

# Three ways the US could influence Asia’s macro-outlook

## 1) US growth outcomes and implications on Asia’s exports

A higher reliance on the US: Asia ex China has become more reliant on the US as a source of end-demand in this cycle. For starters, the US’ share in Asia ex China’s exports has risen, and Asia ex China’s trade surplus with the US has increased by US\$210bn to US\$384bn in the past six years, whereas it runs a large trade deficit with China. Moreover, recent dynamics in the Asia trade cycle indicate the US is the primary game in town. We track real imports as a way to ascertain trends in end-demand, and it has been the case that US real imports have been strongest across key regions. We attribute this to the fact that trailing US growth has been strong, supported by the capex cycle (in particular IT capex). This indicates that Asia ex China’s dependence on the US as a source of end-demand has increased.

What if we are wrong? Our base case remains that the US economy will soft-land. But if the US does go into a deeper slowdown, Asia should face moderate downside in this scenario. If the US enters into a recession, Asia would not be immune and should face downside to growth from a contraction in exports and tightening of financial conditions.

If the US goes through a deeper slowdown, Asia could lose the tailwind exports recovery, and we would expect moderate downside to our 2024 and 2025 growth forecasts of 4.5%. One key reason why it could be manageable is that policy makers would be able to offset a large part of growth downside by deploying more forceful monetary and fiscal easing that we see in our base case.

If the US falls into recession, we believe that Asia would face deeper downside to growth compared with previous US downturns. First, as detailed above, the region’s dependence on the US has increased. Second, Asia ex China will likely not benefit from the countercyclical support it had from China in previous cycles as the economy is facing much bigger structural challenges. The starting point of wide augmented fiscal deficits and high debt to GDP ratios indicates that there is less room for an aggressive counter-cyclical response. The persistent debt deflation challenge means we do not expect China to play the same countercyclical support role as before. Even if they do take up meaningful stimulus, it would be important to watch whether it would be more of the same supplycentric policies or if they would pivot to boosting consumption. With this backdrop, the region could face contraction in exports as well as tightening of financial conditions, at least in the initial phase of a recession.

Who is more exposed? Economies with higher exposure to the US would be Japan, Korea and Taiwan. For China, its exposure to the US may have lessened but remains relatively high, and the starting point of weak domestic demand indicates it needs exports as a growth engine. For India, its exposure to the US could arise more from services exports rather than goods per se. However, it could also see tailwinds from its strong structural growth story acting as a mitigating factor to growth downside. For Australia and Indonesia, while direct exposure is low, there could be indirect effects arising from weaker commodity prices.

## 2) The Fed and the Dollar

As our US economics team has noted, the July FOMC minutes and Jackson Hole have indicated a September start to the rate cut cycle, in line with the team’s base case. With our US team continuing to see a soft landing, they see the Fed cutting to a terminal rate of 3.625% in this cycle.

For Asia ex-Japan (AXJ), we have been flagging that with the inflation backdrop already fairly benign for most parts of the region, most Asian central banks would be able to join in on the rate cut cycle once the Fed cuts. We see central banks in economies with weaker domestic demand momentum (BOK, BOT, PBOC), or higher nominal rate differential vs. the Fed (BI, BSP) embarking on rate cuts first. That said, still-low interest rate differentials vs. the US could limit the magnitude of rate cuts from AXJ compared to previous cycles. As such, we continue to see a shallow rate cut cycle in Asia in our base case.

If the US enters a deeper slowdown, the Fed would likely deliver a larger magnitude and faster pace of rate cuts than we have penciled in our base case. Our chief global economist Seth Carpenter noted that the Fed could cut rates to its own estimate of neutral (i.e., 2.8%) in this scenario. As policy rate differentials with the US could move more rapidly in favor of Asia, it could then leave room for Asian central banks to deliver more rate cuts and provide some growth offset. In this scenario, central banks in more exposed economies such as Korea and Taiwan, or economies that have higher rates at the starting point such as Indonesia, Philippines and India, could cut rates by more than we expect in our base case. For Japan, a sharper pace of Fed rate cuts could mean the yen would face more appreciation pressure than our FX team expects in their base case, in turn weighing on inflation, which could keep the BOJ on hold for longer.

However, if the US enters into a recession, while we could see an even larger magnitude in Fed rate cuts than in the deeper US slowdown scenario, heightened FX volatility and a generally stronger dollar could lead Asian central banks to take a pause and focus on FX stability before resuming cuts. Moreover, the policy response is unlikely to be sufficient to complete offsetting the growth drag.

## 3) Tariffs and trade tensions

As we enter into US election season in the coming months, one key risk to Asia’s outlook would stem in form of potential increase in tariffs in the scenario of a Republican presidency. For instance, former President Trump has talked about imposing 50% tariffs on imports from China and also potentially imposing 10% tariffs on the rest of the world.

If 50% tariffs are imposed on imports from China, there could be a meaningful adverse effect on Asia's growth, but the final drag on growth would depend on the eventual set of trade policy measures taken up by the US and how those measures would weigh on corporate confidence. China could remain most exposed as compared to the rest of the region, which has reduced its export exposure to China over time. The feed-through to corporate confidence is crucial to track as it was the greater drag on growth during the 2018-19 trade tensions episode. Weak corporate confidence resulted in a sharp slowdown in corporate capex, which weighed on growth more than the direct impact of tariff increases. This time around, if tariffs do rise, the drag on corporate confidence could be mitigated because it would be a recurrence/escalation of a pre-existing issue rather than emergence of a new trend. Moreover, we believe that there would be an acceleration in supply chain diversification, which could help provide some offset to growth in the region excluding China over a period of time.

## Key economy specific factors we are also monitoring

Although we expect developments in the US to be a key driver for Asia, there are some economy-specific factors that could still drive the macro outlook that we provide an overview of below.

## China – will it exit the debt-deflation loop?

Although a robust exports backdrop is helpful, China still faces significant challenges emanating from the debt-deflation loop. Even as the property sector continues to pose a drag on growth, policy makers are still aiming for 5% real GDP growth and look to achieve this via a significant reallocation of investment into manufacturing and infrastructure.

The issue at hand is about the scale of the investment. At a macro level, China is still investing 41% of its GDP, even if its demographic trends suggest that its investment ratio should naturally decline. More importantly, using investment to target relatively high real GDP growth is not without its cost, and the end outcome we are seeing is that the returns on new investments are declining sharply.

Moreover, these excess investments have brought about the persistence of deflationary pressures. China’s GDP deflator – the broadest measure of prices, taking in all goods and services of a country, has now contracted for five consecutive quarters.

This deflationary backdrop poses a few challenges. High real rates are posing a burden on debtors. Weak nominal GDP growth backdrop is affecting corporate revenues and profits. Although the corporate sector initially bore the brunt of weaker returns, they are now cutting wage growth. Wage growth decelerated sharply to 4.5%Y in 2Q24, vs. 6.2% in 1Q24 and 8-9% pre-Covid, in turn creating a loop of even weaker aggregate demand and deflationary pressures.

Debt growth is unlikely to slow below nominal GDP growth. In this case, debt-to-GDP ratios should continue to climb. Our China economics team projects that China’s debt-to-GDP ratio will reach 312% by end 2024, a level higher than the US and some 30 percentage points above where it was at end-2021.

Finally, lower income households could face disproportionate downward pressures. Periods of high inflation have typically hurt lower income households more, but deflation hurts them as well. An instructive example is Japan post the 1990s. As deflationary pressures became entrenched, wage growth of lower-income households underperformed. Inequality also widened.

We think a sustainable exit from the debt-deflation loop entails returning to an environment of moderate inflation where the GDP deflator grows by around 2-3%. To get there, we think policy makers could use fiscal expansion to increase social security-related spending (healthcare, education, public housing), which would help households reduce their precautionary savings and boost consumption growth. These issues are especially pertinent for the 300 million migrant workers, as the limited access to public options in healthcare, education and housing is keeping their precautionary savings high.

As it is, China’s consumption to GDP ratio is very low and its household saving rate is very high at 31%. Typically, a rising age dependency ratio means a natural decline in the saving rate, but in China’s case the opposite has happened, indicating the scale of the challenge.

The current supply-centric policy stance – by using investment to sustain real GDP growth – will likely keep the debt-deflation loop alive. The solution, we believe, lies in boosting consumption, which should get the economy out of deflation, improve nominal GDP, and stabilize debt to GDP. But the small steps that have been taken in this direction to date are unlikely to be enough, in our view.

## India – Will political pressures force a shift toward redistribution?

Growth is broadening out: 2Q24 GDP showed growth remains on a strong footing and the recovery is now broadening out. GDP growth out-turns have been strong even though government spending was weak due to the general elections, and in turn that implies upside risks to our F2025 growth estimate of 6.8%. Gross fixed capital formation (Investment) growth re-accelerated to 7.5%Y (vs. 6.5% in 1Q), while real private consumption growth surged to a seven-quarter high of 7.5%Y. At the margin, incoming data such as capital goods imports growth and two-wheeler sales growth (a key proxy for rural demand) is also indicating some broadening out in growth drivers from public capex to private capex and from urban to rural consumption. We see more legs to the broadening out of the recovery. The key to the sustainability of the cycle is for policymakers to continue to nurture this trend by staying the course on creating a conducive environment to lift public and private capex rather than pivoting toward redistribution. As we have highlighted before, we see increasing investment as a durable and sustainable approach to creating jobs and lifting people out of poverty without igniting macrostability risks.

In our base case, we expect the current policy stance to continue. The first budget following the election in July has already reaffirmed the conduct of fiscal policy by prioritizing fiscal consolidation with a focus on capex spending as well as the incentive schemes for job creation and skilling (See The Viewpoint: India – Invest or Redistribute? ## Japan – Will BOJ tighten prematurely?

Near-term risks of BOJ action diminished, focus on wages and prices outlook: Although Governor Ueda’s comments at the post-July MPM press conference were more hawkish than necessary and contributed to volatility in Japan’s asset markets, subsequent comments by Deputy Governor Uchida helped to reverse the hawkish tilt. Specifically, the mention that BOJ was not at risk of falling behind the curve on rate hikes and that BOJ would not raise policy rates when financial and capital markets are unstable indicates that near-term risks of a policy rate hike are low. Indeed, Governor Ueda’s remarks at the Diet hearing on Aug 23 and subsequent remarks by Deputy Governor Himino both highlighted that adjustment in the degree of monetary easing would be based on BOJ being able to confirm with rising likelihood that its outlook for economic activity and prices will be realized.

Risks of a near-term rate hike have diminished: Incoming inflation data suggest there has been a pause in momentum in underlying inflation in July (see BoJ Watch: Momentum of Underlying Inflation Pauses for Now, 27 August, 2024). Weighted median CPI moderated to 1.1%Y in July (vs. 1.4%) in June, while core services inflation – the key metric for tracking wage to prices pass-through given higher share of labor cost – decelerated to 1.3%Y in July (vs. 1.7% in June). Against this backdrop, we think there is now less urgency for BOJ to act at the September and October meetings.

Our base case is the BOJ will hike in Jan-25: Going forward, there are several catalysts that could lead to improvements in the inflation outlook. First, we see continued passthrough in Spring wage negotiation results to macro-level wage data, which should bring scheduled earnings on a common sample basis to above 3% by the September print. Moreover, Rengo – Japan’s largest trade union – could release the wage hike target for the 2025 spring wage negotiations in late October (its 2024 Spring wage negotiations were released on October 19 last year). If strong negotiated wage growth is sustained, it could provide BOJ with more confidence on the 2025 wage growth outlook. The announced 5% minimum wage hike would also take effect from October, further supporting wage growth. The BOJ has indicated that Japan’s corporates tend to revise their services pricing once in April and once in October (April is start of Japan's fiscal year and October is midpoint). In this sense, the October and November CPI prints (released Nov 22 and Dec 20,

respectively) should reflect increased wage pass-through to services inflation and give BOJ sufficient confidence to hike in January, with risks of an earlier hike in December.

Policy backdrop to remain accommodative, supporting Japan’s growth outlook: Post-January meeting, we expect BOJ to remain data-dependent. Overall, we believe the policy stance would still be accommodative enough to ensure that Japan’s structural shift toward sustainable moderate inflation and higher nominal GDP growth can be maintained. (see Japan Macro and Strategy: A Revitalised Japan, May 19, 2024).

## Indonesia – Will it retain an orthodox policy approach?

A potential shift in its policy approach resulted in market volatility: Post the Presidential elections, investors had concerns the incoming administration might seek to change the direction of fiscal policy, shifting toward a more expansionary stance that could take the public debt to GDP higher, marking a departure from the orthodox prudent policy approach. If so, this could have significant implications that could also lead to a shift in Indonesia’s structural story. This uncertainty resulted in a period of market

volatility.

But policy makers have re-emphasized the prudent policy approach: In response, policy makers have been emphasizing that they will still stick to the prudent policy approach that has been the hallmark of Indonesia’s macro policy stance – one that has engendered relatively robust growth with benign macro stability risks. Indeed, the recent budget proposal for 2025 highlighted that policy makers are targeting a fiscal deficit of 2.5% of GDP, below the mandated 3% fiscal rule, while at the same time, taking steps to include funding for the incoming administration’s proposed programs.

What to watch for next: The key issues at hand remain whether the commitment toward fiscal prudence will hold and whether we will have to wait for steps taken by the new administration post the inauguration on October 20. Another catalyst to watch for is Bank Indonesia’s monetary policy – where we expect them to take up rate cuts from November onward. Finally, the trend in commodity prices and the potential spillover from weak growth in China should also be factors driving Indonesia’s growth outlook.

Korea – How will exports and corporate reforms shape the outlook?

Korea exports… Incoming trade data pointed to continued strength in Korea’s exports. August daily exports growth re-accelerated to 13.7%Y, after a temporary slowdown in July, amid shifts in summer holiday timing at major manufacturers. In particular, select tech exports including semiconductors, computers and mobile phones continued to post strong growth of 42%Y in the month, indicating tech demand remains robust. The US IT capex cycle has been a key driver for Korea’s exports, and our Chief Korea Economist Kathleen Oh estimates that exports to the US have accounted for 30% of Korea’s trade recovery in this cycle (see South Korea: Impact of Potential US Demand Slowdown on Korea, August 7, 2024). In this context, the natural question for Korea’s outlook is the sustainability of the US IT capex cycle and, more broadly, US end-demand.

…to continue to benefit from US capex cycle: On this front, our US team’s base case of a soft landing in the US indicates that growth could be sustained at a robust 2.1%Y through 2025, providing some floor for Korea’s non-tech exports. US IT capex growth – which accelerated to a seven-quarter high of 7.5%Y in 2Q24 – should continue to maintain solid momentum against this backdrop of solid growth and continued secular AI demand. Indeed, our global tech team has noted that consensus forecasts expect global semiconductor revenue to remain in double-digit %Y growth territory through 2Q25. As such, Kathleen expects Korea export momentum to remain solid through the rest of the year and record high single-digit growth against a higher base from last year.

Corporate governance reforms provide additional but not crucial tailwinds: Some investors have pointed to the slow pace of corporate governance reform as a headwind for Korea’s outlook from a market perspective. However, from a macro perspective, we think exports will continue to be the major driver for Korea’s outlook, with the corporate reform programme serving as a favorable longer-term micro story for equity markets and a bellwether for broader structural reform momentum. On this, our Korea equity strategist Joon Seok does not expect a decision on the recently tabled 2024 tax bill containing key value-up tax elements to be reached until November (Korea Musings: On Value-up Tax Plans, July 25, 2024). However, he sees the capital market/corporate reform agenda becoming a more bipartisan initiative despite some difference in views, as evident from the opposition party’s recent proposal of its own reform agenda. Kathleen also expects Korea's fiscal revenue outlook to improve from next year amid a broader growth recovery, which would put the government in a more favorable position to call for tax benefits (see South Korea: Does Korea have fiscal capacity to push "value-up" incentives? May 12, 2024).
