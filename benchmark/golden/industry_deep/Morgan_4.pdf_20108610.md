## Greater China

• China: Caixin PMI moved up to a two-year high

• Trade report in line with expectations

• Taiwan: May PMI beat expectations

Headline CPI came in higher-than-expected on food and energy, while core CPI stayed benign

• Exports below expectations, led by tech sector easing

• Next week: China inflation and credit, Taiwan CBC

China<sup>’</sup>s May Caixin manufacturing PMI moved up 0.3-pt to 51.7, slightly beating market expectations. This is the fourth consecutive month of improvement in the Caixin manufacturing PMI, hitting the highest in the past two years. The improvement stands in contrast to the decline in NBS manufacturing PMI (which fell 0.9-pt to 49.5). This is not unusual. In fact, NBS and Caixin manufacturing PMI readings have moved in opposite directions in nine out the past 12 months (Figure 1). This can be attributable to the differences in sampling methods (Caixin PMI is biased towards export-oriented smaller enterprises) and statistical approaches (different seasonal adjustment approaches), but may also point to uncertainties in the economic outlook at enterprise levels.

Figure 1: Monthly change in China manufacturing PMIs
![](images/9f3e158a3e950d0624d997879bc3e2b90cae5b6d3489722ed361edd9abf400d8.jpg)
Source: NBS, Caixin, J.P. Morgan

Comparing the May manufacturing PMI readings from Caixin and NBS, the common messages are: (1) the output component remained at above 50; (2) new order and export order components declined; (3) the employment component improved; (4) input and output price components picked up.

The near-term softness is mainly related to the ongoing weakness in the housing market (which is a drag on real estate investment and housing-related consumption), moderation in consumption activity and exports after a strong start in 1Q. The policy adjustment since late April will be useful to manage the downside risk to economic activity, but may not change the trend of a near-term slowdown. We maintain our 2Q GDP growth forecast of 3.5%q/q saar (vs. 7.4% in 1Q).

## Trade report in line with expectations

China<sup>’</sup>s exports remained steady in May following the strong growth in Jan-Apr, growing 7.6%oya with a modest 0.9%m/ m sa fall (Figure 2). By product breakdown, export performance is not much different across products, with a 0.3%m/m sa marginal gain in low-end consumer goods, and a 0.5% and 0.7% fall in mechanical & electrical products and high-tech products. Auto exports fell 4.9%. By destination, May exports to EM countries (Russia: +7.8%m/m sa, LatAm: +8.8%) significantly outperformed exports to the US (-3.5%) and EU (-1.8%).

Figure 2: China merchandise exports and imports US\$ billion,sa, 3mma
![](images/d47bfa246d702d94fe3ed1936765740b080c56477ad489f3085138a58291c6cc.jpg)

Nominal imports came in roughly in line with expectations, rising 1.8%oya or 0.2%m/m sa in May, led by EM Asia (3.3%m/m sa) but dragged by imports from the US (-8.2%), EU (-4.5%) and Brazil (-5.3%). Imports of commodities turned out to be mixed, with rising imports in crude oil (6.9%m/m sa), coal (5.0%) and copper (18.4%) but declining imports in steel products (-6.5%) and iron ore (-0.5%), mostly a reversal of April movements.

Exports have been a stable growth driver this year. The trade surplus registered US\$338.3bn in Jan-May, the highest on record for the same period. The improvement in global final demand, along with the strength of Chinese exports (the scale of production and lower export prices), jointly lifted China<sup>’</sup>s export volume by 12.2%oya (average in Jan-Apr).

Chinese exporters<sup>’</sup> diversification efforts across products and destinations may continue to support export performance in the near term. Nonetheless, risk may intensify on exports going forward. The rise of protectionism, the wider use of tariffs, 301 investigations, export controls, investment restrictions and sanctions, may lead to further distortion or disruption to the international trade system and add pressure for the Chinese economy. In particular, Trump<sup>’</sup>s threat to hike tariffs on Chinese goods to 60% (and to 10% globally) poses a major risk into 2025 and beyond. Under that scenario, it may trigger significant depreciation of CNY against the USD, acceleration of global supply chain relocation, and China<sup>’</sup>s

retaliatory measures (e.g. retaliatory tariffs and export controls on strategically important minerals). Still, it is not easy to decouple from China, hence the process of global supply chain relocation may be lengthy.

## FX reserves rose US\$31.2bn

China<sup>’</sup>s May FX reserves beat market expectations, rising US\$31.2bn to \$3,232.0bn (Figure 3). This partially offsets the sharp \$44.8bn fall in April. Gold reserves stayed unchanged, after solid increases in the prior 18 consecutive months. With an elevated trade surplus, we expect the current account surplus for goods and services moved up to \$24.6bn (vs. \$15.9bn in April). Meanwhile, the US dollar index eased from 106.2 to 104.7 in May, leading to an estimated currency valuation gain of US\$19.6bn. Taken together, we tentatively estimate a US\$13.0bn implied capital outflow in May (vs. US\$-38.1bn in April).

Figure 3: Monthly change in China's FX reserves
![](images/a0b03351235557ceae27893cd84c74eeab438ba3c5fe963a99f1a932adcb1d27.jpg)

Last week, the SAFE disclosed that they have lifted the QDII quota to 167.8bn by end of May, up by US\$2.3bn compared to one year ago. Demand for QDII products has been strong. Most products had a very high subscription rate, and thereby a purchase limit for investors. That is partly due to more attractive investment returns. Our strategist estimates QDII funds have returned on average (weighted by AUM) around 6.9% to investors year-to-date, compared to an uninspiring 1% from non-QDII mutual funds onshore. The incremental addition in QDII quota marks the PBOC<sup>’</sup>s efforts to open more room for official channels to facilitate overseas investments, though the capacity to digest the accumulated capital outflow pressure seems limited.

We think tariffs are becoming more of a concern for CNY. Near-term focus is on the EU tariff on China EV exports, which likely will be announced on June 10. Our auto analysts estimate that China NEV exports to Belgium account for 11.4% of China<sup>’</sup>s total NEV exports, 8.0% for England, 5.6% for Spain, 2-3% for Netherland, Germany and France. Although the EU<sup>’</sup>s tariff increase on EV will likely be much smaller than the 75%pts hike from the US, the impact could

be larger.

For the rest of the year and beyond, the upcoming US election and potential 60% tariff on Chinese goods if Trump is reelected or a potential trade war 2.0 will be essential for CNY movements. The 2017-2018 trade war suggests around 1% CNY depreciation against USD in response to a 2% tariff increase. Extrapolating the relationship to a 60% tariff scenario suggests around 15% depreciation for CNY. However, the relationship could go non-linear. On the one hand, the CNY may depreciate less because: (1) China<sup>’</sup>s share in US imports is ⅓ less and US share in China imports in nearly ¼ less; (2) actual average tariff may be lower than 60%; and (3) the PBOC likely will let the CNY depreciate, but USD/CNY may still face strong defense at important levels (e.g. 7.50, 7.80 or 8.0). On the other hand, if the tariff war 2.0 involves spillover of tariff increase by other trading partners or US tariff increase on goods produced in a third country by Chinese companies, the impact could be more disruptive, and the implications on CNY will be more complicated.

## Taiwan: May PMI beat expectations

After tracking a significant downward trend throughout 2022, Taiwan<sup>’</sup>s manufacturing PMI have been volatile with moderate upward trend (from subdued level) since early 2023. The momentum to trend up seems to have accelerated this year. In May, headline manufacturing PMI rose another 0.7-pt to 50.9 (Figure 4), adding to the 0.9-pt gain in April, the highest reading since May 2022.

Figure 4: Taiwan manufacturing PMI output and IP
![](images/e40ea41226c9c61a9ebe64388a94a413acf9290c991f08ab1d04e995faece2b2.jpg)

The latest signals from Taiwan<sup>’</sup>s manufacturing PMI have continued to be encouraging. The solid gain in the output component (up 1.2-pt to 52.6 in May) hints at solid near-term momentum in industrial production activity. Regarding external demand conditions, signals from the manufacturing PMI export orders component have been rather benign with a tick lower by 0.1-pt to 49.2 in May. It remains the highest reading since March 2023, hinting at a constructive near-term outlook on external demand. Meanwhile, regarding the latest trend in Taiwan<sup>’</sup>s inventory cycle, the manufacturing PMI finished

goods inventory indicator fell 1.2-pt to 48.9 in May, partly offsetting the 2.2-pt gain in April. As such, the manufacturing PMI order to inventory ratio came in at 1.05 in May (compared to Jan-Apr average at 1.01).

## CPI rose 2.2%oya on food and energy

After delivering downside surprise for two consecutive months in March and April, Taiwan<sup>’</sup>s CPI inflation beat expectations in May. Headline CPI rose 2.2%oya or 0.2%m/ m sa (Figure 5). The monthly gain was most evident in energy, up 0.9%m/m sa. It reflects the continuing impact of the recent electricity price hike. Meanwhile, food prices rose 3.4%oya or 0.2%m/m sa, mainly driven by vegetable and fruit price increases due to the late April rainstorm and supply disruptions. Core CPI remained benign, rising 1.8%oya or 0.1%m/m sa. Excluding vegetable and fruit, May CPI rose 1.9%oya.

Figure 5: Taiwan headline and core CPI %oya
![](images/3eaee0c67ad5f6efb4529e3c8fb044f9b7003c28c8b37db69470d906f9f29a22.jpg)

The downside surprises in April CPI, the benign May CPI (if short-term food price seasonality is excluded), and the general easing in underlying inflation trends are encouraging. Looking ahead, assuming some moderate indirect effect of the electricity price hike in the coming months, we expect Taiwan<sup>’</sup>s headline CPI inflation to stay around 2% in the middle part of the year, before easing to around 1.5% into the year-end (especially considering the higher base effect in 2H23). Overall, we expect headline CPI inflation to average 1.9% in 2024, and core CPI inflation to average at 1.8%.

The CBC<sup>’</sup>s March monetary policy meeting minutes also raised concerns about stickiness in prices for food away from home and rental prices. In this regard, price increases for food away from home have eased lately, while rental CPI ticked up to 2.6%oya in May. Taken together, we maintain our baseline call, looking for the Taiwan central bank to keep policy rates on hold in the upcoming June CBC monetary policy meeting next week.

## Exports below expectations on tech easing

Following solid sequential growth trend in 4Q23 and 1Q24, Taiwan<sup>’</sup>s trade reports for April and May showed some moderation in export activity. Merchandise exports came in below expectations again in May, rising 3.5%oya. Seasonally adjusted, May exports fell 2.5% m/m sa, adding to the decline of 7.6% in April, with the sequential trend growing moderately at 5.9% 3m/3m saar. Adjusted for export prices, real exports fell 2.1% m/m sa.

Tech exports fell 4.2% m/m sa in May, adding to decline of 8.3% m/ sa in April, with the sequential trend still showing moderate growth at 6.5% 3m/3m saar (Figure 6). Further breakdown shows some easing in exports of information and communication products, suggesting the significant rally in AI-related exports in recent quarters may be moderating somewhat in the near term. Breakdown by export destinations suggests near-term easing in exports to the US following significant out-performance in recent quarters. Exports to other major DM markets (Europe and Japan), as well as exports to China/ HKSAR, continue to show softer underlying growth trends.

Figure 6: Taiwan export breakdown by products US\$ bn, sa, 3mma
![](images/856608254ac0d782c9a41c3e16fdbd03ca842fc928e5d8cf748942074dccf13a.jpg)

Going forward, we look for steady upward trend in Taiwan<sup>’</sup>s tech exports in the coming quarters, including structural expansion in exports of AI-related products and HPC-related demand growth, though the recent easing in tech export momentum is worth monitoring. The recent sequential upturn in Taiwan<sup>’</sup>s capital goods imports is encouraging, suggesting tentative recovery in the domestic capex trend, likely reflecting Taiwan manufacturers<sup>’</sup> improving sentiment on the export sector outlook.
