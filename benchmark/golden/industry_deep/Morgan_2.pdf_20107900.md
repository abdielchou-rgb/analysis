# The Problem with Steepeners

Curve steepeners are popular among all investor types. Yet, the possibility of "insurance cuts", improving fiscal and historical trends, and consensus positioning means there is an underappreciated path to bull flattening. Duration longs offer better risk/reward than steepeners. Add 3m30y receivers.

• Investors think steepeners work in many scenarios: Curve steepeners are immensely popular among investors of all stripes – albeit for different reasons, varying from traditional Fed cuts-driven bull steepening to term premium expansion in the long end.

• Investors under-appreciate bull-flattening risk: Investors think curves can both bull steepen and bear steepen, but under-appreciate the bull-flattening risk. We think bull steepening and bull flattening are both viable scenarios, and thus see bullish duration as a better option than curve steepeners. We look at the key arguments cited in favor of steepeners.

• Argument 1: The curve can bull steepen in a Fed cutting cycle; counter argument – the curve can bull flatten instead if markets see cuts as "insurance" like in mid-2019.

• Argument 2: 2023 suggests steepeners can keep doing well; counter argument – history suggests that bear steepenings are mean reverted by bull flattenings.

• Argument 3: Perceived fiscal deterioration and a supply/demand mismatch can expand term premiums, driving bear steepening; counter argument – the near-term fiscal picture is improving, supporting demand for longer-duration Treasuries.

• Argument 4: A Trump election scenario is linked to a dovish Fed and fiscal expansion – supporting twist steepening; counter argument – many hurdles and checks lie between an expansionary fiscal policy or a dovish Fed post-elections.

## The Problem with Steepeners

Curve steepeners are immensely popular among investors of all stripes – albeit for different reasons, varying from (1) traditional Fed cuts-driven bull steepening, to (2) investors expecting a higher term premium driven by perception of the worsening fiscal situation, and a linked supply/demand mismatch in Treasuries. Some investors even see steepeners (3) for a Trump presidency scenario where they expect a dovish Fed coupled with significant long-end issuance.

While we agree with the case for bull steepening, we think that investors do not appreciate the possibility of a bull flattening. Investors should consider that: (1) The Fed cutting cycle can be "insurance" style as opposed to traditional easing, which has historically supported bull flattening as opposed to bull steepening; (2) An improvement in the fiscal picture in the near term can bring back support for the long bond; (3) Historical trends suggest that bear steepening mean reverts with bull flattening; (4) It's not clear that a Trump presidency may necessarily drive a dovish Fed; and (5) Highly consensus positioning in steepeners could help the curve to flatten.

How did steepeners become so popular? As it became apparent in 3Q23 that the Fed had likely reached the peak rate for the cycle, investors began to position for rate cuts via steepeners. A bull steepening made sense if the Fed's next move was going to be a series of rate cuts. But it was the bear steepening in late summer 2023 that supercharged the popularity of steepeners (see Exhibit 1 ). Investors who had positioned for a traditional bull steepening became convinced that, bullish or bearish market, the steepener trade works in every scenario.

## Five arguments for steepeners, and why they may not work

Argument 1: The curve can bull steepen in a Fed cutting cycle

Counter argument: The curve can bull flatten instead if markets see cuts as "insurance" like in mid- 2019

In general, we do sympathize with bull steepening as a highly plausible scenario amid Fed cuts. But we think that bull flattening can play out in a Fed cutting cycle too. The key deciding variable for bull steepening versus bull flattening is whether the markets see potential Fed cuts as an "easing cycle" versus an "insurance cycle".

In an easing cycle, the market will expect the Fed to take short-term rates below neutral, and deliver a lot more cuts at a much faster pace than is currently priced in (\~1.5 cuts through the end of 2024). This will likely cause the 2s10s curve to bull steepen.

Exhibit 3: Moves in 2s30s and 2s10s around the 2019 rate cuts
![](images/27828a47d7dca338cd6ce9b757641feb1b5a9cf88e09e656e763df3979ea1c34.jpg)
Source: Bloomberg, Morgan Stanley Research

Exhibit 4: Moves in 2s30s and 2s10s around the November-December 2023 rally
![](images/2c2a2607a073c99df182210971edbe8fd8ed83f2e95689d87601790a1de9d99c.jpg)
Source: Bloomberg, Morgan Stanley Research

But insurance cuts are different. Importantly, two historical examples support the case for bull flattening: (1) The 2019 insurance cuts; and (2) The November-December 2023 rally, where the market priced a shallow cut cycle (see Exhibit 3 and Exhibit 4 ).

For markets, the 2019 rate cuts are an important template. In 2019 Powell characterized rate cuts as "insurance", and linked them to a decline in the neutral rate (quote from Powell below) – helping the market to reprice neutral rates lower (see Exhibit 5 ), and long-end yields to fall and the curve to bull flatten (see Exhibit 3 ).

Powell remarks on insurance cuts, July 2019: Yes. So I gave three reasons for what we did, and that is to insure against downside risks to the outlook from weak global growth and trade tensions. So that is—in a sense, that is a risk-management point, and that is a bit of insurance.

Powell remarks on neutral, July 2019: The median Committee participant’s assessments of the neutral rate of interest and the longer-run normal rate of unemployment have also declined this year, reinforcing the case for a somewhat lower path for our policy rate.

Additionally, the bull flattening after the November 2023 FOMC meeting (see Exhibit 4 ) is another template. At the November meeting, the Fed was seen as dovish – and brought in buyers for the long end and helped to bull flatten the curve. It helped that Powell labeled Fed policy as "well above mainstream estimates of the neutral policy rate" in November – once again like in 2019 – helping neutral rates to reprice lower (see Exhibit 6 ), and long-end yields to decline.

Exhibit 5: Repricing of neutral rates after the July 2019 FOMC
![](images/01138a67c8073470e47f392c6fd456d3fc0a02479889f889bf9226a19587e109.jpg)
Source: Bloomberg, Morgan Stanley Research

Exhibit 6: Repricing of neutral rates after the November 2023 FOMC
![](images/9a1db0d14864da35a2a458436dd6dde431e446c3585ffc5f8e0de90cf2c761d1.jpg)
Source: Bloomberg, Morgan Stanley Research

We think that "insurance cut" cycles are more supportive for bull flattening because: (1) Insurance cuts can be seen as the Fed trying to return closer to neutral, effectively making markets reassess neutral rates being lower; and (2) It can drive demand back in the long end as investors feel safer owning more duration – depressing term premiums.

Argument 2: 2023 suggests that steepeners can keep doing well

Counter argument: History suggests that bear steepenings are mean reverted by bull flattenings

In essence this argument comes down to whether the 2023 trends will continue or not. On a fundamental level, the trends that drove weak performance in Treasuries are: (1) Strong growth data – and the possibility of a higher r\*; (2) Fed hawkishness; and (3) A negative carry environment (i.e., inverted yield curve) further disincentivized ownership of long-end Treasuries. We noted the drivers of the sell-off in The Real Drivers of the US Treasury Bond Market Rout, October 16, 2023.

Simply put, the strong macro conditions and a hawkish Fed drove buyers away in 2023. But we think that these macro trends will reverse. Our economists expect cooling inflation, and three Fed rate cuts in 2024. And as macro trends change, we think that the buyers who shied away from Treasuries in 2023 will return (see 2024 US rates outlook: Macro will bring the buyers, November 18, 2023).

At a technical level, we note that history teaches us that bear steepening is typically followed by bull flattening (see November 2023 example above).

Tracking abnormal versus normal curve moves: We define bull steepenings and bear flattenings as "normal" curve moves, and bull flattenings and bear steepenings as "abnormal" curve moves. Separating the curve moves into normal and abnormal helps to understand which market moves are more fundamental or normal and can sustain, and which ones are abnormal and less likely to sustain.

Exhibit 7 shows that, on a rolling 12-month basis, the amount of bull flattening typically follows and offsets the amount of bear steepening. The difference between the bull flattening and bear steepening – i.e., the difference between the two time series – can be seen as the net abnormal curve move.

Exhibit 8 shows that, on net, the abnormal curve moves tend to show significant mean reversion. Put differently, history suggest that bear-steepening moves tend to mean revert with offsetting bull-flattening moves.

Argument 3: Perceived fiscal deterioration and supply/demand mismatch can expand term premiums, driving bear steepening

Counter argument: The near-term fiscal picture is improving, supporting demand for longer-duration Treasuries

Another argument for the curve steepener is that even if the Fed doesn’t cut rates, the US fiscal deficit is on a widening trend, which will require investors to demand a higher term premium. Closely linked is the argument that the Treasury will have to issue a lot of longend supply – worsening the supply/demand balance in Treasuries.

In the near term, recent CBO data point to an improved short-term deficit picture, driven by a variety of factors, including a Y/Y revenue increase due to a boost in payroll tax collection versus a year ago (and strong collection versus recent years; see Exhibit 11 and Exhibit 12 ), as well as lower outlays. Recent CBO data show that the overall deficit decrease is mainly due to higher revenues, while the line items within the outlays category also decreased Y/Y.

Importantly, one can tie a lot of these one-time increases in deficits last year – like COLA adjustments, lack of capital gains tax receipts, FDIC spend on supporting banks, and higher interest expense – to higher rates and higher inflation. As inflation cools, and the interest rate outlook is for eventual rate cuts, it is natural that some of the deficit expansion reverses from 2023.

In the medium term, we noted that we expected that the peak in fiscal expansion was behind us, which appears to be the case as these factors continue to roll off. We expect this trend to continue, as lingering Covid-era programs expire and inflation decelerates.

Similarly, we do not expect any meaningful legislative action to add to near-term deficits ahead of the 2024 election, and account for more gradual IRA-related spend over the budget horizon in line with the CBO's revised assessments.

Post-November, we do see scope for moderate deficit expansion based primarily on tax cut extensions, but the extent of the expansion should depend on the outcome of the election. Importantly, many of the top priorities from both Republicans and Democrats have been passed in some form in the last eight years – limiting the need for big-ticket fiscally expansionary programs even post the elections.

Argument 4: A Trump election scenario is linked to a dovish Fed, and fiscal expansion – supporting twist steepening

## Counter argument: Many hurdles lie between expansionary fiscal policy or a dovish Fed under Trump

In a recent piece from our US public policy and global economics teams, they argue that under any presidential election outcome, there are significant checks and balances that would likely protect the Fed's independence from political interference. In a scenario where investors see the mix of policies under the President Trump being potentially inflationary, they should think about the risk than an independent Fed could counteract any inflation pressures with hawkish monetary policy.

We think that the scenario of Trump getting elected could result in a whipsaw price action where investors may initially cling on to the belief that such an election outcome means a dovish Fed amid fiscally expansionary policies that may drive the curve steeper initially. However, it is also possible that before or after the election, investors figure that the Fed will look to counteract any actions that raise the risks of inflation – which may flatten back the curve.

Thus, we do not see Trump election scenario as necessarily beneficial for steepeners, but more likely a whipsaw combination of steepening and flattening.

## Argument 5: Investors will add more steepeners

## Counter argument: We see steepeners as highly consensus

The sheer popularity of curve steepeners among investors of all types is a hindrance to its own performance. Put differently, the trade is too consensus. In our conversations with investors – from asset managers to insurance companies to hedge funds – we see a common liking for curve steepeners, although many investors like different types of curve steepeners – from 2s10s to 5s30s to those in the front-end SFR space. Many investors are also short long-end USTs on cross market short US- long EUR/UK trades.

One way of looking at curve positions is to look at the CFTC positioning data for asset managers, leveraged funds, and other reportables. While the three categories are – by design – going to net close to zero (there is one long and one short for every open interest on a future contract), it is notable that both asset managers and other reportables – the category which includes central banks and corporate Treasuries – are running the highest steepener exposure in the last five years (see Exhibit 13 and Exhibit 14 ).
