# APAC Focus: Made in China, made by China – Seeking alpha as Chinese companies go global

![](images/bdb2bcbc1d6d82a551f68f420cced719eb0fda399ce16aec5235c811e7a91757.jpg)

While investors are worried about trade restrictions and question China's global competitiveness, overseas revenue is in reality rising for the 32 Chinese manufacturers we cover, up at a 20% CAGR in 2023-25E to 31% of total revenue vs 28% in 2022 and below 6% in 2010. With this growth comes margin expansion too. Leveraging the insights of over 20 UBS analysts globally, we build a "China+1" country scorecard, identify sectors and markets with the most debate on Chinese competitiveness, and dive deep into LatAm and ASEAN as +1 destinations. We introduce a list of 10 most preferred stocks under the "going global" theme.

## Made in China: export upgrades increases China's global competitiveness

Better exports, in the form of product upgrades, mix improvements and more service exports, create significant overseas growth opportunities for Chinese auto, battery, construction, solar, grid equipment and selective consumer sector supply chains, where China has projected its domestic competitiveness to global markets. We forecast unit export value (US\$/t) to rise another 7% over 2023-25E, on top of the 12% gain already notched up from 2011 in 2023E.

## Made by China: "China+1" brings another revenue source from overseas

Chinese manufacturers have been investing overseas in recent years, motivated by the opportunity to navigate trade restrictions, benefit from cost differentials and access destination market demand. Our country-by-country scorecard analysis confirms LatAm and ASEAN are well-placed to gain potential capacity expansion by Chinese corporates. Our sector-by-sector analysis shows companies in auto parts, construction machinery, data centres, two-wheelers, and variety retail as being active in overseas expansion.

## Executive summary

Since China's entry into the WTO, Chinese companies' overseas revenue exposure has risen from under 3% in 2003 to 11% in 2022, mirroring the nation's export growth. But the story doesn't end there. With export upgrades (Made in China) and "China+1" capacity expansion (Made by China), Chinese listcos are well-placed to continue to grow their overseas revenue, in our view. We expect manufacturing names under our coverage to grow their overseas revenue from 28% of total revenue in 2022 to over 31% in 2025E, with grid equipment, NEV bus, battery materials and equipment makers set to deliver over 40% overseas revenue growth pa in 2022-25E.

Rising revenue contribution from overseas

Overseas business normally generates a higher gross profit margin (GPM) than domestic business. Hence, growing overseas revenue exposure should drive a GPM improvement. Among the Chinese manufacturers under our coverage, we expect construction machinery, battery materials, solar equipment makers, and Yutong Bus, to sustain their overseas GPM premium, and robotic vacuum cleaner (RVC) and solar module makers to continue improving GPM in overseas markets over 2023-25E.

In the past decade, China has successfully diversified its export destinations from the US and Europe to developing countries (ASEAN, for instance, is now its largest trade partner), with support from the Belt & Road Initiative (BRI). We expect storage battery and auto exports growth to remain strong, despite meagre overall China export growth. According to the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank (AIIB), China's global value chains (GVCs) exports as a share of total exports increased from 38% in 2000 to 45% in 2019, indicating that China is moving up the GVCs.

"China+LatAm". We see substantial potential for overseas capacity expansion in LatAm ("China+1" expansion: How can Chinese corporates benefit from "China+LatAm"?), as 1) United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA) has granted Mexico duty-free access to the US market; 2) capacity expansion by supply chain leaders, such as Tesla and GM, will bring supply chains with them to Mexico; 3) there is an abundant skilled workforce in Mexico; and 4) Mexico has a geographical advantage in supplying and serving clients in US. However, there are also significant uncertainty over Mexcio's preferential tariff treatment under USMCA and the country's infrastructure readiness.

"China+ASEAN". We also believe mutual cooperation between China and ASEAN will create significant opportunities ("China+1" expansion: How can Chinese corporates benefit from "China+ASEAN"?), as 1) China and the ASEAN have been each other's largest trading partner since 2020; 2) ASEAN's productivity and market is \~50% of China's; and 3) ASEAN as a whole is more competitive.

## What's priced in?

We have observed that international and Chinese companies have divergent views on competition outside of the Chinese market. Chinese manufacturers are, as a whole, confident about their chances of gaining market share in the auto parts, solar equipment and battery materials sectors in both developed and developing markets (excluding China), whereas their international peers are not. In the solar equipment sector, Chinese manufacturers see price and product quality differentials versus international manufacturers narrowing, a trend not recognised by their international peers. In the battery materials sector, only Chinese manufacturers think the price premium will expand and product quality gap will narrow in the developed market.

Investors have little confidence over the sustainability of Chinese auto export sales due to anti-subsidy investigations in the EU but overlook Chinese auto's global competitiveness in the industry's transition to electric vehicles and intelligent systems, as well as cost leadership. Investors also appear sceptical on Chinese consumer brands' ability to catch up, or even potentially lead the market, and develop brand power in overseas home appliances and retail markets. ## Our most preferred sectors

Investors generally equate slow export growth of China (UBSe 1%/3% in 2024/25E) to a weak overseas revenue outlook for Chinese manufacturers. This is only party true, given there are potential bright spots arising from exports being upgraded, especially in industries, such as auto, battery and machinery, where Chinese companies are already competitive against international players in the Chinese market. In addition, we expect the "China+1" overseas capacity expansion strategy could lead to rising revenue and net profit contributions from overseas. Among the industries covered by UBS analysts, we find Chinese manufacturers in the auto parts, construction machinery, two-wheeler and solar modules sectors have more incentives to expand production capacity overseas due to lower production cost, trade restrictions, potential market and brand awareness.

## Our most preferred stocks

We have identified 22 stocks under UBS China coverage that may benefit from rising overseas exposure, as well as their global peers on which UBS has Sell or Neutral ratings. BYD and Yutong are our top picks in the auto supply chain, given their improving brand recognition and systematic cost advantage versus local carmakers. We like Linglong because its Serbia plant could help alleviate potential issues of shipping cost hike and helps secure order from European OEMs such as VW. Yadea appears best positioned to benefit from the macro trend of global EV transition in our view. In the construction supply chain, we prefer CCCC and Hengli, which have already established solid businesses in overseas markets, supported by BRI. Within the consumer sector, we prefer Miniso, Haier and Pop Mart for their rising brand awareness in overseas markets. In the grid equipment sector, Huaming, Sieyuan and Willfar are our top picks, based on their favourable growth and margin profiles offered by overseas markets. We also like CIMC, as we think the incremental earnings contribution from its container manufacturing and overseas engineering businesses have not been fully priced in.

## Export Upgrade

## What does China? What will China export?

As the world's largest exporter, China accounted for roughly 15% of global exports in 2022, but what' s interesting is not just the magnitude of growth in Chinese exports but how their composition has improved. In the past two decades, the structure of Chinese exports has shifted, with Chinese manufacturers gaining global market share in machinery and chemicals, while losing market share in primary products, electronics and light manufacturing.

At the same time, Chinese manufacturers have been gaining market share in global value chains, particularly in storage battery and auto parts exports, while losing market share in the locomotive sector, although it remains competitive in this area. We expect Chinese manufacturers to continue gaining share in the storage battery, solar module and auto sectors in the next two years.

## What is the export upgrade trend?

We define the upgrade trend in Chinese exports based on: 1) a rise in unit export value,which can be attributed to product upgrades and inflation; 2) an improvement in the composition of exports towards more high-value products and upgraded manufacturing products, along with market share expansion; and 3) growth in service exports, driven in particular by patent fees and after-sales services.

We attribute unit value increase to product upgrades and the rising cost of inputs. For example in the RVC sector, new products with more or advanced functions have pushed up the selling price. Unit prices for both Ecovacs and Roborock products rose after new products were launched at end-2022. Besides product upgrades, inflation has also contributed to higher unit prices on Chinese exports, with the cost of production inputs, labour and logistics all continually climbing in China.

The unit value of Chinese exports continues to rise

The upgrade trend in Chinese exports can also be seen in an improving mix of exports. The composition of Chinese exports has improved markedly in the past five years, with Chinese manufacturers seeing consistent growth in export sales in most areas of advanced manufacturing since 2019, while they have lost export market share in labourintensive products.

## 1. Supply chain competitiveness

China's supply chain competitiveness can be partly attributed to a continual inflow of FDI in the past two decades. Despite a 12% YoY drop in global FDI in 2022, the flow of FDI into China, which is the second largest FDI recipient country in the world, saw a 5% increase. The increase was concentrated in manufacturing and high-tech industries.

Since joining the WTO in 2001, China has received the second largest amount of FDI globally, with the Chinese manufacturing sector being the main recipient over the past 15 years. FDI has driven the upgrade of manufacturing technology and industrial structures in China. China is the only country that ticks all the industrial categories in the United Nations’ industrial classification.

China’s share of global manufacturing value-added and export domestic value-added rate (DVAR) keeps rising. DVAR refers to the proportion of domestic value added in exports, which is used to measure exports of high value-added products and can reflect a country's export upgrade development. Generally speaking, the higher the DVAR, the stronger the competitiveness of the products produced by a country, and the higher the position of the country in the global value chain.

## 2. Inflation allows for price hikes

Inflation in the prices of production inputs, such as metals, labour and logistics could help push up export prices over the long term. Owing to the competitiveness of Chinese export, Chinese exporting companies can pass most of the rising cost of inputs to consumers and support a unit price hike in Chinese exports.

The Chinese government has issued various policies to support industry upgrade and export upgrade, including export rebates, BRI, free trade zones and the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership) Agreement. There are various policies from China that aims to provide support for export-oriented industries, especially for private companies.

## "China+1" expansion, a different path to gain overseas exposure

China has been actively investing overseas over the past 10-plus years, which translates to overseas assets reaching US\$5.5trn at end-2021, 5x the 2011 level. The US, Australia, UK were the top three countries receiving Chinese investment in 2005-H123, with Chinese investors' focus falling mostly on energy, metals and transport in the same period. However, Indonesia, Brazil and Saudi Arabia were the most popular destination for Chinese investment during 2020-H123. The proportion of reinvested earnings represent 50% of China's overseas direct investment (ODI) in 2022, against 18% in 2008, indicating Chinese companies' overseas subsidiaries are making more profit. China's ODI generated US\$174bn of goods export in 2022, representing 5% of total, according to China's Ministry of Commerce. In the UBS Evidence Lab China C-Suite Business Survey (> Access Dataset), 15% of Chinese corporate executives indicated that they are allocating most of their investment in export supply chain capabilities in overseas locations, and 23% of them indicated they are not doing so now but have plans to do so.

## Why expand capacity overseas?

We have identified six main motivations Chinese companies have for expanding their capacity outside of China: 1) taking advantage of potential cost savings, such as lower labour costs, in overseas locations; 2) navigate trade restrictions; 3) going where supply chain leaders have relocated; 4) positioning to better capture local demand; 5) leveraging policy support, such as BRI; and 6) enhancing international brand image. We collected inputs from UBS China analysts across more than 20 sectors on their views regarding Chinese overseas capacity expansion. Our result shows that Chinese companies in the auto parts, pet food, diary, construction machinery and two-wheelers sectors are actively seeking overseas capacity expansion opportunities.

## Mexico

Mexico has shown signs of being well-placed to take advantages of Chinese overseas expansion. Leasing demand for industrial space in Mexico has picked up notably from

2019-Q123, with Chinese companies accounting for 43% of leasing demand in the country. Around 40% of the investment announced by Chinese listed companies in 2023 was made by auto and auto components companies. However, at current stage FDI has not reflected picking up capacity expansion in Mexico.

We attribute Mexico's attractiveness to: 1) the USMCA trade agreement ; 2) reshoring trend of supply chain leaders, such as Tesla; 3) proximity to the US market; 4) workforce availability; 5) US trade restrictions on Chinese imports; and 6) the IMMEX programme in Mexico.

## EMEA

The EU and China are each other’s second largest trade partner. China's trade with the EU accounts for a rising proportion of the EU's total trade. The EU's high per-capita GDP creates a strong potential local market.

The EU has, by international standards, relatively progressive ESG policies: the Renewables Energy Directive has set out the target to raise the share of renewable energy in the EU’s overall energy consumption to 42.5% by 2030 with an additional 2.5% indicative top up to allow the target of 45% to be achieved. Besides that, individual countries in the EU support 'green industry'. For instance, the German and Hungarian governments support the auto industry shifting to electric cars. The EU’s increasingly strict export restrictions, such as the Net Zero Industry Act – which sets benchmarks for the diversification of EU supplies, at least 10%/40%/15% of the EU’s annual consumption for extraction/processing/recycling and no more than 65% of the EU’s annual consumption from a single third country – has attracted Chinese renewables energy companies to expand production capacity in the EU.

Hungary, Germany and Guinea were the top three countries receiving Chinese greenfield investments in the EMEA region in 2020-H123, with transport, metals and energy being the three largest sector focuses. The EU and Africa account for 57% of all the industrial parks that China has invested in around the world. Chinese companies tend to invest in industrial parks that focus on traditional industries, such as manufacturing and agriculture; while they have also collaborated in the development of high-tech and logistics parks in Europe.

## Automotive

Rising competition and a price war in the Chinese market remain a major concern for investors, and export growth seems more acceptable to investors than domestic growth. Meanwhile, many investors worry about weak macro, as reflected in property sales and some other areas of consumption, without imminent stimulus policy in sight. Recently, investor impressions seem to be that exports could face significant protectionism, as the EU has launched an anti-subsidy investigation relating to Chinese EV imports.

## Automotive parts

We believe investors have a good understanding of the drag on auto parts sales from slowing growth in overseas auto markets and the potential impact of continuing auto industry competition on parts suppliers’ earnings but may not realise companies with higher proportions of exports may have a certain degree of risk resistance. We believe the market may be overly bullish on the growth prospects of some suppliers (such as Desay SV) that have businesses that are skewed towards electric and smart auto components, and likely overlooking potential upcoming pricing pressure.

## Consumers

We believe investors are sceptical over Chinese brands' ability to catch up, or even potentially lead the market, and develop brand power in overseas market. To us, some companies have already demonstrated their capabilities in overseas markets and we expect them to deliver sales and profit growth acceleration. With business expansion and improving profitability in overseas direct markets, we believe Miniso has not been fully priced for its potential growth in overseas markets.

We believe investors doubt the sustainability of Haier's overseas revenue growth amid EU and US recession risk, as growth in the EU and US appliances industry has been soft since 2022. We are more optimistic than the market on Haier’s global expansion. We think investors are still overlooking GEA's potential market share gains and category expansion strengths in the US, as well as Haier's market share gains in other regions, including APAC and Europe.

## Construction machinery

The US and EU conducted anti-dumping and anti-subsidy/anti-dumping investigations into AWPs (Aerial Work Platform) made in China in 2021/2023. In light of this, we believe the market's recently concerns are about the anti-dumping and anti-subsidy regulations in EU as well as the potential change in the preferential trade terms for Mexico under USMCA. However, investors could be overlooking the fact that China's export volume to the UK is not particularly large at present and some leading Chinese OEMs have established assembly capacity in EU. While leading Chinese OEMs' GPM could be under pressure in the near term, we see potential upside in the long term for those that have production capacity in overseas markets.

## Grid equipment

Additionally, some investors are concerned whether: 1) grid can boost capex growth, given macroeconomic pressure; and 2) stronger grid spending would continue if renewable installations slow down. Investors are uncertain about whether the acceleration in the sector's sales growth is sustainable and expects Chinese companies global market share to be flat or increase by only a limited extent.

## GDS

We think the market has priced in weak demand and oversupply in China and does not give much credence to GDS's overseas expansion as investors are less familiar with the ASEAN IDC (Internet Data Center) market. Also, investors worry about competition in Malaysia and Indonesia, as well as potential equity financing for ASEAN projects, which is potentially dilutive for current shareholders. However, we believe GDS has the potential to re-rate to 13-14x 2024E EV/EBITDA, mainly driven by its overseas business, peaking interest rate and deleveraging.

## CIMC

We believe the market has not priced in demand driven by better-than-expected exports and more intermediate goods trade. Additionally, the increase in overseas income brought by CIMC's overseas related business has not been reflected in the shares, in our view.

## Others

Beside the above concerns over Chinese companies doing business overseas, investors also worry about industry oversupply in the solar module sector in Europe and are expressing low confidence in the overseas market expansion in ASEAN by Yadea and GDS.
