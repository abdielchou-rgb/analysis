AI continues to be one of the most profound developments of our lifetimes, and I continue to believe it has the ability to push US productivity growth comfortably above 3% p.a. by the end of this decade. There will be hiccups along the way, but with Nvidia already up +47% YTD (the best in the entire S&P 500), and now the 3<sup>rd</sup> largest company in the world, it is clear that the enthusiasm for AI is undimmed.

The recent performance of the Magnificent Seven has helped ease financial conditions substantially, and our economists have boosted their US growth forecasts notably in recent weeks. But even with this upgrade, the read through to Europe is not expected to be significant, with Euro Area growth ‘only’ up twotenths at DB to 0.4% in 2024. In Germany, the weak data so far this year means that had it not been for the US/Europe upgrade, it would have been downgraded further from our below-consensus -0.2% for 2024. The contrast between Germany and the US at the moment is stark.

But with US growth surprising on the upside, there remain signs of inflation persistence. 3-month core CPI is running at 4.0% on an annualised basis, which has led investors to push back the timing of rate cuts from the Fed. By contrast in Europe, underlying price pressures appear weaker, and our economists expect the ECB to cut rates at the meeting-after-next in April. Could we have the Fed battling with sticky inflation this year, just as Europe and China are seeking to combat more disinflationary forces? That would have currency implications, and our FX strategists remain bullish on the Dollar versus the Euro.

Otherwise, both domestic politics and geopolitics remain in focus, particularly given the number of elections this year. This week marks two years since Russia invaded Ukraine, and there are few signs that the end is in sight. The market implications have been diluted over time as energy prices have subsided, but the geopolitical consequences remain significant. And elsewhere, the disruption to commercial shipping in the Red Sea has once again demonstrated the vulnerability of global supply chains. Looking forward, this week’s announcement that Ursula von der Leyen will run for a second term as EU Commission President starts the countdown towards the European Parliamentary elections in June. And then in November, we have the US Presidential election, where there are significant divergences between the major candidates’ views. Before we reach the summer, this election could be the major talking point in financial markets.

David Folkerts-Landau, Group Chief Economist

## 2024 GLOBAL GROWTH: A balancing act

![](images/6e3403254b389b7cbbe0ccb3edb5d8a7141a0dea1cfb824ccbcec9e1952efce4.jpg)

We expect global growth to run at 2.9% in 2024 and 3.2% in 2025.

Soft landing is now the baseline with the economy expected to remain resilient in 2024.

Europe is skirting around recession and a soft landing in the US will help. The 2024 GDP forecast lifted to 0.4% (previously 0.2%). This remains a sub-trend performance. Activity expected to be broadly stagnant in H1. Sustained growth only expected in H2.

Operating below potential but lacks endogenous momentum to close negative output gap. Policy support from public spending key to recovery.

EM growth should outperform DM; central banks in EM will have more space to ease as the DM cycle turns lower; the shape and scale of China rebound will be important for commodity/services exporters.

Inflation may have peaked, but upside risks remain as price pressures persist.
Economic growth in key countries has proven stronger than expected, and we no longer expect a recession in the US and the EU.

Outperformed in 2023. Easier financial conditions, improvement in forward looking indicators and sentiment suggest performance should continue in 2024.

Labour market data has been robust but coming into better balance, supporting sturdy consumer spending while allowing for a smaller impulse to inflation.

Inflation has softened materially, with core PCE below target in H2 2023. Near-term inflation may firm somewhat but expect broader disinflationary trend to remain intact.

Transmission of the ECB’s monetary tightening has been strong, weighing on interest sensitive spending (investment, housing, parts of private consumption). Some signs of a peak in transmission, consistent with avoiding recession and returning to growth in 2024. Manufacturing beginning to bottom. Rapid disinflation to boost consumer purchasing power. Inflation remains above target but declining faster than the ECB anticipated with inflation likely back at 2.0% yoy in Q3-24, a year before the ECB expects.

China will target above 4.5% growth in 2024, supporting a more accommodative macro stance.

1.5-2% fiscal expansion combined with 45bps PBOC rate cuts could lift 2024 GDP to 4.7%, up from 4.1% in 2022-23.

GDP has been broadly stagnant since winter 2022. An energy recession was narrowly avoided and strong transmission of monetary tightening is restraining growth now.

Headline inflation peaked a year ago, core peaked in the spring and has been decelerating faster than anticipated in recent months.

At the margin, the flow of data lately has been slightly more positive – growth not quite as weak as expected, inflation not quite as low as expected.

The ECB hiked to 4.00% in September, about 200bp above neutral, and signalled an extended pause.

The ECB says only by keeping policy rates at this restrictive level for sufficiently long will inflation sustainably return to target.

In December, ECB announced next steps in QT. PEPP reinvestments to be reduced by 50% in H2 2024 and 100% in 2025.

We expect the first rate cut in April and the ECB to cut by 25bp per meeting for the remainder of 2024 (150bp in total this year). We expect further 50bp of cuts in 2025. 200bp of cuts in total will reduce the policy rate to 2.00% in mid-2025, broadly in line with neutral.

We do not exclude possibility of the ECB cutting by 50bp at some point this year.

The UK economy fell into a very small technical recession in H2-23. Tailwinds should gradually offset remaining headwinds from fiscal and monetary policy. The MPC expects GDP to expand by 0.3% this year.

With regards to the labour market, latest data show that the unemployment rate has fallen to 3.8% but there are clear signs that the labour market is easing too, with pay settlements easing, vacancies falling, and recruitment difficulties softening sizeably.

MPC members think rates may be sufficiently restrictive with focus now on how long to maintain such a restrictive stance. As for QT, the BoE ramped up its QT envelope to GBP 100bn – from GBP 80bn in the previous 12m, driven by higher passive and active runoff.
