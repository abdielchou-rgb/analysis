## Shades of grey

JPMorgan Chase Bank NA

It is often said that economic forecasts lend credibility to weather predictions. To be sure, both disciplines deal with complex systems in which unprecedented recent developments (COVID and climate change) have increased uncertainty. While we continue to emphasize limited visibility in key elements of the 2024 outlook— most notably whether we are moving on to a path of a sustainable expansion—it is notable that markets and economic forecasters are increasingly embracing a softlanding scenario.

On growth, we see low 1H24 recession risks. With financial conditions having eased materially and moderating inflation boosting household purchasing power, pressures that could spark a near-term break have abated. The year-end signal from the global composite PMI and labor markets aligns with our forecast that growth is proceeding at a trend-like pace as we turn into the new year.

Sustaining growth at this pace will likely require a narrowing in last year<sup>’</sup>s wide sectoral and regional gaps. The latest signals around convergence are not encouraging. A 1.3%ar rise 2H23 global factory output was more muted than expected and the recovery continues to stand on a fragile base as output gains are heavily concentrated in Asia tech activity (Figure 1). DM final goods demand was weak in 2H23, held back by Western Europe, which led to a pickup in inventory accumulation. The message from survey data is weak, with the December global manufacturing PMI sliding back into contractionary territory and this week<sup>’</sup>s US January regional Fed surveys nosediving. We expect next week<sup>’</sup>s DM January flash PMI to move even lower.

Recent signs of life in global goods demand is a bright spot. Global consumer goods spending growth has perked back up to a solid 2.4%ar in the three months through November, and this week<sup>’</sup>s US retail sales report for December was strong. Our global capex nowcaster has also picked up in recent months. However, any sustained pickup in global goods demand will likely require better news from Western Europe, where a cautious consumer has been a sustained growth drag. A sharp slide in European inflation provides the fuel for lift as real labor income is now accelerating at a faster than 2%ar. But the clock is ticking. The loss of pricing power for businesses will, absent a pickup in domestic spending, eventually motivate a pullback in hiring.

After two years of 5%ar gains, global core inflation tumbled to a 3.3%ar in 2H23. While optimism is increasing that the “last mile<sup>”</sup> of disinflation will soon follow, there are reasons to be cautious. Most of last year<sup>’</sup>s disinflation came in goods but commodity prices are no longer falling and traded goods prices point to a moderating deflationary impulse from China (Figure 2). The recent disruptions in Red Sea trade could add as much as 0.7%-pt to annualized global core goods inflation in 1H24. Positive inflation outcomes look likely to require material core service disinflation. But absent a material easing in labor markets or weak service sector demand, fundamental supports for this slide will not be in place. On balance, we maintain global core inflation of 3%ar in 1H24.

Against this backdrop, central bankers have become more optimistic that they can calibrate policy stances that are perceived to be materially restrictive. However, this optimism is cautious and the nearly unanimous message from a large number of DM central banks in recent weeks, including the Fed and ECB, is that rate cuts are unlikely to start this quarter (Figure 3). The realization of our forecast for growth resilience and sticky core inflation aligns with a more gradual midyear start to DM easing.

Ever since Chair Powell<sup>’</sup>s dovish press conference in December, Fed rhetoric has attempted to temper market enthusiasm for March cuts—including comments this week from the more dovish Atlanta Fed president Bostic that he expected the first cut not to come until 3Q24. In the Euro area, a weak growth backdrop and sharper drop in inflation warrant an earlier ease. Even still, President Lagarde indicated a “summer<sup>”</sup> start to rate cuts and pushed back forcefully against market expectations for as much as 150bp of cuts this year. Recognizing that incoming reports have shifted the balance of risks, we have pulled forward the start of ECB rate cuts to June, in line with our forecast for the Fed. We also now look for BoE easing to start in August. Next week we expect Norges Bank to remain on hold following its surprise hike in December.

## BoJ to tighten guidance, not tools

Being the only central bank to have yet raised its policy rate from the emergency lows of the pandemic, the BoJ is charting its own course and remains focused on assuring the rise in inflation over the past two years is sustainable. Despite the weak headline figures in Japan<sup>’</sup>s latest CPI inflation (December) and wage growth (November) reports, the underlying details point to ongoing firming of inflation dynamics. We think the recent data, alongside firms<sup>’</sup> indications to raise wages, are helping to gradually bolster the BoJ<sup>’</sup>s confidence that such structural changes are indeed taking place—a necessary condition to start normalizing policy. That, in turn, should be enough for the BoJ to change the tone of its policy statement next week, removing the strong easing bias from its forward guidance and potentially introducing more flexibility into its JGB purchases. But greater confidence will be needed to remove its negative interest rate policy, which we continue to expect to be a mid-year event.

## BoC still battling sticky core inflation

The Bank of Canada is expected to remain on hold next week and, as with most other DM central banks, recent comments have pushed back against an early policy pivot. Indeed, stubbornly elevated BoC core inflation measures—stuck in a 3% to 4% range for the past year—are challenging a return to the 2% target. The latest 4Q Business Outlook Survey suggests a gradual normalization of expected inflation, wage growth, and corporate pricing behavior. The BoC is in no hurry to ease, and it is hard to see a softening of the currently hawkish forward guidance next week. This contrast in inflation outlooks has led markets to shift toward lower odds of the BoC easing than the Fed during 1H24 and a shallower BoC cutting cycle this year. We still expect the first BoC cut in 2Q23, as signs of an expected downward trend in inflation and wage growth, along with weaker demand, should become apparent by then.

## Imbalances a later risk to China growth

China<sup>’</sup>s 4Q23 GDP growth came in broadly as expected at 5.8%q/q, saar, resulting in full-year growth (5.2%y/y) modestly above the official target of “around 5%.<sup>”</sup> But the latest data also point to persistent imbalances in the economy as policy favors production and investment over consumption. December retail sales disappointed (declining 0.2%m/m) while various already-depressed measures of housing activity fell further. Soft home sales look to have spread from private to SOE developers, and home price declines expanded to toptier cities. Manufacturing output also fell in December (-0.8%m/m) but earlier strong gains means 4Q IP is running 10.6%ar above 3Q. FAI in manufacturing and infrastructure moved up as well thanks to policy supports.

Recent policy actions in China should allow growth to remain around 5%ar during 1H24, and we continue to think largescale stimulus is unlikely. This week<sup>’</sup>s hold by the PBOC (against market expectations for a rate cut) was in line with our expectations. Reports of a ¥1tn special government bond look to be a change in the composition of the budget deficit (3.8% of GDP) rather than additional fiscal support. But sluggish domestic demand is feeding deflationary pressures, and the GDP deflator fell 1.4%oya in 4Q (Figure 4). This pressure could last longer should the policy-induced imbalance between supply and demand persist. Our forecast for 2H24 growth to moderate to a 4.3%ar pace could face downside risks if deflation weighs further on consumption or the housing market correction spreads.

## EM Asia CBs ponder softening inflation

Signs of softening in core services inflation are opening the door for policy easing among some EM Asia central banks, well after rate-cutting cycles began elsewhere in EM. Recent data point to cooling labor markets in Korea and Hong Kong; weak travel flows from Mainland China also likely weighed on tourism-related sectors. These developments support our expectation for sequential easing of core inflation across the region during 1H24, and we see some risk that Indonesia and possibly Thailand could ease earlier than forecast. But financial conditions could accelerate policy easing: Bank Indonesia held policy steady this week but has been focused on external stability concerns. Further easing of global financial conditions would prompt us to bring forward our forecast for two 25bp rate cuts by BI in 2H24. These cuts are likely to be preceded by a normalization of liquidity conditions.

## Upside-down growth surprises in LatAm

For much of last year, Mexico<sup>’</sup>s economic growth outperformed the rest of Latin America. While the divergence likely continued in 4Q23, recent data signal a shift in risks for this year<sup>’</sup>s outlook: stronger-than-expected in Brazil and the Andeans, and weaker in Mexico. For Brazil, we anticipate some payback from weakened agriculture in 2H23 driving a substantial acceleration in growth this year. Meanwhile, we revised up Colombia<sup>’</sup>s GDP forecast to 1.2% for 2024, and data confirm an expected firming of growth in Chile and Peru. A more tempered El Niño could lead to subdued inflation as well in the region, especially Peru. By contrast, while we continue to see above-trend growth in Mexico for the first half of this year, we recently revised down our 4Q23 GDP forecast to 2.5%ar on cautionary signals from confidence, sales, and labor market indicators.

## Some hawkish news for some EMEA CBs

A range of recent developments are likely to lead several EMEA central banks to retain their hawkish guidance against the broader trend toward easing expected elsewhere in EM. In South Africa, markets see elevated chances of a March move, but we think the easing cycle kicks off only in May. The departure of one dovish-leaning MPC member reinforces that view, while the governor suggested the SARB needs to see inflation come down to 4.5% (from 5.5%oya in November) to start easing. Recent news on El Niño (solid rains) and fiscal policy (benign despite an election year) have helped reduce inflation risks. By contrast, in Israel the fiscal deficit deteriorated more than expected, to 6.6% of GDP, largely due to increased defense spending. This sizable impulse comes on top of a recovery in consumer demand as the economy adjusts to the wartime reality. This confluence should constrain the BoI<sup>’</sup>s space to ease later this year, even with very soft inflation recently. We look for another 25bp cut in February.

In Türkiye, we expect the CBRT to hike by 250bp to 45% next week as it signaled for tightening to be completed as soon as possible. The inflation outlook deteriorated in January due to a higher-than-expected minimum wage hike, automatic tax hikes, and administered price hikes. Hawkish forward guidance should leave the door open for further CBRT rate hikes if needed. We maintain our end-2024 policy rate forecast at 45% to rein in inflation, but do not rule out further hikes beyond January. We also do not expect premature rate cuts this year.
