# The Lower Rate Playbook

The start of a Fed easing cycle will not trigger a new EM market cycle, in our view. We identify interest rate betas to US and local rates and screen for best positioned sectors and companies. We expect defensive leadership from Healthcare and Staples, but see select opportunities in Financials.

Easing cycle, not a new market cycle: Our economists see the Fed cutting rates 25bp in each meeting from September, for a cumulative 175bp. However, this does not look to us like a classic transition from late to early cycle. 1) We are not (yet) moving through (or priced for) a global recession. 2) China is refraining from playing its usual countercyclical role. 3) Semis normally lead the recovery, but we think they have put in a relative performance peak for reasons outlined here. History shows that rate cuts are not necessarily a good catalyst to buy equities; we see MSCI APxJ and EM as fully priced. Instead, we recommend buying defensives, which also tend to be geared toward lower rates.

Look for leverage to lower Fed rates: Our economists see a mixed and relatively shallow policy adjustment for Asia/EM, meaning that benefits may be more concentrated for those exposed to easier US financial conditions (see Asia Economics: The Viewpoint: How Asian Central Banks Will React). We see South Africa, Indonesia, Hong Kong, Singapore, and Saudi Arabia as potential frontline beneficiaries, with ample domestic policy space to ease. Mexico and Colombia are expected to lower rates more than the Fed, but domestic policy uncertainty may limit any re-rating – our Latin America strategy team prefers Brazil.

Estimating equity interest-rate betas: We build a composite PCA signal for each market using local policy rates and 10-year yields alongside 12-month forward Fed Funds expectations and US 10-year yields – recognising the role that US cost of debt plays in the cost of EM capital. We highlight the most-levered market-sectors in Exhibit 5 and stocks in Exhibit 7 .

Defensives a good play on lower rates and recession risk... Historically, Healthcare, Staples, Utilities, and Gold have outperformed as rates come down; we also see them offering a defensive skew to returns as US growth slows and China refrains from major stimulus. Real Estate is another lower-rate beneficiary – we seek select opportunities outside of China (the Philippines screens well and our analysts see rate cuts unlocking deep value in Hong Kong).

...with select opportunities in Financials: We also see this easing cycle as more benign for Financials, given our relatively hawkish outlook for domestic rates (including a further hike in Japan), strong structural growth in India, and the potential for hedging policies and a recovery of wealth management income in Singapore. However, we remain cautious on Australian Financials given even more stretched valuations and a constrained RBA easing cycle. We are also mindful of US yield exposure in regional insurers.

## An Easing Cycle, Not a New Cycle

Historically, MSCI EM valuations tend to peak around or before the Fed's rate cutting cycles, which goes against the common misperception that lower rates boost equity valuations. Equities start to react negatively and much earlier than the Fed when economic conditions turn down, and earnings risks outweigh the easing from lower rates. We turned cautious and recommended adding to defensives from 24 July, and continue to favor defensives as a good play in the face of recession risks and a lower-rate environment.

## Estimating Asia/EM equity betas to interest rates

## An Asia/EM-specific approach needed

In the US, policy rates, Treasury yields, and deep corporate credit markets provide a rich and relatively stable set of interest rate factors for equities. In Europe, while there are national differences, there is a uniform policy rate and relatively high correlation between sovereign yields, outside of the early 2010s crisis. However, for Asia and EM, we have much more policy and bond yield volatility and variance with core US/European rates – which requires a unique approach.

In our experience, both local and US interest rates matter for valuation dynamics of Asian and EM companies. Here we want to distill this down into one factor, although we acknowledge there is further information embedded in the shape of the curve and the differential between local and US rates.

To help us extract the most relevant interest rate signal, we combine local rates and our US rates data set and extract the first principal component as an aggregate signal.

To ensure balance between local and US rates, we select two interest rates from the US – rolling 12-month forward Fed Funds pricing and US 10-year yields, as well as two locally – policy rates and local 10-year yields.

This should not be interpreted as an aggregate financial conditions index or policy rate in the macroeconomic sense. Nonetheless, we think it's a useful proxy for assessing the equity market beta for stocks in each market.

## Identifying structural market and sector betas to interest rates

Over the longer run, we find Healthcare has been the most sensitive to lower rates in both MSCI EM and MSCI Pacific (i.e., DM Asia), followed by Consumer Staples and Utilities. In short, Defensives have been a good play on lower rates. On the flip side, Energy and Financials have historically lagged during periods of falling rates, which represents a combination of macro/growth correlation to the cycle and relative factor exposure towards value (which itself is negatively correlated with interest rates).

## Overweight sectors

Overall, the model has tilted more in favour of rate-sensitive and defensive sectors, with a net long position in our favoured markets – India, Singapore and South Africa. Our overweight sectors and key stock opportunities in our APxJ and GEM Focus List are:

• Singapore Financials and Communication Services: Showing strong earnings estimate revisions breadth and bottom-up analyst views, albeit neutral relative valuations. We highlight SingTel, United Overseas Bank, and Sea Ltd as key recommendations from our analysts.

• South Africa Financials: South Africa as a market remains overweight in our framework given the positive tilt after elections and a potential recovery in the earnings cycle. In our GEM Focus List we highlight FirstRand Limited, which is also a Top Pick from our analyst.

• India Financials and Consumer Discretionary: Strong nominal GDP growth, support from macro stability, strong PMIs, and therefore superior corporate earnings are all backing our bullish views on India, especially in Financials and Discretionary. We have ICICI Bank and Maruti Suzuki Limited in our APxJ and GEM Focus Lists.

• Philippines Real Estate: The sector is likely to enjoy tailwinds from the early start of the rate cut cycle. We hold Ayala Land in our APxJ and GEM Focus List as the preferred play given its relatively strong earnings growth outlook and undemanding valuation.

• Brazil Financials: Nu Holdings is our analysts' top pick and represented in our GEM Focus List, while following MSCI rebalancing it is also the largest constituent within the Brazil Financials sector. It is on track to build the largest and profitable banking franchise in Latin America, in our analysts' view.

• Hong Kong Utilities: We see the Utilities sector as a defensive, bond-proxy type exposure that should continue outperforming into a lower Fed and HKD interest rate cycle, particularly given our broader caution on the Hang Seng index. We note that our HK Property team also sees substantial upside as rates come down, which may be complementary or attractive for those with a more bullish risk outlook (see Hong Kong Property: Rates to Rescue).

## Underweight sectors

In contrast, our underweight recommendations are skewed towards more cyclical sectors and those in markets where we expect a relatively hawkish easing cycle – including Australia and Taiwan. Our underweight sectors are:

• Mexico Communication Services and Industrials: We have downgraded Mexico to underweight (see here for details) given the negative impacts on Mexico's investment narrative from the proposed constitutional and judicial reforms. While Mexico has benefited from near-shoring trends, the US growth slowdown and capacity constraints may limit further gains until policy uncertainty is resolved.

• Australia Financials: We see our economists' relatively hawkish view on the timing and depth of the RBA's easing cycle as a negative for the Australian banks, given high valuations and potentially weaker mortgage volume outlook. Instead, we hold Macquarie Group in our Focus List, given its exposure to global alternative asset markets, where the origination pipeline and valuations may be helped by lower rates.

• Taiwan Materials: We are cautious on the sector given its exposure to a global growth slowdown and margin pressure from excess Chinese capacity.

• UAE and Qatar Financials: We see these sectors as negatively geared towards lower rates given NIM sensitivity for the banks. In contrast, our Middle East Banks analysts see a turn in the Saudi Arabian cycle that may present opportunities on the long side or paired off (see Saudi Arabia Banks, Economics and Sovereign Credit: A Turn in the Cycle, 28 Aug 2024).

• Thailand Materials: Earnings estimate revisions and profitability remain challenged amid regional excess capacity in commodity chemical markets.

• China Consumer Discretionary: Our economists see ongoing deflationary dynamics and a cautious approach to consumer-oriented stimulus that is resulting in ongoing competitive pressures on margins.

## Macro Debate – Where Is Asia/EM in the Global Cycle?

## Phase transition underway

Our global economics outlook has centred on disinflation, driven by a policy-induced slowdown in the US and deflationary trends in China (see Global Economic Briefing: The Global 360). The market has oscillated between a no-landing and hard-landing narrative for the US, but we think the mix of disinflation and resilient growth through to mid-2024 has been best explained by a stronger-than-expected supply expansion (buoyed by migration and stronger investment).

Recent evidence of a growth slowdown is to be expected, following a long period of above-neutral policy rates. Fed Chair Powell's speech at Jackson Hole has noted the progress on loosening the labour market and stated that "we do not seek or welcome further cooling in labor market conditions." The debate is now wide open on the pace and depth of Fed rate cuts, and how broadly they will be mirrored around the world (see Friday Finish – US Economics: Setting Up a September Cut).

On the EM side, we had earlier seen some adjustments of very high interest rate premia, with Brazil's BCB lowering its Selic rate from 13.75% in August 2023 through to a pause at 10.5% in May 2024 and Banco de Mexico cutting rates 25bp in March, to 11.00%. Japan is a notable exception, having hiked rates at its July MPM to 25bp and signaling further rate hikes ahead. Our economists believe the market overreacted to the hawkish guidance at the press conference, but we still expect another rate hike in January 2025, to 0.5%, bucking the trend of global easing (see Japan Economics and Strategy: Answering your questions on Japan macro developments, 6 Aug 2024).

## Asia and EM – a relatively shallow policy easing cycle

Looking ahead, our economics team's base case forecasts call for a relatively shallow easing cycle, as discussed in Asia Economics: The Viewpoint: How Asian Central Banks Will React. We can group central banks into the following categories, given our base case forecasts to end-2025:

• Deepest easing: We see the largest rate cut cycles coming in Colombia (-325bp to end-2025) and Mexico (-225bp).

• Fed matched: Saudi Arabia, Hong Kong, Singapore, Czech Republic, and Hungary are expected to match the Fed rate cuts between now and end-2025 (-175bp).

• Cautious cutters: Our economists see China only easing policy modestly, despite deflationary dynamics (-20bp), while Taiwan (-25bp) starts from a low rate. Australia, South Korea, Poland, and Chile are balancing slower disinflation and/or household leverage concerns (-75bp). Meanwhile, Indonesia, Thailand, the Philippines, and South Africa (-100bp) are expected to remain relatively cautious in their policy adjustments.

• Hawkish surprise: Our economists expect no rate cuts in India or Malaysia given strong growth outlooks. In Brazil the debate is whether or not the BCB will hike rates in the near-term, but we expect no cuts by end-2025. Meanwhile, we expect Japan will push ahead with another +25bp hike in January.

## The Historical Experience of Fed Easing Cycles

## Historical precedents for market/sector reaction during Fed cutting cycles

## Five historical precedents since 1995

We explore five historical Federal Reserve easing cycles dating back to 1995. We exempt the rate cuts following the COVID shock because subsequent events bear little resemblance to today's markets or historical experience, and also because they continued an easing cycle already underway from the preceding summer. Here we summarise the key precedents:

• August 1, 2019 – "Trade Tensions": -75bp, from 2.375% to 1.625% on October 31, 2019.

• September 18, 2007 – "Global Financial Crisis": -325bp, from 5.25% to 2.00% on 30 April 2008, followed by another -200bp in cuts to 0-0.25% from October 8, 2008 to December 16, 2008.

• January 3, 2001 – "Dot Com Bust": -425bp, from 6.5% to 1.75% on 11 December 2001, followed by another 75bp in cuts to 1.00% between November 6, 2002 and June 25, 2003.

• September 29, 1998 – "LTCM/Russia Default": -75bp, from 5.5% to 4.75% on November 17, 1998.

• July 6, 1995 – "Mid-Cycle Adjustment": -75bp, from 6% to 5.25% on January 31, 1996.

In terms of depth, our base case forecast for 175bp in Fed Funds rate cuts would put it in between US soft landings (75bp in 1995, 1998, and 2019) and hard landings (-525bp in 2007 and -425bp in 2001).

A caveat on EM precedents: We note that EM-specific events in the historical record may confound or exaggerate some of the event-study relationships around Fed easing cycles. In particular, we note that the 1995 soft landing and 1998 post-LTCM cuts came in the wake of regional crises (1995 in Mexico, 1997-98 in Southeast Asia and Korea), so that the starting point of weak performance and low valuations was a key factor in the subsequent apparent recovery.

## Limited implications for relative Value/Growth performance

In soft landing scenarios, the historical differences in returns between Value and Growth were marginal. We believe this was because the Fed policy rates on average were still high in absolute terms during the early stages of the Fed's pivot (>5% in all scenarios, except 2019). Therefore, the duration effect was not yet meaningful to help Growth outperform Value.

We believe the situation will be similar in the upcoming Fed pivot too: Given history, we might see a muted performance differential between Value and Growth during the first four rate cuts, and any meaningful outperformance of Growth over Value might only come if the market starts to price in further rate cuts. We believe Growth investors are likely to be patient during the early stages of the pivot.
