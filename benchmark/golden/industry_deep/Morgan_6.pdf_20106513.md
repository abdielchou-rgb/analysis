## Approaching all-time high amid reliance on momentum--the real picture; noticeable loss of stable investors

\* This note is based on data as of 4:00pm JST on February 13, 2024.

Nikkei 225 approaching 38,000 range: The Nikkei 225 rally has heated up by more than expected, surging to 38,010 on February 13. With US and German equity markets hitting all-time highs, speculation that Japanese equity will be next appears to be the lone driver. Of course, very few negatives are evident in domestic and overseas markets (volatility has stayed low), and US 10-year yields of around 4.1% (= dollar strength, yen weakness) have made it difficult for both bulls and bears to react (with regard to risk assets), so more than a few investors believe conditions overall are just right for Japanese equities.

However, as we explain below, our quantitative and derivatives strategy (QDS) team believes both futures and cash equity are looking increasingly overreliant on momentum and are now out of step with fundamental trends such as earnings and stable buying by overseas investors. Meanwhile, powerful flows led by algorithmic-type trading are currently driving the market toward its all-time high (December 29, 1989, close of 38,915). In current circumstances, we cannot rule out the possibility that speculative players specialized in fast finger trading will continue to monopolize, and it looks as though we will see a game of chicken between (1) the Nikkei 225 hitting a new record high, and (2) a shift in overseas market conditions or a change in the balance of Nikkei future orders leading to warning signs of a near-term ceiling (a typical example of a technical indicator would be a long lower shadow on a daily candlestick).

The QDS team<sup>’</sup>s view on near-term trading suggests a period of observation, based on the belief that (1) investors should prepare for profit-taking by speculators combined with a pickup in volatility (Nikkei 225 VI in the 25 point range) once the Nikkei 225 reaches a new record high, which we believe will not be sustained at this time, and (2) investors bullish on Japanese equity over the medium term should wait for excessive speculative long positions to be closed out (Nikkei 225 in the 35,500-37,000 range could provide a good reentry point).

1.　 Expansion of CTA long Nikkei future positions: First, net long positions in Nikkei futures held by trend-following CTAs have expanded at a rapid pace and are now looking precarious. Our QDS team estimates that these CTA positions are now at around a three-year high and roughly 55% of them were accumulated since December last year (we estimate a Nikkei 225 breakeven level of around 35,550). We believe the positions have built up simply with the backing of risk-on sentiment in overseas equity markets (in the US especially) and not for reasons specific to Japanese stocks. We note that this trend-following strategy in Nikkei futures increasingly appears closely connected with the situation in US stocks (Figures 1-2).

It is also true that gaps that open up between trend-following speculative positions and earnings momentum do not always revert. Looking retrospectively, there have been times when trend followers have hit the mark. However, on this occasion, we believe that the statistically low-risk environment (i.e., low volatility and low stock correlation) has boosted the re-leveraging capacity of trend followers in a broader sense (i.e., including CTAs and risk parity funds). Compared with long/short funds focused on bottom-up fundamentals, there has been a noticeable increase in the Japanese equity exposure of systematic (quantitative investing) funds (Figure 5). There is a danger that the kind of trend-led aggressive buying we seem to be seeing currently in futures and other index-related products will lose steam sooner or later.

2.　Euphoric cash equity momentum backing semiconductors as the only winner: Second, in the cash equity market, momentum factor performance looks overdone, to an extent that we have not witnessed for around six years, amid flows suggestive of semiconductor stocks being the one and only area of strength. At the same time, the number of overseas investors (average number of investors per stock) has started to decline again (as of end-January). With a narrower range of participants, momentum appears to have the run of the market.

Our QDS team<sup>’</sup>s calculations of overseas investor cash equity flows (by sector, as of February 9) suggest semiconductor/semiconductor equipment stocks and autos/auto parts stocks remain the most popular targets of net buying year to date (Figure 7). Comparing the popularity rankings with trading orders made during 2023, it would seem that the trend in total overseas investor transactions and the logic behind them is an extension of what we saw last year.

As a result, the momentum factor in Japanese equities continues to outperform, returning +11% YTD (long/short portfolio). Overseas investors often used to remark that momentum investing does not work with Japanese equity, but we calculate that it has delivered a strong performance, with a Sharpe ratio (12 months) of over +1.0 (Figures 9-10).

This does not mean, of course, that we can feel completely happy at the emergence of momentum returns. As noted above, the effects of momentum in futures and other index products are spilling over into cash equities through arbitrage-related trades, so momentum effects in the cash equity market occurring since December 2023 are themselves (indirectly) inextricably linked to US stocks. Furthermore, although we need to allow for the possibility of coincidence, after past cases in which momentum factor performance in the Japanese equity market has delivered a Sharpe ratio of over +1.0, a global risk-off event or “shock<sup>”</sup> has occurred, triggering an about-turn. Until now, many observers have theorized that this is because, when global market conditions are excessively upbeat, the Japanese equity market (having been left behind at inexpensive levels) is the last place where speculative money ends up, urged on by the “search for yield<sup>”</sup> and “leveraging plays<sup>”</sup>. Although this time around is likely to be different, the performance of trend-following strategies in the cash equity market does appear statistically overdone.

This point is underlined by the fact that the number of overseas investors holding cash equity is on the decline. By compiling the number of overseas shareholders per stock (average, compiled from various report disclosures) for all TOPIX 500 constituent stocks, it is clear that overseas investor numbers are declining for both (1) the top 100 stocks by market cap (= large caps) and (2) the lowest 100 stocks by P/B ratio (= value stocks) (Figures 11-12). Overseas shareholders included in such reports are, of course, likely to be medium/long-term investors. Given the increase in net buying by overseas investors in total since last year, it would seem the market trend thus far reflects (1) a handful of medium/long-term overseas investors substantially increasing the amount of their shareholdings per stock, and/or (2) a sharp rise in the number of small-lot/short-term overseas investors that are not represented in the data compilation. Putting aside whichever of these is the real cause, it seems unlikely from the perspective of both stock and investor numbers that the current ongoing stock market rally is leading to the formation of wide-ranging medium/long-term capital inflows. In other words, we need to be mindful that the current cash equity market is being driven by momentum of limited breadth.
