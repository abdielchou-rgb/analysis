## The Sovereign Supply Outlook

Investor focus on sovereign bond supply ebbs and flows. Investors usually cite supply, ex post, as a factor in market movements for which other explanations may not be obvious or easily accepted. For example, investors often cited supply to explain the 2023 summertime rise in Treasury yields (see The Real Drivers of the US Treasury Bond Market Rout for our view on the move).

Twice a year, we update our projection for sovereign bond supply across the G7. In doing so, we help set and solidify expectations for the amount of supply coming down the pipeline. Relative to our 2024 year-ahead outlook for supply, our expectations remain very similar today.

We expect net coupon government bond supply (not including bills) to increase significantly in 2024, after taking central bank activity into account. We also expect the macro environment to encourage investors to buy this supply at lower yields than we saw last year. Exhibit 1 shows that, across the G7, we expect gross coupon bond supply to increase by US\$1.05 trillion in 2024 vs. 2023.

After netting out redemptions, shown in Exhibit 2 , coupon bond net issuance across the G7 should increase by a lesser US\$782 billion - amounting to an 18% Y/Y increase. US Treasury supply drives the majority of this increase (up 30% Y/Y), with the euro area, UK, Canada, and Australia contributing as well.

![](images/57451df6403c29aecbedc15697526d163f8364dc8dde79c28bd778d136806d48.jpg)
Source: Morgan Stanley Research estimates, National Treasuries

Exhibit 2: Gross coupon bond issuance net of redemptions, notional in USD, '07-'23 actual, '24 expected
![](images/23e952f3ded145ea7487b18b1719515fe9e137058f0ebfdaf7f9254342ca6848.jpg)
Source: Morgan Stanley Research estimates, National Treasuries

Exhibit 3 shows the impact we expect G7 central banks to have on net coupon bond supply. We show this effect with a negative number because central banks remove supply available for public purchase.

We expect G7 central banks to remove the least amount of coupon bond supply in 2024 since the Great Financial Crisis. The Bank of Japan is the only G7 central bank buying bonds in 2024, reaching US\$226 billion worth, on our estimates. The Bank of England is the only central bank selling bonds back to the market, reaching \$61 billion worth on our estimates.

From gross coupon supply net of redemptions, we subtract net central bank purchases in order to understand the amount of supply available to the private sector, shown in Exhibit 4 . Net coupon bond supply after central bank purchases should amount to US \$2.55 trillion in 2024, up from US\$1.66 trillion in 2023, for an increase of \$892 billion.

Our projected increase in G7 coupon bond supply available for purchase won't be the largest Y/Y increase in the past 15 years. 2009, 2018, and 2021 saw larger Y/Y increases. Nevertheless, if the economic environment turns into one that encourages investors to buy government bonds, sovereign supply won't inhibit yields from falling, in our view.

We often get asked, "who will buy the supply?" The question seems perfectly logical and understandable, given the sizes on offer. While we provide estimates for how much supply will be available for purchase, we don't see much value in estimating who will provide the demand. Why?

Even if we could provide an accurate accounting of which investors would buy, we would also have to forecast when those investors would buy and at what price. Otherwise, knowing who will buy the supply has little meaning to those interested in predicting market movements and adding alpha to portfolios.

Given the near-impossibility of forecasting the answer to "when and at what price", we focus on understanding and forecasting the macro environment in which investors of all types will make the decision as to whether they buy, hold, or sell.

Putting demand to the side, doesn't supply affect prices by itself? Yes, but the timing of that effect matters. We discuss the impact of supply on price in Between a Rock and a Hard Place. In short, supply moves prices when expectations for changes in supply form, all else equal. And until we get past the US general election, we don't think expectations for supply will change much.

## Lasso-ing the Dollar

In January, we described an approach to generate fair value estimates for G10 currencies versus the USD. The first step in the process uses LASSO variable selection to determine which independent variables explain a high proportion of exchange rate variation while minimizing multicollinearity.

The approach selects different variables over time to explain variation in currencies. For example, Exhibit 5 shows which variables the model selects as providing high explanatory value for AUD/USD over time:

Exhibit 5: Variables that explain AUD/USD over time
![](images/60e93abe5d753f371ffb2c4bcc500e48661db8785120995b57db64c9c435a8a3.jpg)
Source: Bloomberg, Morgan Stanley Research

The chart suggests that commodity prices, yield differentials, and market sentiment on the Chinese revenue/growth outlook are key correlates for AUD/USD. This approach suggests AUD is around 2.8% undervalued against USD given substantial recent rises in copper prices and equity baskets tied to metals and mining and China revenue.

Using a similar approach, we can aggregate across all G10 currencies to determine which variables matter to the USD over time.

Exhibit 6 shows the frequency with which the LASSO model selects each variable as significant for CCY/USD. A wider band for any of the variables means that the model chooses that variable for a higher proportion of G10 currencies against the USD.

Exhibit 6: Variables that explain USD movement over time
![](images/620f22331ea9dd158a47ac55e47eb066e9a14493f81e1d39e960edfbfcd6863f.jpg)
Source: Bloomberg, Morgan Stanley Research

It's clear from the chart above that there was a clear structural break in spring 2022 among which variables highly correlate to the USD. From the onset of COVID until spring 2022, 10y US real yields and 1y yield differentials explained a significant portion of variation in the USD.

After spring 2022, yield differentials took a backseat to stock indices (S&P 500 and Euro Stoxx 50) and sectoral equity baskets (China-linked equities and stocks levered to inflation). That shift may be related to the Russian invasion of Ukraine and associated movements in commodity prices.

By contrast, copper prices are notably constant in their explanatory power for movements in the broad USD. This relationship illustrates that copper is a particularly powerful barometer of global growth expectations. Copper is used in a wide variety of manufacturing and construction applications.

While Copper is widely priced in US Dollars (and therefore has a mechanical negative relationship to movements in the USD), there are reasons to believe that there is a unique and causal copper-USD link. A number of commodities are priced in USD, yet our variable selection process indicates copper is a better explanatory variable. And causality tests suggest that copper prices granger-cause movements in the DXY (p = 0.024) while the opposite is not true (p = 0.467).

## Euro area

We investigate curves behaviour in previous ECB cutting cycles. We find that, compared to historical moves after the ECB first cut, markets seem to underestimate the steepening of both EUR 2s10s swap and 10s30s in the coming year (though comparisons between cycles might be less informative for 10s30s given the sensitivity to volatility).

Recently, slopes have been rangy despite the ECB easing approaching. In our opinion, the historically high correlation between the EUR and USD front-end explains partly the lack of steepening ahead of the ECB easing cycle. We believe EUR 1y1y swap offers a better risk/reward than EUR 2s10s swap as things stand and like receiving it as a structural trade.

In cash EGBs, we look at recent Bank of Italy statistics regarding the holders' composition of Italian debt. We note that the latest data print reveals one of the biggest buying flows from non-residents investors since October 2023. We argue that going forward, the support from households buying could gradually phase off given their yield sensitivity on the view that rates will drift lower from here going ahead.

We note that support to fill this gap could come either from domestic banks, which have been net sellers of BTPs recently, or from non-residents. On the latter, we review their composition and their domicile as it could have implications on future Italian debt demand.

## United Kingdom

In the UK, the April Inflation Report might determine whether or not the BoE will start cutting rates at the June MPC, and will be a highly important data release. Focus will be on the service reading, with the BoE forecast of 5.5% being the key threshold that could determine the price action.

Market easing expectations for the current year appear to have stabilized around \~60bp, a plausible amount given that the modal view within the MPC is likely to be for three cuts, and plenty of data could alter the evolution of the Bank Rate.

Lastly, we look at the current developments in the repo market, as the increasing take-up of the STR facility, together with a cheapening in GC repo, prompted talk that a stop in active QT sales in the near term is likely. While we think active QT sales should stop next year, this reflects the high amount of passive roll-off and could be only temporary.

## Japan

We expect that JGB price action will continue to be driven largely by changes in rate expectations as influenced by movements in USD/JPY as well as fluctuations in the term premium as influenced by speculation about a possible reduction in BoJ JGB purchases and UST yield.

We think that positioning for near-term BoJ normalization may stay at an elevated level due to sticky USD/JPY price action. However, we don’t think the market can price in a much more hawkish BoJ path. Japan’s unexpectedly weak GDP print for 1Q 2024 (even considering some technical factors) suggests a fourth successive contraction in private consumption.

We believe that markets will want to see hard evidence of this “virtuous cycle” before pricing in a more hawkish BoJ rate hike trajectory. We suggest sticking with a bullish bias on duration in the long-end, as we think the lower UST yields should push the long-end term premium lower. We continue to suggest being long 20y JGBs against 10s and 30s.

With the BoJ not conducting another reduction in JGB purchases on Friday (particularly for 3-5y), we believe the market will take some relief, and this could in turn help to stabilize ASW spreads from the short end through the 10y sector. We thus suggest sticking with previously recommended 5y ASW longs as well as 5y JGB long vs pay 7y OIS (DV01 1.75 vs 1) positions.

## CPI fails to convince markets

A slightly weaker core CPI print relative to the Bloomberg median failed to convince the Treasury market that the Fed will think about rate cuts in the coming months. The probability of a July meeting rate cut moved sideways to about 25%, while the probability of a rate cut by September did go up to nearly 50% (see Exhibit 7 ). ## EUR Curve: Curves in ECB easing cycles

This week, we investigate curves behavior in previous ECB cutting cycles. The study is based on four easing cycles, including one from the Bundesbank from 1992. During three of the cycles, the Fed started cutting ahead of the ECB or Bundesbank; in 1992 and 2008, the Fed had started cutting \~2y and \~1y ahead of the Bundesbank and ECB, respectively. In 2001, the ECB lagged the Fed by \~ four months while in 2011, the Fed had been on hold since late 2008.

We decided to focus on swap curves (vs. EUR 6m) and when unavailable the reconstructed Deutsche Mark swap curve (i.e., before 1999). The table in Exhibit 16 shows the level of different slopes and flies one month before the first cut, on the day of the first cut as well as three months and one year after the start of the easing cycle.

For the current cycle, our economists expect the ECB to start its easing cycle in June, with a 25bp cut. Hence we report the levels of slopes and flies one month before (i.e., from last week), and the levels implied by forwards (marked with a \*).

Noticeably, currently slopes are extremely inverted compared to previous cycles, which is something we highlighted before in Land Of Confusion, where we studied curves in ECB plateaux. It still holds given 2s10s and 10s30s barely steepened since the ECB reached its peak rate in September 2023.

More specifically, EUR 2s10s swap was inverted only before the start of the easing cycle in 1992, while EUR 10s30s was flatter only in 2008. The macro environment was different to today's with notably the eurozone being in a recession, stocks selling-off, leading to high implied volatility for stocks which in turn tends to flatten 10s30s and 30s50s.

For the above reasons, we explained in Land Of Confusion that for the back end of the curve - which is sensitive to volatility - drawing conclusions from the plateaux period ahead of the 2008 and 2011 cuts was difficult. In Exhibit 18 , we also report the level of key macroeconomic indicators such as CPI, PMIs, NFPs etc. one year into the cutting cycle as well as equity indices and EPS one-year change.

Exhibit 17 gives a clearer view of the behaviour of curves, with the change in slopes and flies from one month before the first cut to one year post, as well as the breakdown of that change through time. Below, we highlight the main findings:

EUR 1s2s: After the first cut, 1s2s steepened in all the cycles where data is available though the magnitude of of the steepening varied markedly. This reflects in the difference between the average and median change in 1s2s. The largest steepening was in the 2008/9 cutting cycle as markets underestimated the amount of cuts the ECB was going to deliver during the first year of the cutting cycle by \~170bp (see Exhibit 32 in SkiPPing A Heartbeat into CPI).

EUR 2s10s: Within the window studies, 2s10s steepened consistently in the three months after the first cut and by a minimum of 9bp. In the month leading to the first cut, however, results are less homogeneous with rangy 2s10s in 2001 and 2011 amid unchanged ECB expectations.

We note that in previous plateaux, from the peak in rates to the first cut, 2s10s steepened on average by 36bp (median: 48bp) (see Land Of Confusion). In the current plateau, 2s10s barely steepened, suggesting a larger upside potential. Additionally, 46bp of steepening is priced in forwards over \~1y vs. an historical average steepening of 77bp. Especially, in the three months post first cut, the slope steepened in every cycle while 4bp are priced at the moment.

EUR 2s5s10s: There is no clear pattern on the fly; it cheapened 25bp+ in 2001 and 2008, in sympathy with the largest steepening of 1s2s, while it richened 25bp+ in 1992 and 2011 over a one-year horizon. Markets currently price a 16bp cheapening over \~1y.

EUR 10s30s and 5s10s30s: Over a one-year period, 10s30s steepened 35bp+ in two instances (2008 and 2011) as equities recovered and PMIs edged higher (see Exhibit 18 ). In 2001, the Eurostoxx 50 index dropped 20% over one year, likely pressuring EUR 10s30s lower over that period. We note in 2008 and 2011, 10s30s did not steepen in the three months after the first cut and the 5s10s30s cheapened, once again likely linked to volatility dynamics. Markets price do not price any steepening the year following the first ECB (expected) cut.

## Are we close to the steady state of reserves supply?

The other relevant topic drawing attention in recent weeks is the increasing take-up of the STR facility along with the move higher in the Repo GC/Bank Rate spread ( Exhibit 42 ), which has led to market talk that we are getting closer to the level of steady state of reserves supply, and that it might prompt a stop in active QT sales in the near term.

We believe active sales are likely to stop over the next year, though this might be only temporary, with the decision to be taken at the September MPC meeting when the BoE is expected to set the target reduction of the APF portfolio for the Oct'24/Sep'25 period. Indeed, over the past two years, the total APF reduction target was set to \~£100bn, and we suspect the bar for increasing the target is high. Given that we have APF redemptions at \~£90bn, we think it is plausible for active sales to stop, as they would be irrelevant anyway. The big question remains – what happens to active sales in the medium term, especially when we move towards the steady level of reserve supply?

According to a recent speech from Andrew Hauser, former Executive Director for Markets, a steady level of reserves can be estimated within a range of £335-495bn, the so-called "Preferred Minimum Range of Reserves" (PMRR), which should amount to the sum of banks’ transactions and precautionary demand. As current reserves are at \~£760bn, we should be far from an equilibrium level. That said, when considering APF portfolio roll-off and TFSME redemptions, the level of reserves can drop close to the £500bn handle by the end of 2025.

In the last MPC press conference, the recent increase in the STR take-up was addressed by Deputy Governor Ramsden following a related question; he pointed to temporary factors behind the move, such as month-end flows, the past syndication, and gilt redemptions. Gilt redemptions have likely played an important role given the significant BoE holding of the recently expired gilt worth \~£19.6bn, implying a significant drop in reserves as the bond expired.

That said, there is also evidence that liquidity is shrinking, when looking for example at the SONIA/Bank Rate spread which is (slowly) moving up ( Exhibit 43 ), despite the spread being far from a level that implies reserves scarcity (for instance pre-QE). Exhibit 43

## Early QT speculation

## Unexpected cut to 5y–10y offer amount

The BoJ’s surprise announcement on Monday (May 13) of a reduction in its offer amount for that day’s JGB-buying operation in the 5y–10y sector has seemingly left many market participants wondering whether a commencement of quantitative tightening (QT) might be imminent.

The BoJ has, of course, gone on the record as saying that it has no intention of using the size of its JGB purchases as a policy tool and will simply allow the Financial Markets Department to exercise its discretion with regard to day-to-day offer amounts as deemed appropriate given prevailing market and supply/demand conditions.

But Monday’s reduction was somewhat difficult to fathom given that supply/demand had not appeared especially tight in the 5y–10y sector of late. It thus seems inevitable that some market participants will conclude that the decision was motivated by a desire to curb the JPY’s slide (and thus consistent with the aforementioned recent change in the BoJ’s tone vis-à-vis exchange rate fluctuations).

But as we explained in "Will the BoJ Move On Yen Weakness?", we ourselves are not convinced that the BoJ will in fact dial back its JGB absorption by way of response to the currency’s weakness. While the import price index did indeed climb further in JPY terms in April, the percentage increase to date has not been particularly alarming by longer-term standards (or by comparison with 2022) (see Exhibit 48 ).

Moreover, the average USD/JPY exchange rate used in calculating the April import price index was already on the high side at 153.5. Given that USD/JPY has more recently been hovering around 155.0, there seems to be little danger of the JPY-denominated import price index surging sharply higher in May unless there is a significant change in contractcurrency prices. Any immediate impact on underlying inflation thus looks likely to be minimal at this juncture.

Furthermore, it is difficult to envisage the BoJ being able to exert major influence on the FX market even if it were to commence QT in the hope of arresting the JPY’s decline. Investors could conceivably view a reduction in JGB purchases as reason to price in a somewhat more hawkish normalization trajectory.

As we discussed in "JPY | MoF Intervenes?", however, speculations for significant Japan-led Fed-BoJ monetary policy “convergence” are nevertheless liable to remain muted. And it also bears noting that USD/JPY ended up falling only very briefly in the wake of Monday’s reduction in 5y–10y JGB purchases.

We thus agree with our economists that the BoJ is unlikely to mount an immediate monetary policy response to the JPY’s weakness. We believe that Monday’s reduction was basically aimed at testing the market’s reaction, with the central bank perhaps also assessing that such a move would be easier to digest given that US interest rates were at the time declining in the wake of weak US jobs data.

The reduction puts the BoJ on track to buy around JPY5.7 trillion in JGBs per month in gross terms, which is basically equal to average monthly redemptions for the next year (see Exhibit 49 ). Any further reduction will thus constitute “QT” and is accordingly likely to require authorization by the monetary policy board.

Given that the BoJ has said only that it plans to start reducing its JGB purchases at some point in the future (without mentioning the possible timing or pace of curtailment), we see only limited scope for any further cuts to be made at the discretion of the Financial Markets Department. Our economists are currently forecasting that QT will commence from October.

## Looking ahead

We expect that JGB price action will continue to be driven largely by changes in rate expectations as influenced by movements in USD/JPY as well as fluctuations in the term premium as influenced by speculation about a possible reduction in BoJ JGB purchases and UST yield.

We think that positioning for near-term BoJ normalization may stay at an elevated level due to sticky USD/JPY price action, but we don’t think the market can price in a much more hawkish BoJ path. Japan’s unexpectedly weak GDP print for 1Q 2024 (even considering some technical factors) suggests a fourth successive contraction in private consumption (see Exhibit 50 ).

This may give market participants cause to question the BoJ’s optimism vis-à-vis the existence of a “virtuous cycle between wages and prices” that will support a recovery in household spending once real wage growth finally turns positive.

As discussed in "Japan | Term-Premium-Driven Market", we believe that markets will want to see hard evidence of this “virtuous cycle” before pricing in a more hawkish BoJ rate hike trajectory, and are conversely unlikely to do so in the absence of relevant data.

We suggest sticking with a bullish bias on duration in the long-end, as we believe the lower UST yields should push long-end term premium lower. We continue to suggest being long 20y JGBs against 10s and 30s.

## Vol and elections: An event study

In our conversations with investors, a common question is how to approach rates volatility as we near the presidential election. There are, after all, a number of channels through which the election could impact volatility – for example, uncertainty around the winner and the policies pursued by that winner.

To provide a preliminary answer to this question, we look at rates volatility around previous election dates. More specifically, we look at both implied ( Exhibit 119 ), and realized vol ( Exhibit 120 ) for all elections in the 2000-2020 period. Our analysis focuses on the 3m10y tenor to avoid shorter-term monetary policy impacts. We use our backfilled SOFR data for 2012-2020 elections given this is what is currently traded, and LIBOR data prior to 2012.

However, we do note that idiosyncratic factors can dominate the election. Again, this model suggests that moves in yields in the 3m period surrounding elections is indeed higher on average than non-election periods.

## What does this mean for vol markets going forwards?

Our analysis has established both a theoretical and an empirical link between MBS hedging and rates volatility. Why should investors care about this, and what could it mean going forward?

By and large, we echo two points our MBS strategists have flagged, and which we think have not been fully appreciated by vol investors. These two points are:

1. There will likely be a change in the composition of MBS holdings as the Fed reduces its SOMA holdings. This could leave more MBS in the hand of investors who hedge their negative convexity, and thus result in increased realized vol.

2. Regulation changes on capital requirements could result in Banks increasing their hedging activity

Finally, we note that a rally in rates could increase the negative convexity of the outstanding MBS universe. Many outstanding MBS were issued when rates were amply lower than they are currently, resulting in a reduced amount of convexity to be hedge. This could result in increased MBS hedging, were interest rates to return closer to average MBS issuance levels.

Enhancements to a G4 10y government bond futures momentum strategy have produced higher Sharpe ratios and stronger returns, relative to total return government bond indices for the G4, US, Germany, Japan, and the UK since 2000. ## Trading Strategy 1 – "Trade Longs/Fade Shorts"

When the 5-day moving average crosses above the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract (long duration) and hold for a 25-business-day period. When the 5-day moving average crosses below the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract and hold for a 25-business-day period. In short, this strategy buys futures when the Simple Moving Average Crossover (SMAX) generates both a long and a short signal, given the historical outperformance of long signals traded long and underperformance of short signals traded short. Given that the SMAX could generate both a long and a short signal within the predefined holding period, an investor may have a 200% long position since each of the two signals would be traded in separate portfolio sleeves.

## Definition of terms

Buy/Long: The analyst expects the total or excess return (depending on the nature of the recommendation) of the instrument or issuer that is the subject of the investment recommendation to be positive over the relevant time period.

Sell/Short: The analyst expects the total or excess return (depending on the nature of the recommendation) of the instrument or issuer that is the subject of the investment recommendation to be negative over the relevant time period.

Selling protection or Buying Risk: The analyst expects that the price of protection against the event occurring will decrease over the relevant time period.

Buying protection or Selling Risk: The analyst expects the price of protection against the event occurring will increase over the relevant time period.

Pay: The analyst expects that over the specified time period the variable rate underlying the swap agreement that is the subject of the investment recommendation will increase.

Receive: The analyst expects that over the specified time period the variable rate underlying the swap agreement that is the subject of the investment recommendation will decrease.

Unless otherwise specified, the time frame for recommendations included in the Morgan Stanley Fixed Income Research reports is 1 - 3 months and the price of financial instruments mentioned in the recommendation is as at the date and time of publication of the recommendation.

When more than one issuer or instrument is included in a recommendation, analyst expects one part of the trade to outperform the other trade or combination of other trades included in the recommendation on a relative basis.
