## Could Easier Financial Conditions Mean Fewer Rate Cuts?

US labor market data – perhaps helped by immigration/labor supply inflows (discussed below and detailed in the Friday Finish) – resurrected the idea of "immaculate disinflation". Despite robust economic growth – evidenced by labor market strength – inflation should continue moderate, led by slowing wage inflation on the back of more supply of labor.

Labor supply – the lynchpin keeping the theme in play – continues to increase, according to data from the household survey of employment. The influx of labor supply relative to demand allows for a continued deceleration in wage growth.

The interplay between the supply of labor (increasing) and the demand for labor (decreasing) changed dramatically over the past two years. Labor demand, proxied by the total number of employed and job openings, stagnated over the past two years. Labor supply, meanwhile, rose dramatically (see Exhibit 1 ).

As such, the labor deficit – once as high as 6.2mm jobs – improved to a deficit of only 2.4mm jobs this February (based on February 2024 employment data and January 2024 job openings data, see Exhibit 2 ).

The labor market remains historically tight, on a pre-pandemic basis (see Exhibit 3 ). But the pace at which it loosened over the past year suggests that wage growth should continue to decelerate for the next 6 months, at least (see Exhibit 4 ).

However, many investors remain skeptical. If the Fed lowers rates, based on lower inflation alone, less restrictive/more accommodative monetary policy may sow the seeds of stronger growth, more labor demand, and another bout of labor market tightening.

Even a modest reduction in the fed funds target range, in line with the December 2023 dot-plot, seems to worry investors. Given how financial conditions eased over recent months, investors feel that rates cuts – even those in line with market pricing – might ease financial conditions further, on top of conditions they think have eased too much already.

We think this view underestimates the restrictive stance of monetary, masked by a tremendous fiscal and liquidity impulse (discussed last week). Instead, this view suggests that recent changes in financial conditions translate into economic activity very quickly. The view also suggests that changes in financial conditions from a year or more ago don't matter much.

However, these views do not align with analysis from Federal Reserve Board staff. In June 2023, the Fed published on a new index to measure US financial conditions, called the US Financial Conditions Impulse on Growth (FCI-G), shown in Exhibit 5 and Exhibit 6 .

As of January 2024, it implies that quarterly changes in financial conditions over the past year would add 0.38pp to GDP growth over the next year.

However, quarterly changes in financial conditions over the past 3 years would subtract 0.33pp from growth over the next year. Which index displays a better relationship with measures of growth?

Exhibit 7 and Exhibit 8 show how the 3y look-back FCI-G aligns better with changes in growth (measured by the ISM composite PMI) than the 1y look-back FCI-G.

We believe the longer look-back FCI-G better aligns with the traditional view of how monetary policy works. The 3y look-back period, as of January 2024, captures quarterly changes in financial conditions from January 2021. That means it captures the entirety of the financial conditions tightening that occurred in 2022.

However, given the rolling nature of the 3y look-back period, we know that the easier financial conditions in 2021 – shown clearly in how the 1y look-back FCI-G evolved in 2021 (see Exhibit 7 again) – should fade as 2024 progresses. That places the onus on financial conditions in 2024 to remain easy – in order to prevent the tightening of financial conditions in 2022 from dominating the 3y look-back period.

In our view, a decline in 10y Treasury yields needs to play an important role in keeping financial conditions easier this year. If not, we suspect the restrictive stance of monetary policy – evident in decelerating private sector credit growth, relative to nominal GDP – will meet head-on with a sharp deceleration in federal government credit growth (see Exhibit 9 and Exhibit 10 ).

## The Household Employment Survey and Immigration

Signs that the US labor market continues to slow – slowly – mount. Our economists, referencing a Brookings Institute report, suggest still-robust job growth may find an explanation in immigration trends.

These trends have shown clearly in the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) household survey data for well over a year now (see Exhibit 11 ), but the BLS data do not align with other sources (see Friday Finish: Immigration Headcount).

Monthly data on the foreign-born labor force and noninstitutional population come from the BLS household survey. As such, the data aren't strictly comparable from one year to the next, given annually updated population controls.<sup>2</sup>

Nevertheless, the household survey data for February suggest a breakout from the (unadjusted) pre-pandemic trend – hinting at 1.7 million more foreign-born persons reside in the US versus what the generalized trend would have suggested had the pandemic not occurred (see Exhibit 12 ).

The BLS's perspective on population growth differs dramatically from that of the Congressional Budget Office (CBO) – the source data from which the Brookings Institute published its report. CBO numbers suggest a much more dramatic increase in total population growth in 2023: 3.3mm for the CBO vs. \~2.3mm for the BLS.

• The CBO estimates derive from the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) and the Census Bureau data. The Census Bureau collects immigration data from two sources itself: (1) the monthly Current Population Survey (CPS), a.k.a., the household survey, and (2) the annual American Community Survey (ACS).

• The BLS data currently available, derived from the household survey, hints at total immigration around 2.3mm in 2023. That estimate includes legally admitted immigrants, refugees, temporary residents such as students and temporary workers, and undocumented immigrants.

• The CBO data suggests total immigration of 3.3mm in 2023, of which 2.3mm were "other-foreign-nationals", which include undocumented immigrants (see CBO’s Estimates of Net Immigration of People in the Other-Foreign-National Category in 2023)

The discrepancy of 1mm raises questions. Our economists think the lagged data on immigration used by the BLS might explain at least some of this discrepancy. The release of the ACS for 2023 - expected in September 2024 - should help clarify that view.

In the meantime, the household survey remains critical, as the data contributes to the calculated unemployment rate – which, at 3.9% in February, made a new high for the postpandemic cycle.

Indeed, even data from individual US states confirm a loosening in the labor market. US State-level unemployment rates rose into the end of December 2023 – with 46% of the 50 US States triggering the Sahm Rule (23 out of 50). Those 23 States account for 49% of US real GDP (see Exhibit 15 and Exhibit 16 ).

• These BLS revised these State-level unemployment rate estimates on March 1 and incorporated updated inputs, new population controls, reestimation of models, and adjustment to new census division and national control totals.

## Lower yields driven by weaker data prints

As we note above, the rally in duration since the beginning of March appears to have been driven by weaker data prints in comparison to last month. A question that arises, then, is whether yield changes driven by data prints are likely to persist on days without prints.

When analyzing yield curve movements on days when there are no data prints, we find that yield moves on days without data releases tend to reverse moves on past days with economic data releases. We identify days without data prints as days when the Bloomberg Economic Surprises Index (ECSURPUS Index) is null. Having isolated these days, we then regress changes in yields during periods without prints (usually 1 or 2 days at most) against changes in yields on the most recent day with a data print. ## Distracting headlines notwithstanding, the basic picture remains unchanged

This week has seen a slew of market-moving media headlines ahead of the March 18–19 BoJ monetary policy meeting. QUICK Monthly Market Survey (Bonds) results published at the end of February had pointed to a consensus that the central bank would bring an end to its negative interest rate policy (NIRP) in April (see Exhibit 45 ), but the past few days appear to have seen somewhat of a rethink.

One media agency has claimed that at least one of the nine policy board members considers a NIRP exit “appropriate”, while another has suggested that the government has now given its tacit consent for an exit in either March or April.

And in more official communications, board member Hajime Takata declared in a February 29 speech that “achievement of the 2 percent price stability target has finally started to come in sight” and alluded to the possibility of “shifting gears down from the current extremely powerful monetary easing”.

But there have, meanwhile, also been some more dovish headlines, including a suggestion that the BoJ might “revise down its assessment on consumption and factory output this month” (albeit while leaving its views on the outlook unchanged) after having seen GDP contract for a second straight quarter in 4Q 2023 and a contention that the BoJ has yet to reach a consensus on terminating NIRP at the March meeting or wait until April.

The latter report has claimed that while the BoJ has grown increasingly confident that this spring’s wage hikes will exceed those seen in 2023, it has yet to convince itself that higher wages will be adequately reflected in price-setting decisions.

Figures released by the Japanese Trade Union Confederation (“Rengo”) on March 7 indicate that affiliated labor unions have asked for an average wage hike of 5.85%, well up from 4.49% in 2023. The first official report on management responses is not due until next week on March 15, but with numerous anecdotal reports indicative of a general willingness to offer sizable pay increases.

Our economists expect that the BoJ will already feel sufficiently confident that wages are now in a sustainable upward trajectory. Assuming that the March 15 figures do indeed offer confirmation of businesses coming close to meeting unions’ demands as our economists envisaged, the remaining question for the central bank will be whether higher wages are likely to be reflected in price levels.

The net upshot appears to have been a modest increase in the market-implied probability of the BoJ exiting NIRP in March rather than April. But that precise timing should ultimately be less important from an interest rate perspective than the subsequent trajectory of monetary policy, and the key point in that regard is perhaps that we have yet to see a significant repricing of what might happen within the next year or so (see Exhibit 46 ).

## Ahead of the March BoJ MPM: What's our focus?

Our economists are forecasting that the BoJ will kick off its “normalization” process in March but see that any subsequent rate hikes will come only gradually. This is in part because exogenous conditions and domestic data do not currently look conducive to inflation accelerating once again prior to reaching +2%.

US interest rates have dropped back towards their recent floors as this month’s economic data have left much to be desired (see Exhibit 47 ).

And domestically, the MoF’s Financial Statements Statistics of Corporations by Industry have shown a strong surge in corporate capex. Another question for market participants is what might be done about the current “inflation-overshooting commitment” whereby the BoJ has effectively promised to keep expanding the monetary base until CPI ex fresh food exceed 2% in a stable manner.

Our economists expect this commitment to be ended (along with the NIRP) at the March meeting. As discussed in "Japan | Lower BoJ Expectations Meet Positive Carry Trades", the BoJ would find it difficult to reduce JGB purchase size further under this commitment, as the current average monthly gross purchase size is almost equivalent to the expected redemption from the BoJ's holdings.

Removing the commitment would make it possible to commence (de facto) quantitative tightening. The ASW curve as a whole has cheapened as such a scenario has been partially priced in (see Exhibit 50 ), but whether or not this cheapening continues is ultimately likely to depend on what actually happens to the BoJ’s JGB purchases following an exit from the NIRP.

## United States

USD | Chase the growth rabbit

Confucius said "if you chase two rabbits you will lose them both." USD is currently caught between two roughly balanced forces of US equity outperformance on the one hand and US growth outperformance on the other. We find that, historically, these two forces combined create choppy but low FX returns and dampen volatility.

Ultimately we think that USD will chase one of these rabbits more than the other, and the more likely one is growth outperformance. The strong correlation of US to RoW interest rates has made it more difficult for FX markets to price in clear policy divergence themes, but persistent growth differentials should make this apparent over time.

Until this transpires, we remain neutral on USD overall, but express our USDpositive bias via EUR/USD puts.

We continue to see Europe as the 'weak link' here, where already below-trend growth, amplified by weakness in China, should generate disinflationary forces via a negative output gap. A faster and deeper pace of cuts from the ECB versus the Fed should manifest itself in a lower EUR/USD.

## USD | Fixing into the print

As of March 8, inflation swaps imply an expectation that headline February CPI data released on March 12th will print at 3.18% - 0.5 standard deviations higher than the median economist’s forecast. We expect that a 0.5 standard deviation upside CPI surprise (i.e. the magnitude of the CPI surprise implied by the spread between the February CPI fixing) would be associated with a 0.3% rise in the DXY by 9am.

A common question we get from investors is: why are you still neutral on USD? Why aren't you more bullish?

After all, US growth continues to look impressive when compared to the rest of the world. Investors are increasingly focused on supply-side factors like immigration and productivity that suggest a higher potential growth rate for the US. Contrast this with RoW growth, where fiscal support in China continues to underwhelm and Europe growth, while beating expectations, still suggests a negative output gap.

In fact, if we take consensus estimates for 2024 growth between the US and the DXYweighted RoW, we're left with a nearly 1.6pp gap (see Exhibit 59 ), which we've only seen three times in the past 20 years excluding the Covid era (see Exhibit 60 ).

Despite this clear growth differential, there are two reasons why we're not sold on the idea that USD is a clear buy at this point:

• US and RoW rates have been too correlated, diminishing the US-RoW growth divergence pass-through to FX;

• US equities have outperformed the RoW which, paradoxically, is more USDnegative.

The lack of rate divergence has surprised us given the noticeable growth divergence we've seen. The correlation between US and EU rates, for example, is well above historical averages (see Exhibit 61 ). As noted here, there may be some good rationales for why the timing of the first cut might be correlated, but over time this should correct as investors focus on the pace and depth of cuts.

But another clearer factor has been restraining USD's strength: the outperformance of US equities. We explored this topic in 2020 when the US equity market was outperforming the RoW meaningfully and FX correlations to equities were near historical highs (see Exhibit 62 ).

Investors asked us: wouldn't the US equity outperformance attract capital into the US, turning the correlation positive? We found, in fact, the opposite: the more the S&P 500 outperforms global peers, the weaker USD gets, not stronger (see Exhibit 63 ).

Exhibit 61: The correlation between US and EU rates is near historical highs

But how does this interplay with rates? After all, if expectations for US data continue to expand versus global peers, then the correlation between US and RoW rates could break down, but the US and RoW equity divergence could continue.

How does USD perform when both US equities and US yields are outperforming the RoW? USD gains marginally: To test this thesis, we calculate the average returns since 2007 in scenarios conditioned on US equities outperforming/underperforming RoW peers and the US 10-year yield rising/falling relative to global peers.

The results present some interesting takeaways (see Exhibit 64 ). First, rate outperformance and equity outperformance pull in opposite directions. USD strengthens most when RoW equities and US yields outperform, and weakens the most when US equities outperform and US yields underperform. Both rates and equities rising more than peers (or falling less than peers) or vice versa see a mixed bag.

Second, when US equities are outperforming and US yields are compressing towards others, the market tends to trade in a uniform fashion of USD weakness with a low level of dispersion. This is consistent with this scenario likely corresponding with regime 1 of our four-regime USD framework, which sees widespread and uniform USD weakness.

Third, the scenario of both US equity and US rate outperformance sees surprising weakness in oil-sensitive currencies like CAD and NOK. As noted above, CAD and NOK underperform likely because of higher energy exposures. We also see noticeable divergence in other risk currencies; AUD weakens while NZD strengthens, for example.

In sum, though, we think that this broader scenario of simultaneous US equity and rate outperformance suggests a neutral approach to USD. Varied and idiosyncratic but generally low average returns possibly help to explain the low-volatility FX environment we find ourselves in. While it does perhaps suggest a modest USD-upside bias, it appears difficult to trade.

We maintain our short EUR/USD exposure via long-dated puts to position for a potential change in market regime and a potential breakdown in the high US-EU rate correlation. Enhancements to a G4 10y government bond futures momentum strategy have produced higher Sharpe ratios and stronger returns, relative to total return government bond indices for the G4, US, Germany, Japan, and the UK since 2000. ## Trading Strategy 1 – "Trade Longs/Fade Shorts"

When the 5-day moving average crosses above the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract (long duration) and hold for a 25-business-day period. When the 5-day moving average crosses below the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract and hold for a 25-business-day period. In short, this strategy buys futures when the Simple Moving Average Crossover (SMAX) generates both a long and a short signal, given the historical outperformance of long signals traded long and underperformance of short signals traded short. Given that the SMAX could generate both a long and a short signal within the predefined holding period, an investor may have a 200% long position since each of the two signals would be traded in separate portfolio sleeves.

## Definition of terms

Buy/Long: The analyst expects the total or excess return (depending on the nature of the recommendation) of the instrument or issuer that is the subject of the investment recommendation to be positive over the relevant time period.

Sell/Short: The analyst expects the total or excess return (depending on the nature of the recommendation) of the instrument or issuer that is the subject of the investment recommendation to be negative over the relevant time period.

Selling protection or Buying Risk: The analyst expects that the price of protection against the event occurring will decrease over the relevant time period.

Buying protection or Selling Risk: The analyst expects the price of protection against the event occurring will increase over the relevant time period.

Pay: The analyst expects that over the specified time period the variable rate underlying the swap agreement that is the subject of the investment recommendation will increase.

Receive: The analyst expects that over the specified time period the variable rate underlying the swap agreement that is the subject of the investment recommendation will decrease.

Unless otherwise specified, the time frame for recommendations included in the Morgan Stanley Fixed Income Research reports is 1 - 3 months and the price of financial instruments mentioned in the recommendation is as at the date and time of publication of the recommendation.

When more than one issuer or instrument is included in a recommendation, analyst expects one part of the trade to outperform the other trade or combination of other trades included in the recommendation on a relative basis.
