## Wait and see for the Fed, but not for credit

Fed minutes and recent communications continue to indicate a prudent stance toward inflation. The minutes of the January FOMC meeting reiterated that "participants generally" thought it would not be appropriate to cut rates "until they had gained greater confidence that inflation was moving sustainably toward 2%." Furthermore, "several participants" pointed to the risk of financial conditions being too loose at the time of the meeting, and "most participants" noted the risks of easing policy too quickly (see here).

The market is pricing in fewer cuts in 2024, but sees little chance that rates will be unchanged or higher by year-end. The rates market is now signaling an 84% chance of a first cut by June. Two weeks ago, the market was pricing in 1.6 cuts by then. Similarly, expectations are now for 3.2 cuts by YE24, versus 4.6 cuts two weeks ago. Still, the CME FedWatch Tool indicates that the market sees less than a 1% chance that rates will be unchanged or higher by year-end.

In credit, primary and secondary markets both seem sanguine. The credit market is sending constructive signals, as yield-driven demand remains strong, with higher yields continuing to drive spreads tighter, and in this week's high yield section, we argue that yields are even more attractive than they appear at surface level. Issuance has been markedly heavier than expected in both investment grade and high yield. IG issuance is particularly high, reaching record levels for the start of a year with nearly \$300bn of gross supply. However, new issue concessions averaged 3.8bp last week (versus 4.4bp on average since 2017), oversubscription was 4.5x (versus a 3.2x average since 2017), and the secondary market tightened across the board. Meanwhile, HY supply has been strong, up 67% y/y, at \$51bn.

Spreads are trading at or close to post-COVID tights across cash and derivatives markets. Investment grade is at 90bp (-4bp w/w) and reached a new tight since November 2021, high yield is at 316bp (-4bp w/w), close to its tightest since April 2022, and the Leveraged Loan Index is at \$96.35 (+\$0.13 w/w), near its highest since May 2022.

The CDX indices broke through the narrow ranges they had been trading in since the beginning of the year. As of midday Thursday, CDX IG was at 50.6bp (-3.4bp w/w) and CDX HY was at \$106.5 (+\$0.61 w/w). Both are at their tightest levels since March 2022 without adjusting for the rolls. Note that investors are now the longest risk in CDX across series in the past three years (see here).

## A shift to single-As

Beta compression has been a central theme in credit markets since December. As a result, we think 5y and 10y BBB industrials have compressed too much and see better value in single-As. We highlight basket trades along this theme.

The BBB-A industrial basis is now at 42bp, only 1bp away from the post-GFC tight reached in September 2021. The basis was above 60bp in mid-November. Regressions imply that BBBs are trading about 7bp rich to As.

The BBB/A spread ratio is close to its lowest level in two years outside of a short period during the regional bank turmoil. At 1.67x, it is nearly a standard deviation tight to its historical average after compressing nearly 0.2x over the past two months.

BBBs have benefited the most within industrials since the Fed signaled a pivot in mid-December, outperforming As by about 11bp, beta adjusted.

The most liquid parts of the curve have made the largest moves, leaving 5y and 10y single-As more attractive than BBBs. Single-A issuance has picked up, and investors seeking beta have flocked to the liquid parts of the curve, leaving the 5y and 10y points near the tight ends of their historical ranges. The spread ratio at the 5y point trades in the 17th percentile of its range back to 2010, while the 10y is in the 15th percentile.

Amid the recent rally, the BBB/A spread ratio has compressed to 1.67x, the tightest level since June 2023 (Figure 1). Index spreads have rallied by 11bp, to 90bp, over the past two months, since the Fed signaled a pivot. Beta compression has been a broad theme in the market since December, with both BBB/A and BB/BBB ratios compressing to nearly a full standard deviation tighter than historical averages. Even though we believe spreads have more room to tighten given the technical backdrop, at current levels, we think investors are not receiving fair compensation for holding riskier BBBs.

# More yield than meets the eye

Most of high yield is being priced to maturity because of low dollar prices. Since issuers rarely let bonds turn current, we think investors should prefer yield-to-current metrics over YTW. Re-racking index yields to account for this enhances the yield ofering of high yield by around 45bp, which should support demand.

For our latest US high yield company rating changes, click here. For our sector ratings, click here.

Dollar prices remain low across high yield. Twenty-eight percent of the index trades below \$90, and more than three-quarters trades below par, owing to the low coupons set during COVID and the subsequent spike in rates.

We think investors should consider yield-to-current for bonds trading below par, not just yield-to-worst. Low dollar prices have skewed the often-cited yield-to-worst metric to understate actual realized yields, in our view, since issuers rarely allow their bonds to become current (ie, less than one year to maturity).

The index yield is 45bp higher when accounting for this dynamic. This should structurally enhance demand for the high yield market, which could allow spreads to remain tighter for longer. We expect spreads to reach 85-90bp in investment grade and 290-315bp in high yield within the next six months.

We present trade ideas for bonds that we think should benefit from the yield-to-current shift. Figure 7 displays a basket of 14 bonds rated Overweight by Barclays' fundamental analysts that trade with a workout date equal to maturity.

## Market is pricing most of high yield to maturity

After the sustained series of rate hikes in 2022-23, the average dollar price of the high yield index fell from a 2021 high of \$105.77 to lows of \$83.72 before recovering to \$92.36 today (Figure 1). Still, with dollar price so depressed and duration at the 20-year lows ex-COVID, it should come as no surprise that a large amount of the index is being priced to maturity. In fact, more than three-quarters of the HY index is trading with a workout date equal to maturity (Figure 2).

Although we are in unprecedented times with yields so high and spreads so tight, it's unrealistic to expect that the vast majority of the index will be held to maturity. Historically, high yield bonds rarely mature and are typically called before becoming current (less than one year to maturity). Yields and spreads are not reflecting this at the moment. As such, we conduct an exercise, similar to that undertaken by our European colleagues last week, in which we reprice the market to reflect eligible bonds being called at par (or the applicable call price) 12 months prior to maturity. The resulting market yield is 44bp wider than current levels (Figure 3), reflecting additional return that investors should receive if the bonds are taken out as is typical rather than held to maturity.

The intuition here is that yields will be higher since the principal will be paid back sooner. Because dollar price is so low, the pull-to-par efects from returning principal a year earlier accounts for more yield than the two relinquished coupons. Of course, not all bonds will be called 12 months before maturity. They may be bullets, they may be tendered below par, or they may be called even sooner. Our methodology for calculating yield-to-current is as follows:

1. We assume no change to bonds trading below \$85 and rated single-B or below, as these bonds are closer to distress and more likely to be subject to tender or default rather than a par take-out.

2. We assume no change to bullet bonds, as they are structurally incapable of being called.

3. Any bond that is trading with a workout date other than maturity is left alone, as some call potential is already being priced in.

4. Similarly, any bond trading above par is left unchanged.

5. Finally, any bond maturing in less than 18 months is left unchanged, as efects this close to maturity are more unpredictable and dificult to model.

Under these assumptions, more than half of the bonds in the index reprice wider if we assume a workout date 12 months before maturity. We find that the overall index yield widens by 44bp, while CCCs widen by 59bp. These results should be encouraging to investors, since they mean bond holders will receive better yields on current bond holdings than are currently priced in.

In spread terms, we find that the overall index spread widens by 28bp, which leads us to believe that, although spreads are at record tights, they are perhaps not as tight as they appear. Figure 5 looks at the T-spread of the high yield market at both the index and rating bucket levels. When applying the above methodology to the index, we find that the T-spread of BB/B/CCC indexes should be 34/26/38bp higher when accounting for the yield dynamics. In percentile terms, this would mean that high yield index spreads, currently in their fourth percentile post-GFC as priced, would actually be in their 14th percentile.

The starkest diference is in BB spreads, currently in their third percentile, which would re-rack to the 20th percentile. Worth noting, however, is that BB issuers are most likely to be able to let their bonds become current, a dynamic that has led to the BB-heavy maturity wall discussed in our default forecast. One could also argue that these re-racked spreads should be compared with historical re-racked spreads for a more apples-to-apples analysis. We argue that the current price-spread relationship is unprecedented, however, since high yield spreads have never been this tight for the current level of dollar price (Figure 6). The last time spreads were this tight (2020-21), dollar price was higher and most bonds traded with a workout date prior to maturity.

Therefore, adjusting spreads and yields to an early take-out should actually ofer a better comparison with history. Either way, our analysis shows that while spreads are tight, they are not as tight as they seem.

## Rate reduction ABS market size and liquidity

RR ABS has been a quiet market for much of the past decade, but issuance has increased meaningfully in recent years, as the product has been used as a tool for expense recovery following severe weather events such as Winter Storm Uri. The outstanding RR ABS market size is nearly \$20bn now.

While we may not see over \$20bn in RR ABS issuance this year like we did in 2022, about \$2bn has priced year-to-date, which is a faster pace than we expected in our 2024 ABS supply forecast.

## Longer-duration alternatives in ABS

Much of the ABS universe consists of AAA-rated notes with weighted average lives (WAL) of three years or less. For investors looking for duration, RR ABS is a logical place to start, given the sector's inclusion in the Bloomberg Agg, its credit quality, its uptick in primary supply over the past few years, and the availability of tranches with 20+ year WAL at new issue.

Beyond RR ABS, a few more ABS subsectors that may be worth examining for investors looking for alternatives to IG corporate duration include whole business, structured settlements, solar, data center, wireless tower, and fiber. While not all of these sectors ofer the 20+ year WAL that can be found in rate reduction ABS new issue, investors can frequently find 5+ year WAL in these sectors.

## Rate reduction ABS overview

Below we provide a quick refresh on the RR ABS product and its economics. Additional insights on the RR ABS market can be found in a research note<sup>1</sup> from Structured Finance Association (SFA) and Moody's rating methodology paper.<sup>2</sup>

## Purpose

RR ABS are typically issued by utility companies to recover incurred costs associated with various initiatives such as environmental cleanups, energy transitions, and recovery of costs related to hurricanes and wildfires. The utility company raises capital by issuing bonds that are serviced by charging fees (surcharges) to the utility's consumers, based on usage, over time. For example:

• United Electric Cooperative of Texas issued the UNIELC 2022 deal to cover expenses incurred due to Winter Storm Uri in 2021.

• Centerpoint Energy Inc. of Indiana issued the SIGECO 2023-A deal to cover costs associated with retiring coal plants.

• PG&E Corp of California issued the PCG 2021-A deal to recover costs related to wildfire damages.

Per Moody's, the total surcharges paid to a utility in regard to its issued RR ABS notes are usually 10% or less of the customer's monthly payment. Through securitization of future surcharges, utilities are able to reduce the cost of borrowing. RR ABS are able to impose surcharges on utility customers based on "special legislation and financing orders typically issued by utility regulatory bodies."

## Note structure

The substantial majority of RR ABS notes are rated AAA. Tranches vary in WAL anywhere from under two years to over 25 years at new issue. RR ABS notes commonly amortize sequentially.

## Key credit considerations

To service the debt, utility companies forecast the future customer base and expected consumption that determines the surcharge rate for customers' consumption. The risk is that the customer base shrinks and/or consumption decreases, thereby afecting the cash flow from the ratepayers negatively. There is also some risk that the right to collect charges is impaired, but we view this as an unlikely occurrence.

## Risk mitigants

Per Moody's, the concept of "non-bypassability" of the charges is a key risk mitigant (ie, every customer serviced by the utility must pay the surcharge). This concept supports a linked risk mitigant, which is a true-up mechanism where any realized or expected shortfall in cash flows can be rectified by increasing the charge. Additionally, transactions typically have some type of credit enhancement from reserve accounts that are able to service the debt at least for a few months if cash flows from surcharges fall short.

Hefty credit support should prevent AAA losses, but we are increasingly wary of mezzanine holdings.

• Delinquency rates have picked up in the sector, including in multifamily, and are likely to keep increasing. The maturity pipeline is very heavy in 2024, even when accounting for extension options.

• We estimate that around 58% of the 2023 maturing loans are still outstanding. Deals that remained in their reinvestment periods have not have paid down, as managers kept the advantage of cheap in-place CRE CLO funding. • Rating agency actions have been almost non-existent, and we do not see any bonds on negative watch.

• In spite of the uptick in delinquency rates, CRE CLO spreads have tightened about 50bp over the past three months. Given better liquidity and diversification profiles in BSL CLOs, we do not find the CRE CLO sector very appealing.

## Delinquencies likely to increase further

We think that delinquency rates are likely to continue increasing. As discussed in our 2024 multifamily outlook, rent growth has slowed, and we expect another slow year for the sector in 2024. In addition, as expenses such as insurance costs increase, NOI growth is likely to be negative, increasing the risk of term defaults. Figure 7 shows that a significant share of CRE CLO loans have less than 1x DSCR. While the reported DSCR usually does not take interest rate caps into account, those caps need to be renewed and have become costly.

Moreover, as 2021/22 loans mature, most will struggle to refinance given higher interest rates and lower property valuations. While most of the maturing loans have extension options, the higher cost of interest rate caps may make the extension option less feasible. DSCR ratios for most loans maturing in 2024 are below 1x (Figure 8), implying the need to buy an in-the-money interest rate cap.

## OC and IC tests

CRE CLO deals are generally structured with overcollateralization (OC or par value) and interest coverage (IC) tests to protect investors in the senior classes (usually those investment grade rated) in a deal. Broadly speaking, the OC ratio for any given tranche is the ratio of the total collateral value and the amount outstanding of all the tranches above the given tranche (including itself), while the IC test calculates the ratio of the scheduled interest from the nondefaulted collateral and what is owed to the bonds, again senior to the given tranche including itself.

As loans turn delinquent, their collateral value declines, reducing the OC ratio. Similarly, as less interest is collected, the IC ratio also falls. If either of these values fall below the specific threshold (both usually around 120% of the lowest IG-rated tranche), then interest stops flowing to the lower-rated tranches and is instead used to pay down senior tranches, until both ratios get back to or above the specified threshold.

With delinquency rates increasing over the past year, these ratios have fallen. As of the latest remittance data,<sup>4</sup> three deals have failed their tests. RCMT 2023-FL12 failed both, while M360 2021-CRE2 failed its OC test and RCMT 2022-FL10 failed its IC test. In addition, we also saw a few issuers contribute funds to the CRE CLO trust, likely to prevent failure of the IC test (Figure 13).

We also highlight that the managers have some leeway in marking delinquent loans as defaulted. For example, if a loan is already 30 days delinquent or if the lender/servicer is in negotiation with the borrowers, the loan might not be marked defaulted and will not afect the OC and IC ratios. As a result, we have seen various examples where deals with higher delinquency rates still report very high OC and IC ratios. Both of the above trends will likely prevent a rapid amortization of the deals, even when delinquencies are picking up.
