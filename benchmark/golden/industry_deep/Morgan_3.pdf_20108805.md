# Govt Announces New Counter-Inflation Measures: Earlier Positive Real Wage Growth Possible

Resumption of electricity/city gas subsidies will lower headline inflation, possibly resulting in positive real wage growth realizing earlier than we had expected. Along with additional cash handouts to pensioners and low-income households, it could become a positive catalyst for consumption.

# Govt Announces New Counter-Inflation Measures: Earlier Positive Real Wage Growth Possible

## Announcement of new counter-inflation measure

Prime Minister Kishida announced new counter-inflation measures at a press conference on June 21. The government will reintroduce its electricity and city gas subsidies from August to October. Gasoline subsidies will be extended during the year. He also indicated his intention to provide additional cash handouts to pensioners and lowincome households. The size of the subsidies for electricity and city gas will be disclosed in more detail later, but the prime minister said that the overall size of the subsidies, including both those for electricity/city gas and gasoline, would be designed to lower the headline inflation by at least 0.5%pt YoY.

Due to the effects of the subsidies, we expect the Japan-style core CPI (excl. fresh food) YoY to be lower than our previous forecast this summer, though we need to wait for further details to examine the impact on CPI. It would depend on the size of the subsidies, but real wage growth may turn positive YoY as early as in August 2024. We think real wage growth turning positive can be a potentially positive catalyst for personal consumption. Nominal wages are likely to rise to towards around 3% , reflecting the results of the spring wage negotiations over the summer. On the other hand, August headline CPI YoY would dall, potentially resulting in real wage growth turning positive YoY. In that case, the August Month Labour Survey (published on October 8) may attract attention in the equity market.

It would be a tailwind for our BoJ outlook for a July rate hike. We think that appropriate fiscal and monetary policy coordination is desirable, given the important timing for Japan in terms of fully exiting deflation. We have been concerned about the risk that early removal of electricity and city gas subsidies would be a policy mistake (Japan Economics: CPI to Reach Around 3% in Summer Mainly on Surge in Electricity Charges: CPI Outlook Update (After Mar 2024 Tokyo CPI) (1 Apr 2024)). Indeed, recent data indicate a deterioration in the consumer confidence index, and we believe that an increase in media reports about the higher utility bills may have contributed to the deterioration in consumer sentiment (Japan Economics: Uneven Recovery: Review of Month-End Indicators (2 Jun 2024)). We hope that the scale of the electricity and gas subsidies in summer will be sufficient.

## Review of Nationwide May CPI — Not That Bad After All: BoJ's Measure of Underlying Inflation May Attract Attention

The same economic indicators can be interpreted in starkly different ways depending on the knowledge and understanding of the viewer. That is precisely the case with the nationwide CPI results for May.

Typical reaction #1: “Japan's inflation accelerated. The Japan-sytle core inflation (excluding fresh foods) reached 2.5% YoY in May (vs. April: +2.2% YoY). This would be a tail wind for an early rate hike by the BoJ.”

Our comment: We, as well as many other economists, would not agree with such a simplistic view. That said, many investors may see it this way as an initial reaction. A Bloomberg report entitled “Japan’s inflation picks up, backing case for BoJ rate hike” was its most read article concerning the BoJ after the CPI was released.

Typical reaction #2: “The content was weak. We need to look at the core index excluding one-time factors in order to gauge the outlook for monetary policy. Electricity charges rose sharply in May, so core inflation was not actually strong. The core-core CPI, which excludes energy (electricity, city gas, propane gas, kerosene, gasoline) and fresh foods, slowed to 2.1% YoY (vs. April: +2.4% YoY). The US-style core CPI excluding all food and energy, the indicator generally used in the US and elsewhere, slipped to 1.7% YoY (vs. April: +2.0% YoY) to mark the first time below 2% in 16 months. In addition, the Japanstyle core was 0.1pt below the consensus among economists.”

Our comment: It is true that the May figure was buoyed significantly by the large increase in levies on electricity charges to encourage the use of renewable energy sources like solar and wind power. That said, we also need to examine the BoJ's measures of underlying inflation to evaluate the underlying inflation trend, not just the simple core measures.

Our view — Not that bad after all: The BoJ’s trimmed mean and weighted median are both likely to show a renewed acceleration in inflation in May. The BoJ may view the May CPI results as solid. Service inflation has not accelerated, but not decelerated either, which was likely in line with the BoJ's outlook.

We have estimated the BoJ’s upcoming Measures of Underlying Inflation (due on 25 June) ahead of the announcement in light of the nationwide CPI data for May. The weighted median by our estimate rose again to 1.3% YoY (vs. April: +1.1% YoY). The trimmed mean, which dropped below 2%YoY in April for the first time in 20 months, likely rebounded to 2.2% YoY in May.

Note that BoJ Governor Kazuo Ueda stated in a speech in May that the bank itself must look beyond simply its traditional monthly Measures of Underlying Inflation, noting, ‘To capture underlying inflation in the current phase, it is necessary to take a new approach, including economic models. We think that the BoJ is also confident regarding wage hike trends at small businesses based on its hearing surveys via its regional branches.
