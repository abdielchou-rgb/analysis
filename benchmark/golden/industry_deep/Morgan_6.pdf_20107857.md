## United States

• Real GDP increased at a slower-than-expected 1.6% pace last quarter

But details within 1Q GDP lead us to revise up expectations for current-quarter growth to 2.5%

• The firm 0.3% gain in the March core PCE inflation figure left the year-ago reading unchanged at 2.8%

• Next week<sup>’</sup>s Fed meeting to highlight continued wariness of elevated inflation

Coming into this week we had a good sense that the economy grew at a solid pace in 1Q and that inflation had heated back up in that period. Relative to those expectations, growth came in on the lighter side while core consumer price inflation was hotter. Real GDP increased at a 1.6% annual pace, about a point below most forecasts. Real domestic demand increased at a 2.8% pace last quarter, and while this was also below expectations and a slowing from the second half of last year, it is solid growth nonetheless.

Consumers once again did most of the heavy lifting for domestic demand last quarter, with real consumer spending growth up 2.5%. While spending got off to a slow start this year, momentum picked up toward the tail end of 1Q and, largely because of that, we are raising our expectations for the pace of real GDP growth in 2Q from 1.5% to 2.5%. If realized, the GDP disappointment in 1Q would be just about offset by a better outcome in 2Q.

Meanwhile, the March CPI report indicated that, like January and February, the March core PCE would also continue the firming trend from a more benign second half. In the event, that number came out with a hotter-than-anticipated 0.32% monthly sequential gain. Alongside some upward revisions to the January and February readings, this left the year-ago increase in core PCE unchanged at 2.8%, a disappointing outcome for a series that had been marching lower most months over the past year.

This stalling in the disinflation process will be front and center as the FOMC meets next week. Over the past few meetings the Committee has indicated that while it intends to lower the funds rate it wanted to first see more signs of easing in inflation pressures to gain confidence that inflation is headed back to two percent. Last week, Chair Powell indicated that— unsurprisingly—the 1Q inflation data hadn<sup>’</sup>t engendered that confidence. Because of this, policy easing would come later than previously was anticipated. We expect this message to repeated next week, with the Committee intending to keep policy in its current restrictive stance for as long as it takes to gain that confidence.

The Fed will share the calendar with a busy week for economic data, much of which will relate to the labor market. While 1Q price inflation news has been a downer, the news on wage inflation has so far shown a more encouraging moderation. An important data point here next week will be the 1Q Employment Cost Index (ECI), which we expect will show a 1.0% quarterly gain for 1Q. The JOLTS report has recently indicated a slower decline in job openings, and next week<sup>’</sup>s March report is expected to show another step down in openings. Most important will be next Friday<sup>’</sup>s April employment report. We think nonfarm employment growth remained strong, increasing by 250,000 in April. We also forecast a 0.4% increase for average hourly earnings and a stable unemployment rate at 3.8%.

## GDP cooling a little through the noise

As mentioned above, the GDP-related news this week was largely offsetting, as 1Q growth disappointed but we raised our 2Q forecast. We continue to think that the economy<sup>’</sup>s momentum will cool over time, in part because of the effects of tighter monetary policy, but there hasn<sup>’</sup>t been very dramatic slowing realized so far and this cooling is likely happening with uneven performance across quarters (Figure 1).

## Core PCE inflation firm in 1Q

The recent core PCE inflation data came out a bit firmer than expectations, in part because of upward revisions to previously-released figures. In March, overall PCE prices rose 0.3% while the core index also increased 0.3% (0.317% to three decimals). While zoomed out to year-ago changes, it still appears that the broad trend for core inflation has moderated lately, as the core price index was up 2.8%oya in March (down from 2.9% in December, 3.6% in September, 4.3% in June, and 4.8% in March 2023). But there has been a strong run for core inflation lately, with core prices jumping 4.4% saar over the most recent three months (Figure 2). January was by far the strongest recent month for core inflation, but the more recent February and March figures were still solid on net (with monthly gains averaging 0.29%). The supercore measure (core services ex. housing) has behaved similarly to the broader core index lately, with a surge in prices in January and more modest, but still solid, growth reported since then.

## The FOMC is still waiting

The recent upside surprises on inflation likely have been much more important for Fed policymakers than the downside surprise on 1Q GDP, especially considering that the stronger inflation news has come alongside upside surprises in terms of many key labor market indicators. With all of this in mind, we think the FOMC likely lacks the confidence needed to lower rates or signal that rate cuts are coming very soon. The FOMC is widely expected to be on hold next week, and we look for no dissents to that decision. There will be no new dot plot and we believe the post-meeting statement will be little changed from the one released after the previous meeting in March.

Instead, we expect the most interesting development will be the press conference where we expect Chair Powell<sup>’</sup>s opening remarks will echo the ones given last week when he offered that first-quarter inflation data haven<sup>’</sup>t increased the Fed<sup>’</sup>s confidence of getting back to two percent inflation. If asked about the possibility of further hikes, we expect the Chair will indicate it<sup>’</sup>s not his base case but also something he can<sup>’</sup>t rule out. We also anticipate that he will not defend the March dot plot as still being a relevant guide to the policy outlook. We think there<sup>’</sup>s a little better than even odds that the Committee announces a halving of the monthly cap on Treasury runoff, to be implemented mid-month.
