## On a 1990s scale, where in the bull-run are we?

As the Fed finished an aggressive (300bps to 6%) hiking cycle in Jan 1995, US stocks began a bull run, delivering 27.9% annualised returns over 6 years before the bubble burst in March 2000. The rally got its intellectual anchor in Greenspan extolling improved productivity amidst a shift in the nature of investment from the material to the intellectual. The Fed is close to dovish pivot today and semiconductors companies tell us that we may be at a productivity tipping point in AI. How can we judge if we are in another spell of US exceptionalism that potentially delivers years of strong returns? Are we in a bubble already? Or is any comparison to the 1990s just contrived?

## The good news: Today’s is not the anatomy of a bubble

The 90s bull run saw two phases: a broad, steady climb from early ‘95 to mid ’98, and then a narrower, more explosive phase from late ’98 to early ‘00. Today’s sectoral patterns, narrowness, correlations, are similar to the second phase of the market; valuations are not far off either. However, there’s no bubble ready to go pop. There are notable differences between then and now in realised margins, earnings, free cash flow, in signals from options markets, and in IPO/M&A activity. While we do have sectorspecific enthusiasm today, it’s based on delivered shareholder returns. Then we had sector-specific euphoria based on hype.

## The bad news: Today's macro doesn't support a sustained bull run

The lack of a bubble doesn’t automatically imply the market will rise for several years. Difficult to measure though it is, productivity growth looks nothing like it did in the 1990s. Sure, this can change, but data today on electronics and info tech orders, capex intentions and actual capex doesn’t at all suggest the capital deepening associated with a productivity boost. Our metric of globalisation shows it is stalled (weakening, actually) compared to the late 1990s, when it grew the fastest. The economy is late cycle today. Its current configuration resembles how it stood at the end of the '90s bull run (early 2000) more closely than how it stood at its beginning (early 1995). Real disposable income growth is weak and likely to get weaker. These variables need to start looking up for the bull run to persist.

The rally that began in Jan 95 with the last hike of a 300 bps Fed cycle and culminated in bursting of the Nasdaq bubble was punctuated by two spells. Phase I, from Feb 1995 to June 1998, saw a more even performance across sectors, with Financials and Healthcare performing as well as Tech. Phase II, from Oct ‘98 to Mar ‘00 was both explosive and narrow around Tech. Defensive Household Personal Products, Pharma, Food Beverage, Utilities delivered near zero or negative returns. Today’s (from Sep ‘22 bottom) industry pattern mirrors Phase II (Oct ’98-Mar’00) closer (defensives underperforming), except today the rally within Tech is even narrower. Semiconductors, Software, Media /Communications have performed strongly, but, unlike the 1990s, Tech Hardware, Telecoms are underperformers.

The proportion of market gains attributable to the top 10 stocks by market capitalisation has been rising steadily through the past decade. Leadership by now has become narrower than it was through most of the ‘90s rally. However, as the ‘90s rally hit the final euphoric stage, contribution from the 10 largest stocks rose to nearly 50% of S&P500 returns (34% presently). Intra market stock correlations were rising in phase I of the 90s rally and fell in phase II as Tech tore away from the rest of the market. Today’s intra market correlation looks similar to phase II, the bubble phase of the ‘90s.

S&P500’s CAPE ratio rose steadily from 15x in the early 1990s to peak at 44.3x in 2000. It followed a similar pattern in the 2010s, rising again from 15x to peak at 39x in 2021 (34x currently). Across industries, today’s valuations are shy of the 1990s peak only in Software Services, Tech Hardware, Telecom Products. Outside of Tech, today’s valuations are also lower (than the 1990s peak) in Pharma, Cap goods, Energy and Retailing.

The other sign of dotcom Euphoria was a significant outperformance of small caps in the final year, and particularly in the final 3 months of the rally (besides Tech, Small cap Utilities, Industrials and Energy were also strong performers). They peaked on Mar 9th whereas the broader market peaked on Mar 27th, 2000.

The frenzy in the second half of the 1990s was manifest in the number of IPOs coming onto the market. As an average, between 1995 and 2000, there were 475 IPOs annually in the US across all US exchanges). By comparison in the post-Covid era we have seen an average of 222 transactions a year. Even including SPACs, which proliferated in 2021, the post-Covid average annual number of transactions increases to only 375. The \$ value of these transactions is even more different between the two eras.

Through the 90s bull run, especially through phase II from late ‘98, options markets betrayed nervousness. Both volatility and skew (price of put relative to call) rose. The bulk of the returns then were coming not from earnings but from valuations. By contrast, more assured in their assessment of return to the shareholders amidst steady earnings, both volatility and skew are simultaneously and atypically low today. Perhaps they’re guilty of complacency at the margin, but equity derivatives certainly don’t see today’s bull run as anything like a bubble.

The hope among investors is that an AI boom will provide a major productivity lift, which, in turn will mean strong growth and low inflation (even with high wages). The joint expectations of earnings growth (\~12% for coming 12m in S&P500) and inflation (2.59% on the US 5y5y inflation swap, in line with a 10y average) are in line with this Goldilocks/ productivity increase narrative. However, productivity growth so far is significantly lower than it was in the 1990s. This can change, and the micro will know that before the macro, but thus far the narrative is not being backed up by the data at a macro level.

Despite the strong performance of stocks, capex remains weak. This is true both at a headline level and also particularly for Tech capex. Roughly half of US’ overall investment is in Tech. The largest component here is software, but research & development and information processing equipment are also important. Growth rates here are a fraction of what they were in the second half of the 1990s. If Tech companies are truly bulled up on the future, this should be showing up in capex.

One may argue that an AI ‘tipping point’ has only now been reached, and that capex will pick up now. However, we don’t get this signal in orders data for core capital goods, nor in orders to inventories for Tech and electronics segment. Company earnings calls admittedly sound bullish and signal greater capex to back this up, but this is thus far not moving the needle for even the forward looking macro data. Without a process of capital deepening, one may question the prospect of a sustained surge in productivity.

On most metrics that we use to date the economic cycle (such as the unemployment gap, difference between current and future conditions of consumer confidence, real policy rate, equity and credit valuations, etc.) the US economy is late cycle. The values of our cycle clock indicators are much closer to where they were in March 2000, the end of the bull run, than to Jan 1995, its beginning. Late cycle is not a sufficient condition for a bust or a recession, but it usually has been a sufficient condition for mediocre returns.

Our measure of US’ adjusted real disposable income is barely growing, and is certainly much weaker than it was in the second half of the 1990s. We’ve argued that the US consumer (particularly Goods PCE) has been exceptionally strong, and will not persist. If real DPI were to strengthen it will extend both the economic cycle and stock rally, and we will need to change our view. Today, though, we see greater downside risk to real DPI : the bulk of deflator driven gains are behind us. Nominal income, taxes and transfers are likely to get weaker/ become a drag. With a starting point of low savings rates, lower real DPI growth should mean weaker consumption and downward earnings revisions going forward.

1990s were the peak decade of globalisation, and saw global trade growing 2.5-3x faster than global growth, lifting global and US earnings. European markets did nearly as well as the S&P500 (though not the Nasdaq) through the ‘90s boom. Globalisation stalled post 2007 and is arguably in modest retreat currently. If the largest stocks in the US fail to deliver on earnings, there is little strength in the global economy to get the global cycle going again. The rise of the Chinese property market and trade were key drivers of the low inflation, low rate, high growth global economy that helped US stocks recover from the dotcom bust. The risk are skewed in the opposite direction today.

Like S&P500’s valuations, the dollar rose through the 1990s, particularly in the second half of the decade. We have seen similar patterns in both variables from 2010. The key difference between then and now is the rise of china and EM growth that followed the .com bust. Today they are moving in the opposite direction. Lack of strength in EM FX will imply a much more modest fall in \$ as the US cycle comes off its present exceptionalism; one that’s limited to G10 currencies. We expect the \$ to weaken against Gold, JPY and, to a lesser extent, the EUR. Our view on interest rate differentials (US rates falling more than EUR) implies EUR should trade 1.20-1.25. However, weak growth in Eurozone will limit gains in the currency.

Weak global trade and domestic growth has meant that European earnings revisions have been broadly negative for the last 9m. The delta on growth and earnings from here could be modestly positive, but, already, driven by valuations, European stocks have pushed to levels consistent with above trend growth and PMI levels. Tighter credit spreads have helped valuations, but this risk compression is now approaching the limit. We think any growth improvement in Europe is likely to underwhelm the reality being priced, keeping us cautious on cyclical stocks.
