## Introduction

With the US election less than six months away, we are conducting deep dives into the economic policy issues that will be most impactful to the US economic outlook. Recently, we assessed the budget deficit over the coming years and the potential effects of a change in immigration policies, especially for inflation (see "Fiscal outlook: A chicken in every pot is costly" and "How large was inflation mitigation from immigration?").

In this report, we consider the potential impact of the election on US trade policy. We focus on potential changes that could come under a second Trump administration, as that is the outcome with the clearest differences from the status quo. We begin by recapping the trade war of 2018/19 and detail the economic effects of those policies. We then document some of the policy changes that are under consideration. Finally, we provide an initial assessment of the potential impact of another round of trade restrictions in 2025, with particular focus on growth, the labor market, inflation, and the Fed.

## Biden vs. Trump on trade

Trade policies have shifted dramatically under the two most recent administrations in the US. After decades of moving towards a more globalized economy supported by various free trade agreements, unfettered free trade has gone out of style in the US. In its place has been a freeze on further multilateral free trade agreements and greater acceptance of industrial policies aimed at building up a domestic manufacturing base, particularly for critical goods.

With this new normal in US trade policies, there are actually several broad strategic trade goals that are unlikely to change based on who wins the White House. These similarities include:

n Stronger support for the use of industrial policies (e.g., IRA, CHIPS)

n A commitment to improving the resilience of US supply chains through on / near / friend shoring

n Ensure that the US has or builds the capabilities to manufacture critical products with defense applications (e.g., chips and other advanced technologies)

n View China as economic adversary / competitor

Despite these similarities, the past two administrations have also taught us that there are different strategic goals. Tactics for dealing with trade issues will also differ. For this, we need look back no further than the trade war under the Trump administration which led to a significant increase of import tariffs across a variety of goods, especially those imported from China. Two clear divergences in trade policy views between Republicans and Democrats at the moment are that:

n A Trump administration is more likely to use tariffs as a tactic to correct large and persistent trade imbalances while also gaining leverage in trade negotiations

n A Trump administration is more likely to target imports from traditional US allies, such as Europe

n A Biden administration may be more inclined toward industrial subsidies

As we discuss in what follows, these differences in the approach to trade policies could have material effects on the US economic outlook and thus monetary policy.

The trade war of 2018-19 consisted of a series of tariffs on the import of goods either by type or trade partner. There were also several rounds of intensifying tariffs that saw a broadening out of the goods impacted or an increase in the tariff rate applied to those goods. ## The effects of the last trade war

In 2019 we wrote about the economic effects of the last trade war (see here ). We briefly summarize the results in this section.

## 1. Spike in trade policy uncertainty

The trade war in 2018-2019 lifted trade policy uncertainty to unprecedented levels, at least dating back to the early 1960s. As evidenced in Figure 2, trade policy uncertainty, as measured by the Federal Reserve Board staff or the Baker, Bloom and Davis methodology (2016), rose to record high levels during the last Trump Administration. According to Fed staff estimates based on historical statistical relationships, the two spikes in trade policy uncertainty in 2018 and 2019 were likely to subtract more than a percentage point from GDP into the first half of 2020. <sup>3</sup>

## 3. Manufacturing employment softened

The surge in trade policy uncertainty and decline in manufacturing sentiment also appeared in the hard data. After rebounding from the oil price crash of 2014-2016, manufacturing employment gains peaked just ahead of the start of the trade war in mid-2018. Manufacturing employment then decelerated quickly from 25k jobs per month added in May 2018 to an outright contraction in jobs by mid-2019 (see Figure 7).

Using county-level data over that period, we found that those counties that had higher shares of manufacturing employment prior to the trade war, and thus were more exposed to tensions that adversely impacted trade, underperformed economically (see "Manufacturing counties bearing the brunt of the trade war"). For example, a higher share of manufacturing employment was associated with a sharper rise in unemployment (Figures 8 and 9). Our point estimates suggested that a 10 percentage point rise in manufacturing share was associated with a 7bps rise in the unemployment rate.

## 4. Core goods prices accelerated

Finally, we identified inflation effects from the trade war. During the second half of 2018 and into early 2019, core goods prices accelerated sharply. On a six month basis, core goods inflation was the highest in eight years (Figure 10). While some of this acceleration was not associated with tariffs, there was a noticeable uptick in inflation rates that fell under the new higher tariffs, such as recreational commodities, apparel, and household furnishings and supplies. In particular we found that the tariffs explained about one half of the acceleration in core goods prices and accounted for a 15bp rise in core CPI inflation (see Figure 11 and the following report for more detailed analysis: "Tariffs: The other side of transitory inflation").

## A look ahead: Trade policies considered by the Trump team

Having summarized the most recent experience with trade under the Trump administration, in this section we focus on the trade policies that may be pursued if Trump wins the election in November. As noted at the outset, the reason to focus on Trump policies is that a Biden victory would largely represent a status quo outcome that would bring a continuation of the current trade approach. The policies referenced below are all taken from the following website: https:// www.donaldjtrump.com/agenda47

n Universal baseline tariff: A minimum 10% tariff on all imported goods and services<sup>4</sup>

n Reciprocal tariffs: Matching the tariff rates imposed on exports of US goods and services to other economies

n China: Withdrawal of China's most favored nation status. Higher tariff rates on imports from China; 50-60% have been mentioned

n Other tariffs: Return of tariffs on steel or the introduction of new tariffs on imports from Europe (e.g., autos)

## Economic impact of tariffs: What could trade war 2.0 mean?

The trade policies just outlined would represent a considerable escalation of the tariffs implemented last round, both in terms of scope of goods and services targeted and the tariff rates considered. We would stress that which policies are ultimately pursued and the details of the policies remain highly uncertain. For example, it is unclear which tariffs, if any, would be used simply as leverage in negotiations to attain more favorable outcomes for the US. It is also unclear if Congress would grant Trump trade promotion authority or attempt to push back on Trump's tariff authority. Nonetheless, what follows is a tentative first attempt to assess the impact of these policies. In much of the below, we consider the universal baseline tariff as that represents the most sweeping and unique pillar of the policies under consideration.

## Fiscal: Tariffs could boost revenues

Existing analysis suggests that some of the proposed tariff policies, such as the universal baseline tariff, could increase fiscal revenues. For example, work by the Center for a Responsible Federal Budget (CRFB) suggests that the minimum universal baseline tariff of 10% could raise \$2-2.5tn over the next decade. This analysis assumes that tariff rates already at or above 10% would remain in place at those rates, i.e., that the universal baseline tariff would not add 10 percentage points to all existing tariff rates. Under dynamic simulations, these estimates account for a reduction in output of about 1%, which is important given the adverse growth effects detailed earlier in this report (see here).

Alternative estimates from the Tax Foundation find a somewhat higher impact on revenues of about \$300bn per year and \$3tn over the next decade (see " Trump’s \$300 Billion Tax Hike Would Threaten U.S. Businesses and Consumers "). Including interest, this work found that this tariff could potentially reduce debt ten years ahead by about 7 percentage points of GDP and by 4 percentage points when accounting for the adverse effects on economic activity. These range of estimates highlight the importance of accounting for dynamic effects of the policies.

We would caution that the actual revenue increases could be considerably smaller. First, the above estimates appear to apply the universal tariff to all goods imports including those that are covered by existing trade agreements – namely, the USMCA. Recall that this signature trade policy achievement of the Trump administration covers just under 30% of US goods imports on average. If a second Trump administration were to tear up this agreement, it could deter other countries from entering into any bilateral trade agreements with the US. In short, if the aim of the tariffs is to provide leverage in eventual bilateral negotiations, this could arguably backfire if they violate agreements already in place.

Second, as recent trade policy changes have taught, exporters to the US can undertake measures to avoid the increase in tariff rates, though a blanket approach like a universal baseline tariff would limit this ability. The adverse growth effects could also be understated if implementation of these tariffs kicks off further escalation via retaliatory tariffs. As such, the extent of any deficit and debt reduction should be treated cautiously and could well be meaningfully smaller than these outside estimates suggest.

## Growth: Tariffs could temporarily dent US growth exceptionalism

As 2018-2019 showed and the analysis above indicates, tariffs could have significant adverse effects on economic activity, particularly if implemented with the breadth and magnitude being considered. We highlight several existing analyses which provide examples of the possible extent of the impact.

Analysis from the Tax Foundation's general equilibrium model finds that a 10% universal baseline tariff would reduce the size of the US economy by about three quarters of a percentage point and cost the equivalent of about 500k full-time jobs. This assumes no retaliation by the US's trading partners. If this retaliation occurred, US GDP would shrink by a further 0.4 percentage points with a resulting loss of another 322k full-time jobs. Together, the economy would be a bit more than 1 percentage point smaller as a result of the universal baseline tariff (see here).

In separate work, estimates of a global tariff war, not dissimilar to that found above, would shrink US GDP by a bit more than 1 percentage point (see here). This also broadly fits with analysis by the Fed Board staff that the spikes in trade policy uncertainty during 2018 and 2019 reduced real GDP by around 1 percentage point (Figure 14).

Taken together, this evidence suggests that the implementation of the universal baseline tariff alone would likely reduce US GDP by a percentage point or more over time. If combined with additional tariffs, such as a ramp up in tariffs on imports from China at the magnitude floated (i.e., 50-60%) or expansion of tariffs to imports from Europe, including autos, these could represent a lower bound of the negative impact on economic activity.

## Inflation: Tariffs would lift price pressures

Attaining the precise impact of tariffs on consumer inflation is complicated by several uncertainties: How much will currencies respond to potentially offset the tariff? Will foreign firms adjust their prices to defray the impact and maintain market share? Will domestic firms raise their prices in the face of higher costs from their foreign competitors to capture greater profits? How quickly will supply chains shift to avoid the tariff? While many of these elasticities can be estimated, knowing how all these uncertainties will interact in response to a given set of tariffs is complex. Nonetheless, if we abstract from many of these considerations, we can arrive at a starting point for estimating the inflation impact of tariffs.

At present, goods imports represent 16.6% of personal consumption expenditures and nearly 19% of core personal consumption expenditures. If a 10% tariff was enacted on all goods imports, and a variety of simplifying assumptions were followed – e.g., the full tariff is passed through to consumer prices, there was no currency adjustment, domestic firms do not adjust their prices on goods that compete with the tariffed items, foreign firms do not adjust their ex-tariff prices, among others – this would lead to a 1.7pp and 1.9pp rise in headline and core PCE prices. Clearly, this is a lot of "ifs" and likely overestimates the potential inflation impact of these tariffs.

If instead we assume that the 10% universal baseline tariff is simply a "top up" to imports with tariff rates below 10% and does not impact items with tariff rates above 10%, the inflation effect would be somewhat smaller. Using tariff distribution data from the WTO, about 90% of US imports would be impacted by the 10% baseline rate, but the ultimate rise in the tariff rate would be more subdued because about 40% of imports have a tariff rate greater than 0% but less than 10% (see here).

Using this distributional data, the average tariff rate would rise by more than 7 percentage points (from roughly 3% to 10.3%), which would translate into a roughly 120bp rise in headline PCE and 140bp increase in core PCE prices.

We use a similar approach to assess the potential inflation impact of an increase of tariffs on imports from China. If the average tariff rate on these imports jumped from 2022 levels of about 12% to 50%, this would raise the headline and core PCE price levels by 20-30bps.

As mentioned, the ultimate inflation impact of tariffs is highly uncertain as it depends on a variety of margins. That said, the scope of the tariffs considered, both in terms of breadth and magnitude, is considerable and would have a noticeable inflation impact if enacted.

## Implications for the Fed

Experience with the trade war in 2018-19 presented an example where rising tariffs dented global trade and growth and pushed the Fed in a more dovish direction, ultimately resulting in a mid-cycle adjustment of three 25bp rate cuts.

We would caution against assuming a second trade war in 2025 would lead to a similar monetary policy outcome this time around for several reasons. First, in 2019 inflation was stuck below target and the Fed was worried about inflation remaining too low, not too high as is now the case. Second, the economy and the labor market in particular appear to be entering the trade war with more momentum. For example, the 6-month moving average of payroll gains in early 2019 was about 120k (on current vintage data); it is currently twice that rate. Third, depending on the election outcome, a re-ignition of the trade war could be coupled with additional fiscal support for the economy and/or other policies that could further stoke inflationary pressures, such as more stringent conditions at the border.

In short, because a trade war creates tensions in the Fed's dual mandate – by denting growth but lifting inflation – at a time when growth and inflation are both elevated, we would not extrapolate the Fed's dovish pivot in 2019 to 2025. As we argued in our recent Fed update note, on the contrary we see the potential for inflationary policies to push the Fed to remain on hold this year rather than cut rates 25bp as in our base case.

## Conclusion

The future of US trade policy is one area where the 2024 presidential election is likely to have critical implications. Although both Democrats and Republicans now broadly agree on a few trade-related topics – e.g., addressing trade issues with China, supporting domestic industrial policies, and tabling any expansions of multilateral free trade agreements, at least for now – in this note we detailed a variety of ways in which a Trump administration could impact the US economic outlook through protectionism.

The experience of 2018-2019 serves as a useful, although possibly watered down, template for how these policy changes could proceed and how they might impact the economy. In the preceding analysis we detailed how a universal baseline tariff could raise revenues while also denting growth. These tariff policies would also further lift inflation pressures.

Ultimately, trade protectionism would act as a negative supply shock, slowing growth while lifting inflation. Such a shift could counteract at least some of the very positive supply dynamics that are currently allowing strong growth to coincide with disinflation. With inflation already well above the Fed's target, and upside inflation risks still top of mind for monetary policy makers, we see trade policies as possibly adding another reason to keep the Fed on hold into 2025.

Macroeconomic fluctuations often account for most of the risks associated with exposures to instruments that promise to pay fixed or variable interest rates. For an investor who is long fixed-rate instruments (thus receiving these cash flows), increases in interest rates naturally lift the discount factors applied to the expected cash flows and thus cause a loss. The longer the maturity of a certain cash flow and the higher the move in the discount factor, the higher will be the loss. Upside surprises in inflation, fiscal funding needs, and FX depreciation rates are among the most common adverse macroeconomic shocks to receivers. But counterparty exposure, issuer creditworthiness, client segmentation, regulation (including changes in assets holding limits for different types of investors), changes in tax policies, currency convertibility (which may constrain currency conversion, repatriation of profits and/or liquidation of positions), and settlement issues related to local clearing houses are also important risk factors. The sensitivity of fixed-income instruments to macroeconomic shocks may be mitigated by indexing the contracted cash flows to inflation, to FX depreciation, or to specified interest rates - these are common in emerging markets. The index fixings may - by construction - lag or mis-measure the actual move in the underlying variables they are intended to track. The choice of the proper fixing (or metric) is particularly important in swaps markets, where floating coupon rates (i.e., coupons indexed to a typically short-dated interest rate reference index) are exchanged for fixed coupons. Funding in a currency that differs from the currency in which coupons are denominated carries FX risk.
