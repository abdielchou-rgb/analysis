# The EM Fixed Income Sustainable Debt Asset Class

## Introducing our inaugural publication detailing the EM fixed income sustainable debt asset class

We introduce a new publication in which we will periodically detail the instruments, size and developments within the EM fixed income sustainable debt asset class. Our focus is from a markets perspective on the bond types and categorisation of the rapidly evolving EM sustainable debt universe.

The EM fixed income sustainable debt asset class is now \$1.1tn in size, making it 11% of the EM hard currency debt stock but only 3% of EM local currency debt. Use-of-proceeds bonds are the largest part of the asset class, with Green bonds dominating at 62% of outstanding notional, Sustainability bonds have 16% of outstanding and Social bonds 10%. SLBs (Sustainability-Linked Bonds) are 7% of the asset class, with other segments having small weights.

Private sector companies are the largest issuer type of EM sustainable debt, with Financial and Non-Financial Corporates totalling 53% of outstanding. Governments are only 13% of the asset class with \$144bn outstanding but government-related entities (that include quasi-sovereigns and local governments) are another \$192bn (18%).

• China<sup>’</sup>s local currency non-financial corporates are the largest segment of EM sustainable bonds, while Chile is the largest government issuer of hard currency sustainable bonds.

We look at EM government hard currency SLBs in detail, highlighting the mechanisms used in these bonds and the variety of triggers referenced for coupons to vary should sustainability criteria be met or missed.

EM corporate sustainable bonds make up close to 14% of the hard currency EM corporate bond universe (\$315bn). These bonds became more common in recent years, with the trend broadening out into more types, countries and industry segments. We find that EM corporate sustainability bonds have been integrated into not only ESG focused funds but also traditional EM dedicated funds, likely helped by the lack of a material spread give up (or <sup>‘</sup>greenium<sup>’</sup>).

J.P. Morgan<sup>’</sup>s suite of labelled bond indices tracking the sustainable debt asset class began with the creation of a green bond index in November 2020 and has since grown to encompass 10 labelled bond focused indices tracking global green, social and sustainability bond issuance. The suite has a strong focus on on emerging markets, with six flagship indices dedicated to tracking labelled bond issuance within emerging markets.

We also look at new developments for the EM fixed income sustainable debt asset class, focusing on the emerging themes around biodiversity and adaptation, as well as development finance and impact investing.

This category of bonds is by far the largest segment of the EM fixed income sustainable bonds universe, with 5,624 bonds and a notional outstanding of \$1.0trn.

Debt-for-Nature Guaranteed Bonds: One notable sub-category of <sup>‘</sup>use of proceeds bonds that differ in their structure is those that involve a debt-for-nature swap with a guarantor. These are bonds that are intended to finance the protection of key natural habitats, e.g. ocean environments for <sup>‘</sup>blue<sup>’</sup> bonds, and so we classify these as <sup>‘</sup>use of proceeds<sup>’</sup> bonds. However, in their structure they are significantly different from vanilla bonds as they have a guarantor from a more highly rated entity (see later).

B) Sustainability-Linked Bonds (SLBs): These are bonds that do not necessarily have a specific use of proceeds from their issuance, but where the bonds themselves have triggers in their payout levels depending on <sup>‘</sup>key performance indicators (KPIs)<sup>’</sup> linked to sustainable or social goals. For example, in the Chile USD 4.34% 2042 bonds, the prospectus states that: “If either KPI1 or KPI2 is not met, there is a coupon step up of 12.5bp for each KPI,<sup>”</sup> where KPI1 relates to an annual Greenhouse Gas target and KPI2 relates to a percentage of electricity to be generated from renewable sources.

The bonds in this category are a small part of the overall EM fixed income sustainable bonds universe, with 183 bonds and a notional outstanding of \$81bn.

C) Other Sustainable Bonds: Given the growing nature of the EM fixed income sustainable bonds universe, there are a number of debt types which fit together under an <sup>‘</sup>Other<sup>’</sup> category. These bonds are conceptually different from the labelled bonds and SLBs in their structure or framework. We consider two types of bonds currently in this category: i) Multilateral Development Bank Bonds issued as part of support for sustainability efforts in EM countries; and ii) Development Finance debt which looks to widen the sustainable bonds envelope to attract more capital for sustainable purposes. Given the evolving or less easily defined nature of the debt in this <sup>‘</sup>Other<sup>’</sup> category, it generally does not appear within our EM fixed income sustainable debt asset class data tracking detailed through this note. This may change over time as these parts of the asset class develop further and tracking debt instruments becomes easier.

We will look at each part of the asset class in more detail, before detailing the size and characteristics of each in the next section.

## Blue Bonds

There is a subset of green bonds, known as blue bonds, that are increasingly receiving investor attention. Blue bonds emphasize “the importance of the sustainable use of maritime resources and the promotion of related sustainable activities<sup>”</sup> (link). The proceeds of blue bonds can be used for projects that focus on coastal climate adaptation, marine ecosystem management, sustainable coastal tourism, offshore renewables, marine pollution, etc.

The first sovereign blue bond was issued by the Seychelles government in October 2018, which, according to the World Bank (link), went towards the expansion of marine protected areas, improved governance of priority fisheries and developing the blue economy of the country.

## Case Study: Indonesia’s first blue bond

In May 2023, the Indonesian government issued a US\$150mn sovereign blue bond on the Japanese (Samurai) bond market, the first such issue on a publicly traded market. Proceeds will be used in areas such as coastal protection, sustainable management of fisheries and aquaculture, marine biodiversity conservation and mangrove rehabilitation (source: UNDP).

## Debt-for-nature swaps

Debt-for-nature swaps are debt relief schemes, primarily designed for sovereigns, in which the creditor voluntarily chooses to cancel or reduce a debt in exchange for the debtor committing to make a financial contribution to a conservation project. Often, these transactions will involve an intermediary such as an NGO which will take on and restructure the debt in order to facilitate the debtor<sup>’</sup>s conservation efforts – known as <sup>‘</sup>trilateral<sup>’</sup> swaps. Because of the guarantees from higher-rated third-parties on the new bonds that have been swapped into, these bonds are typically have lower spreads than vanilla (senior unsecured) bonds issued by the government.

The first debt-for-nature swap occurred in 1987 between Bolivia and its creditors, with the NGO Conservation International arranging the swap valued at \$650,000. As a result, 3.7 million acres of land near the Amazon Basin were set aside for conservation (link). Since then roughly 140 deals have been completed (link).

According to an IMF report (link), 34 out of 59 developing countries most exposed to climate change are also at high risk of fiscal crisis, which means that there is a common set of countries seeking both debt reduction and having environmental objectives.

## Case Study: Conserving the Galapagos Islands via debt-for-nature swaps

In June 2023, the world<sup>’</sup>s largest ever debt-for-nature swap deal was brokered by Credit Suisse on behalf of the government of Ecuador, in which \$1.6bn of debt was purchased for \$644mn. As a result, the country agreed to an annual payment of \$18mn for the next 20 years to aid conservation efforts in the Galapagos Islands, according to WEF (link). Additionally, the new bond has an \$85 million <sup>‘</sup>credit guarantee<sup>’</sup> from the Inter-American Development Bank and \$656 million of political risk insurance (PRI) from the U.S. International Development Finance Corporation. PRI can be used to protect against currency inconvertibility, government interference, and political violence including terrorism.

## B) Sustainability-linked bonds

According to the ICMA definition, sustainability-linked bonds (SLBs) are “any type of bond instrument for which the financial and/or structural characteristics can vary depending on whether the issuer achieves predefined Sustainability/ESG objectives... Issuers commit explicitly to future improvements in sustainability outcome(s) within a predefined timeline,<sup>”</sup> and, “those objectives are (i) measured through predefined Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) and (ii) assessed against predefined Sustainability Performance Targets (SPTs)<sup>”</sup>.

SLBs are often not “use-of-proceeds<sup>”</sup> bonds: the issuer may choose to earmark the funds for a specific purpose, but does not have to and, in most cases, does not. This could present greenwashing risks and a Bloomberg report analysed nearly €70billion of SLBs and concluded the majority of the targets were “weak, irrelevant, or even already achieved<sup>”</sup>(link). As a result, some large institutional managers will not even include SLBs in their impact strategies (link). However, for EM hard currency government SLBs that we look at later in this report, this does not seem as much of a risk as the targets look relevant and ambitious.

## Case Study: Explaining a typical SLB structure with Uruguay’s bonds

Uruguay issued its first SLB in late 2022, with two KPIs to be achieved by 2025: (1) a reduction of GHG emissions per real GDP unit relative to 1990 and (2) maintenance of native forest area relative to 2012. Failure to meet certain thresholds would result in a coupon step-up for each of the KPIs missed. Conversely, outperformance would result in a coupon step-down for each successful KPI. Together, the KPIs “address two different, but complementary, environmental global public goods: mitigating global warming and preserving a key carbon sink.<sup>”</sup> (Link).

## C) Other Sustainable Bonds

## i) Multilateral development bank bonds

Multilateral development banks (MDBs) are supranational banks set up by sovereign states, which become the MDBs<sup>’</sup> shareholders. As such, they usually have very high credit ratings, enabling them to issue bonds at low interest rates relative to other sovereigns and corporates.

These institutions will typically provide support to lower- and middle-income economies by offering financing in the form of loans and grants, as well as technical assistance. In many instances they can become lenders of last resort: for example, MDBs provided \$222bn of financing during the global financial crisis.

Due to their economic and social development angle, bonds issued by these institutions are also of interest to sustainability-minded investors. Although not a necessary requirement, these bonds can be labelled.

Examples of MDBs are the Asian Development Bank, the European Investment Bank, the African Development Bank, the European Bank for Reconstruction and Development, or the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development. The latter is the oldest development bank in the world, and a part of the World Bank. In 2020, the total assets of the 12 largest MDBs amounted to US\$1.7tn, the largest of them being the European Investment Bank, with almost US\$0.7tn in assets (link).

Tracking the bonds issued by MDBs that can specifically be classified as <sup>‘</sup>EM sustainable bonds<sup>’</sup> is not straightforward and these are generally left out of our tracking

of the size of the asset class.

## Case Study: the IBRD’s financing activities

In 2022 the IBRD issued US\$40.8bn bonds, disbursed US\$28.2bn funds and committed US\$33.1bn in new lending. Some of the results achieved by the projects the IBRD financed in the year include (link):

• Access to essential health and nutrition services for 16.7mn people

• Improved access to water for 6.8mn people

• Development of disaster risk reduction systems in 46 countries

• Increase of 1,492GWh of renewable power generation capacity

## ii) Development Finance Debt

This is a growing but less established category where debt is linked to sustainable development goals, without having a specific <sup>‘</sup>use of proceeds<sup>’</sup> target in bond documentation or payout formally linked to a sustainable KPI. The aim is to attract capital to support sustainable development goals by creating an overall set of stated development aims for say a government or corporation and then debt issued by that government or corporation would also be considered sustainable debt. Additionally, debt can be certified to contribute to SDG targets and thus be considered sustainable debt. For example, Latam Airlines Group SA issued bonds in October 2022 with the issuer putting in place an overall development finance reporting framework (see https:// www.jpmorgan.com/content/dam/jpm/cib/complex/content/investment-banking/ development-finance-institution/DFI-Annual-Report-2022.pdf#page=15). Data on the size of this part of the EM fixed income asset class is difficult to aggregate due to its new and evolving nature and not generally included in our totals on the asset class size.

## Growth of Sustainable Debt within EM fixed income

The EM fixed income sustainable bond market has been increasingly steadily in the past few years. While tracking EM fixed income sustainable bonds issued has challenges as we go back in time due to data collection and labelling, it looks clear that over the past few years the asset class has been steadily increasing (Figure 8) and observable issuance trends so far in 2024 have seen a notable presence of sustainable debt in primary supply. The increase from 2021-2022 is likely partly a function of the expansion of the labelled debt data available and not necessarily a reflection of increased issuance in that period.

## 2. Breakdown of the EM Fixed Income Sustainable Debt Asset Class

In this section, we break down the EM fixed income sustainable debt asset class to highlight its current composition. We look to categorise the debt stock along the following lines:

\- Sustainable debt characteristics: whether bonds are labelled bonds (Green, Social, Sustainability, Transition or Other), blue-guaranteed bonds (debt-for-nature swaps), or SLBs.

\- Country of issuance: which country the government, quasi-sovereign or subgovernment, or corporate issuer is from.

\- Issuer type: whether the issuer is a government, government-related entity, corporate (financial or non-financial), Development Bank or Other type of issuer.

\- Currency of debt: whether debt is issued in a foreign (hard currency) or the country<sup>’</sup>s local currency. This usually maps to the law under which the bonds are issued (local law or international law) but not perfectly.

## Government SLBs and Typical Triggers (KPIs)

There are only a few EM government SLBs, although with already a variety of innovative triggers. There is increasing focus on EM governments adding contingent payouts in their bonds, particularly related to debt restructurings. But the government SLB market has already been advancing down this path and is a template for implementing contingent payouts (i.e. coupons that change by design under different states of the world), with coupons stepping up (and down) if sustainability targets are missed (or met). The market has generally not actively priced these triggers in these instruments (as opposed to say GDP warrants which have pricing models), although this will become more important over time and the step-ups in coupons will become material given they could total 100bp in some of these instruments. How the market could price the probability of a country achieving 40% of women on boards of reporting companies in the country or the probability of maintaining a certain forest area in order to be able to accurately price these bonds is an open question.

All EM government hard currency SLBs have Greenhouse Gas (GHG) triggers but a range of others as well. Figure 23 shows the EM government hard currency SLBs and outlines how the sustainability-linked triggers work. These bonds all have <sup>‘</sup>general government purposes<sup>’</sup> in their use of proceeds, so the money is not earmarked for sustainable purposes, but rather the government is incentivised to meet sustainable targets by the use of triggers in the bonds. All these bonds include a trigger related to

greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, but there are a variety of other triggers including forest conservation, female representation on the boards of companies, and renewable electricity generation.

## E) EM Corporate hard currency sustainable bonds asset class

In this section, we focus on the hard currency portion of the EM corporate sustainable bond asset class. International investor involvement in EM corporate bonds has been mainly in hard currency bonds. The local currency EM corporate bond universe, while larger, tends to have insufficient liquidity as most bonds are not Euroclearable and are held to maturity by local investors.

Which instruments are included in our definition of EM corporate sustainable bonds?

The EM corporate sustainable bond universe we track is a subset of the external currency EM corporate bond asset class which currently stands at \$2.5trn. We use a combination of sources to identify bonds as sustainable including Bloomberg tags, rating agency news and Bond Radar. More details on our definition of the EM corporate hard currency asset class can be found in our reference presentation (link), slide 108 titled “Dissecting the asset class<sup>”</sup>. The types of sustainable bonds (also referred to as GSS bonds in this section) we track are consistent with those discussed throughout this report and include green, sustainable, sustainabilitylinked, and social bonds. Given differences in classification sources and asset class definitions, our tracking of hard currency EM corporate sustainable bond universe presented in this section may differ from the estimates noted in earlier sections of this report.

EM corporate sustainable bonds make up close to 14% of the hard currency EM corporate bond universe (\$315bn). EM corporates priced the first external green bond in 2013, but the issuance of sustainable finance bonds started to pick up more substantially in 2018. Sustainable corporate bond issuance has increased steadily in recent years to account for \~30% of new supply in the past two years, eclipsing the pace in the US HG and hard currency EM sovereign markets (3% and 16%, respectively, in 2023). Such bonds became more common substitutes for traditional bonds in liability management/refinancing transactions, with the trend broadening out into more types, countries and industry segments. We find that EM corporate sustainability bonds have been integrated into not only ESG focused funds but also traditional EM dedicated funds, likely helped by the lack of a material spread give up (or <sup>‘</sup>greenium<sup>’</sup>).

Korean and Chinese corporates are the largest issuers of sustainable EM corporate bonds. Combined, corporate issuers from these two countries contribute over \$140bn of sustainable bonds. Brazil and India are the second largest with about \$20bn apiece. Corporates from another six countries /markets – UAE, Saudi Arabia, Mexico, Czech Republic, Supranational, and Hong Kong SAR – have over \$10bn in sustainable corporate bonds. There are also 27 EM countries where corporate issuers have anywhere from \$100mn to \$9bn in external bonds outstanding. By type, green bonds make up the lion<sup>’</sup>s share of sustainable bond stock at \$187bn (8.2% of all hard currency EM corporate bonds). This is followed by sustainable bonds at \$62bn (2.7%), SLBs at \$33bn (1.5%) and social bonds at \$32bn (1.4%). A more recent type has been transition bonds, first issued in 2020 and accounting for \$2.5bn in issuance since. With growing interest in sustainable investing amongst investors, we believe GSS EM corporate issuance is likely to continue to grow.

Sustainable bond issuance has broadened out in recent years by region, type, and industry. By region, Asia still contributes over half of the issuance, but this is down from 70%+ until 2017 and the contribution from countries outside of China has also picked up with Korea now becoming the biggest issuer. In other regions, corporate issuers from countries such as Saudi Arabia and UAE became quite active in issuing sustainable bonds over the past two years. While being dominated by green bonds up to 2020, GSS issuance expanded to include a greater share of sustainability, SLB and social bonds. Industry composition remains highly skewed towards defensive sectors such as financials and utilities but has been broadening as well. Indeed, sectors such as diversified, industrials and infrastructure have seen more sustainable bonds price since 2021.

Despite the strong pickup in issuance of sustainable bonds, we are not convinced that this is strictly incremental supply which is coming on top of what would have been issued without such structures. Rather, we think the rising importance of the sustainability theme (and perhaps to some extent lower cost of funding) may have been leading issuers to take on sustainable structures, implying that at least some portion of these bonds are replacing supply that would have come anyways in non-GSS format.

Anecdotally, we have seen this trend in some tenders/buybacks and GSS issuance last year. Regardless of the driver, we think sustainable bond issuances are likely to remain a significant part of the market with the contribution potentially moving even higher.

Sustainable Bond Greenium

In September 2022, we launched a framework for assessing the secondary market ‘greenium’ for EM corporate USD denominated sustainable finance bonds including green, social, sustainable, and sustainability linked (or GSS bonds). The framework captures around 100 EM Corporate GSS bonds, matching them to traditional bonds within the same issuer curves or interpolated curve points on the issuer<sup>’</sup>s curve. This has allows us to make spread comparisons of GSS versus traditional bonds and track performance through time. Overall, we have found that EM corporate GSS bonds trade fairly close to traditional bonds on a duration and issuer adjusted basis. This is good news for investors looking for opportunities in EM corporates with a sustainable angle. There is some differentiation, though, with the more polluting sectors and segments for example having a higher greenium and different behavior in times of market strength versus weakness and we discuss these dynamics below.

A common perception is that GSS bonds offer a spread premium, or so called greenium, i.e., trade tight to traditional bonds. Some market participants also expect GSS bonds to perform better during times of market stress. For EM corporates, we have found that the illiquidity premium, which rises when market sentiment sours, drowns out any greenium. Fundamentally (and properly compared on a like to like basis), labeled bonds should trade at similar levels to traditional bonds given the same ranking in the capital structure. Nevertheless, dislocations exist, due to market dynamics, divergent technicals, pricing conventions, duration mismatches, and small sample sizes. We also believe that such dislocations get amplified in times of stress as the investor base for EM corporate GSS bonds is still less seasoned than that for traditional bonds.

Such dynamics have tended to result in a higher greenium (i.e. labeled bonds trading at lower than traditional bond spreads) when the market backdrop was strong and lack of greenium/discount (i.e. labeled bonds trading flat or wide to traditional) when market conditions were challenging. For instance, while EM corporate GSS bonds were trading with a greenium in the early part of 2022 (i.e. until late February), such tightness progressively dissipated as markets sold off and more uncertain backdrop started to get priced in. Over the past two years, bouts of volatility and risk-on reversals have led EM corporate GSS spread pickup to shift from greenium to discount, albeit within a narrow range of -8bp to +5bp on an aggregate basis.

## 4. New Developments and Themes

## Other DFI or Impact Investing in EM Fixed Income

## Development Finance Bonds

This is a growing but less defined category where debt is linked to sustainable development goals, without the debt having a specific <sup>‘</sup>use of proceeds<sup>’</sup> target or payout formally linked to a sustainable KPI. The aim is to attract capital to support sustainable development goals by creating an overall set of stated development aims for say a government or corporation and then debt issued by that government or corporation would also be considered sustainable debt (see https://www.jpmorgan.com/content/dam/ jpm/cib/complex/content/investment-banking/development-finance-institution/DFI-Annual-Report-2022.pdf#page=15). Additionally, debt can be certified to contribute to SDG targets and thus be considered sustainable debt. For example, Latam Airlines Group SA issued bonds in October 2022 with the issuer putting in place an overall development finance reporting framework Data on the size of this part of the EM fixed income asset class is difficult to aggregate due to its new and evolving nature.

## Emerging investment themes

While green bond proceeds so far have mostly been invested in projects focused on reducing GHG emissions (renewable energy, clean transport, etc, as shown in Figure 4 , we expect growing interest on adaptation to climate change and biodiversity over the coming years.

## Biodiversity

## Why do investors care?

Biodiversity is estimated to have a substantial impact on long-term returns. According to some models, over half of the world<sup>’</sup>s GDP – roughly \$44 trillion – is moderately or highly dependent on nature (link). Partial biodiversity collapse could reduce global growth by 2.3% pa by 2030, according to the World Bank (link). Regulatory changes over the coming years are also expected, following the widespread adoption of the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework at COP15 (link), aimed at halting and reversing nature loss. Furthermore, there are an increasing number of investor-led initiatives supporting the integration of natural capital into investment decisions.

• Sustainable water and wastewater management

## Adaptation to climate change

## Why do investors care?

The past eight years have been the hottest on record globally, each posting temperatures at least 1°C above pre-industrial levels. In June 2023 global-mean temperature breached the 1.5°C threshold of the Paris Agreement for the first time, highlighting the urgency of not just mitigating GHG emissions but also the importance of learning to live on and adapt to a warmer planet. We expect investors to focus increasingly on adaptation solutions going forward. We cover the topic in more detail in our global cross-asset report on Adaptation to climate change.

Adaptation strategies can serve to avoid future losses, deliver positive economic returns and provide social and environmental benefits. The Global Center for Adaptation estimates that investing US\$ 1.8tn globally by 2030 could generate about US\$ 7tn of net economic, social and environmental benefits by reducing the impacts of climate hazards and contributing to broader economic and sustainable development. Data from SwissRe suggests that the cost-benefit ratios of adaptation investments in the infrastructure, real estate and agricultural sectors vary between 2:1 and 10:1, with benefits typically higher in lower income countries.

A huge adaptation gap exits, with the adaptation needs between 2030 and 2050 at 5-10x the scale of current adaptation investments. According to the Climate Bonds Initiative 19% of the GSS+ bonds in their database by count have “some degree of climate

resilience-related use of proceeds<sup>”</sup>. However, only 12% were issued in emerging markets. The great majority came from developed markets (80%), whilst the reminder were issued by supranationals, such as multilateral development banks (link).

Adaptation can also be a diversifier away from more traditional climate finance, as those sectors most at risk to climate change are notably different from those with the highest GHG emissions.

Further, we see taxonomies and regulation as a catalyst for accelerated adaptation investments. More than 140 countries have adopted climate adaptation polices, whilst most sustainable taxonomies include adaptation activities.

## Investing in Adaptation to climate change through nature-based solutions

Nature-based solutions (NbS) are defined by the IUCN as “actions to protect, sustainably manage, and restore natural or modified ecosystems, that address societal challenges effectively and adaptively, simultaneously providing human well-being and biodiversity benefits<sup>”</sup>. While issuance to back such solutions remains very limited, particularly in the corporate world, we expect these to become more widespread in the following years due to increased investor interest on nature-based solutions as a means of adapting to climate change due to their inherent benefits:

1. They can help avoid losses due to natural disasters

2. They can enhance the return profile of traditional grey infrastructure, and often be more cost-effective

3. They typically have significant co-benefits beyond adaptation

4. They remain an untapped source for investors

We discuss these benefits in more detail in our global cross-asset Nature-based Solutions for Climate Change Adaptation report. The Framework Convention on Climate Change provides an exhaustive list of climate adaptation good practices (link). For simplicity, we can group these solutions into one (or more) of the following four categories (here and here):

Restoring and preserving forests, wetlands and peatlands can help regulate water flow during droughts, preserve biodiversity, absorb pollutants and stabilize soils.

Trees, vegetation and green spaces can help cities adapt to climate change by reducing the heat island effect in cities, reducing pollution concentration, stabilizing the soil, reducing erosion and absorbing water during floods.

Disaster risk reduction initiatives (DRR) can involve natural infrastructure such as wetlands, freshwater ecosystems, parks, etc. to manage flooding, supply clean water and absorb the impact form waves during storms and hurricanes.

• Sustainable agriculture can reduce land and water use through a circular approach and soil health improvement, and increase food security via crop diversification.

## Ecosystem restoration and conservation

Forests, wetlands and peatlands can provide numerous adaptation solutions. Healthy forests and watersheds can regulate water flow in the event of drought; forests also stabilize soils and slow water runoff; coastal wetlands can limit erosion and partially

absorb the impact of waves during hurricanes; on top of being a source of water in times of drought, inland wetlands can absorb water during floods and act as a filter by absorbing pollutants.

Within EM we flag the green bonds of Klabin, Suzano, and CPMC, all of which have a large % of proceeds dedicated to sustainable forestry.

## Urban green solutions

Aside from their aesthetic value and the physical and mental health benefits they provide city dwellers, trees, vegetation and green spaces can help cities adapt to climate change by reducing the heat island effect in cities, reducing pollution concentration and limit damages to infrastructure by stabilizing the soil and absorbing water during floods.

Within EM we flag Greentown China Holding<sup>’</sup>s green bonds with 40% of proceeds earmarked for sponge city design and building.

## Case study: China’s sponge cities

In 2014 China introduced the Sponge City Initiative, in which NbS would play a leading role in addressing surface-water flooding and enhancing water conservation. The policy targets turning 80% of Chinese urban areas sponge-like by 2030, and 30 cities were selected in 2015-2016 as pilot cases.

Examples of NbS to be deployed in sponge cities include green facades or roofs, the creation of flood parks (green spaces designed to store water during floods), planting trees alongside urban draining and incorporating green landscaping and constructed wetlands or lakes.

The World Bank estimates that to meet the 80% goal, investment in sponge cities needs to scale to up to USD 1tn a year by the end of the decade. Most of this is expected to be met by the public sector, but there is opportunity for the private sector as well. Sponge city-related activities specifically feature in the 2021 Green Bond Endorsed Projects Catalogue for China. The green bond by Greentown China Holdings mentioned above is an example of this.

## Disaster risk reduction

Natural infrastructure such as wetlands, healthy freshwater ecosystems, parks, etc. can be used to manage flooding, supply clean water and limit erosion. For example, wetlands and vegetation reduce the speed and thus damage of water flows during a flood, as well as encourage absorption. Mangroves can protect against erosion and also absorb the impact from waves.

Within EM Manila Water<sup>’</sup>s sustainability bond is an example of a bond used to finance the improvement of water infrastructure and regeneration of coastal ecosystems.

## Case study: Manila Water Sustainability bond

In July 2020, Manila Water issued a US\$500mn Sustainability bond, with the use of proceeds targeted towards financing projects related to (1) Sustainable water and wastewater management, (2) Terrestrial and aquatic biodiversity conservation, and (3) Affordable basic infrastructure categories. One example project it has

contributed to is the Rizal Province Water Supply Improvement, which has increased the water supply in the East Zone by 100MLD.

Separately, Manila Water employs nature-based solutions such as reforestation in watershed areas to solve for water quality risk. These solutions include the protection of 9,515 hectares in the Ipo, La Mesa and Upper Marikina watersheds as well as the planting of over 1m trees in these catchment areas and beyond. NbS such as the seeding of appropriate fish species have also been employed to help control algal blooms. Reforestation and nature-based anti-sedimentation actions have knock on implications for climate adaptation in the Philippines, which is particularly vulnerable to climate change, helping to regulate flooding and have the added benefit carbon sequestration.

## Sustainable Agriculture

Sustainable agriculture is an umbrella-concept for several approaches that include some of the following techniques: minimum or no tillage, mixing crops in a single plot, crop rotation, cover crops, crop-livestock integration, use of compost, organic manure and nitrogen-fixing crops, biological management of pests, efficient water harvesting, use of local resources and renewable energy sources, waste recycling, etc.

While we have not come across these type of bonds in the EM universe, we expect this to change in the future. In DM, Mondelez is an example of a bond where a substantial % of proceeds are earmarked for sustainable agriculture.

## Case study: Mondelez green bond for sustainable agriculture

Of the EUR 2bn green bond Mondelez issued in 2021, EUR 900mn have been allocated to purchase ingredients from suppliers that have contractually agreed to participate in Mondelez<sup>’</sup>s sustainable agriculture programs Cocoa Life and Harmony Wheat (the rest remains unallocated):

Cocoa Life trains farmers on sustainable agricultural practices to increase their resilience to climate change, offers them financial support to incentivize forest protection and monitors deforestation risks in supply chains. Mondelez plans to invest another EUR 600mn in Cocoa Life in the 2022-30 period. The firm targets 100% sourcing through Cocoa Life by 2025 (from 80% in 2022).

The Harmony Wheat program offers farmers training on regenerative agriculture. Additionally participating farmers commit to a Harmony Charter and associated external audits, set aside 3% of their fields for planting flowers and rotate crops to reduce fertilizer use. Currently 98% of the wheat used to manufacture Mondelez<sup>’</sup>s biscuits in Europe comes form Harmony farms.
