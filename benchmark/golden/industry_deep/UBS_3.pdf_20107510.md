## SUMMARY

## 1. Is AI driving global markets?

AI’s narrative has been evolving from a successful US tech story to a key macro topic. To get a truly big picture view of the theme’s penetration into the global cross-asset landscape, we used network analysis – a powerful means to visualize complex markets' interdependencies as intuitive maps. Using Nvidia as our lens, we show that AI’s sphere of influence has greatly expanded in the past two months, reaching equity indices across Europe and Asia and some high-yielding segments of the fixed income market. Sectorwise, new connections have formed with healthcare, financials and industrials – all in focus as prime beneficiaries of the AI revolution – while links with energy and commodities remain elusive. Non-random patterns suggest this reflects a genuine (yet potentially temporary) recalibration of global markets in response to the growing AI theme rather than a wave of generic risk-on mood.

## 2. Are we in a new Tech bubble?

We see the chance of a 'bubble-burst' like in '73, '89, '97/'98 and '00 as remote. There are few signs of overvaluation in software (which is our largest overweight), though we are more concerned on semis (we are neutral). The total Addressable Market numbers for both semis and software look realistic, though that doesn’t make forward valuations for semis (2030) look particularly cheap. We see no sign of overinvesting, and balance sheets look strong. Earnings for semis, however, are running well above trend, performance has decoupled from market drivers, and both software and semis are crowded. Within the Magnificent 7 we continue to like MSFT and Meta.

## 3. Does historically high immigration explain US resilience?

A recent CBO report estimated a very substantial increase in immigration. We expect this has had some impact on growth, though less than fiscal policy, for example. It also does not explain the degree of weakness in the household survey estimates of employment, or why the unemployment rate has risen. It is also a puzzle why the immigration does not seem to be showing up in higher remittances, lower rental vacancy rates, increased electrical hookups, or increased spending on non-durable goods. However, it is difficult to ignore that something is afoot, given the scale of what is reported by the Department of Homeland Security along the Southern border. The faster population growth should help potential GDP grow faster, and it may help explain the differences in wage growth across groups and occupations.

## 4. How fast will the BoJ normalize?

More quickly than markets currently price if US growth holds up, Japanese consumption spending rebounds and the JGB market remains relatively stable. But this is not just about raising the policy rate. It relates to how the market reacts to the Bank pulling back from QE after having bought many more bonds than its peers. With net issuance still large, domestic demand alone will struggle to make up the gap, and large-scale repatriation is not realistic because portfolios have largely been rejigged already. Instead, we believe foreign demand for JGBs will be critical. Foreigners alone bought with the BoJ and are now in a position to exacerbate market weakness, selling if they sense asymmetric price risks. They are the market’s third largest holder, and trade more actively than other participants, accounting for between 45 and 70% of cash bond and futures turnover. For them JGBs are also not inherently ‘safe asset.

## 5. What if there is ‘no landing’?

If the expansion remains resilient (payrolls \~250k pm) and inflation gets stuck at 2-1/2% or higher, we believe there would be real risk the FOMC resumes raising rates in early ’25, taking them to 6.5% by mid ’25. Current equity valuations are already in excess of what would be implied by the yield on corporate bonds. Both the rise in the discount rate combined with the requirement to slow GDP growth can cause up to a 15% correction, with risks of more. Under the surface we would expect a big rotation away from Growth towards Value. Bond equity correlation is likely to remain positive in a high inflation scenario implying the need for portfolio hedges elsewhere. We can find these in FX, making use of low volatility, buying USD or Gold calls and against JPY and Asian FX puts.

## 6. How might a Trump 2.0 play out in markets?

Pricing in the growing possibility of a Trump victory, many equity investors are more optimistic. They tend to regard the 2016 election and the subsequent 2017 experience as the template of how markets will behave. We are concerned that this thinking misses how different the starting points of the US economy, earnings expectations and broader risk premia are this time. Public debt, already set to rise to 116% of GDP by 2033, could rise to 125% of GDP if Trump tax cuts are not rolled back. Issuance pressure could mean much steeper curves, rendering the ex-ante equity risk premium unattractive. Tariffs and lower immigration could also worsen the growth-inflation mix. Tighter monetary and looser fiscal policy relative to the counterfactual should imply a stronger dollar, particularly against EM.

## 7. Is the global consumer awakening?

Global consumption in 2023 was depressed, but high frequency data for the first few months of the year suggests a sharp pick-up. In our global nowcast we estimate a broad basket of (73) consumer variables improved by roughly 1 standard deviation since October, and has added 70bp to global growth (which in turn equates to 60% of the global pick-up in momentum). A further improvement in real wage growth, still low levels of consumer 'intent' in surveys, and scope for lending rates to fall, suggest a further pickup in consumption is possible.

## 8. Is China exporting deflation?

China export prices have started to slow significantly compared to the rest of the world, starting about a year ago. While that may reflect China's weak domestic inflation, it is difficult to find evidence of that spilling over strongly into other countries (we estimate the impact on US inflation at c7bp, and Chinese import prices look little different from those of North Asian exporters. That said, it is notable how broad-based the current weakness in China’s export prices is, extending essentially to all major sectors, and not just Tech. Some of that impulse may still be ahead, which could adversely affect other manufacturing economies and currencies, as well as parts of EM equities.

## 9. Has R\* moved higher?

With most central banks close to, or already having started, their easing cycles, the question is where are they heading. That's where the so-called r\*, or neutral rate, models are useful. The problem with these models, however, is that they come with very large confidence bands (200bp), are focused on the very distant future (5-10y) and can show wildly different things. For the US, for instance, two of the models we show have the long run neutral rate about 30bp below pre-pandemic levels, while the other two have it 63-95bp above. From a markets perspective we should care more about shortrun r\*. We specified a short-run model of r-star that suggests that a real rate of just below 2% into next year should suffice to return inflation to target (not far from what was in the March SEP).

## 10. Why is volatility (still) so low?

We have seen low volatility manifest across various asset classes: Equity volatility has remained suppressed due to dispersion, macro data resilience, a higher quality corporate backdrop, and shifting hedging dynamics. We think higher volatility could result from a deterioration in labor market data, stickier inflation, an earnings downgrade cycle, and/ or an exogenous shock triggering a coordinated unwind in crowded pockets of the market. In rates, we expect downward pressure on vol to continue until the market starts to anticipate more aggressive Fed easing. For FX, we entered 2024 with the view that FX volatility can stay low based on several global trade and rates-related factors. We see some scope for a pickup in volatility from Q3 onwards as potential rates divergences, US elections, and resulting fiscal policy uncertainty could start to pose more of a risk at the margin.

## 1. Is AI driving global markets?

As AI’s implications in areas from productivity to interest rates draw increasing attention from analysts, the media, and even Fed speakers, its narrative seems to be evolving from a tech and/or US story to global macro driver. For instance, in UBS's machine learning model linking stock market performance to macro drivers, almost all of the YTD index returns in global equities can be attributed to the influence of a basket of AI 'winners' in the US (UBXXAIW on Bloomberg), even though AI stocks account for only a third of YTD S&P gains.

To explore whether AI stocks are shaping broader market dynamics, we used network (or graph) analysis to create visual maps of markets' interconnectedness, shown in Figure 2Big techund bslra fom- p (J2F 0,4). to Figure 5… with'AI ners pagd omk uc. By offering both a granular and big-picture view of market changes, this framework provides a powerful and intuitive means to track emerging themes, such as AI's embedding into the global cross-asset landscape. In the diagrams in Figure 2 and Figure 4 we show 90 global financial instruments as nodes linked by lines that reflect the correlation strength of their 2-day returns within two distinct periods in 2024. Node size indicates the average degree of interconnection and colours differentiate asset classes. Proximity between nodes suggests closer links, with clusters forming around highly interconnected instruments. To zoom-in on AI’s penetration, in Figure 3 and Figure 5 we highlight the specific links of Nvidia. Throughout, we’ll be contrasting two YTD return windows of approximately 35 days: the initial one to Feb 20, and the subsequent period leading up to today. Note that the latter segment includes some pivotal moments for AI in 2024, such as Nvidia’s Q4 earnings release and its GTC conference, where discussions around AI’s tipping point to widespread enterprise and governmental adoption propelled its perceived inevitability in the macro conversation.

Comparing Figure 2 with Figure 4, the evolution of AI’s position in global markets becomes apparent. In the first period, big tech and respective sectors formed a distinct cluster orbiting on the edge of the market network. In the second period, cohesion within the Magnificent 7 group – minus Tesla from the start – dissipated, leading its members down divergent paths seemingly influenced by their respective alignment with the AI theme. Nvidia, Meta, Amazon and, to some degree, Microsoft thus wove themselves deeper into the market fabric, while Google and Apple retreated to the periphery. The transition is even starker when spotlighting Nvidia in Figure 3 vs Figure 5. The connections it forms double from one period to the next, extending beyond its original big-tech family and some old tech friends like ILS and Taiwanese stocks to forge ties with multiple global equity indices and even some high-yield segments of fixed income markets. While this dramatic shift could simply reflect a generic wave of risk appetite, the less dense nature of the latter period’s network and its neater asset class separation seem to tally with a broader market recalibration in response to AI’s ascent.

AI stocks' newfound links with sectors like healthcare and financials aligns with the expected trajectory of its benefits. Its links with steel and industrials should also not surprise too much, given their role in the building and cooling of its physical infrastructure in data-centres. Rather, it is the lack of direct links with energy and related commodities that we find noteworthy. As the quest for AGI proceeds, the industry’s focus is shifting from textual processing to the more computationally expensive fields of multimodality and physical perception, pushing up power consumption. Still, the AI & energy dynamic and the net effects may well unfold in unexpected ways, as efforts are under way to develop more efficient biologically-inspired architectures and AI models are showing some success at optimizing energy consumption (including their own, e.g. via intelligent cooling of data-centres), which might even make them pivotal elements in the energy transition

Though revealing, our analysis comes with the caveat that correlation doesn’t necessarily imply causation. As with other unsupervised machine learning methods, the purpose is to identify patterns as opposed to predict specific outcomes. Indeed, beyond AI’s increased resonance, our analysis here also highlights other emerging themes in global markets, such as gold distancing itself from the rates space, the healthcare sector gaining centrality (perhaps as a result of GLP1 obesity treatments?), a changing relation between FX and the rest of the market and the strengthening ties between FX volatility and interest rates. These themes suggest there is a lot more value in market structure analysis to be gleaned, and this can also pave the way for further analysis.

## 2. Are we in a new Tech bubble?

A bubble usually means prices fall 80% when they burst. This was the case for the Nikkei post Dec 1989, the Nifty 50 post Jan 73, some of the Asian markets in the Asian crisis and TMT post 2000, when the peak to trough decline was 83%. This would imply that to be in a bubble, stock prices are approximately c4X overvalued. We see almost no chance of that being the case. We are overweight software (it has been our biggest overweight) but benchmark semis (where we would focus on the laggards). In our recent note we screened for 5 main factors (tacticals, earnings, valuation, quality and decoupling from macro factors).

There are six characteristics of a bubble that we do not in general have:

1) Extremes of over-valuation - not generally seen. For software, we do not find clear cut overvaluation. Software trades on P/E relatives where it can outperform 83% of the time, a FCF yield (in year-ahead earnings adjusted for shareholder based compensation) of 2.3%. The most important valuation measure is market cap to total addressable market (TAM). We think software spending globally could eventually rise to 2.5% of global GDP by 2027 (compared to 2.3% in the US currently) and putting software companies on an average margin would leave them trading on a P/E of 15X. The previous bubble (TMT, Japan and Nifty 50) have seen multiples peak at P/E 45X- 72X in the bubble areas (which at the time represented 30% to 40% of global market cap).

For semis we are more concerned; the price to sales (admittedly with a survivorship bias) is at extremes and if we take the market cap over a total addressable market that implies semis account for 0.8% of GDP in 2030 v 0.6% today, then they trade on c6.2X 2030 sales or a P/E of c30X (assuming the post 2020 average margin ex Covid). We do still find cheap areas (the laggards such as SEC on a 9% FCF yield for 2025 and the analogue companies such as ADI or Infineon (which trade on 34X and 17.8X 24 earnings).

2) Total Addressable Market numbers are broadly realistic. Today semis and software together account for c3% of US GDP, less so elsewhere. Software and semis are the new ‘oil’ of the global economy and oil consumption in the US is currently 2% of GDP (peaked at times of OPEC-induced stress at close to 10% of GDP) and thus we think semis and software revenues rising to 3.5% of GDP is quite plausible on a medium term basis.

3) High earnings risk. We can proxy this by looking at over-investing or earnings decoupling from trend. Neither software nor semis are over-investing. However, semis earnings are 16% above trend while software is marginally below.

4) Performance decoupling from earnings. This is not happening in software, but it is in semis, although the decoupling is not extreme.

5) Decoupling from macro drivers. On our macro models, software has slightly lagged, semis have overshot and are discounting an ISM of c60 (c3.5% GDP growth), even excluding the Gen AI-related names.

6) Very overbought/overowned. Semis were dangerously overbought in late March, in our view. This is not the case for software. Both sectors are crowded on UBS data, but this was also the case 6 months ago (and curiously analyst Buy ratings for software are abnormally low).

7) Deterioration in balance sheets or quality. Balance sheets look rock solid (net cash) and semis still score top of the quality screen (based on historical growth and RoE/ RoE volatility).

Magnificent 7: the only name that looks bubbly to us has been Tesla. We have struggled with Apple - 80% a hardware company on a software model with high China risk in a mature hardware cycle (see here) and also with Alphabet (online advertising is mature, the margin of search will fall). We continue to like MSFT (significant pricing ability, 2.4% FCF yield) and Meta. Nvidia trades on 25.2X Jan 2026 price earnings but an extreme 14X (Jan) 2028 EV/sales.

## 3. Does high immigration explain US resilience?

In a report released in January, the Congressional Budget Office estimated substantially more immigration than other statistical agencies, based on the magnitude of the surge in apprehensions reported along the US southern border. As shown below, the US Census Bureau estimates that immigration accounts for the bulk of US population growth. Brookings scholars Wendy Edelberg and Tara Watson argue in a recent publication that the CBO estimates fall within a plausible range, and that the higher immigration assumptions could explain the sustained strength of employment in the monthly BLS employment report.

But does it in fact explain this strength, or the broader resilience? We expect the rise in net international migration has helped, but has had much less impact on the resilience of growth in 2023 than fiscal policy, for example. It also not does explain the degree of weakness in the household survey estimates of employment, or why the unemployment rate has risen.

We published a deep dive on the topic titled "US population growth" that reviewed a range of evidence and the economic impact. The CBO estimated net international migration in calendar year 2023 was 3.3 million, more than double the most recent Census estimate for the year ending July 1, 2023. That is a lot for a country where the resident population grew an estimated 1.7 million in 2018 and 1.5 million in 2019. The table below shows the estimates of net international migration from the CBO, the Census Bureau projections under three assumptions (low, middle and high), and the Social Security Administration Trustees' (SSA) assessment. The CBO estimates for 2022 to 2026 far exceeded the others.

If there were 4 or more million people dropped on the US in two years, that should move some needles. That would be a large incremental addition to the 44 million rental households in the US. But we see little evidence that rental vacancy rates are falling, as the figure below shows. In addition, according to data sources such as Zillow, Apartment List, and BLS estimates, rent growth in the marketplace has been slowing. If units were being filled, or the non-immigrant population pushed into units covered by the survey, one might expect downward pressure on rental vacancy rates and upward pressure on rents. Another place we might expect to see more evidence of a surge in immigration would be remittances tracked by the Bank of Mexico. While the source migration of what DHS is encountering is from Asia and Africa too, not just Central and South America, among that Mexico is less than half. But the fall in remittances, and the composition, would appear inconsistent with a surge in immigration, unless there were larger two-way flow. Checking electrical hookups or meters from utility companies in available data from the Energy Information Agency, utility companies were not reporting a rash of new customers in 2023. School enrolment data may be too lagged, but nothing there yet either. One might expect more non-durable goods spending. Nondurable goods include food, clothing, shoes, gasoline, medical products, and personal care products. Compared to 2013 to 2019, 2023 posted the second slowest pace of growth, in a year where services spending growth was second strongest, and durable goods spending growth was middle of that pack. If this is not noticeably boosting consumption growth, it didn’t really boost resiliency.

However, it is difficult to ignore something is afoot, given the scale of what is being reported by the Department of Homeland Security along the southern border. As a result, we marked up our estimates of population growth, as shown in the chart below, and our assessment of US potential GDP growth. We think the potential impact can be seen in the differences in nominal wage growth across groups and occupations. We wrote a note on this two years ago. Looking across the labor market, we assessed the tightest segments reflected the shortfall in immigration during Covid. We show one example of that being undone in the chart comparing wage growth, below. The convergence we expect reflects a less tight labor market. We also estimated that the labor market could expand at a faster clip too, but those assumptions depend heavily on other assumptions for the labor force participation rate, and our calculations fall well short of the pace of recent nonfarm payroll employment gains. In the end, household survey employment and labor force estimates over the last six months have been weak. The unemployment rate has risen not because of improved labor supply. The immigration boost would not explain that.

## 4. How fast will the BoJ normalize?

As quickly as economic and financial conditions allow, is our short response. And that goes both for raising the policy rate towards neutral (which is seen as somewhere in the 1.0-2.0% range for the time being) and pulling back from QQE (allowing corporate bonds and commercial paper to run off their balance sheet, special loans to expire and initially stemming growth in their JGB portfolio as well as working out a plan to dispose eventually of ETFs).

The longer answer depends on three things more specifically. First, whether US growth holds up and Europe and China continue to skirt a recession. Second, whether Japanese domestic consumption will rebound solidly in Q2—we believe it will, boosted by Prime Minister Kishida's one-off income tax cut in June and expectations for further acceleration in core wages to 3%y/y in FY2024 from 2% in FY2023 after shunto; this would propel the virtuous cycle in underlying inflation from corporate profits through wages to spending and inflation expectations, which is not well anchored to 2% as yet. Three, how markets trade, which is the worry that chain could be strengthened or broken in the interim, by fresh lows in JPY or bond market volatility. We expand on all of these things here, envisaging the policy rate will be raised again from October to 1.0% by end-2025 (60% probability) or July to 1.5% (40%).

Reducing JGB purchases must be managed more iteratively. This is because, in its singleminded pursuit of reflation, the BoJ has been fully financing the country's free-spending and heavily indebted government. They also drove down its funding costs by taking another 50% of JGBs off institutional balance sheets besides. Over the past two decades, some large historical holders like the GIPF and Japan Post Bank have also modernized by broadly diversifying their portfolios, including having made large investments overseas. Thus nobody really knows what the underlying capacity of the market to reabsorb bonds is now. Nor are many confident that the government can deliver on its pledge to reduce issuance despite nominal growth and tax revenues recovering. That's a product less of Mr Kishida's public support ratings than of the impact of sharply deteriorating demographics and associated increases in public health / long-term care costs plus a big increase in defence spending to adapt to a changing geopolitical environment.

We recently published extensive bottom-up estimates of how many JGBs each major investor group would be able to buy in benign and stressed-yield scenarios. Our conclusions are that Japanese banks have a lot less capacity than is commonly assumed. That is mainly because their asset growth will slow in post-QE environment and they have few alternative investments to sell. The main scope comes from running down BoJ deposits (reserves), but that is not possible absent QT and wouldn't inject any new demand into the system anyway; it just transfers bonds from the central bank to the private sector at lower prices. They are also wary of capital losses, with risks to yields skewed highly asymmetrically to the upside. It's a very different environment to what took place in Italy and Greece, for example.

Lifers and the social security sector are already mature in terms of asset growth. The former has already repatriated the bulk of its foreign bond portfolio and the latter is largely a passive rebalancer hugging predefined and static benchmarks. Households likely will buy a lot more bonds, but this again is just going to cannibalize JGB supports elsewhere via any rundown of bank deposits. It leaves foreigns as the most consequential single group. They alone bought bonds and have become the market's third largest holder (after the BoJ and lifers). This gives them the capacity to sell a lot of bonds if they sense weakness. In such a situation the central bank must avoid being redrawn in to backstop prices. Buying a lot more bonds to fulfil its financial stability mandate when the economy is no longer in deflation could quickly be construed as debt monetization, potentially posing broader risks to confidence and stability.

This also implies that repatriation fears are a red herring. We calculate that, even with much higher JGB yields, net foreign bond sales would be considerably smaller than in 2022. We also don't expect such a scenario to play out any time soon, and are more worried about the impact of the carry trade unwinding to push down USDJPY. But forewarned is forearmed.

## 5. What if there is ‘no landing’?

To many, "no landing" implies inflation remains enough above target to keep rates elevated but the expansion continues without the FOMC pushing unemployment up to wring out the remaining inflation. That could be one outcome, however, we expect if the expansion remains resilient (NFP at roughly 250k per month) and inflation gets stuck at 2-1/2% or higher, there would be real risk the FOMC resumes raising rates again by early next year, reaching 6.5% Fed Funds by mid next year (Figure 24"No landig cmefrt hs p,bu  Fk y).

Rates: If the Fed responded to elevated inflation by tightening, the curve would flatten sharply but 10s will likely still sell off. From a historical perspective, 10s are quite rich relative to spot FF (Figure 2610s leFtarg: dy hioc wv ,un p -f ). Most of that is simply that term premiums are depressed, but this year negative carry has been so destructive to most rates investors' performance that we think it would be difficult to hold 10s near 4.50% if carry deteriorated sharply. The worst performance for 10s would be in the unlikely scenario that inflation is elevated and the Fed doesn’t tighten in the face of higher inflation. We wouldn’t expect real yields to rise that much, but breakevens would likely move dramatically. Note that elevated inflation does not ease the longer run debt burden in the US. Bills, FRNs, and TIPS have their coupons adjust nearly immediately if inflation is high, and they make up 1/3 of total debt. Adding in nominal coupons with less than one year to maturity takes this up to nearly 45% of debt adjusting its coupon very quickly. If we further assume that elevated inflation causes a persistent term premium in rates, it doesn’t take long for the interest cost as a % of GDP to be higher due to elevated inflation.

Equities: The correlation between positive economic surprises and market returns has fallen to zero. Investors are in the early stages of worrying about an economy that may be running too hot. Equities will obviously do very well if into a no landing inflation doesn’t pick up strongly. In this scenario we would be looking for the few cyclicals that are not already yet pricing in very strong growth. These are small cap, Korea/ Japan, UK retailing, Banks, and Mining. However, unless productivity booms, and it's not obvious so far that this is the case, a continuation of strong growth should be accompanied by higher inflation and higher rates. Both the rise in the discount rate combined with requirement to slow GDP growth to well below trend can easily cause a 10% to 15% correction. The risk is that the correction is deeper than that because current valuations are already in excess of what would be implied by current yield on corporate bonds. In a high inflation scenario we would expect both government bonds to selloff and credit spreads to widen, making for a big catch down in multiples (Figure 27US Bartedcop bnyil &P50's ju /E). Under the surface we would expect a big rotation away from Growth towards Value.

FX: In principle the most direct FX impact of a US no landing scenario is a stronger USD. But what is the most efficient expression? In our 5 Feb ’24 note on this theme we recommended what were then 1-year, 10-delta call USD calls against JPY and CHF. We argued that the carry gap and the fact that the BOJ and SNB are currently following policies seen as FX negative allowed such trades to act as cheap US no landing hedges, even if implied volatility levels were not especially cheap. The odds of persistent BOJ intervention are diminished if general USD strength and higher USD rates are the drivers. Gold's relationship with higher real rates has weakened considerably in recent years and this would be our favoured expression to protect against pressure on US assets, including the dollar, from a potential sharp selloff in the US Treasury market (Figure 27US Bartedcop bnyil &P50's ju /E). Bond equity correlation is likely to remain positive in a high inflation scenario (Figure 28Averag bond-quity cl CPIp f ) implying the need for portfolio hedges elsewhere. We can find these in FX, making use of low volatility today, through buying USD or Gold calls and against JPY and Asian FX puts

EM: The reaction of EM to US inflation and rates has historically been non linear. As US rates rise modestly amidst improving growth, EM assets tend to hold up well. However, if we begin to see US rates rise more than 30 bps within a quarter, EM assets begin to suffer. EM currencies would likely suffer strongly in a US non-landing scenario, particularly so Asian currencies, if higher commodity prices are a part of the higher inflation landscape. Carry protection is unequally distributed towards LatAm, but is at very low levels in the rest of EM (Figure 29). Fiscal vulnerabilities in EM are likely to come to the fore, and South African and Brazilian long end could come under significant pressure. If high inflation is coming from US specific growth, or worse, tariffs, EM equities will likely strongly underperform already falling US equities.

EM assets suffer a non linear negative reaction when US rates rise by more than 30-40 bps within 3m.

## 6. How might a Trump 2.0 play out in markets?

Over the past century, a Republican sweep (7 elections in the past 100y) has, on average been followed by 7.3% returns in 3m, and 6% returns over 12m. A Republican President with a split Congress (only 2 elections in the timeframe of our analysis) has seen similar 12m returns (Figure 30). Pricing the growing possibility of a Trump victory, many investors are more optimistic (Figure 31Relativ prfomnc Idusy g FX hwk b T  ). They regard the 2016 election and the subsequent 2017 experience as the template of how markets will behave.

We are concerned that this thinking significantly underestimates how different the starting points of the US economy, equity markets and broader risk premia are this time. The US economy is much later cycle, with the unemployment rate below NAIRU and inflation still running well above Fed’s targets (Figure 32). Despite the fact that global growth is weaker today than it was before Trump’s 2016 election win, earnings expectations across equity markets are higher (Figure 33). An important difference is in risk premia (credit spreads, volatility, skew). They were wide at the time of the 2016 election, and as they contracted in the 12m post, equity valuations rose too. Today’s risk premia are approaching the tightest levels in 20y (Figure 34). There is limited room for equity rerating to fuel a rally.

Importantly, US public debt is 20pp higher than it was when Trump won the election in 2016. Irrespective of who wins the election, this burden is likely to increase. The CBO, which assumes a rollback of Trump tax cuts, still sees US Public debt to GDP rising by 17 pp to 116% of GDP over the coming 10y. Our base case assumes a partial extension of Trump’s 2017 tax cuts (even the Biden administration looks likely to extend these for middle to lower income groups). If a Trump administration backed by a Republican Congress were to fully extend the tax cuts, it could add \$350bln to the fiscal bill every year until 2033. An additional decline in estate taxes and business deductions could push the tax cut cost to \$450 bln a year. This would imply a cost of \$2.8trl over 8y, or a rise, relative to the counterfactual, in public debt of 7-10pp of GDP to roughly 125% of GDP by 2033 (Figure 35).

The direction of yields will be driven mainly by how growth does. Based on a cautious growth outlook, we believe they will head lower in '24 and '25, but the back end could strongly lag any bond rally should Trump tax cuts be extended. Sustained higher yields will mean the equity risk premium on offer is low, creating a headwind for equities.

Unlike Trump 1.0, when the incremental boost to growth and sentiment from new tax cuts was very strong, we think the incremental boost to the economy from an extension of tax cuts would likely be limited. More consequential for equities could be a renewal of the trade war that defined the middle and latter phase of Trump’s Presidency. Trump’s team plans for tariffs on trading partners, particularly China, to be raised even more aggressively than in the first term. We estimate a 1% rise in import prices leads to a 12bp rise in US CPI. The realised impact of tariffs could be diluted by dollar appreciation, demand destruction and by tariffs being absorbed in exporter or importer margins. Nonetheless, a 10% tariff, if levied across all imports (lesser room for substitution) risks pushing our core PCE forecast (Q4/Q4) for 2025 from 2.1% to roughly 3.3%. This rise in inflation may not sustain. We would be more worried about the hit to growth and sentiment.

The other channel through which US growth inflation mix could worsen is through lower immigration. The positive labour supply shock of the past 3 years has not only boosted payrolls but also aggregate demand, particularly demand for goods. Immigration is likely to come lower next year from '23 and '24’s estimated average rate of about 3.3 mln a year (Figure 36). Under a Democrat administration this could come down towards 1.2-1.3 mln a year, but under a Republican sweep it could come down to as low as 0.5-0.7 mln, which could mean a tight labour market & high inflation even with weaker consumer demand.

Tighter monetary and looser fiscal policy relative to the counterfactual should imply a stronger dollar against EUR, but particularly against EM. In conclusion, a step down in immigration and trade wars should hit growth when the economy is already late cycle. An exhausted bond market, high earnings expectations and already tight risk premia make us more cautious on the potential for equities under Trump than is the consensus.

## 7. Is the global consumer awakening?

As we noted in our global growth (nowcast), growth in the second half of '23 was particularly depressed. Restrictive monetary policy and still negative real wage growth had pushed real GDP growth for the median economy to just 1.6%, compared to its long run average of about 2.7%. Every component of our nowcast was running below its long run average, and weakness had spread from manufacturing/production to 'domestic' data. The good news, however, was that we were starting to see a sharp pick up in momentum at the start of this year, seemingly almost entirely concentrated in the 'consumer' part of the nowcast (73 variables in our >320 variable nowcast).

As we show in Figure 40, consumption in the national accounts data has been weak in both EM (0.4 stdev below normal in Q2-Q4 in '23) and DM (0.8 stdev below normal). Figure 41 shows how retail sales has been a touch weaker than aggregate consumption, running below its long run average for 24 consecutive months. That's in part because it is more skewed towards goods demand, which has not yet fully reverted back to trend after the pandemic surge. We also show the US separately in those charts. The general perception is that US consumption has been much stronger than the rest of DM. That's true in the national accounts data, where the gap vs the rest of the G7 reached a 20-year high in H2-23, but mainly because the other countries were so weak. Annual consumption growth in the US in Q4 was just a touch above its long run average (z-score of 0.3 on data back to 2000).

Consider now year-to-date developments. In Figure 42 we update our global nowcast decomposition, showing how the improvement in consumption has continued. From the trough in October ('23) to the latest data in March ('24) our global consumption basket has improved by a full standard deviation and added 70bp to global growth (60% of the aggregate improvement since then). The difference with our update last month is that global growth seems to have further accelerated (from about 2.7% in Feb to a bit over 3% in March) and that strength seems to be broadening out, to now also include autos, 'business' (see footnote) and construction categories. Consumption in March adds 40bp more to global growth than its long run average. Figure 43 shows that the range of the data is wide and clearly very volatile, but the trend is unmistakenly up. The 'services' category (which by number of variables is only ⅓ the size of the consumer category) is still contributing below average in Figure 42 because of the heavy weight of PMI services in this category (still below their long run average).

The pick up in the 'consumer' is important: our forecasts assume that as inflation recedes, consumption gets a boost from improving real disposable incomes. That appears to be happening, as shown in Figure 39 and Figure 44. However, the timing of that was highly uncertain ex ante. Using very long run (10y) averages, there is a tight link between real wage growth and consumption (r-square of 0.45 in a cross-country panel), but the relationship is very poor when looking at higher frequency data. We worried that it might take some time for consumers to notice the affordability improvement, in part because the cumulative gap in real wage declines vs trend was large, and in part because while price growth is slowing the overall level of prices is still increasing. Also, as we have shown for the US, inflation is not the only drag on consumption; debt service in the US has reduced real disposable income by a full pp (equal to 20% of nominal income growth).

Although the incremental gains from disinflation are limited from here, survey data suggests consumption could further improve. In the Michigan data for the US, for instance, the percentage of respondents who believe high prices are still a reason to delay purchases of durable household goods (30%) or autos (40%) has declined, but are far from normalized (roughly 20pp above 2019 levels for both). Likewise, the ECB's Consumer Expectations Survey suggests people significantly reduced consumption (69% of respondents) to cope with higher prices, either by shopping around, trading down or outright reducing purchases (Figure 45). As inflation normalizes those behaviors should continue to reverse.

## 8. Is China exporting deflation?

While most of the world has been struggling with inflation and weak trade, China has seen the opposite: deflation and an export surge. Overcapacity has increased across several sectors. Weak domestic demand is a factor, but strong supply appears to be playing a key role. Cyclically, things may improve somewhat: demand is bottoming out, and energy, food & tech should generate modest inflation. Beyond this, however, we see a rising likelihood of China exporting disinflation and challenging growth in other manufacturing economies over the next 1-2y. To be clear, there isn’t robust evidence of this today, but several symptoms are lining up in a manner that suggests China’s rising manufacturing investment may have global consequences. This is in addition to the weak growth impulse radiating from China’s slower property investment and imports. A weaker RMB is a third potential conduit for global disinflationary spillovers.

The data clearly shows China exporters gaining global share in recent years. However, those gains came mostly during the pandemic when supply chains collapsed, and China’s export prices were going up, not down. The fall in China’s export deflators (and the potential ‘export’ of disinflation) is much more recent, starting about a year ago. While that may reflect China's weak domestic inflation, it is difficult to find evidence of that spilling over strongly into other countries. In the US import price data, for instance, China’s prices have declined just 3% (not 12% as implied by the aggregate export deflators), which is similar to other North Asian exporters. For illustrative purposes: if one were to make the extreme assumption that all Chinese imports were for US consumption (rather than production), that would equate to an inflation impact of just 7 basis points. That said, it is notable how broad-based the current weakness in China’s export prices is, extending essentially to all major sectors, and not just Tech. Some of that impulse may still be ahead of us.

We present a scorecard along six dimensions that points to Europe, Japan, Thailand and Korea being most exposed to potentially rising trade competition from China. Mexico, Brazil & the US appear less at risk. Commodity exporters such as Indonesia, Australia, South Africa and Colombia also face challenges as China’s investment in the more commodity-hungry property sector remains weak. Higher weight for core goods in CPI baskets and much larger imports from China as a proportion of consumption suggest that EM inflation is more exposed to a potential disinflationary impulse from China ahead.

We see EM FX as the most directly exposed asset class. China export price disinflation would essentially exert the same pressure on other currencies as a weaker CNH. CZK, HUF, THB and COP, and CNH via options, are our preferred shorts. PLN, INR, BRL and, tactically, KRW should hold up better. Potential deepening of China export disinflation is likely to pose bigger challenges for EM equities than DM, in our view. Korea has been one of our key Overweights within EM for the last 6m but, longer term, it appears relatively vulnerable, as does Indonesia. Latam and India appear more insulated. We see tactical value in parts of Chinese equities, and remain long index calls over the next 3-6m in an anticipation of a modest cyclical upturn. Sectorally, global ex China autos appear most vulnerable. See here for our list of companies potentially positively / negatively affected by China exporting disinflation. The main beneficiaries are likely in local debt amid disinflation. 2-5y debt in Brazil, India, Korea, and Mexico should outperform the US, FX neutral.

## 9. Has R\* moved higher?

With most central banks close to, or already having started, their easing cycles, the question is where are they heading. That's where the so-called $\Gamma ^ { \star } ,$ or neutral rate, debate is useful. R\* models essentially try to determine the landing zone for policy rates once all the cyclical dust has settled: where short term interest rate would be in the absence of business cycle shocks, with output (and employment) at potential, savings equating investment, and inflation stable and at target (see this BIS summary).

The problem with such models is that this utopian state rarely, if ever, occurs. Hence, the estimates extracted from the models are not only very far in the future (5-10 years) but are also surrounded by error bands that can be hundreds of basis points wide. The other problem is that different models can show very different things. In Figure 52Long-ru (eal)\* stimf d, for instance, we show 4 different models (a VAR, a Term Structure model, and two semistructural models i.e. our own and Holston-Laubach-Williams (HLW)). The range of estimates for the medians of those models was between 0.8% and 2.2% (for the neutral real fed funds rate), and the error band shown around our own model is about 190bp wide (it was 320bp wide in the middle of the inflation surge in '22). Figure 55and Figure 56 show how sensitive the HLW model is to incoming data: the vintages of different estimates for the US and Eurozone move around a lot.

Notwithstanding the uncertainty, there is a debate on whether, after decades of declining, neutral real rates may be increasing again. Factors that could be affecting savings-investment forces are persistently large fiscal deficits, higher public investment for the green transition, a higher dependency ratio, the potential for AI to boost productivity growth etc.. However, prior to the pandemic many of those variables never seemed to contribute much to the estimates and the structural forces that were pushing interest rates down are still in place (low potential growth, ageing, inequality, scarcity of safe assets). We are not going to resolve the debate here but, in the semi-structural models (HLW and UBS) estimates of long run r\* are 30bp lower now than they were prepandemic (Dec '19), while in the Hordahl-Tristani and Lubik-Matthes models,they are 63bp and 95bp higher, respectively. We lean a bit more towards the lower estimates because those models do a better job of the trend-cycle decomposition (and reducing the pandemic distortions).

What we should care about more, from a markets perspective, is short run r\*: what is appropriate policy in the next 3 years or so, to guide inflation back to target with minimal job loss. While the long run dot in the Fed's Summary of Economic Projections (SEP) has been remarkably stable, the short run implied real rate in their forecast has been wildly volatile, fluctuating between 1½-2 ½% the last 12 months (currently 2% for 2024). Figure 51 charts the Fed's policy rate, the current FOMC median for the FF rate in '24 and '25, two versions of a Taylor rule, and the SEP's min-max range for long run neutral (all nominal). As is evident, the Fed is choosing to run much tighter policy than its own policy rules imply, possibly because it is nervous about the sustainability of disinflation or the momentum they still see in the labour market. In order to get to their (largely unchanged) long run r\* estimate they need to run policy tighter in the short run than what historically would have sufficed.

In Figure 53, we show our estimate of a short run r\* model, which we constructed with an augmented version of something proposed by the Fed here. The key feature of the model is that, in the short run, the IS curve is dominant (the output gap determines inflation); adding a Phillips curve doesn't add any explanatory power. We've augmented that with i) the trend-cycle decomposition of HLW to get to the time varying intercept of the IS curve; 2) Bayesian techniques to back out uncertainty from posterior distributions (instead of bootstrapping) and (3) we add more controls, in particular the Brookings fiscal impact measure and the Gilchrist-Zakrajsek bond spread. Using that model, we believe a real rate of 1.8% until roughly Q1-2025, would suffice at this stage to return inflation to target (not far from what was in the SEP), compared to the actual real rate of 2.7%. One important point to note though, is that these estimates are extremely sensitive to the output gap assumption. If we used the output gap behind the HLW model or the CBO's estimate – both assume the output gap is essentially closed – then the implied short run real rate would be closer to zero (Figure 54). That's because the model would assume there is no positive output gap to eliminate/cool down. That seems implausible given the current state of US momentum.

## 10. Why is volatility (still) so low?

While we see volatility 'under the hood', equity volatility at the index level remains largely suppressed. We have since seen low volatility manifest across various asset classes, as well (e.g. rates, FX, and credit). We have written extensively on why equity volatility is so low. We see five key reasons: 1) Low intra-index correlations, driven by sector divergences resulting from Covid-era disruptions, differing sensitivities to rising yields, and an earnings revision bifurcation between Tech+/AI vs. everything else; 2) Resilience in macro data, particularly on the labor market front; 3) A higher quality broad market mitigating higher interest rate and macro risks (Figure 57); 4) Shifting hedging preferences, with investors favoring upside over downside risk of late; and 5) Volatility supply from option-selling ETF issuance – not a primary driver, in our view, but keeping volatility suppressed at the margin.

We think it takes one (or more) of the following to drive equity vol higher: 1) A deterioration in the jobs data, particularly with equities now operating until recently in a 'good is good / bad is bad' environment (Figure 58); 2) A coordinated unwind of crowded longs, with equity positioning having become more stretched YTD (Figure 59); 3) A coordinated earnings downgrade cycle – This de facto entails a fade in the AI tailwind, which is not our base case; 4) Inflation higher for longer – Equity markets have largely digested higher real yields, though rising inflation breakevens are starting to drive equity volatility higher at the margin (Figure 60). Price action post-March CPI supports this argument; and/or 5) An exogenous shock ('unknown unknown') – À la March 2020. While there is significant risk premium embedded for US elections fo instance, this is a known unknown, and elections tend to see volatility offered postevent. However, in a Trump 2.0 administration scenario, we could see muted returns and a moderately higher equity volatility environment resulting from higher economic/fiscal policy uncertainty (e.g. 2018 trade war).

We have seen bond volatility compress relative to equity volatility YTD, in line with our view (here, here; Figure 61). We see this persisting as we approach the first rate cut: 1) as monetary policy uncertainty declines, and 2) as equity volatility could moderately rise in the back half of this year; Though we would be cautious about thinking rate vol can drop back to its 2020-21 levels the way that equity and FX vol has. To get back to the 2021 levels, 5s would likely need to move back below 1%. Part of the decline can be attributed to high strikes bid up in Q4 by fear of 10s hitting levels last seen in 2000, and low strikes bid up on continued concerns about a recession in 2024 that would force aggressive Fed easing. There is much less concern about either of those tails now. We expect downward pressure on vol to continue until the market starts to anticipate more aggressive Fed easing. If the market is simply pricing a few cuts to get real FF to a less extreme level, we don’t think that puts any upward pressure on vol. It is when we see pricing for the possible need for the Fed to make financial conditions easy that vol is likely to rise.

For FX, we have seen the process of declining G10 FX volatility that started in Q4 2022 extend further YTD. We see three key reasons for this trend lower: 1) The trade shocks caused by events such as the pandemic and the European energy crisis after the start of the Ukraine war have both faded as significant factors. Even in a world where China continues to grow its share of global exports, the current account and balance of payments positions of major economies are far from stretched levels; 2) Limited rates divergence – The market has tended to price other major central banks as very likely to follow the Fed’s decisions closely (Figure 62). This leads to less room for volatility and has been exacerbated by the data-driven delays to the start of the Fed’s cutting cycle; and 3) An absence of political shocks, elections, or referenda with the potential to cause major surprises.

Higher FX volatility hinges on: 1) The start of potential rates divergence with the SNB having cut already and expectations high that the ECB and Bank of Canada aren’t far behind, even as the Fed’s expected move keeps getting pushed back; and 2) The possibility that as the US elections in November get closer, the FX market will start to price in greater risk premia for shocks like aggressive trade tariffs in 2025. If the market really does become more critically concerned about US debt dynamics as sharp rises in gold prices might suggest, ultimately this should also boost FX volatility.

Maxwell Grinacoff, CFA Gerry Fowler Artour Danilov Sean Simonds Mike Cloherty Shahab Jalinoos

Equity volatility has remained suppressed due to dispersion-driving factors, macro data resilience, a higher quality corporate backdrop, and shifting hedging dynamics. We think higher volatility could result from a deterioration in labor market data, stickier inflation, an earnings downgrade cycle, and/or an exogenous shock triggering a coordinated unwind in crowded pockets of the market.

We think fear of being short rate vol has worked its way out of market pricing since the SVB crisis in 2023, in part because carry has been challenged elsewhere. The inverted curve means there is a significant hurdle for a longer duration portfolio to beat the short term return on cash. This is likely to continue creating interest in yield enhancing strategies including selling vol.

We entered 2024 with the view that FX volatility can stay low based on several global trade and rates-related factors, and we could see this extending further into Q2. But looking ahead, we see some scope for a pickup in volatility from Q3 onwards.
