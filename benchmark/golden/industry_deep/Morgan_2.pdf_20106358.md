# No Sliding Out of DMs

The US is still too strong for EM to handle, especially as the EM rate differential versus the US falls further. This is likely to keep EM assets on the back foot and lagging risk in general. We leave our USD longs in place, namely THB, PHP and CLP. Sovereign credit is all about alpha at this point.

FX & EM Strategy: While the year-to-date divergence in performance between EM and DM is notable, with both EM FX and bonds lagging, it is consistent with the divergence in both flows and economic data surprises. Positioning by EM-dedicated funds is cautiously optimistic but with room to add more risk. However, to see more sustained gains for EM it needs a less US-centric Goldilocks scenario, with China in particular playing a more positive part, while the path of rate differentials between EM and the US needs to slow its narrowing. For EM credit, the additional positive would be market access and debt workouts for the lowest-rated countries.

Sovereign Credit Strategy: Our overall view remains on the more cautious side, with a preference for lower-beta credits across the IG and BB space. For the lowerrated credits, it's clear that idiosyncratic drivers are key across the space, as evidenced by recent volatility, with alpha more important than beta at this point. We reinforce our like stances on Angola, Argentina, Egypt, El Salvador and Ghana in addition to outright Venezuela long trade recommendations. LatAm Macro Strategy: In light of recent volatility in US rates, we revisit one of our favourite analyses from last year and explore the latest rates spreads and valuations in LatAm versus the US. Asia Macro Strategy: We continue to be cautious on Asia FX/rates markets. We are long USD/PHP, long USD/THB, short CNH/INR and short TWD/INR. In rates, we like receiving 1-year CNY NDIRS, long CNY FX-hedged 1-year CGBs, paying 1-year INR NDOIS and receiving 2-year KRW NDIRS versus USD IRS without beta adjustment. ## Positioning within EM is light enough to offer some improved performance from here

Within EM credit, cash balances at 6.3% are above the four-year average of 5.7%. Allocations to HY have been increasing again to 5.4% OW. However, this is well below 2021 and 2022 levels and where EM funds would normally be. Also, this allocation has much less single B and a bit more BB than history. The six-month z-score of non-dealer protection bought on EM CDX is around zero. So, overall EM funds themselves clearly do have room to increase their risk appetite within current AUM.

That said, not only have they not seen signs of inflows but they have also had plenty of primary supply to deal with as a way of adding risk, with January ending as a record month of supply. For now, January also had a very high amount of amortisations but that will reduce into February (EM sovereign and quasi-sovereign amortisations stand at US\$13.2 billion in February, versus US\$30.2 billion in January; see EM Technical Watch, January 25, 2024). Overall, additional risk appetite within EM funds can thus turn some valuations around, yet we suspect that it will take a broader improvement in EM sentiment to see bonds trade much better.

Within EM local, GBI-EM had a good performance in the last two months of 2023. Most investors managed to outperform the benchmark by having the right positioning (mainly OW LatAm). Despite the sell-off in EM local markets in January, investors' performance is in line with the benchmark, indicating that the sell-off is not hurting EM investors yet ( Exhibit 7 ).

## Yet larger macro factors changing are needed for a more structural shift towards EM trading well

• A less US-centric Goldilocks: From a US point of view, recent data are as close to Goldilocks as possible and assets such as US credit and the dollar are trading strong accordingly. And while the direction of moving towards Fed rate cuts is positive for the rest of the world as well, the US is just too strong, with last week's NFP print the latest example. A real Goldilocks for EM would need to see the US slow further while activity in EM instead picks up (and hence improving the economic surprise indices above).

• A more positive story from China: The asset performance in China has been disappointing due to weak growth and a lack of policy response. The bad news is that the government doesn't feel the urgency to provide stimulus that the market would like to see (see Asia Economics: The Viewpoint: China – What’s holding policymakers back from aggressive easing? January 22, 2024). The good news is that the sell-off in Chinese assets hasn't spilled over to other markets thanks to the PBOC support of CNY. Should CNY depreciate in line with the equity outflows and weak balance of payments, its impact on Asia and EM FX would be much bigger as investors would become concerned about China using FX to export deflation pressure. While better growth in China would be a necessary but not sufficient condition for EM to turn around, it would still help as the stimulus from China is likely to be investment-focused and hence commodity-intensive, which supports EM.

• Rate differentials path moves back in favour of EM: Policy rate differentials between EM and the US spent much of the last two years increasing, which was a key support for FX, in particular LatAm. However, this differential is now coming down, which is a key reason why we saw FX struggle more into 2024. In turn, any signs that EM central banks are turning more hawkish while the Fed cuts faster would be positive for EM FX.

## • In EM credit, the additional positive would be market access and debt

workouts: While the recent issuance from Côte d'Ivoire is encouraging in opening up access to SSA issuers, there is more to go to alleviate funding concerns. This is key as much of the cheapness of the index is driven by single B and below rated credits. Once the Fed starts its cutting cycle, we do think that market access would open up further and countries like El Salvador, Angola, Kenya and Nigeria would be able to issue. The other key positive would be for countries like Zambia, Ghana and Sri Lanka to actually finalise their debt restructurings.

## Implications of a potential de-escalation in the Middle East

We take a look at regional implications of a potential de-escalation: Last week's newsflow around progress towards a potential de-escalation in Gaza has raised questions on implications for credit markets and regional curves, even if follow-on communication on the status of negotiations has been more mixed. We try to summarise some of the impact of a potential de-escalation below beyond the likely reduction in risk premia that would affect the region overall, based on previous work we have done.

In Jordan, while bonds have already rallied somewhat, we think that the curve should re-steepen too: Within MENA HY, a de-escalation should be positive for the JORDAN sovereign curve. As we have outlined before, the JORDAN sovereign curve is sentiment and flows-driven, with EM investors a significant part of the market. A de-escalation would be an important boost for sentiment, and we think that should see spreads tighter. However, a key overhang would be the risk of continued geopolitical incidents, including repercussions from the recently announced operation by the US. We hold off from outright longs but think that the curve should be steeper.

Egypt should be a beneficiary of a de-escalation from three perspectives: First, it would remove the risk of an influx of migrants, which would be constructive as Egypt has flagged the limited fiscal room. Second, the lowering of political risk across the MENA region would be a boon for all affected credits, which included Egypt given its proximity. Lastly, assuming the de-escalation is lasting and leads to a resumption of the flow of traffic through the Suez Canal, this would improve the receipts from the route.

## Egypt and Nigeria: Comparing across two narratives

Last week saw a flurry of Nigeria and Egypt headlines as both nations face the same problems: dollar liquidity challenges amid the need for a better policy framework. With our stances being at polar opposites (like Egypt and dislike Nigeria), below are two reasons why:

Egypt is teeing up for a more coordinated response: We believe that the 200bp rate hike last Thursday, which was out of consensus but in line with our economist’s forecast, should be interpreted as a precursor to an FX adjustment. Previously (2016, October 2022, January 2023) this was certainly the case, and this hike could follow the same script. The difference between the parallel market rate and the official rate underscores the need for the authorities to close this gap soon to ease the liquidity crunch.

Nigeria’s approach appears less coordinated. Last week saw the currency adjusted by over 30% to USD1,413 with little policy support to try to anchor the currency. The monetary policy meetings are set to resume in February after the two previous meetings were cancelled, where there is a growing expectation of a substantial policy rate hike (MPR at 18.75% versus CPI at 28.9%Y). Between now and then, the risk is that the parallel market could start rising again as the market is still starved of dollars.

## Senegal: Initiating with a duration preference amid political risk

Senegal is on the cusp of joining the ranks of African oil producers in 2H24. The initial estimates suggest that it would start by producing 100 kbpd (comparable to Ghana and Chad), but crucially these estimates do not yet incorporate phase 2 and 3 of existing oil fields, for which the final investment decision (FID) is expected later this year. This would give a much-needed boost to fiscal and external accounts, which have come under pressure in recent years owing to the massive investment needed to prepare for oil and gas production. Indeed, history suggests a notable improvement in macroeconomic variables in the five years after commencing production.

Nonetheless, the key focus for investors in the short term will likely be on election-related political developments, which could weigh on the curve. In our view, the outcome of the vote (whenever it takes place) is unlikely to alter the long-term trajectory of the economy. Against this backdrop, we initiate our sovereign credit coverage of Senegal with a preference for dollar paper (2048) over euro. With the SENEGL USD 10-year (2033) bond spread at 460bp (YTM 8.2%) versus the single B rated average of 583bp, we think that the credit trades cheap to peers in aggregate, even more so against regional peers like Côte d'Ivoire and Nigeria ( Exhibit 23 ). On the curve, we prefer the long end, which is insulated from issuance risk should Senegal come to market. Medium-term outperformance should be driven by the commencement of oil production.

## Does China have CNY 2 trillion offshore to buy onshore stocks?

Bloomberg reported that the government is considering mobilising CNY 2 trillion (almost US\$300 billion) from its offshore assets to purchase onshore stocks via Northbound Connect. Investors have been asking the question of whether it is possible and the potential market impact (see also our equity strategists' take on this topic in China Equity Strategy: Reported Stock Market Rescue – Past, Now and Implications, January 23, 2024).

The SAFE publishes China's Net International Investment Position (NIIP), which summarises China's foreign asset holdings. As of September 2023, China has around US\$9 trillion in foreign assets. The split of assets is quite evenly divided: around US\$3 trillion in FX reserves, US\$3 trillion in direct investment and the remaining US\$3 trillion in portfolio and other investment.

Within US\$3 trillion in portfolio and other investment, US\$1 trillion is in publicly traded equity and debt (US\$609 billion and US\$465 billion, respectively). US\$465 billon is in currency and deposits. The remaining US\$1.5 trillion is in trade credits, loans, insurance and pensions.

## 1. Sell current portfolios in equity and bonds in NIIP to buy onshore stocks

The authority could sell some of its assets to fund the purchases. But it is important to identify who holds these assets. Purchases are possible only if such assets are held by the government or SOEs.

The first component would be equity holdings via Southbound Connect, which were US \$364 billion. These holdings are owned by onshore institutional and retail investors. The second component is bond holdings via Southbound Connect, and our estimate is that onshore bond investors hold about US\$59 billion of bonds in Hong Kong, a significant increase since the introduction of Southbound Connect in September 2021.

The third component is China's sovereign wealth fund holdings. Official data show that it holds US\$1.24 trillion assets with around US\$880 billion invested in onshore assets versus US\$350 billion invested in offshore assets. Over 50% of its assets are in alternatives while investment in equity and bonds is about 28% and 15%, respectively. We assume that 85% of its offshore assets would be publicly traded assets rather than less liquid alternatives.

This gives roughly US\$300 billion of assets owned by the sovereign wealth fund. We are not able to track the remaining US\$300 billion. This US\$300 billion in unexplained other assets could be owned by SOE banks, SOEs and private companies.

The sovereign wealth fund could act as a potential candidate for such purchases but it has spent the last decade increasing its holdings in foreign assets. Even if it has US\$300 billion in foreign assets which could be sold, we are not sure that the government is willing to reverse the diversification progress it has made in the past.

The other unexplained US\$300 billion in foreign assets could be possibly used but, as discussed above, it is unlikely that it is fully owned by the government and SOEs.

## 3) Use FX reserves to buy onshore stocks

China has US\$3 trillion in FX reserves and its allocation is unknown. But based on other central banks' disclosures, it is very rare for a central bank to allocate reserves to the stock market in local currency because assets' safety, stable return and liquidity are the most important factors when managing FX reserves. CNY 2 trillion, should the government choose to allocate this to the onshore stock market, would equal almost 10% of the PBOC's FX reserves.

## Conclusion

Overall, our analysis suggests that there are three possible sources for fund the purchases – the sovereign wealth fund's foreign assets (US\$350 billion), unexplained assets (US \$300 billion) and deposits (US\$465 billion). The government might be able to use a combination of these three sources to fund the purchases.

However, as explained above, the plan still lacks details and we are not able to identify a definitive source to fund such purchases. We will wait for more details from the government should it choose to proceed with the plan.

## Impact on CNY

Should the government decide to sell foreign assets and buy onshore stocks, it would be buying CNY and act as a decent support for CNY given the size. US\$300 billion would be a third of China's trade surplus and almost three times the bond outflows seen in 2022, which caused the significant sell-off in CNY.

However, the government might just make the money available rather than using it because the signalling effect is more important than its actual usage. From this perspective, its impact on CNY could be limited given the lack of immediate flows. A better CNY performance would require a better growth story in China, in our view.

While capex spending remains meaningfully above revenue spending, overall infrastructure-related capex spending is budgeted to slow to 5% in F2025 (versus the revised estimate of 19% in F2024). Indeed, it appears that the government is attempting to transition the economy from government expenditure-led to private expenditure-led, but we will await the full budget in July (post-elections) for confirmation.

The bond market welcomed the fiscal deficit, as 10-year G-Secs rallied 8bp post the budget. We want to make a few observations:

First, the market expectation for the F2025 fiscal deficit was 5.2%. With elections happening in two months' time, it wouldn't have been a surprise to investors if the budget was a bit expansionary and pro-growth. To the contrary, the government stuck to fiscal discipline and announced a slightly lower fiscal deficit of 5.1%. Strong growth has allowed the government to narrow the deficit and pave the way for private capex to support growth.

Second, with a lower fiscal deficit, gross and net borrowing are both less than the market expectation. Gross borrowing would be INR 14 trillion and net supply INR 11.75 trillion. Both are about INR 1 trillion lower than the consensus.

Our view on bond supply is that the borrowing number matters for the budget day. Beyond the budget day, supply could be an overhang when the external environment becomes more challenging. For example, countries might struggle to issue bonds at a favourable rate when the Fed is hiking rates aggressively. Hence, a better-than-expected fiscal deficit is already in the price after Thursday's rally, in our view.

## Market implications

F2025 should be a good year for G-Secs from a supply and demand perspective: Supply is lower versus F2024 while demand is stronger, especially from foreigners. We are neutral on G-Secs after Thursday's rally and we don’t see a significant rally from here unless the Fed starts to cut rates and the RBI follows the path. But we see a good chance for us to turn more constructive in 2Q24 before the index inclusion. We would look to buy G-Secs on the dip.

In swap space, after receiving 1-year NDOIS in late 2023, we turned to paying 1-year NDOIS with the view that tight liquidity was making paying trades attractive. Liquidity has indeed been tight (see India Economics: Decoding Domestic Liquidity Conditions, January 29, 2024) but 1-year NDOIS has been grinding lower from our entry level.

We would like to keep it for now given the attractive carry/rolldown (almost 20bp/3 months), as the call rate remains sticky. We don’t see the market pricing in an aggressive rate cut ahead of the Fed rate cut and the RBI could only act along with/after the Fed for precautionary reasons. We would hold onto paying 1-year NDOIS and lower the stop to 6.45%.

In FX, INR is the most stable currency in Asia despite the ups and downs in USD. We have been advocating long INR as a carry trade in a strong USD environment and we believe that the current bid for USD could last a bit longer before the Fed's signal to cut rates (probably in 2Q). We like short TWD/INR and short CNH/INR.

## Taiwan investor feedback

We spoke with over ten equity clients in Taiwan recently. On the macro side, the conversations were focused on the outlook for North Asia, Japan and the US. Overall, sentiment is bullish on the AI story particularly and remains cautious on China markets despite the support measures announced. Investors are modestly constructive on TWD for now, but are less certain about the 2H24 outlook given the US election.

The top three topics discussed in our client conversations were:

1) US election: The US election in November featured in almost all of our client conversations despite it being ten months away. Investors see it as a major risk event that could change the market dynamics and support USD, given the prospects of higher trade tariffs and a larger fiscal deficit under a potential Trump administration. Trump’s comments made on Taiwan and its semiconductor industry last year, which were recirculated on social media recently, were also noticed.

Our take is that it is too early to judge or position for the market impact of the election (see Podcast | Thoughts on the Market: What Matters Most to Markets in the U.S. Election, January 25, 2024). Consistent with this, investors are in general modestly constructive on TWD for now, given that the Fed is moving towards rate cuts and the bullish AI story, but are less certain about the 2H24 outlook. 30.0 is generally seen to be a strong support level for USD/TWD.

2) China: Sentiment was cautious on China as investors struggled to see a catalyst to turn things around anytime soon. There was much more interest in our equity strategists’ overweight Japan and India equities call. On China, the most commonly asked questions were: i) What is our take on the recent easing measures announced? ii) Why are the authorities reluctant to pursue more forceful fiscal stimulus this time? and iii) What would we need to see to get more constructive on the outlook?

For our take on these issues, see China Equity Strategy: Reported Stock Market Rescue – Past, Now and Implications, January 23, 2024; Asia Economics: The Viewpoint: China – What’s holding policymakers back from aggressive easing? January 22, 2024; and China Musings: How Can China Reflate its Economy and Stock Market, January 26, 2024.

3) Geopolitics and inflation: Several clients questioned whether the Red Sea disruptions could push inflation higher and delay the timing of central bank rate cuts. Based on the current level of disruption, our economists don’t think so, as the current disruptions to supply chains are small relative to that seen in recent years, and there are still downside risks for the trend of goods prices. Our commodity strategists similarly see a limited impact on crude oil shipping and hence crude oil prices.

## A tradable S\$NEER basket

The MAS struck a balanced tone at its meeting last week. On the dovish side, even though realised inflation has been falling slower than anticipated, the central bank kept its core inflation forecasts unchanged and even revised down its headline inflation forecasts by 50-100bp due to lower COE prices. On the hawkish side, the MAS sounded upbeat on growth, projecting Singapore’s slightly negative output gap to narrow in 2H24.

Taken together, these projections suggest that the MAS may not ease policy this year, which is consistent with our economists’ call. Therefore, while S\$NEER valuations are not cheap, with the basket trading at \~1.7% above the midpoint of the MAS’s policy band, there is no near-term catalyst to drive a meaningful correction in the valuation. S\$NEER could grind higher gradually in line with the MAS’s policy stance of an estimated \~1.5% appreciation slope per annum.

To help investors to trade S\$NEER, we refresh our analysis on creating a tradable proxy for S\$NEER with a smaller set of currencies with good liquidity. We derive the currency composition and weights of the proxy basket by minimising the sum of squares error between the proxy basket and the MAS’ official S\$NEER index over a three-year lookback period.
