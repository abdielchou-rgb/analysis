## United States

• At the September FOMC meeting, we expect the Fed will do what it “should” do and cut 50bp...

… warranted by a shift in the balance of risks and a desire to preclude any further labor market weakening

• Although CPI surprised more firmly in August, we are tracking just a 0.14% gain in core PCE last month

Next week’s retail sales, IP data should firm, suggesting upside risk to 1.5% 3Q GDP growth

Fed officials have made it quite clear that an easing cycle will commence at the upcoming September FOMC meeting next Wednesday. The main question the markets are grappling with is how large the opening move will be. Following the firmer-than-expected CPI release on Wednesday, market pricing moved decisively toward 25bp, but a pair of news reports suggesting it would be a close call moved the market to price around a 50% chance of a 50bp move next week. We agree it is likely to be a close call, but we also believe the Fed will make the “right” move and go 50bp.

Arguments for just a 25bp ease include the possibility of panicking the market with a larger cut and the importance of reaching consensus in the first “consequential” move—especially right before the election. We don’t find the first argument compelling: Powell has proven to be an adept communicator and should be able to convey that a level shift is intended to reinforce their forecast for a soft landing. Plus, the market’s repricing should minimize any risk of panic from a larger move. We are somewhat more sympathetic to the second argument, as we think there is a strong desire to reach a consensus next week—although we take the Fed at its word that the election is not a consideration.

That said, the case for a 50bp cut seems clear to us: various iterations of a Taylor Rule imply policy is currently a full percentage point or more too restrictive; the shift in the balance of risks suggests front-loading rate cuts via a risk management approach; the fact that “several” Fed officials were willing to begin cutting in July supports catching up in September after two disappointing job reports.

## Go big to eventually go home

The case for the Fed to front-load rate cuts this year is clear in our view after the pivot in the risk assessment from Chair Powell at Jackson Hole, the July FOMC minutes, and even Governor Waller last week (“the balance of risks is now weighted more to the downside” on the employment objective). So too is the case for getting back to something close to neutral much sooner than the leisurely pace put forth in the June Summary of Economic Projections, which had the policy rate still above 4% at the end of next year and above 3% at the end of 2026. Assuming the Fed does indeed announce a 50bp reduction in the funds rate target, we expect the median 2024 dot to show a total of 100bp of cuts this year, and for 150bp of easing in 2025 (Figure 1). We also expect Powell to argue this action should help preserve a soft landing (even if he has avoided using that exact phrase).

Conversely, should the Committee take the timid approach and end up cutting only 25bp next week, we would look for the median 2024 dot to show just 75bp of cuts this year. In that case, we would expect Powell to stress a readiness to promptly pick up the pace on any signs of further labor market softening. Indeed, he is likely to have to parry a phalanx of dovish questions from reporters. In either case, we look for relatively modest changes to the economic projections.

We look for the statement to say the Committee has gained further confidence on inflation moving sustainably toward 2%, after citing “further progress” (dropping the preceding “some” from prior iterations). Waller’s recent remarks could provide a template for updated guidance: “With inflation and employment nearer our longer-run goals and the labor market moderating, it is likely that a series of reductions will be appropriate.” Any such guidance would, of course, be couched within data dependent language. We don’t expect any change in balance sheet policy, although the rate reduction could be characterized as normalizing policy akin to the continuation of QT. The minutes for next week’s meeting could indicate that the end of balance sheet reduction is on the horizon.

## Ebbing inflation worries

This week’s comprehensive set of inflation measures collectively suggested a soft reading for core PCE inflation in August, and continue to point to dissipating inflation worries despite slightly firmer than expected CPI and PPI reports. Earlier in the week, the August CPI inflation report showed a core CPI increase of 0.28%m/m, the largest gain since April, which kept the year-ago rate stable at 3.2%. The headline CPI rose 0.2%m/m, marking the smallest annual increase since

early 2021 (2.5%). However, the three-month run rate for core CPI was a reasonable 1.8% annualized pace in August (Figure 1). The primary driver of the underlying firmness in the CPI print was shelter, and excluding shelter the core rose 0.08%. While rent inflation moderated slightly, other shelter categories firmed. Core services increased 0.4%m/m, while core commodities fell 0.2%m/m.

Following the CPI report, the August PPI release also showed firmer-than-expected readings. Headline PPI rose 0.2%, and core PPI (excluding food and energy) increased 0.3%, bringing the year-ago figures to 1.7% and 2.4%, respectively. Despite these upward surprises, the import price index data for August were softer than expected, with the headline falling by 0.3%. This softness was mainly due to imported fuel prices, and prices excluding fuel edged down 0.1%. The yearago pace remained at 1.3%, the fastest annual inflation rate since December 2022.

Considering all the inflation data received this week, we expect core PCE inflation to settle at 0.14% in August. If realized, this would inch the year-ago pace up to 2.7% from 2.6% in July. Much of this move is due to base effects, as the three-month annualized run rate would be just 1.85% if our tracking estimate is accurate. The overall easing of inflation concerns—particularly in the Fed’s preferred PCE measure— is one factor that reinforces our conviction that a 50bp rate cut next week is appropriate.

## Jobless claims still doing fine

Amid an array of economic data that has mostly pointed to slowing growth in employment and rising unemployment, the data on jobless claims continue to look quite good. Weekly initial claims are hovering around 230k, which is roughly unchanged from the same time last year. Seasonally adjusted claims were elevated last summer and then took an especially large drop in mid-September, after which claims remained mostly under 220k until May of this year. Residual seasonality may have boosted seasonally adjusted claims in the summer and we would not be surprised to see claims take another step down through the rest of this month.

Continuing jobless claims are also sending a less worrying message than they were at the start of the summer. Continuing claims took a clear step up from late May to late June, but since have stayed in a narrow range. That said, one worrying item of note is that the exhaustion rate—the ratio of claimants drawing their final payment (after exhausting eligibility) to first payments six months’ prior—kept steadily rising through July, the latest month for which we have data (Figure 2). The 12-month average of the exhaustion rate is now at late 2015 levels. This appears at odds with the unemployment data, which shows only a very small change in the mean and median weeks of unemployment, perhaps pointing to a shift in the mix of people who are using unemployment insurance.

## Next week’s data should be mostly decent

Next week brings some key activity data that will help firm up current-quarter GDP tracking, including retail sales, industrial production, and housing starts—along with a first look at September business surveys. The auto sector was likely a drag on retail sales and a support to IP in August, contributing to a forecasted 0.2%m/m fall in overall retail sales but supporting a 0.7%m/m gain in IP as production schedules show a rebound in factory output following July shutdowns. Stripping out the motor vehicle sector we expect a decent 0.3%m/ m increase in retail sales, and also a 0.3%m/m rise in manufacturing output. If our forecasts are realized they could lend some upside risk to our 1.5% forecast for 3Q growth.

Hurricane Beryl was also likely a drag on some July indicators, which will support an August rebound. In addition to IP, where the Fed estimated weather held back both manufacturing and mining, housing starts also likely declined in response to the hurricane. Single-family starts had plunged in July, with a large decrease in the south, and while starts were probably set to decline in response to elevated inventories some of the unusual drop could be weather-related.

We had expected shelter price inflation to moderate in August, and while that was largely realized for rent (0.37%m/ m) the OER (0.50%m/m) and lodging away from home (1.8%m/m) categories actually firmed. The margin between OER and rent was one of the largest in recent decades, although that came after a sizable--but less unusual--undershoot the prior month. We think this is temporary and that based on more mild inflation in new rent measures the rent and OER segments will continue to moderate in coming months. Excluding all of shelter the core rose 0.08%m/m, and excluding just OER and rent it increased 0.14%. Shelter gets a smaller weight in the PCE than the CPI, which is one reason we expect that the PCE will be up less than the CPI. Focusing just on core services, the total was up 0.4%m/m, ex shelter 0.2%, and ex OER and rent 0.3%, with all of those being the largest gains since April. In contrast core commodities fell 0.2%m/m, similar to recent months.

Away from the shelter measures, there was sizable 3.9%m/m increase in airline fares, the most in over two years, whereas medical care services surprised to the downside again, falling 0.1% after a 0.3% decline the prior month, though these prices are still up 3.2%oya. Outside of core, food prices rose 0.1%m/m while energy declined 0.8%, with both of these just a touch softer than our expectations. Food price inflation, likely one of the more visible metrics for a typical household, has posted moderate gains over the last year that are similar to the pre-COVID pace, following the big run-up in prices in 2021-2022.
