## US elections: Fork in the road

Anshul Pradhan, BCI, US | Amrut Nashikkar, BCI, US | Andres Mok, CFA, BCI, US | Demi Hu, BCI, US | Samuel Earl, BCI, US | Michael McLean, BCI, US

Excerpted from US Rates Research: US elections: Fork in the road, published on July 9, 2024

We look at the implications of the upcoming US elections for the level of yields, the shape of the curve, interest rate volatility and swap spreads, highlighting the interaction between fiscal, monetary and bank regulatory policies. We believe rates markets are complacent and consensus views will likely be challenged.

## Key takeaways

We believe rates investors will view the US election outcome through the lens of the monetary and fiscal policy implications. For the former, changes to trade and immigration policies, which can be enacted via executive action, will be key to watch. Hence, a unified government is not a prerequisite for major market moves and the presidential election outcome itself would matter greatly for rates markets, in our view.

We believe that inflationary and/or expansionary fiscal policies would likely lead to higher rates, with the shape of the curve dependent on which factor dominates. More of the former with less of the latter would result in a flatter curve. Vol markets suggest that there is complacency about the potential for widely diferent policies following the elections. While swap spreads have tightened significantly, the embedded deficit outlook still seems benign.

In a Republican sweep, investors would likely focus on the extension of Trump tax cuts, the risk of higher across-the-board tarifs, and tighter immigration policy. On balance, the resulting higher nominal growth expectation would likely lead to higher yields. The yield curve would move in parallel in this scenario, with the markets paring back the depth of the easing cycle as well. Long-end swap spreads would likely tighten the most and we think rate vol would rise across the surface.

In a Republican presidency with a divided Congress, investors would still likely be concerned about the inflationary efects of potentially higher tarifs and tighter immigration policy, but with less of a boost to growth from easier fiscal policy, as some tax cuts would likely expire. Short-term rates would likely rise, but long-term rates not so much. The yield curve would flatten, swap spreads would likely move sideways, and rate vol would rise somewhat.

In a Democratic presidency with a divided Congress, investors would likely expect limited deficit expansion. Trade and immigration policies would likely see minimal changes. This scenario is the most bullish for USTs, as the market would price out the risk of an inflationary shock and a significant deficit expansion in a Republican sweep. The yield curve would be somewhat steeper, swap spreads would widen the most, and rate vol would fall across the surface, in our view.

In a Democratic sweep, Biden's proposed FY25 budget suggests that fiscal policy could be deficit-neutral, but with significantly higher taxes and spending, increasing uncertainty about the net efect. This would still lead to a decline in yields, as the market would likely price out the risk of an inflationary shock and massive fiscal expansion. In this case, the yield curve would steepen, swap spreads would widen, but less so, as bank regulatory policy would come into focus, and vol would rise on Fed-sensitive parts of the surface.

## Is 2024 Half Full, or Half Empty?

Venu Krishna, CFA, BCI, US | Japinder Chawla, CFA, BCI, US | Rex Feng, BCI, US | Madhuresh Rai, CFA, BCI, US | Young Chen, BCI, US

Excerpted from U.S. Equity Insights: Is 2024 Half Full, or Half Empty?, published on July 11, 2024

Having passed the midpoint of the year, we check in on performance through the first half and what's still to come in 2024. Big Tech carried 1H24 (both EPS and returns); upcoming earnings season implies more of the same. 2H is seasonally stronger but choppier; estimates imply broader EPS growth pickup starting in 3Q.

1H24's +14.5% price return was the S&P 500's 3rd-best over the last 15 years, beaten only by 1H23 (+15.9%) and 1H19 (+17.4%). Like last year, the top 10 biggest stocks accounted for slightly more than 3/4 of first half SPX returns. Moreover, the contribution of a single stock (in this case NVDA) rose to a 15-year record in 1H24 (just shy of 1/3 of total index returns).

Big Tech, momentum and late-cycle stocks were 1H winners. Big Tech (APPL, AMZN, MSFT, GOOG, META, NVDA), delivered 42% returns in 1H24 vs.the rest of the SPX at +8%. Trend following strategies saw a record first half with the MSCI Momentum index returning nearly 28%. Our Late Expansion basket returned \~10% through the first half, primarily due to its significant short exposure to consumer durables.

The June rally was mostly driven by multiple expansion, a stark reversal from earlier in 1H when it was mostly better earnings that were driving returns. Big Tech in particular is seeing NTM P/E multiples expanding again. Big Tech traded within a fairly tight range of 27x-29x NTM P/E through the first 5 months of 2024; that multiple is now at 33x.

We now enter the seasonally stronger second half. While the SPX generally delivers higher median returns in 2H, volatility also tends to be higher, with larger median drawdowns when the index falls. The third quarter is typically the choppiest in any given year. Concentration risk is once again on the upswing: the percentage of SPX constituents beating the index t12m rose to an 8-month high of 37% in May but fell sharply to a multi-year low of 26% by the beginning of July.

Street expects Big Tech to (once again) carry 2Q24 earnings season... Consensus estimates imply that Big Tech earnings growth will continue to dominate in 2Q24, with nearly +32% EPS expansion expected vs. the rest of the SPX at +3.3%. Outside of Big Tech, consensus looks to Healthcare and Energy for modest upside contribution, while Materials and Industrials are expected to detract.

...before a broader pickup in earnings growth later in 2H. While still quite strong, +32% Big Tech EPS growth would represent a second sequential deceleration (from +56% in 1Q24, and +63% in 4Q23) on the way to an eventual convergence in earnings growth between Big Tech and the rest of the SPX. Overall SPX EPS is expected to total \$59 in 2Q24, jumping to \$63 in 3Q24 and \$65 in 4Q24.

US investor feedback: rotation is the pain trade

Emmanuel Cau, CFA, Barclays, UK | Matthew Joyce, CFA, BBI, Paris | Magesh Kumar Chandrasekaran, CFA, Barclays, UK | Emmanuel Makonga, Barclays, UK | Arihanth Bohra Jain, Barclays, UK

Excerpted from Equity Market Review - US investor feedback: rotation is the pain trade, published on July 12, 2024

US investors we met were bullish equities and very long secular growth stocks. Yet caution is mounting given high positioning, softer data and a high earnings hurdle - rotation into laggards on lower rates is the pain trade. Europe/Cyclicals tilt has been cut, but not Banks, which are well owned. Appetite for UK is strong.

Goldilocks-ish: The large majority of investors we met were bullish (consistent with Figure 1 showing AII Bull-bear is back to the highs), long equities and not too worried about a major pull back. Clients see trend GDP growth supportive of earnings and look for one Fed cut this year, enough to keep the party going for equities. But clients see less room for error at current levels, positioning is full in a lot of popular trades, and conviction has come down ('market is fine but hard to see much upside from here'). A lot of things clients like have indeed worked well already and now look pricey, but there is not much appetite to rotate into laggards. Although several macro funds we met think rates have peaked and are more attractive now than at the start of the year (when the market was pricing in 6-7 cuts by the Fed), most are in the higher for longer camp. So rotation on lower rates into under owned parts of the market, like small caps or bond proxies, seems to be the new pain trade. Price action post US CPI yesterday corroborates that.

Nothing but secular growth: Stocks related to popular themes like AI, weight loss drugs, data centers or electrification are core holdings in many funds. So clients run very concentrated portfolios in very consensual stocks, but don't seem too worried about it, as performance has benefited from it. As said above, clients are in the 'higher for longer' camp, thus are sceptical of a material broadening of the rally, and see secular growth stocks as long term winners, regardless of near term macro wobbles. So more rotation out of Big Tech into laggards would likely hurt performance. Ex-secular growth, clients have sympathy with our inclination to reduce beta, at least in European portfolios, and seem to have de-grossed into summer.

Lower rates and rotation the new pain trade. We pitched our recent upgrades of Utilities and Real Estate and downgrades of Materials and Energy (see On edge – July Chart Pack, 3 Jul). We received mixed feedback, as clients are mostly in the higher-for-longer camp, but there was still some interest given the prospect of rates cuts and, in the case of Utilities, potential indirect exposure to AI. Clients have reduced beta a bit, but mainly via adding to Staples, which is getting a lot of interest again, and Quality-Growth plays. Utilities and Real Estate don't seem to be well owned. Small caps are getting more interest, but it doesn't feel to us clients have moved there yet. We reiterated our recent upgrade of EU small caps on Thursday in European Equity Factor Insights – Jul'24, on the view that lower rates should help the space (Figure 2).

A high hurdle to clear for Q2 earnings: Clients note Q2 earnings estimates haven't displayed the typical downward pattern into this reporting season, despite economic surprises rolling over lately. ## Economic Outlook

## Clarity on rates, confusion on politics

Christian Keller, Barclays, UK | Mark Cus Babic, Barclays, UK | Akash Utsav, Barclays, UK

Excerpted from Global Economics Weekly, published on July 12, 2024

Slowing US inflation now makes Fed cuts in both September and December likely. Inflation dynamics are less clear in the UK and EA, but cuts by the BoE in August and ECB in September, after next week's likely hold, remain our baseline. US and French politics remain uncertain and expectations for China's Third Plenum are muted.

More clarity on path of policy rates

US inflation data paves the road to Fed cuts in September and December...

Following recent indications of a moderating US labour market and signs of decelerating growth, US inflation this week surprised sharply to the downside, as core CPI rose just 0.06% m/m, 10bp slower than in May, and far below our forecast of 0.22% m/m (consensus: 0.2% m/m). Importantly, the softness was broad across the basket, with shelter and transportation services driving a slowing in core services and core goods recording another month of deflation, and our measures of "supercore" were largely flat. Admittedly, inflation rates as low as these are likely unsustainable. For instance, outright declines in lodging and airfares are surely not the makings of sustained disinflation. Even so, there is little doubt that this was an encouraging print, and we marked down our year-end core CPI forecast by 0.2pp to 3.0% y/y in response. Folding in the PPI data as well, we now track June core PCE at 0.17% m/m and revise down our Q4/Q4 2024 core PCE forecast by 0.1pp to 2.7% y/y.

Such inflation data are likely to bolster the FOMC's confidence that inflation is moving sustainably towards 2%, with comments by a couple of speakers (Musalem, Goolsbee) characterising the June print as "encouraging" and "excellent". Moreover, with the inflation outlook becoming more benign, the Fed's communication this week, including Chair Powell's testimony to Congress, also indicated a subtle shift towards a greater focus on the labour market, and the Chair described the labour market conditions as having "now cooled considerably," rather than "gradually moderating."

On both of these grounds—inflation and a more balanced labour market—the recent data have reinforced our baseline view that the Fed will begin its easing cycle in September. In addition, we now think the Fed will also cut in December, rather than waiting until March to continue the easing cycle. In 2025, we now expect three 25bp rate cuts (compared with four previously), in March, June, and September, which leaves our end-2025 federal funds target range forecast unchanged, at 4.00-4.25%.

That said, we still expect this cutting cycle to be gradual, as fundamentals (eg, household wealth and the remaining tailwind from immigration) remain in place for resilient activity. Indeed, the pace and source of the recent increase in unemployment does not resemble prior instances, when higher unemployment was followed by a recession, and the usual nonlinear dynamics are not evident in the current episode. Indeed, while marking to market raises our near-term unemployment rate forecast by 0.1pp to 4.1%, our baseline does not pencil in further increases, and unemployment could again edge slightly lower in 2025 as immigration and thus labour supply moderate further. In addition, the activity data remain solid. Next week, we expect 0.3% m/m in control group retail sales growth (even as the decline in auto sales, afected by a cyberattack, drags down the headline) and a 0.4% m/m gain in industrial production.

## ...with positive spillovers to the rest of the world

After the soft CPI data, markets now price in a September cut with roughly 90% probability, and more than six cuts are priced cumulatively by the end of next year. This not only afects US yields, but has also supported assets sensitive to USD liquidity conditions globally. Most notably, the JPY appreciated sharply following the US CPI release, further boosted by an opportune intervention by the Ministry of Finance. And, more broadly, the USD has retreated notably month-to-date and bonds have rallied across DM and many EM markets. In short, this week serves as a reminder of how dependent global financial markets remain on core US rates.

## Less clarity in the UK, but a BoE cut in August is still our baseline

This may be especially relevant in the context where the macro indicators have been less clear in supporting rate cuts. In the UK this week, May GDP surprised notably to the upside, growing 0.4% m/m, compared with our and the consensus forecast of 0.2% m/m, propelled by services. Taking this on board, we mark to market our Q2 and Q3 GDP forecasts from 0.4% q/q to 0.6% q/q and from 0.2% q/q to 0.3% q/q, respectively. This comes alongside somewhat more hawkish communication from the MPC. Speaking after a long blackout period during the election campaign, hawks Jonathan Haskel and Catherine Mann stuck to their previous lines in sounding cautious on the path for inflation and signalled that they are unlikely to vote for a cut in August. Perhaps most importantly, Chief Economist Pill also surprised markets by indicating he may remain unconvinced about cutting in August.

However, just a day after markets had priced a lower probability of a BoE August cut following Pill's speech, they reversed much of that again on the back of the US CPI print. Hence, even for the rate path of other advanced central banks, the Fed seems to matter, at least in the perception of markets.

Moreover, even if Pill votes for a hold, we do not think he represents the median voter on the committee at this stage and still expect a majority of the MPC to vote for a cut in August. We expect next week's data to be consistent with this, as we should see further incremental easing in headline and core June CPI, to a respective 1.9% y/y and 3.4% y/y (both -0.1pp from May), as well as a step down in regular private sector average weekly earnings to 5.5% 3m/y, from 5.8% currently.

## ECB to hold next week and continue cutting in September

Meanwhile, the ECB is all but certain to remain on hold next week. We do not expect any major changes to the policy statement either, with some characterisation of developments in economic activity as disappointing relative to the June projections and assessing inflation developments as broadly in line. Instead, our main focus at this meeting will be signalling for September. On this front, we think the Governing Council (GC) will be unwilling to pre-commit to another cut, but the president is likely to indicate that a September move is probable should data evolve as currently projected. Though unlikely to indicate this, we also think the GC's decision to cut on 12 September may, on the margin, be a little easier if it thinks the Fed will follow suit six days later. We continue to forecast a 25bp policy rate cut at the September forecast meeting, followed by a further 25bp cut at the December meeting.

## BoJ hike in July receives support from wage growth

Elsewhere, Japan continues to be highly afected by Fed policy—and the BoJ's divergence from it—as this week's attempt to restrengthen the JPY (which has been over 160 against the USD since late June) with another intervention showcases how uncomfortable the government seems to be with the resulting JPY weakness. In this context, the beginning of the US easing cycle is likely to help, as should a hike by the BoJ in July.

The stage for this hike continued to be set this week, when the BoJ released the latest Sakura report, in which it indicated that the virtuous cycle between wages and inflation is underway, even among smaller firms. Moreover, May wage growth data surprised sharply to the upside, with scheduled pay on a common establishments basis rising to 2.7% y/y from 2.1% y/y (consensus: 2.2% y/y) as shunto negotiation outcomes are being gradually reflected in base pay. Finally, delaying the hike from July could mean that the BoJ might be forced to wait until December, since the ruling LDP’s presidential election might take place on the same day as the BoJ's September meeting, and the October meeting could be constrained by a potential snap general election for the lower house, as well as the US presidential election.

Next week, we expect May nationwide inflation to remain little changed (2.5% y/y for core), though we see upside risks in the months ahead.

## PBoC's new 'floor' on rates at odds with deflation risks

In China, June headline CPI disappointed expectations, slowing 0.1pp to 0.2% y/y, while core remained subdued at 0.6% y/y. In addition, credit flows missed expectations and eased further, reflecting weakness in loan demand in the private sector. The external sector continues to provide some ofset to subdued domestic demand—with export growth picking up again to 8.6% y/y in the June release, from 7.5% y/y in May–but cannot fully outweigh it. Next week, we expect a sharp slowdown in Q2 GDP growth to 0.2% q/q SAAR, alongside deceleration in June retail sales and investment.

Despite these deflationary tendencies, the PBoC is taking steps to prevent government bond yields from falling in favour of an upward-sloping yield curve. In essence, this is a YCC that creates a floor rather than a ceiling. After earlier steps to allow the central bank to borrow bonds, thus allowing it to sell them short, the PBoC followed up this week by introducing temporary overnight repo and reverse repo operations to both narrow and raise the floor of the interest rate corridor to 1.6% from 0.35%.

Next week brings the much-awaited Third Plenum, though we do not expect this event to end with any meaningful announcements. In summary, individual policies such as the PBoC's YCC may aim at specific objectives (eg, financial stability); however, China's current overall set of monetary, FX, and fiscal policies does not add up to the consistent package needed to support a domestic-demand recovery.

## Biden's drama could be a global macro story

Just as the outlook for US policy rates becomes clearer, the visibility on US politics is becoming ever more uncertain, as President Biden's unscripted press conference this week failed to silence the calls for him to withdraw from the race. Given the strong views of Trump's team on trade tarifs, migration and taxation, a Trump win—especially if combined with a Republican sweep in Congress (ie, winning both the Senate and the House)—would likely have significant implications, not only for the US, but also for the global economy. To the extent that the question around the Democratic candidate increases the chances of a Trump win or even that of a Republican-held Congress, the drama around Biden is truly a global macro outlook story.

## Plus ça change... en France

Similarly, the first week after France's final election round has not brought any clarity on who will become prime minister. The election resulted in a hung parliament, which makes the composition of a future government highly uncertain, as neither the left (which narrowly won last Sunday) nor the presidential coalition is currently in a position to govern without the support or tacit consent from other political blocs. Given the absence of relevant historical precedents and the limited tradition of coalitions in France, we believe the formation of a stable government—should it prove possible—might require protracted negotiations. Next week, the election of the president of the National Assembly (18 July) and the designation of the parliamentary top jobs will be a crucial step in gauging the balance of power between the various blocs.

## The new UK government kick-starts its policy agenda

In contrast, following last week's election, the new UK Prime Minister has started his job and the ministerial roles have been filled. Labour has also started with its structural reforms, publishing details on the National Wealth Fund, which aims to crowd-in private investment, and the planning reform. Next week, the King's Speech (17 July) will see the government's policy agenda announced more concretely.

The analyst expects the six-month excess return of the country’s index eligible bonds to exceed the six-month excess return of the Bloomberg EM USD Sovereign Index.

## Market Weight (MW):

The analyst expects the six-month excess return of the country’s index eligible bonds to be in line with the six-month excess return of the Bloomberg EM USD Sovereign Index.

## Underweight (UW):

The analyst expects the six-month excess return of the country’s index eligible bonds to be less than the six-month excess return of the Bloomberg EM USD Sovereign Index.

## Rating Suspended (RS):

The rating has been suspended temporarily due to market events that make coverage impracticable or to comply with applicable regulations and/or firm policies in certain circumstances including where the Investment Bank of Barclays Bank PLC is acting in an advisory capacity.
