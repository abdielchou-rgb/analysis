# FOMC Reaction: Supply-Side Story

As we expected, the Fed continues to see 3 cuts this year, and adopted the strong supply-side narrative we have been calling for in its forecasts. We maintain our call for 4 cuts this year, but higher near-term r\* places our 2025 rates path under review. Our strategists stay long SFRZ4, and neutral agency MBS.

## Key Takeaways

• Rates decision: The FOMC held the policy rate unchanged in a range of 5.25-5.50%, where it has remained since July 2023. Powell believes Jan and Feb inflation data are not a new trend; he remains patient. We continue to expect a June start, followed by cuts at the Sep, Nov, and Dec meetings.

• Outlook: Stronger supply-side story is told in a sizable upward revision to growth this year, with only modest upward revision to inflation. Committee remains at a 3-cut median starting "at some point" this year. Despite a higher long-run rate, long-run growth was unchanged at 1.8%, indicating the Fed sees recent supply-side factors driving growth higher as temporary. Powell noted the Fed will begin tapering QT "fairly soon". We continue to expect the Fed announces the details in May that a slow taper will begin in June. We expect end to QT in Feb-25.

• Rates: Our rates strategists see the message from the March FOMC meeting – highlighting the role of the supply side – as in line with their long SFRZ4 trade going into the FOMC, and expect markets to remain interested in pricing more rate cuts this year. They maintain their long SFRZ4 recommendation.

• FX: We continue to expect USD to gain vs. EUR as the market increasingly prices an ECB pivot, global growth outside the US slows, and investors debate the level to which the Fed may reduce rates during this cycle.

• MBS: Our MBS strategists maintain their neutral index positioning, suggesting that the 2019 cutting cycle is the right framework, and they think the index should trade between 40bp and 55bp, and overweight G2/FN.

## Supply Side Story

The statement remained unchanged, but the SEP and Q&A show the Committee has adopted the stronger supply-side story we have been flagging. Powell remains noncommittal about when rate cuts will occur, but he is still confident inflation is on a gradual, though bumpy path to 2%. We continue to expect sticky services inflation will keep the Fed on hold until June – delivering 100bp in cuts in 2024 (Jun, Sep, Nov, Dec). Our call for an additional 200bp in 2025 is under review – a higher near-term r\* implies a higher path than we have penciled in. We discuss further below.

## Statement and Projections

The FOMC has adopted in their projections the tremendous supply-side effects that we have been highlighting. In his prepared remarks, Chair Powell noted that "GDP expanded 3.1% bolstered by strong consumer demand as well as improving the supply conditions...Participants generally revised up their growth projections since December reflecting the strength of incoming data including data on labor supply...Strong job creation has increased by the supply of workers, reflecting increase in participation among individuals aged 25-54 years and a continued strong pace of immigration." There are historic shifts in global immigration flows happening, and the US economy is benefitting. See Friday Finish – US Economics: Immigration Headcount (8 Mar 2024), Friday Finish – US Economics: Immigration and (Dis)Inflation (1 Mar 2024), & Morgan Stanley Global Macro Forum: Immigration and Labor Supply – Implications for the Economy and Markets (4 Mar 2024).

The FOMC statement was unchanged, as expected, and the Summary of Economic Projections (SEP) revealed a stronger supply-side assumption, with growth revised up 70bp, to 2.1% 4Q/4Q this year, while inflation was revised up a lesser 20bp, to 2.6% ( Exhibit 1 ). Despite upward revisions to growth and inflation, the median participant still expects three cuts this year – as we expected – with the range of 2024 fed funds rate projections narrowing from 3.9-5.4% in December to 4.4-5.4% in March.

The January and February inflation data lead to a view of more upside risks to inflation than in the prior SEP. Concerns about the persistence of inflation took another cut out of 2025. The median participant now sees three cuts in 2025 as well, instead of four cuts

in the prior SEP ( Exhibit 2 ).

We have been highlighting the risk that, at any given forecast meeting, the longer-run dot could move higher. This was that meeting. The 2026 median dot shifted up from 2.9% to 3.1%, and the median dot for the long-run fed funds rate moved up 10bp, from 2.5% to 2.6%, perhaps reflecting the view that supply-side factors like productivity growth and immigration are important drivers of economic growth. Next year's higher federal funds rate projection shows the FOMC is thinking about short-run r\* and that higher rates for longer may be needed to keep inflation down. Despite higher longer-run rates, longer-run growth was unchanged at 1.8%, indicating the Committee sees recent supply-side factors driving growth higher as temporary.

## Powell flexes a bit of muscle in the Q&A

In the press conference, Chair Powell said he does not see the strong January and February inflation data as a new trend. He remains patient on the first rate cut but pushed back on Voters calling for later cuts by saying that, although the FOMC is very consensus oriented, they could still cut with dissenters – "people do dissent, life goes on, it's not a problem." We think when the Fed cuts in June it may still come with no dissents on the promise that the Fed will approach every meeting with careful consideration before adjusting policy again.

Powell further elaborated how the rise in labor supply has allowed job growth and economic activity to run stronger without being inflationary. He explained that more labor supply means a larger economy where inflationary pressures are not increasing. "Strong hiring alone is not a reason to hold off on rate cuts. Last year, we saw strong job growth with inflation coming down quickly. We now know it was due to labor supply."

Chair Powell remains noncommittal on when they expect to start lowering interest rates. His comments were the same from his semiannual testimony where he said the FOMC would "begin dialing back policy restraint at some point this year." Because of the upside risks to inflation, the FOMC is not yet on an unequivocal cutting bias.

Powell implies that he did not mark up his inflation projections. On the evolution of inflation since the December SEP – "The inflation data came in a little bit higher as a separate matter and I think that caused people to write up their inflation. But nonetheless, we continue to make good progress when rating inflation." Further, he is still confident that softer shelter inflation will show up in inflation, but the timing is less clear. Powell also acknowledged inflation may look stickier in the back half of the year – "In the second half of the year you have some pretty low readings so it might be harder to make progress as you move that 12-month window forward. We're looking for data that confirm the low readings we had last year and give us a higher degree of confidence that what we saw was real any inflation moving sustainably down to 2%, toward 2%."

The FOMC will not overact to these two months of data. "The January CPI and PCE numbers were very high. There's reasonable seasonal effects there, but we don't want to be dismissive of it. The inflation rate was higher than expectations, but we have it [core PCE inflation] well below 30bp in February so it's not like the January number. I think the two of them together haven't really changed the overall story, which is that of inflation moving down gradually on a sometimes-bumpy road toward 2%. I don't think that story has changed."

## Near-term and longer-run r\*

## More labor supply and faster productivity growth might also point to more

investment and higher r\*. Faster productivity growth and more workers tend to precede more investment in equipment and structures. And more investment demand is consistent with a higher neutral interest rate in the short/medium run, which is perhaps the reason why the new SEP shows a higher median fed funds projection for 2025 (3.9% vs 3.6% prior) and 2026 (3.1% vs 2.9% prior) without changing the inflation numbers for those years.

To reiterate, we have been highlighting the risk that at any given forecast meeting the longer-run dot could move higher. This was that meeting. The new projections included a slight increase in the long-run median dot to 2.6% from 2.5% (the mode remained at 2.5%), suggesting that some FOMC participants see these supply-side developments as having a persistent effect on the economy.

Despite higher longer-run rates, longer-run growth was unchanged at 1.8%, indicating the Committee sees recent supply-side factors driving growth higher as temporary. On this issue, Powell acknowledged that there is uncertainty in estimating long-run r\*, but stated that his "instinct is that rates will not go back to the very low [pre-pandemic] levels”. Pre-pandemic, the fed funds rate was set in a range of 1.50-1.75%.

## The balance sheet – closer to winding down QT

The Committee held an in-depth discussion at the March meeting and is looking to slow the pace of run-off "fairly soon." We see the Fed announcing the parameters of a taper to QT in May and initiating the taper in June. QT ends around February 2025, but could go on for longer. Key expectations:

• The Fed will cut the pace of QT in half for Treasuries, to \$30bn/month, but leave MBS to run off with prepayments.

• The SOMA portfolio falls about \$600bn from the current level, reaching \~\$6.5bn in early 2025. We see the RRP facility falling to roughly \$0, and QT ending in February 2025 when reserves are about \$3.2tn.

• Important parameters – If the SOFR-IORB spread goes to zero or turns positive, it will be important. If the standing repo facility (SRF) is used in size, it will also be a meaningful signal.

• The shift in supply of Treasuries relative to expectations will likely not be substantial, and the effects will be most noticeable in the front end and swap spreads, rather than longer-dated yields.

## SOFR so good

Bottom line: Staying long SFRZ4: Last week, we suggested that rising labor supply has been a game changer, and markets have been led astray by following a demand-side framework over the last year – see Supply Side Curveball. We were looking for the FOMC to ratify this thinking – via the dot plot and in the press conference – and we were recommending a long SFRZ4 contract into the FOMC. We think the Fed delivered a message in line with our expectations, and we maintain our long SFRZ4.

Five key takeaways for US Rates markets: (1) The Fed acknowledged the influential role of labor supply – a key dynamic markets have missed, (2) FOMC sees no change in the overall inflation story, while markets have reacted a lot to recent CPI prints, (3) by raising the 2024 GDP and inflation forecasts, the bar for disappointment has lowered, (4) Powell sounded non-committal on timing of the first cut, in contrast to January, when he offered clear pushback against a March cut, and (5) Slowing the pace of run-off will be appropriate "fairly soon."

3. Lower bar for disappointment: Another notable feature of the Fed's SEP and dot plot is that – if the median Fed participant is willing to cut rates 3 times with 2.6% core PCE projections and a 4.0% unemployment rate – the bar for markets to add more rate cuts has now been lowered.

Remember that data surprises tend to be mean reverting, and from that perspective the risk of a disappointment in monthly readings of payrolls and CPI in the coming months is higher, opening the door wider for the markets to price in deeper rate cuts.

4. In contrast to January, FOMC more prepared for a cut? Finally, it is also notable that Powell had a more emphatic answer w.r.t. not cutting rates in March, when he was asked about it at the January FOMC meeting. In contrast, Powell gave a more non-committal response on the lines of "anything is possible" when asked about the possibility of a cut in May – see gray box below. We think this will keep markets interested in pricing cuts at the May and June meetings – opening more pathways for SFRZ4 to rally.

Powell at March FOMC, on a question about a May cut: Things happen during an inter-meeting period if you look back – unexpected things. I wouldn't want to dismiss anything

Powell at January FOMC, on a question about a March cut: Based on the meeting today, I would tell you that I don't think it is likely that the Committee will reach a level of confidence by the time of the March meeting

5. Slowing the pace of run-off will be appropriate "fairly soon": In the March FOMC press conference, Fed Chair Powell confirmed that in-depth discussions on slowing the pace of balance sheet run-off had taken place, but a decision was not made on the size of the taper, although he guided that the general sense on the Committee is that it will be appropriate to slow the pace of run-off “fairly soon.”

Powell on a question on if "fairly soon" meant May: That is the idea. That is what we're looking at. In terms of the timing, I would say "fairly soon." I don't want to be more specific than that. You get the idea.

We believe this timeline aligns with our view for a taper to be announced in May and for a June start, with the end of QT not until 1Q25. Powell was careful to make a distinction that slowing the pace of run-off does not mean stopping QT. In fact, he pointed out the irony in that, by going slower, they can get farther in their run-off process because it allows for a smoother distribution of reserves across the banking system, which reduces the likelihood of a premature end.

Powell made it clear that money market conditions and the distribution of reserves will determine when the Fed stops QT. He confirmed where money market rates are trading relative to administered rates (RRP and IORB) will guide the Fed’s thinking on QT, not a specific dollar amount of reserves or percentage of GDP.

We look for additional details on QT in the March minutes, but continue to view the following indicators as signposts for assessing the progress and impact of QT:

• SOFR-IORB spread;

• Sensitivity of money market rates to changes in reserves;

• Usage of the Standing Repo Facility (SRF); and

• Distribution of reserves and sources of bank funding.

## USD outlook

We continue to expect EUR/USD to decline, for multiple reasons:

First, while our rates strategy colleagues recommend long US duration positions, we expect markets to increasingly price the potential for ECB-Fed policy divergence in the coming months. Our economists' forecasts imply that the deceleration in European inflation will happen faster and from a lower starting pace than US inflation ( Exhibit 11 ) – affording the ECB room to signal a faster pace of cuts than currently implied.

Taking the entirety of the Fed's message today, with the 2024 dots unchanged but higher baseline levels for 2025 and long-run Fed expectations, one could argue it was a hawkish dot message from Fed vs. expectations. Of course, the summary of economic projections also changed forecasts for growth and inflation, though Powell also commented that there were reasonable seasonal effects on the inflation numbers to start the year. Granted, explaining the price action may not be quite as important as figuring out what it means going forward.

Of course, we didn't just get the SEP and comment on inflation, we got a number of comments on the outlook for QT during the press conference, and our conviction around the timing of an announcement in May for June implementation is now higher given a number of quotes from Chair Powell – for instance "While we did not make any decisions today on this, the general sense of the committee is that it will be appropriate to slow the pace of run-off fairly soon," and then when questioned what fairly soon meant, he replied, "'Fairly soon' is words we use to mean fairly soon."

He was also asked about the longer-term state of the balance sheet, and he said, "Our longer-run goal is to return to a balance sheet that is mostly treasuries. I do expect that once we're through this, we will come back to the other issues about the composition and maturity and revisit those issues. It's not urgent right now. We want to get this decision made first and then we can -- when the time is right, come back to the other issues," which in our minds implies that the Fed is nowhere near contemplating selling mortgages.

Going forward, we continue to think that mortgages should trade within the range we had forecasted after the December FOMC meeting of 40-55bp. In fact, through almost the first quarter of 2024 mortgages have traded in just an 8bp range of 44-53bp. Putting that in context, 2023 saw a 46bp range of 36-82bp, and 2022 saw a 70bp range of 18-88bp. It's interesting that is happening in an environment where implied volatility is isn't that much lower; the MOVE index in 2024 has averaged 108bp, compared to 122bp in 2023 and 120bp in 2020.

If we're going to be in a similar range as the 2019 cutting cycle, it's worth pointing out that the MOVE index in 2019 averaged 63bp ( Exhibit 13 ). If vol falls, OAS could stay the same while nominal spreads tighten. Beyond that, mortgages continue to track expectations for Fed cuts, though have dislocated somewhat recently as mortgages outperformed over the last two weeks vs. expectations of Fed cuts.

Putting it all together, our views haven't materially changed. Going into today's meeting, there was some probability that the market had likely assigned that the Fed would be higher for longer and cutting rates later. That outcome would have been clearly bad for mortgages, but the odds of that outcome should now be much lower in the probability distribution. Removing bad outcomes is good for MBS. Another a good outcome for the mortgage market is more clarity around the path for the Fed and timing of QT, which should help large investors plan for MBS allocations, particularly important in an asset class that is structurally short rate vol.

However, there are still two problems left facing the market. At the start of the year, the market was pricing in 160bp of cuts; we're now pricing in just 75bp. That means that the NIM for banks is lower, and the FX hedging costs for overseas investors are higher. It also likely means that demand from those investors could be lower than we had thought this year. The second, related, issue is that the agency market is likely to need money managers to add to overweights until those other investors come back, but it gets more and more difficult to ask money managers to add MBS as overweights get larger.

## Disclosure Section

Mortgage Backed Securities (MBS) and Collateralized Mortgage Obligations (CMO)

Principal is returned on a monthly basis over the life of the security. Principal prepayment can significantly affect the monthly income stream and the maturity of any type of MBS, including standard MBS, CMOs and Lottery Bonds. Yields and average lives are estimated based on prepayment assumptions and are subject to change based on actual prepayment of the mortgages in the underlying pools. The level of predictability of an MBS/CMO's average life, and its market price, depends on the type of MBS/CMO class purchased and interest rate movements. In general, as interest rates fall, prepayment speeds are likely to increase, thus shortening the MBS/CMO's average life and likely causing its market price to rise. Conversely, as interest rates rise, prepayment speeds are likely to decrease, thus lengthening average life and likely causing the MBS/CMO's market price to fall. Some MBS/CMOs may have "original issue discount" (OID). OID occurs if the MBS/CMO’s original issue price is below its stated redemption price at maturity, and results in "imputed interest" that must be reported annually for tax purposes, resulting in a tax liability even though interest was not received. Investors are urged to consult their tax advisors for more information. Government agency backing applies only to the face value of the CMO and not to any premium paid
