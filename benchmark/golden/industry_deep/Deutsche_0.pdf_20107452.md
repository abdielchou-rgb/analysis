# Sports M&A: Coming for your team

The data behind our US and UK studies on fan engagement for the full complement of 27 sports is available upon request

The recent NFL discussions about whether to allow private equity ownership follow a bevy of global sports M&A deals. That includes eye-watering valuations paid for investments in Manchester United, the Washington Commanders, AC Milan and others. These all took place against a backdrop of rising interest rates and a depressed M&A market.

In this piece, we present eight reasons why we expect the fundamental value of sports leagues, teams, and services to grow and fuel the M&A market in both the US and Europe over the short and medium term.

We conducted a nationally representative study of people in the US and UK to gain insights into how they watch, follow, and pay for 27 different sports. Spliting this data by different demographic segments allows us to identify areas of growth. That allows us to then outline our views on how sports M&A in the US and Europe may differ in the near term.

Artificial intelligence is also driving sports valuations. To predict its progression, we interviewed Prospect, a sports data analysis company that helped England reach a Rugby World Cup final and the Gujarat Titans win the Indian Premier League. We show how digital ‘interest’ metrics can provide a guide to the potential value of teams at different points in their revenue cycle, and how statistical analysis and AI can make leagues more competitive, thereby increasing their value. We also quantify the impact that 'icon players', including Cristiano Ronaldo and Harry Kane, have on clubs when they move.

Potential political intervention and the public perception of sports M&A is increasingly critical to valuations. About a third of people say new owners have bought into their team in the last ten years. Some fans turn away - we analyse the \~23% of fans who do not like big money sports deals.

In the end, we see a multi-speed growth trajectory across the three ‘pillars’ of the sports ecosystem (leagues, teams, and services). We believe that leagues will depend on digital transformation to drive growth in fan engagement with funding from financial sponsors. Meanwhile, teams can rely more on individual investors to promote analysis and management to improve on-field performance. Finally, we see the services sector as being the high-growth segment of the market, driven by strong demand for data analysis, machine learning, and AI-based tools from both leagues and teams.

The appeal of sports as an asset class (against the backdrop of macro uncertainty) stems from some unusual characteristics. For example, teams can be less likely to suffer from the problem that companies face regarding the natural evolution of the business cycle. Few sports that were popular 50 years ago have shrunk today. In addition, brand value and the emotional appeal of sports is higher than those for most other asset markets.

Sports leagues are frequently oligopolistic, and these closed dynamics and network effects ensure that the barrier for new entrants is almost always insurmountably high. Indeed, there are barely a handful of examples of rival leagues usurping profitable incumbent leagues.

## Eight reasons why we expect sport to grow as an asset class

The value of the biggest sports leagues has grown with apparent immunity from global macro uncertainty. The relatively uncorrelated nature of sports is the backdrop to eight key reasons why we see continued growth in the value of the asset class. We discuss each of these reasons in turn.

1. Sport is becoming more important in people’s lives - particularly for young people

2. The growing number of professional leagues (in different sports) is broadening the investible universe for M&A deals

3. The digitisation of both leagues and clubs is just beginning

4. The growing sophistication of player statistics and data analysis

5. Countries are increasingly building sports investment

6. The growth in sports-specific investment funds

7. The business of 'icon players' is becoming more sophisticated

8. Broadcasting and streaming is evolving to be more valuable

## 1. Sport is becoming more important in people’s lives (and they are watching and engaging more)

In-depth data on our US and UK sports study is available upon request. This is split by different demographics and incorporates viewership habits and fan engagement across 27 sports

Sports valuations can only grow in the long term if viewership, merchandise sales, attendance, and engagement grows at an individual level. While statistics from sports broadcasters, leagues, and teams tell part of the story, they struggle to analyse individual fans, and data sets are often difficult to compare.

Therefore, our proprietary study of people in the US and UK (which was a representative sample of the entire population, not just sports fans) was done to determine growth opportunities by assessing how people incorporate sports into their lives, how they watch it, and how they pay for it.

In the end, our assumptions about younger generations were upended – in particular the stereotype that young people tend to push back against the ‘corporatisation’ of sport. ## 2. The growing number of professional leagues (in different sports) is broadening the investible universe for M&A deals

While the major men's sports have been professional for decades, the more fringe mens sports, and many women's sports, have only assumed more professional characteristics in recent years. That offers a greater breadth of opportunities for M&A investment in the medium term.

Just a few examples of niche sports that have increasingly professionalised recently include: the women's pro volleyball series in the US, the T100 triathlon series, the track cycling champions league, the Pro Padel League, and many others.

Women's sports generally lag men's sports in various financial metrics, however, some progress is being made which opens up the potential for M&A opportunities in the medium term:

In North America, the WNBA (established in 1996) is the only established women's equivalent of the Big Four sports. But last year, the Professional Women's Hockey League kicked off, replacing prior incarnations of women's ice-hockey leagues that were less financially sustainable.

Outside the US, there is more success at professionalising women's leagues for the most popular sport, football. In the UK, the Women's Super League (the equivalent of the English Premier League) which was established in 2010 became fully professional in 2018. A new organisational structure will oversee the competition this year and WSL Chair Dawn Airey last year said it could become a billion-pound league.

Across both genders, further professionalisation is occurring in some areas. The new 'Hundred' cricket series in the UK plays women's and men's games back-toback and has recently increased salaries for women while those for men remained static (on average women still lag men).

## 3. The digitisation of both leagues and clubs is just beginning

There is considerable potential here to increase fan engagement and analyse data through the use of ‘super apps’ and other tech connections. This is already increasingly the appeal of sports for Big-Tech firms that are increasingly purchasing sports broadcasting rights.

One example is Formula 1 where Liberty Media, the owners of F1's commercial rights has made large strides in building the brand and engagement through digital channels. Its recent \$4.5bn investment in MotoGP gives it the opportunity to apply similar strategies as well as finding synergies, such as potential race double headers.

We dig into further detail later in the piece on the opportunities around digitisation, particularly for leagues.

## 4. The growing sophistication of AI-based sports statistics and data analysis

We laid out our decade-ahead predictions for sports statistics and AI in a 2019 edition of Konzept here.

We argue that the sports statistics revolution is a clear positive for the valuation of sports teams and leagues. That is counter-intuitive to some. The detractors argue that sports statistics will be a zero-sum game once every team has them. We believe the opposite. We argue that the proliferation of sophisticated sports data analysis will level the playing field in leagues that are dominated by a small number of teams. That should increase the level of competition, unpredictability, and excitement in the league, thus making it more entertaining and valuable to broadcasters.

The reason that sports statistics can level the playing field is that it is relatively cheap way for teams to improve relative to buying star players or coaches. That makes it easier for teams with low budgets to compete. This is increasingly the case for 'invasion' sports (such as football, basketball, rugby, hockey) that have few discrete events (as do baseball, cricket etc).

“When used effectively, AI models in sport can come up with unconventional ways to win matches, as well as identifying underrated players who do important things but don’t stand out to the human eye. Across a whole range of sports, the teams that have harnessed these models most intelligently have been able to extract huge value from the recruitment market, and climb their way up the league table.”

## James Tozer – Co-founder and director of analytics, Prospect

One example of AI shaking up sport comes from women’s cricket. Various models have recently identified that the different biomechanics of female players means that the importance of spin bowling (as opposed to fast bowling) towards the beginning of an inning has higher value than it does in men’s cricket. Thus, over the last 18 months, some women’s teams have used spin bowling in a non-traditional way to great effect.

Some negative side effects come from having bad data. Thus, the competitive advantage in sports data analysis lies not only in having the best models but, more importantly, in having the best-quality data. Garbage in, garbage out.

## Case Study – Wigan Warriors

We interviewed executives from Oval, a sports data analytics firm that works with the Wigan Warriors – an English rugby league team. The team is one of the Big Three that dominate English rugby league; however, it had not won the domestic Super League in five years.

In 2023, Wigan began working with Oval to use machine-learning models to influence tactics, selection and recruitment. Although the exact details are proprietary, the point was to use time-stamped data on in-game events (from carries to passes to tackles) and build predictive models and answer questions, such as how and when to run or kick the ball.

The results came fast and, in 2023, Wigan won the Super League title and then the World Club Championship.

Wigan Head coach Matt Peet told us, “Where Oval’s data and analytics have proved really valuable is for fine-tuning our tactics and spotting hidden patterns in the stats, especially for the big knockout matches – and for giving us another perspective on how our players contribute to success.”

## 5. Countries are increasingly building sports investment

This is occurring for various purposes including diversification. The poster child for sovereign sports investment is Saudi Arabia which has invested heavily in Cristiano Ronaldo, the LIV golf series, eSports, MMA, and has reportedly considered a large investment in tennis, cricket, and Formula 1, among others. Reports estimate Saudi Arabia has spent £5bn over the past three years.

Saudi's investment in LIV golf has directly led to an increased valuation for the wider golf market - the case in point is the \$1.5bn investment in the PGA tour (eventually up to \$3bn) by The Strategic Sports Group in January this year. It seems unlikely SSG would have paid as much if it weren't engaged in an unofficial bidding war with Saudi given that the PGA tours annual revenues amount to around \$1.5bn.

## 6. The growth in sports-specific investment funds

Both the number of and their robust fundraising is aiding market liquidity and price discovery, along with providing viable exit routes.

One example is Arctos Partners which recently closed its second sports-specific fund with \$4.1bn. It has investments in the Utah Jazz NBA team as well as the holding company invested in the Aston Martin Aramco Formula 1 team, New Jersey Devils NHL team, and the Philadelphia 76ers NBA team.

The availability of private money is pumping up the values of even the lowest value 'sleepy' corners of the sports market. One example is English cricket. Here, the domestic county league is a very low-budget affair and even the higher-value international teams generate an order of magnitude below many other professional sports. Indeed, the England and Wales Cricket Board receives just £225m annually from media rights and just £85m from other sources including sponsorship.

Despite these relatively low figures, the ECB recently turned down a reported offer from Lalit Modi worth \$1bn over ten years for 'The Hundred' series (which is just one of the four cricket formats). That followed reports of a previous offer from Bridgepoint worth £400m for a 75% stake - all just a year after the competition began.

7. The business of icon players is becoming more sophisticated "Captaincy is 90% luck and 10% skill. But don't try it without that 10%"

## - Richie Benaud

While icon players have been around at least since the gladiators in Rome, it has only been more recently that the business of being an ‘icon’ sports star has more rapidly emerged.

Pele arguably started the trend when he moved to the US to play football (soccer) for the New York Cosmos in 1975. Since then, the US has been a regular stomping ground for football (soccer) stars to end their careers in an environment where the players themselves, the teams, and the league can best capitalise on their celebrity – David Beckham and Lionel Messi are other high-profile examples.

The effect of icon players varies across different sports. The following charts show the effect in the UK and US; there is anecdotal evidence that support for a player (over a team) is becoming more common amongst younger viewers.

A key driver of icon players is the increasing longevity of their careers. In part, this is due to the professionalisation of sports over the last few decades, which has allowed players to focus more attention on their sports career. Longevity has also been dramatically increased by the advances in nutrition and the impact of sports science on training programmes.

A key example of longevity comes from tennis, where the four biggest players of the last two decades have won 34 grand slams since they turned 30 years old (Djokovic 12, Williams 10, Nadal 8, and Federer 4). Before them, the record was jointly held by Laver and Rosewall with 4 each. There are examples of ageing stars remaining world class in many other sports in recent years: Cristiano Ronaldo (football/ soccer), Lauren Jackson (basketball), Tom Brady (NFL), Eileen Olszewski (boxing),

Chris Gayle (cricket), LeBron James (basketball), Jan Frodeno (triathlon). This list goes on.

The near-cult success of players helps the business of sports teams and boosts their value. There is a separate question about where the value is eventually extracted, but there is no doubt that icon players boost the money broadcasters are willing to pay, which trickles down in various ways.

This goes a long way in explaining the big money being paid for some of the highestprofile athletes. For example, Saudi Arabia signed Cristiano Ronaldo to a contract reported to be worth \$75m a year with the possibility that this could rise to around \$220m depending on commercial deals. Meanwhile, the Los Angeles Dodgers have signed Japanese star Shohei Ohtani to a \$700m contract (payable over 20 years).

Even sports with a long history of short contracts are changing. Professional cycling has historically offered short contracts on concerns about swings in fitness and injuries. However in March, the road race world champion Mathieu van der Poel signed a 5-year deal with his team Alpecin-Deceuninck and a 10-year deal with bike manufacturer Canyon.

## 8. Broadcasting and streaming is evolving to be more valuable

Our objective in this section is not to cover every aspect of the sports broadcasting market. Rather, we point out some sports-specific growth areas that can lead to a bigger conversation around the direction of valuations.

The media rights landscape is a market made up of many markets. To some extent competitions undercut each other with broadcasters. This is particularly for sports outside football in Europe where they are not centralised as much as smaller sports are in the US and thus have lower negotiating power.

In the short term, fragmentation of broadcasting is likely to continue in some sports. Sport is one of the few things that people want to watch live and that propped up cable networks for years. In recent years the growth in the market appears to have plateaued, and sports teams are now hoping that streaming platforms, such as Netflix and Apple, will inject more tension into the market even before they are able to strike global deals. Indeed, Amazon and WBD are entering more sports markets with Nascar. Meanwhile, Discovery Plus has aggregated its rights to many of the biggest cycling races.

Rising competition from streaming services has been a key theme in recent years, and we expect this to continue – particularly due to big-tech’s growing involvement. Advancements in technology that allow better statistics and provide a platform for more features like gaming and other capabilities that enhance the fan experience are the cornerstone of this.

So even though TV broadcasting may have plateaued, in the medium term the uptick in streaming and online platforms may more than offset this. We expect bigtech to take on more sports deals and eventually look to create global distribution deals that benefit from, and incorporate, the value of customer data.

This section briefly looks at some reasons for growth in broadcasting markets:

The fragmentation of broadcasting

## Pricing power in pay-per-view

The NFL pushed new boundaries during the playoffs in January when it sold the rights to the Kansas-Miami match (and a regular season match) to NBCUniversal, which put it behind a paywall. Although some said fans would refuse to pay, the opposite was true. An average of 23m people watched the game (including the local audience who could watch for free). NBC called it the “most streamed event in US history” and claimed internet usage in the US hit an all-time record for a single day.

Another notable development has been the proposed joint venture between ESPN, a Disney subsidiary, Fox and Warner Bros. Discovery for a sport streaming service in the US, covering major US leagues but also golf, cycling and racing among others. This is reflective of the trend that sport is increasingly the main reason people keep cable TV but the rise of streaming and fragmented access to various games create inefficiencies if there is no platform consolidation.

One lesson from the episode is that big sports leagues have pricing power, particularly with a pay-per-view audience. Indeed, over 30% of adults in the US and UK have purchased at least one PPV sports event in the last 12 months.

## Under-monetized new forms of media

Social media is both a problem and an opportunity for sports teams and leagues. Consider the NBA, where – in some cases – viewership of full games has fallen, but viewership of highlights on social media has simultaneously risen. This is especially true for younger generations.

A similar question of value transfer is raised by the ‘documentary effect’ following the success of the ‘Drive to Survive’ series. This changed sports documentaries from frequently being marketing vehicles with little value to presenting a more ‘inside’ look at a sport for engaged fans. Since ‘Drive to Survive’ was released, there has been a rush of sports leagues looking to replicate its success. That includes rugby, cricket, football, tennis, cycling and others.

## Multi-speed growth in the three pillars of sports investment

We continue to see different growth trajectories for the three different ‘pillars’ of sports investment:

1. Leagues – With some leagues experiencing a plateau in media rights deals, growth is becoming more dependent on exploiting intellectual property. Success here is mixed and is becoming more dependent on a league’s ability to use technology on a direct-to-customer basis (via super apps or similar tech developments) to increase fan engagement and harvest data. The ability to access international markets is becoming a larger growth priority as the growth plateaus in domestic broadcasting markets.

Whilst boosting tech engagement and exploiting IP is something that large corporates have done for several years, the administrators of some sports leagues have lagged. Many simply lack the scale, revenues, and expertise of larger non-sport corporates. Hence, some financial sponsors are more focussed on taking stakes in leagues over teams to provide them with the necessary expertise and investment.

2. Teams – The increased awareness of how to manage a team towards a profitable investment is driving the emergence of sports-specific investment funds towards taking stakes in teams. However, different types of investors are gravitating towards different types of teams.

i. Established high-profile teams – The appeal for investors in these teams lies in the ability to exploit intellectual property and better engage with fans, particularly outside the domestic market.

ii. Lower-tier clubs – the appeal for investors here lies in the ability to manage the club towards better performance. In particular, this involves identifying teams that have not yet implemented sophisticated data analytics.

Teams are increasingly likely to take a combination of equity and credit in their capital structure. Wealthy individuals are more likely to invest in teams over leagues.

3. Services – This may be the highest growth area (from a lower base). The market for ‘picks and shovels’ in sports has expanded rapidly with artificial intelligence and machine learning turbocharging data analytics. Some teams see the use of this as a zero-sum game (we do not – more on this later in the report). Regardless, their use is becoming standard.

AI-related growth also comes from services including the production of highlight packages for use in both real-time broadcasting and for uploading to apps to boost fan engagement. In addition, there is significant growth potential in technologies that help umpires adjudicate on the field (such as ‘Hawkeye’ and the like).

## The impact of super money

We believe the ‘super money’ that has recently flooded sports will become a constant of the sporting world. That said, we believe these deals will usually remain niche and accessible only to the established mega stars and clubs and, sometimes, used to promote an agenda (business or political) from the sponsoring entity.

The muscling-in of super money to sports is nothing new, and it does come with concerns in some quarters about whether it reduces the value of either the new or incumbent league and associated teams. The example of World Series Cricket in the 1970s showed that incumbent teams can be destroyed by an exodus of players to the new league. That also raises the prospect of a backlash by fans and thus a destruction in overall team and league value.

Understanding how fans feel about M&A is critical as about a third of people in our survey said that the sports team they support has been bought by new owners in the last ten years. And most of those investments happened in the last five years.

Our analysis shows that 26% British people and 19% of Americans think that big money moving into sports is a bad thing. Of course, that means most people are happy with it (or do not care) – but it still leaves a material chunk of disgruntled fans. ## The sports IPO market

While we expect sports M&A to grow, we do not expect a material increase in the number of sports IPOs that are brought to market. One concern here is the impact of a team’s short-term performance on a stock price and how on-field momentum may impact public investor sentiment.

## Private credit

Private credit will likely remain a relative rarity in sports; however, we expect this to become more prominent in certain leagues. Credit may be particularly useful in sports that have a long history and an established and loyal fan base, but that have a short-term liquidity problem. Major US leagues, such as the NFL, already issue investment grade notes.

One example is the \$500m subordinated loan by Ares to Chelsea at the holding company level as the club executes its investment strategy but struggles on the field. In 2022, Ares raised \$3.7bn for sports, media and entertainment deals across the capital structure, more than twice its initial target.

Another example is Pacific Equity Partner’s A\$80m loan to Rugby Australia. The sport has been suffering from decreased engagement following a string of poor performances by the national men’s squad, along with prior controversies and settlements. However, while rugby is a smaller sport in Australia, it has a strong and established base of supporters as well as relatively good visibility on future match schedules and revenues.

## Europe and the US have different M&A characteristics

## Europe

We believe Europe will be a focus for sports M&A and investment in the near term. This will likely be concentrated on football and be particularly the case for lowerprofile teams and leagues. There are several reasons for this:

1. In Europe, it can be easier for smaller investors to own a slice of a team – compared with the environment in the US.

2. European football is set up such that an investor can buy a club, run it well, see it promoted, and then sell at a profit.

This makes commercial rights and sponsorships more important for some teams, but this is also where economies of scale and club dominance are especially pronounced.

There are several headwinds for financial sponsors. For one, there is a limited number of premium assets just as competition among deep-pocketed funds heats up, especially given LP interest and still-robust fund-raising. Other competing investors include sovereign wealth funds and star athletes launching their own ventures, although those are focused on different niches across the spectrum of growth-type deals and TMT-like major pro leagues stakes.

## Private equity

Operationally, sports teams frequently do not have as much room to cut costs compared with some of the bloated companies that traditional private equity likes to source. Instead, PE demand is derived from investments in top-line expansion. This is in part because financial sponsor ownership may be perceived poorly by fans and the actual governance powers are often constrained, not least due to public money being involved. For instance, total PE ownership is constrained at 30% for NBA and 20% for individual funds.

Finally, for the same reason that investing in European football can have fewer barriers to entry, cashing out can also be easier. The number of teams in viable (and promotable) leagues is large. Meanwhile, would-be sellers are less likely to face the kind of owners’ club voting procedures that are common in the US.

## Greater M&A may restructure sports leagues themselves

We believe the increased involvement of financial investors in sports teams may lead to a restructuring of various sports leagues. This may particularly be the case in Europe where some leagues are dominated by the same well-funded clubs every year. As financial investors begin to have more say, they may prefer a league model that has rules closer to some in the US. This may include combinations of salary caps, lottery systems, and more collective dealings that mean all the teams in a league have a better chance of contending for a championship. A more level playing field can lead to wider competition and, in turn, better fan engagement and television rights deals.

As investment companies own more teams across different leagues, we expect an increase (from a small base) in the number of holding companies employing players to play on company-owned teams in different global leagues at different times during the year. Some companies have already been experimenting with this model in cricket, where leagues in different countries are timed to not coincide with each other to maximise cross-promotion.

## The risk of political intervention

Perhaps the biggest risk to sports valuations is the possible involvement of politics. Of course, it has long been difficult to separate politics from sport as the latter has such strong emotional appeal.

Already, there are political issues around how sports are broadcast. For example, various countries mandate that certain sports or matches must be shown on freeto-view platforms or separately approved for a pay-tv deal. For example, the UK maintains a list of sporting events that are of “national interest”. These include the World Cups for football, rugby, and cricket, as well as Wimbledon and others.

It is possible (or even likely) that governments in the future may wish to include more sports or matches on such schedules or be involved in sport in other ways. Policies of this type would certainly appeal to many people who various politicians may wish to target. If implemented, that may immediately dent the financial appeal of certain teams or leagues.

One example is the short-lived European Super League. The plan was to create a new competition in which Europe’s best football clubs would play more regularly against each other. The result, though, was widespread condemnation from many different politicians who also made various threats to make such a league unworkable. This led to the plan being scuppered within days. Although many argued that the timing of the ESL announcement (in the middle of the covid crisis) was to blame, it still illustrates how investors underestimate the potential impact of politics on sport.

Potential conflict of interest issues may also arise if an investor bids for more than one team in a league. In certain leagues, this would certainly be blocked, but as financial sponsors take more and more interest in sports teams, this issue is likely to arise in various nuanced forms in the years ahead.

## Conclusion – The future of sports M&A and valuations

Sport as a single market is in rude health. However, we believe the top end sports market will grow valuations and financial power the quickest, while less popular sports will experience growth at a lower rate. We come to this conclusion on the basis that younger generations are increasingly avid about supporting their favourite teams and they see sports as a critical way to maintain social relationships. As such, this creates a network effect that means young people are likely to increasingly gravitate around similar popular sports.

There are various pathways for sports valuations to increase across the three pillars of leagues, teams, and services. Younger generations are increasingly framing their identity around their favourite sports teams and are willing to pay for it. It also seems women’s sport will likely grow to a position where M&A capital is increasingly allocated towards these teams.

In the end, humans are arguably hardwired to engage in sports competition. And the more people become less physical in their own lifestyle, the more they may wish to watch those people in the world who are the best at what they do.

Macroeconomic fluctuations often account for most of the risks associated with exposures to instruments that promise to pay fixed or variable interest rates. For an investor who is long fixed-rate instruments (thus receiving these cash flows), increases in interest rates naturally lift the discount factors applied to the expected cash flows and thus cause a loss. The longer the maturity of a certain cash flow and the higher the move in the discount factor, the higher will be the loss. Upside surprises in inflation, fiscal funding needs, and FX depreciation rates are among the most common adverse macroeconomic shocks to receivers. But counterparty exposure, issuer creditworthiness, client segmentation, regulation (including changes in assets holding limits for different types of investors), changes in tax policies, currency convertibility (which may constrain currency conversion, repatriation of profits and/or liquidation of positions), and settlement issues related to local clearing houses are also important risk factors. The sensitivity of fixed-income instruments to macroeconomic shocks may be mitigated by indexing the contracted cash flows to inflation, to FX depreciation, or to specified interest rates - these are common in emerging markets. The index fixings may - by construction - lag or mis-measure the actual move in the underlying variables they are intended to track. The choice of the proper fixing (or metric) is particularly important in swaps markets, where floating coupon rates (i.e., coupons indexed to a typically short-dated interest rate reference index) are exchanged for fixed coupons. Funding in a currency that differs from the currency in which coupons are denominated carries FX risk.
