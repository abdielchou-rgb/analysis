## Greater China

• China: August activity data reinforce soft tone on domestic demand

• Most housing figures worsened again as time inconsistency issues linger

• Hong Kong SAR: CPI ticked up another 0.3%m/m sa

• Taiwan: CBC on hold for policy rate, but hiked RRR again and tightened credit to cool housing boom

• Next week: China MLF operation, Hong Kong trade, Taiwan export orders and IP

China’s August activity data do not show signs of improvement in domestic demand conditions. The weak links in the economy remain, and the concern is that a few solidly performing sectors seem to be losing momentum. First, housing activity remained weak, and consumer sentiment stayed soft. Employment expectations in the consumer confidence index slipped to a record low, though the official survey-based unemployment rate edged up only 0.1%pt, to 5.3%. Second, new energy and NEV sectors seem to be affected by targeted tariff hikes from the U.S. that were broadened to the EU and Canada. Auto output fell 2.3%oya in August (vs. a 4.5% increase in January-July), and solar battery output fell 9.0%oya in August (vs. an 18.2% increase in January-July) (Figure 1).

Supportive policy impacts still seem to be lagging: Infra FAI growth slowed further, to 1.2%oya in August (Figure 2), the weakest monthly reading since 2022. This reflects both the slow pace of special local government bond issuance this year and a limited pool of qualified projects. The bright spots in August activity include exports (3.9%m/m sa increase in August) and high-tech manufacturing (rising 8.6%oya in August production). But the lingering softness in domestic demand and intensifying deflation pressure – further dragging on corporate revenue and earnings, household income and

government tax revenue – seem increasingly worrying. Overall, August data appear to still support our below-trend 3Q growth forecast of 3.0% q/q saar.

President Xi last week stressed that government officials should strive to (a tone-down from “must” previously) achieve the 2024 annual growth target (around 5%, and compared to our forecast of 4.6%) and to ensure solid growth toward the end of 3Q and 4Q. In our view, to achieve this target, the government needs to strengthen countercyclical measures (fiscal, monetary and housing) and shift to balanced support between consumption and investment, between service and manufacturing sectors, to steer the economy onto a sustained recovery path. For instance, to support consumption, the government could take measures to boost household income growth, revive the service sector, stabilize the housing market, and lower household savings rates.

Such a policy pivot is not in our baseline assumption yet. But a few potential catalysts and timelines are worth monitoring: (1) if September/October macro data suggest that the economy’s weak links (housing and consumption) turn even weaker and fiscal revenue continues to contract; (2) Politburo meeting and annual Central Economic Work Conference by late November/early December; and (3) U.S. tariff policy after the upcoming elections.

## Most housing figures worsened again

The NBS 70 cities’ new home prices were down 5.7%oya or 0.7%m/m nsa in August (-5.3%oya or -0.6%m/m nsa in July), and secondary home prices were down 8.6%oya or 0.9%m/m nsa (vs. -8.2%oya or -0.8%m/m nsa in July). With that, new home prices declined 8.4% compared to the 2021 peak as of August, and secondary home prices dropped 14.7% (Figure 3). As in other asset markets, demand is unlikely to recover meaningfully until the market believes prices have bottomed or even overshot in their adjustment.

With continued cash flow pressure and a low incentive to purchase land and start new projects, real estate FAI continues to contract rapidly, down 10.2%oya ytd as of August. Compared to the 2021 peak, real estate FAI has declined 31%. We now see a real estate FAI decline of around 10% for the full year, translating into another 1.2-1.4%pts drag on overall economic growth. This is one key reason why we think this year’s GDP growth (4.6%y/y in our forecast) is running the risk of undershooting the government’s growth target of around 5%.

The real estate sector and overall economy are still in a selfreinforcing downward spiral: Housing drags down economic growth, and without any material signs of the economy recovering, income and employment confidence remains low and housing demand languishes, reinforcing the shrinkage of the real estate sector. Our view has been that the adjustment needs to be completed early through a comprehensive strategy. However, the visibility for a policy boost to stop the downward spiral is still low. More policy easing, as a continuation of the gradualist approach, may come around the National Day holiday, such as a relaxation of rules on second home mortgages, easing home purchase restrictions in tier-1 cities, further relaxation of sales restrictions, etc.

## Hong Kong SAR: Base rate cut by 50bps

Following the Fed’s latest rate cut, Hong Kong moved in lock-step under its Linked Exchange Rate regime. The discount window base rate was cut by 50bps today, to 5.25%, the first cut since mid-2020. We have been flagging that Hong Kong’s economic performance is more correlated with Mainland China, but its monetary policy has to follow the U.S. Due to the social unrest and COVID-19 related drags, Hong Kong’s real GDP was 14.7% below the pre-pandemic growth path by 2023. The restrictive policy rate has been weighing on home purchase demand, domestic retail sales on an unfavorable FX exchange rate, etc.

Following the base rate cut, Hong Kong’s note-issuing banks, HSBC, BOC HK and Standard Chartered, lowered the prime rate by 25bps, an effort to lower mortgage financing cost. But given that the rate level remains high and that HKD is rela-

tively stronger compared to other regional peer currencies, the impact of lifting home purchase demand and boosting domestic retail sales likely will be limited. In this regard, the latest policy rate cut may bring a breather, but more easing is needed to deliver a more accommodative monetary policy stance to the economy.

Separately, Hong Kong’s unemployment rate stayed unchanged, at 3.0% sa in August. In the details, the labor force declined 0.2%m/m sa and employment fell 0.3%. The under-employment rate was also unchanged, at 1.2%. By sector breakdown, the employment easing was broad-based, including trade (-1.1%m/m sa), hotel and restaurant (-0.7%), retail (-0.7%) and transport and storage (-0.2%). The latest policy rate cut may lend some support to businesses in a few interest-rate-sensitive sectors. But the impact likely will be mild, and the transmission to the labor market takes time. We expect the city’s unemployment rate to stay around 3% before year-end.

## CPI ticked up another 0.3%m/m sa

Hong Kong’s headline CPI grew a modest 2.5%oya in August, the same pace as in July. Seasonally adjusted, we estimate that headline CPI ticked up another 0.3%m/m sa, following the 1.0% gain in July. This is the fourth consecutive month recording a positive monthly run rate. Major drivers have been housing, utilities and food as the government reduced or phased out some of the utilities subsidy and rates concession arrangement (Figure 5). Netting out the effects of the government’s one-off relief measures, underlying CPI rose a more modest 1.2%oya or 0.1%m/m sa in August.

Despite the continued upturn in CPI inflation in recent months, we don’t think the city’s inflation is running hot. The impact of utility subsidy phase-out is gradually fading. Food prices have been affected by summer holiday and hot weather related seasonality. The Mainland’s high-frequency tracking shows a continued upturn in food prices and solid pork prices in September. That suggests that recent food price gains may still have another leg, though the pace will be modest and the lift on headline CPI will be limited. For the housing price component, there is around a one-year lag in rental price transmission to CPI, and last year’s rental rate turned up further into year-end. Among these three drivers, we think housing prices may take more of the lead in lifting headline CPI going forward. But aside from these, the underlying demand recovery is still mild. Overall, we think the city’s inflation will stay moderate, averaging around 2.3%oya before yearend.

## Taiwan: CBC kept policy rate unchanged

The Taiwanese central bank decided to keep its key policy rate on hold at the 3Q monetary policy meeting (Figure 6). Meanwhile, it raised the reserve ratio on TWD deposits by 25bps (which will dry up about NT\$125 billion of banking sector liquidity) and further tightened housing credit policy (including lowering the mortgage loan-to-valuation ratio for second-home purchases in select regions to 50% from 60%). The central bank is also using moral suasion for banks to properly manage their real estate loans and reduce concentration risks of bank loan exposure. These measures are aimed at slowing credit flowing into the property market, avoiding asset bubble risks and fostering financial stability.

While the U.S. Fed started its rate cut cycle this week, as expected, our long-held view is that rate cuts are unlikely in Taiwan through 2024. Taiwan has a steady growth outlook driven by the tech up-cycle, with a gradual feed-through to the domestic economy. Rate increases in this cycle have been rather modest and have left Taiwan’s real interest rate below the historical average. Hence, we do not expect the Taiwanese central bank to follow the Fed with policy rate cuts in the near term.

With a higher base effect kicking in and a fading of the temporary typhoon effect on food prices, headline CPI appears on track to ease below 2%oya in the coming months. The external demand backdrop has turned somewhat cautious lately, with notable easing in the global manufacturing PMI in the three months through August. Property market conditions are likely to remain a key focus of the central bank. We expect the central bank to keep policy rates on hold in the coming quarters, and further signs of heating in the property market could trigger further housing credit tightening and reserve ratio hikes.
