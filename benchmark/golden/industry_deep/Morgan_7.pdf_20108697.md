# Navigating the Noisy Election

![](images/5ed49795026c3f471f97fc7803081d8cf7c4be67ab2d98bd5b4a778841ac9b60.jpg)

Expect Outcome Uncertainty to Persist: The deeply divided US electorate is reflected in tight polls and prediction markets. With the ongoing adoption of vote by mail, the counting of which takes longer than machine votes, it would not be surprising to have several days of

uncertainty about the outcome following election day.

Policies in Play with Clear Macro Impact, Uncertain Magnitude: Tariffs & immigration are key variables related to election outcomes. Republican win scenarios yield greater potential change in these policies via executive action and, unlike in 2017, they may be sequenced to precede, rather than follow, fiscal expansion via tax policy. This forces consideration of combined effects, whereas in our experience investors in 2017 focused primarily on fiscal benefits. Potential combined impacts are noisy, with risks skewed to the downside for growth. Potential changes at the Fed add a layer of uncertainty. Policy choices under Democratic win scenarios skew more predictable, as most represent a continuation of the status quo and hence, fewer exogenous factors to consider for the economy.

Our Investor Survey Reflects This Uncertainty: There appears to be little consensus on the path forward for key policies and market outcomes related to the election, as well as some key inconsistencies between how investors view the likelihood of policy outcomes and market effects.

Navigating the Noise in Markets: Amid these uncertainties, we see the following signals for investors...

• Policy choices have reliable impacts on equity sectors: lean into those with a positive skew across outcomes. Opportunities exist in sectors where specific policy outcomes might amplify already positive stories. This includes industrials and telecom on potential benefits around tax cut extensions in a Republican sweep. It also includes sectors involved in our "Security" theme, in particular defense, as we see both parties incentivized to spend more on national security, with potentially greater global growth on defense spending if Republicans win the presidency.

• Outcomes skew toward Treasury & USD strength, volatility: History guides us to regard election outcome, and related policy uncertainty, as volatility inducing for both Treasuries and USD. The impact of policy paths across outcomes tend toward strengthening USD. In addition to the already constructive setup for USD, history suggests tariff talk resulting from a Republican White House win could boost the currency further, though a Trump administration's posture toward the Fed is a key risk to this view. For Treasuries, election outcomes mostly affirm our current expectation of a path to lower nominal yields, though a Republican sweep is more complicated, with potential for a higher term premium and divergence between lower real yields and higher breakevens as investors try to parse through the uncertainty of combined policy impacts on the US economic outlook.

• A lack of market precedent means stick with the crossasset playbook favoring carry, convexity, and cheap optionality. Our cross-asset examination of market behavior into and out of prior US elections doesn't reveal much in the way of usable patterns, even when filtering for a variety of election conditions and outcomes. No reliable market trend plus no reliable macro impact means, for the time being, investors should ignore the noise and stick with the playbook. Hence, we still favor European and Japanese equities, and spread-product within fixed income because a benign macro backdrop is good for risk assets; good carry and convexity also helps to weather uncertainties beyond November.

## Executive Summary

Elections have consequences. But when it comes to identifying those consequences for financial markets, it's easy to mistake noise for signal. For example, in the 2020 election, our survey showed investors tended to link a Biden win with negative outcomes for equity markets and, seemingly contradicting this point, a lowering tariffs. Neither outcome came to pass, and it seems in particular investors underestimated the probability and impact of further fiscal expansion. But we have sympathy for such confusion, given the noise of US elections. Candidates promise a lot in terms of policy, but their willingness and ability to deliver on such promises is a signal that's more difficult to focus on.

For the 2024 election, we see plenty of noise for investors to deal with. Consider:

• There's good reason to believe this election will be too close to call, even in the days following the election... The US electorate remains deeply divided. As in prior elections, polls show within-the-margin-of-error gaps between the candidates in a sufficient number of states such that if the election were held today it would not be surprising if either candidate won. Prediction markets reflect this reality, giving each candidate a roughly 50% chance of victory. And with the ongoing adoption of vote by mail, the counting of which takes longer than machine votes, it would not be surprising to have several days of outcome uncertainty, featuring legal challenges and competing declarations of victory.

• There are policies in play that create meaningful uncertainties for the economic & markets outlook... Consider a scenario where former President Trump wins another term. He has promised to use executive power in ways we can confidently say will have substantial influence on the growth and inflation dynamic governing the US economic outlook. These include greater use of tariffs, immigration curbs, and exploring ways to exert authority over the Fed. This may sound a lot like Trump's prior term, but there's a key difference for investors. In that term, executive actions with unfamiliar economic consequences, like tariffs, were sequenced to follow policy choices whose consequences had greater perceived predictability, such as tax cuts, allowing investors to focus on the impact of fiscal policy in isolation for much of 2017. In a hypothetical second term, we argue investors should prepare for a scenario where executive actions precede legislative policy choices, like extending tax cuts. Hence, markets will have to attempt to price in combined effects of policy choices that could push and pull on growth and inflation in different directions, depending on their construction. Our discussion of economic impacts in Republican win scenarios is in line with this, but would add that risks are skewed to the downside for growth, and potential changes at the Fed add a layer of uncertainty. Policy choices under Democratic win scenarios skew more predictable, as most represent a continuation of the status quo and hence, less exogenous factors to consider for the economy.

## Amid the uncertainty, we see value in the following guidance:

• Policy choices have reliable impacts on equity sectors: lean into those with a positive skew across outcomes. Opportunities exist in sectors where specific policy outcomes might amplify already positive stories. This includes industrials and telecom on potential benefits around tax cut extensions in a Republican sweep. It also includes sectors involved in our "Security" theme, in particular defense, as we see both parties incentivized to spend more on national security, which potentially greater global growth on defense spending if Republicans win the presidency.

• Outcomes skew toward Treasury & USD strength, volatility: History guides us to regard election outcome, and related policy uncertainty, as volatility inducing for both Treasuries and USD. The impact of policy paths across outcomes tend toward strengthening USD. In addition to the already constructive setup for USD, history suggests tariff talk resulting from a Republican White House win could boost the currency further, though a Trump administration's posture toward the Fed is a key risk to this view. For Treasuries, election outcomes mostly affirm our current expectation of a path to lower nominal yields, though a Republican sweep is more complicated, with potential for a higher term premium and divergence between lower real yields and higher breakevens as investors try to parse through the uncertainty of combined policy impacts on the US economic outlook.

• In cross-asset strategy, ignore the noise into the election and stick with the playbook favoring carry, convexity, and cheap optionality. Post-election, there's opportunity if we achieve greater policy clarity, but we think that will take time. Our cross-asset examination of market behavior into and out of prior US elections doesn't reveal much in the way of usable patterns, even when filtering for a variety of election conditions and outcomes. Said differently, even if an investor has perfect foresight and knows the results of the elections now, it won't necessarily give them an edge at the asset class level. Hence, we still favor European and Japanese equities, and spread products (e.g,. agency MBS, loans), as the three C's (carry, convexity, and cheap optionality) remain key themes across stocks and bonds. Coming out of elections there's some evidence that policy clarity can drive relative asset performance, but given the policy variables that matter to the outlook in this election, and our prior discussion of the importance of their sequencing and construction, we don't expect such clarity will quickly follow the election result.

## Want to dig deeper? In this report we also offer primers on tax & immigration policy considerations for markets:

• Taxes : As lawmakers contend with the expiring TCJA, we expect a Republican sweep outcome to yield legislation more similar to the original 2017 law, while a Democratic sweep likely portends certain extensions paired with offsets like higher taxes on wealthy individuals & corporations as a partial pay-for. In a divided government, we see a narrow extension of some key provisions with bipartisan support. Each outcome leads to an incremental deficit, in our view, with the largest impact in a Republican sweep (\$1.6 trillion over 10 years) vs. \$600 billion in a Democratic sweep and \$1 trillion in a divided government.

Immigration : If immigration continues to be a top issue for voters, we expect the next administration — Republican or Democratic — to take policy action, including legislative & executive options. We see a potential revival of the stalled bipartisan border bill in addition to possible unilateral executive action to limit daily crossings at the southern border, with key impacts to the economic outlook. Although scenarios can yield different policy outcomes, we see a certain degree of policy convergence between Democratic & Republican lawmakers as President Biden recently released an executive order mirroring the asylum and border changes that the bipartisan border bill would have included if passed.

# A Close Race Challenges Policy Outcome Clarity

## Key Takeaways

• If polls and other indicators continue to show similar results, they suggest a race too close to call.

• We see little reason to argue that these indicators would be wrong given their variety and the structural starting point of a divided electorate.

• A race that's too close to call could, as in 2020, lead to a period of outcome uncertainty, exacerbated by the increased use of vote-by-mail.

The electorate is divided: The 2022 midterm elections marked the fourth time in four years that less than 1 percent of votes effectively determined which side would control the House, the Senate, or the White House. Similarly, eight states were decided by less than 5% of the vote in 2020 (down from 11 in 2016). This pattern, which results in closer races won on thinner margins, means that a smaller number of competitive races tend to decide control of each chamber, and a smaller number of votes can hand either candidate the presidency, as we note here.

Our AlphaWise top issue survey suggests voters' party ID is increasingly influential in how they view candidates' ability to address their concerns. Voters are frequently asked their top concern as well as which party they trust more to handle a given issue, allowing us to gauge "ownership" of that topic by either party. In our monthly consumer survey, we ask respondents for their top issue and whether or not the country is on the right or wrong track with respect to that issue (which we also highlight in our series the Capitol & Markets).

Not surprisingly, then, when considering a variety of indicators, the current race for the White House appears quite close. Consider:

• If polls hold or tighten into election day, they suggest neither presidential candidate is favored to win. In particular, neither candidate is polling with an outside the margin of error lead in sufficient states to win an electoral victory. It's true that former President Trump has sufficient leads in polls to add up to an electoral victory, but not enough outside the margin of error. Further, polls — even six months out from the election — do not have great predictive value, and recent studies show that the polling error doesn’t consistently swing in one direction or the other.

• Polling-based forecast models as well as prediction markets show each candidate hovering around a 50% chance to win.

## Hurry Up & Wait: Expect Volatility in the Aftermath of the Election

Prepare to (again) wait days for the result, introducing market volatility in the days following the general election November 5th. We think it could take days due to a sustained increase in the adoption of alternate voting methods across states, including vote by mail which peaked during the pandemic but has since been institutionalized in some states. Consider...

• Nevada and Vermont switched during the pandemic to universal mail voting (mailing ballots to all active registered voters) for all elections going forward.

• Kentucky locked in changes that allow in-person early voting, adding three days of in-person early voting and allowing counties to establish "voting centers" where any voter can go to vote rather than at a single local precinct.

Six states (Connecticut, Delaware, Illinois, Maine, Nevada, and New York) enacted automated voter registration laws.

• In total, 25 states have enacted legislation aimed at creating more expansive voting provisions, including expanding early voting opportunities, facilitating mail voting, easing voter registration, and expanding mail ballot drop box access or locations, according to the Brennan Center.

Because of the delays associated with sending in mail-in ballots (read more here), it's reasonable to expect a lag in delivery of the actual election result, meaning that there could be days of volatility & uncertainty, driving another "blue shift."

What is a "blue shift?" Because more Democratic voters than Republicans tend to choose the early vote option, and because these votes were counted after in-person voting on Election Day, those who did not understand this dynamic would perhaps be understandably confused by the resulting sequence of events in 2020: Substantial leads in vote count for Republicans in many key areas gave way, over the next few days, to closer margins and/or Democratic leads. This drove significant amounts of uncertainty in the 2020 election, as some key states that counted votes over the days after the election saw a shift from a decisive Trump victory to a narrow Biden win as mail-in ballots were counted.

We expect in that time there could be instances during which either candidate appears to be ahead at different points in the counting, leading to possible competing declarations of victory and widespread confusion about the outcome.

## Economic Implications

## Key Takeaways

• Republican win outcomes have a greater potential impact on the economic outlook given their approach to tariff, immigration, and fiscal policy.

• Republican win impacts are noisy, with risks skewed to the downside for growth. Sequencing and construction of policy choices will matter. The Fed under a Trump administration adds an extra layer of uncertainty.

• The impact of Democratic win outcomes skews more predictable as policy choices mostly represent a continuation of the status quo and hence, less exogenous factors to consider for the economy.

Policy choices that may result from the 2024 election outcome have the power to influence the growth and inflation outlooks. Yet their impacts are largely uncertain given lack of precedent and key unknowns regarding the details of policy construction. In this section, we examine directional macroeconomic impacts along the lines of three variables that we think will be critical to watch after the election: Tariffs, Immigration, and Fiscal Policy (Deficit).

1) Why there are policy differences across the outcomes: Presidents have significant discretion over tariffs, and both candidates have different plans for trade policy. In a second Biden term, we anticipate a continuation of "Competitive Confrontation" when it comes to China, but limited trade frictions with traditional allies. For example, the President has not indicated a willingness to place tariffs on Mexico and has previously extended the pause on Sec 232 tariffs on Europe. We anticipate a continued increase of non-tariff barriers and other trade restrictions with respect to China, in line with the strategy the administration has deployed over the past four years (detailed here). It's possible that President Biden in a second term pursues extremely narrow & targeted tariffs, but we expect any further efforts to resemble the narrow EV tariffs that the administration announced in May (see here for more). In a Trump win scenario, a variety of options are on the table, as tariffs are largely under the control of the president. Former President Trump has cited various tariff plans in the event of a second administration, including baseline tariffs on all imports and tariffs targeting specific classes of goods and/or trading partners. These options come with substantial amounts of uncertainty: scope, timing, and product exemptions, to name a few.

2) Why there are macro (economic) differences across the outcomes: The economic impact of tariffs will depend on the details. The current round of tariffs imposed by the Biden administration are expected to have limited direct economic ramifications as US imports of the newly tariffed goods (EVs, solar panels, and batteries) are either zero or very small, and the targeted sectors are receiving fiscal support through the Inflation Reduction Act, which helps mitigate economic risk. For tariffs that might be imposed beyond these goods, particularly under a Trump scenario, our economists have also used the 2018 tariffs as a case study. The 2018 tariffs boosted CPI inflation only modestly because only part of the goods tariffed were final consumer goods, the Chinese RMB depreciated notably, and there was fairly rapid trade diversion. However, even with the depreciation and diversion, the fact that intermediate goods were tariffed led to negative effects on manufacturing employment in the sectors exposed to tariffs, and fairly negative net effects for the economy as a whole. With broader tariffs, the supply chain reorganization that is underway could be disrupted. In that case the effects on prices, volumes, and overall activity could potentially be much larger, depending on the details of the tariff proposals.

While Congress remains stalled on the Bipartisan Border Agreement (BBA) that would have established a threshold for daily crossings, reports indicate that both President Biden and former President Trump have attempted to use executive actions (including sec. 212f) to limit flows at the border. There is also precedent for tariffs to come into play as a means to achieve immigration policy in a Trump win scenario — the former president threatened potential tariffs on Mexican imports in 2019 if the government did not take action to address immigration levels. The clearest challenge in assessing likelihood of immigration proposals is the scope of executive authority and enforceability of these policy proposals. While he was in office, former President Trump attempted to reduce immigration flows through various executive orders that were reversed by courts; President Biden has indicated reluctance to use 212(f), though he later did so in an executive order. Currently, the CBO assumes that immigration flows will moderate starting next year after peaking at 3.3 million in 2023. The basis for slower flows is an assumption of reversion back toward the pre-Covid net immigration pace rather than policy change. There is not enough information at this point on what domestic immigration policy will definitively look like in 2025, how effective a policy change may be, and international developments that are driving immigration to the US.

2) Why there are macro (economic) differences across the outcomes: Estimating how much slowing could be due to policy change vs. trend normalization makes economic impacts unclear. Our 2025 forecasts include a sharp slowing in US immigration as it reverts to its pre-Covid pace, but policy at home and conditions abroad could change that view. In the three slower immigration scenarios, net immigration ranges from 1.4 million, which would be aligned with the BBA target, all the way to zero. In the BBA scenario, net immigration slowing to 1.4 million would result in 0.8%Y population growth and breakeven payrolls at 135k/month, which compares to 0.9%Y population growth (2.6mn) and 210k/month in our baseline. In the normalized scenario immigration is in line with the pre-COVID average of 800k, with 0.6%Y population growth and breakevens similar to pre-Covid at 87k/month. In the deportation scenario net immigration is at zero, caused by low immigration and a rise in emigration (likely due to stricter deportation policies). The population growth is entirely driven by the native-born population, at 0.2%Y, and breakeven payrolls at 45k. In any post-election outcome, we see stricter immigration policy as highly likely but the extent of changes to immigration policies and enforcement is open to question. The five scenarios outlined in the exhibit below include details on policy changes that could be expected in a second Biden or Trump administration.

In principle, immigration can affect both aggregate supply and aggregate demand, as large negative net immigration can lead to weaker consumption spending, a smaller labor force, and may drive down investment spending. Our US Economics team has noted that the supply effect is likely dominant in the current episode: the upside surprise in US real GDP growth in 2023 was partly driven by this expansion of the labor supply, and a contributing factor could be a lagged disinflationary effect from immigration.

The marked reduction in net migration scenarios explained above, and in Exhibit 8 and Exhibit 9 , imply that the non-farm payrolls breakeven number would be well lower than our 2025 baseline of 210K — in a range between 135k and 45k. That reduction in labor supply is substantial. Economic growth would necessarily be much slower than under the baseline, although because a lot of the jobs filled are in lower-average productivity industries, the effect is not directly proportional. The reduction in labor supply would also have an inflationary force, but the degree to which that inflation is realized depends both on the ability of firms to pass on higher labor costs and how quickly the Fed is able to identify and offset the supply shock.

The supply shock of lower net migration is also the at the crux of our analysis of the recent immigration restrictions proposed by the Biden administration (limiting unauthorized border crossings) on the US economy. While there is considerable uncertainty around its implementation, we estimate that if fully implemented, the breakeven level of payrolls would be approximately 160k per month, and the slowdown in immigration would subtract around 0.3pp from our growth estimates (we are tracking real GDP growth at 2.0% 4Q/4Q for 2024 as of June 12).

## 3 - Fiscal Policy/Deficit

Note: As noted in our tax section and throughout the note, current tax cuts are set to expire by the end of calendar year 2025. Hence, any policy in this area will impact the outlook in 2026 and beyond. Although the numbers we cite are over 10 years, we expect some degree of front-loading (see the section below for more detail) as some individual cuts are only extended for a few years, resulting in a \~\$370 billion first year deficit impact vs. \~\$160 billion as would be implied by an even division of the \$1.6 trillion over ten years.

1) Why there are policy differences across the outcomes: the parties' competing visions for tax cut extensions yield different deficit outcomes, and policy outcomes will depend on the composition of Congress following the election. We would expect a Republican sweep to produce something closer (but not identical) to a full Tax Cuts and Jobs Act extension, and a Democratic win to result in tax policy changes closer in line with the president's FY2025 budget request. Specifically, we expect a Democratic sweep scenario means some tax cuts are extended, but paired with tax increases on certain households and corporates. In a divided government outcome, we see a narrower extension of some TCJA provisions similar to what was agreed upon in the bipartisan Wyden-Smith tax bill. We describe our expectations for each scenario in Exhibit 10 and 11 (and in the tax policy primer section ).

2) Why there are macro (economic) differences across the outcomes: The largest direct impact to the deficit over the next ten years will be tied to tax policy. The current CBO baseline has all measures of the TCJA expiring at the end of the 2025 calendar year. If all measures do expire, it will create significant tailwinds to tax revenues (more details here). In all four scenarios we see either some or most of the TCJA measures being extended, and this would make the deficit larger relative to the case where there is a full roll-off of the TCJA. However, when compared with the CY25 baseline (which includes the current TCJA), anything short of a full extension represents a smaller contribution to the deficit than a continuation would imply. Our fiscal forecasts for 2024 and 2025, combined with the CBO projections into the medium term see government debt rising to the mid-130s by 2030 and then accelerating when the cost of debt fully reprices to market levels. As we highlight in recent work, the US is on an unsustainable fiscal path and austerity would be required to offset continued increases in the level of government debt. ## Combined Effects

The combined effects of the three policies on the economy are nuanced. While a reduction in immigration flows will have a negative impact on growth, the magnitude will depend on the size of the actual reduction in the numbers. The extent to which tariffs affect the real economy will depend on their breadth and scope. Growth would likely register some negative impacts from these two policies. Inflation could face some upside risk from the negative supply shock of lower immigration, but the effects will likely become evident only over time. In the meanwhile, the inflationary effects of tariffs could be more immediate. The effects of fiscal policies will only be evident in 2026, outside our current outlook horizon. However, as noted above, we see a diminishing fiscal impulse on economic activity in CY2024 and CY2025.

Hence, the overall magnitude of the impact is uncertain, but our general observation is that Democratic win scenarios have tighter bands of uncertainty regarding the translation from policy to growth. Policy choices under a Biden win scenario skew more predictable, as most represent a continuation of the status quo.

Republican win scenarios, in our view, come with wider bands of uncertainty regarding the translation from policy to growth and greater downside risk to GDP from those policies. In Trump win scenarios the potential impacts on the economy are noisy, with risks skewed to the downside for growth from tariff changes & restricted immigration. For example, there are direct revenue tailwinds from higher tariffs that would narrow the deficit, but there are also effects of tariffs on economic activity. An example of this are the estimates from Clausing and Lovely (Peterson Institute for International Economics, May 2024), which find that the Trump proposal for 10% tariffs on all imported goods and 50% on Chinese goods would at face value generate substantial revenues. They estimate that over a 10-year window, the tariffs could generate \$3.5 trillion in revenues at face value, but could be offset by more than \$800 billion in losses — net \$2.75 trillion in revenues (\$275 billion/year). However, these estimates do not account for effects that were in play during the 2018 tariffs, such as substitution among importing nations, which are subject to lower tariffs, retaliation by foreign importers on US goods and reduced economic growth.

Further, the impact on monetary policy will depend on the timing & magnitude of the policies enacted, some of which cut in opposite directions. For example, in a Republican sweep scenario, we see restricted immigration resulting in a negative supply shock (negative growth), but with likely upside for inflation. However, by 2026, while the Fed might be worried about the upside risk to inflation, the downside for growth might be more salient as the imposition of tariffs could also create a drag on GDP. Further complicating this dynamic, the TCJA extension, which would be most supportive of growth in a Republican sweep scenario in 2026, introduces another layer of uncertainty regarding Fed policy in 2026.

On the other hand, in a Democratic sweep, we see risks to the downside on growth from stricter immigration policy, but significantly less so than in a Republican sweep. As such, the inflationary effects will likely be small. However, letting some of the TCJA roll off could present a larger downside risk to growth in 2026 when compared with CY2025. This tilts monetary policy likely easier at that point due to the growth effects, but the timing and magnitude of these policies are uncertain.

## Fed: Independence Should Hold, but May Be Tested. Markets Would Face Risks before Economy

In a Biden win scenario, we expect status quo with respect to monetary policy principles of Fed independence. Chair Powell's term is up in 2026, but thus far Biden has not indicated whom he would appoint as a replacement. In any case, we expect a continuation of the status quo (i.e., emphasis on Fed independence and limited interaction from the political sphere).

In contrast, former President Trump's policy advisers have expressed an interest in the president having more influence over monetary policy. As we discussed in US Public Policy Brief: What If? Fed Independence & the US Election, we believe the risk of an overly permissive Fed is overstated. Specifically, institutional barriers prohibit the presidency from de facto controlling monetary policy, including the Senate & FOMC Chair selection & decision-making processes. However, even if that proves ultimately correct, a scenario in which the President's actions create the need for judicial interpretation could create confusion in financial markets. Attempted dismissal of FOMC members, for example, could lead to court cases in which the degree or structure of Fed independence is under consideration, confusing conventional understanding of the monetary policy reaction function.

Key Economic Impact Uncertainty: how would markets react to explorations of Fed structure?In the scenario where former President Trump is reelected in November, under the normal timeline, he would be able to appoint a new Chair of the Federal Reserve Board to take office in January 2026. Given the lags with which monetary policy works, the effects of policy rate changes on inflation and growth would only be realized in the economy in 2H26 at the earliest. Our current forecasts for the US economy cover through end-2025 and envision inflation in line with the Fed’s 2% goal. A change in Fed leadership that keeps rates lower than called for would imply inflation staying above target and possibly increasing in 2026 and beyond. In 2025, the Federal Reserve is slated to start another review of its monetary policy framework. The last review emphasized the asymmetry between shortfalls in employment and deviations in inflation. We do not expect the election to change this process. A sweep of both houses of Congress would be required for a more substantial change in the personnel of the Fed, because doing so would require a revision of the Federal Reserve Act. Such a process would be noticeable well in advance.

# Noisy: Market Behavior Into & Out of Elections

## Key Takeaways

• History shows that US elections are largely noise for key markets going into election day: Even if an investor has perfect foresight and know the results of the elections now, it won't necessarily give them an edge over the next 6 months.

• After election day, it's still noisy, but there's some evidence that expected policy choices start to matter. Changes to trade, fiscal, and tax policy may have been influential in prior post election periods.

## Ignore the Noise Going into an Election

There appears little evidence that markets follow a particular pattern in the 6 months going into an election. Consider the performance of the S&P 500 index during election cycles over the past \~100 years...

• May to November returns in an election year don't look that different from any other year.

• Whether a Democratic or Republican candidate eventually takes the White House doesn't change the trajectory of returns into an election.

• The form of the future elected government — whether it is divided or unified — also has never bothered stock markets before the vote.

• And you can see a similar pattern in UST bond yields, the dollar, and gold.

## Some Signal Potential Coming Out of an Election

As shown in Exhibit 13 to Exhibit 20 above, even when return profiles of stocks, bonds, USD, gold are similar across election outcomes heading into November, these performance trends among the asset classes relative to each other start to diverge after the results. This makes sense — once the elections are over, investors will have a much clearer view on what policies will likely be enacted, approaches to regulation, government spending, etc. Only then have key markets historically started to price in these policies.

A recent example of this policy-based market movement would be the risk asset rally that was ushered in following Trump's 2016 election, which was clearly a reflection of the market contemplating a policy agenda of de-regulation & lower corporate taxes. ## Some caveats...

• First, the economic conditions across these cycles are different — markets are likely going to be driven much more by macro in say, a recession year, than by election politics. Second, policies associated with the two parties — and investors' ranking of the importance of those policies — have arguably shifted over time, making inference even more difficult.

• But the main problem with extrapolating asset trends from prior election cycles is that there aren't that many observations. Data for S&P 500 going back to 1927 reaches the most far back among the major markets, but even then it only covers 23 presidential elections. Of those 23, only 5 could be classified as "closely contested," and only 3 saw both the House and Senate majorities change. It's hard — and probably imprudent — to draw strong conclusions from this.

• Key equity sectors have exposure to policy choices following the election. Opportunities exist in sectors where specific policy outcomes might amplify already positive stories. • Sector impact: According to our clean tech analyst Andrew Percoco, trimming usage of IRA credits or a partial repeal negatively impacts certain clean energy stocks, in particular green hydrogen, carbon capture, and onshore manufacturing. • Policy expectations: As noted, we expect a potential Republican administration to target the rollout of certain IRA clean tech tax credits by leveraging the Treasury's authorities to change guidance or limit application processing. In addition, in a unified Republican government it's possible Congress attempts targeted repeal of certain portions of the law with unanimous Republican opposition, though we believe full-scale repeal is unlikely.

• Is there a ceiling on how much of the IRA can be clawed back? According to a Brookings paper, the IRA could result in \$874 billion of spending in tax credits through 2035, in addition to another \$121 billion in direct spending. So far around \$65 billion of direct spending awards were announced, and the paper estimates \$111 billion of tax credits will be used through 2025. Thus, over 80% of IRA spending will likely be post-election. While we don’t expect a full-scale repeal of the IRA even in a Republican sweep, there are changes we think Republican lawmakers will pursue if given the chance. If we consider the removal/tightening of transport credits as an example, the Brookings data suggests this would lead to a \~\$315 billion reduction in fiscal spending by 2030.

## Noisy Rates Amid Noisy Election

Guneet Dhingra, Francesco Grechi, Eli Carter

For Treasuries, our election outcomes generally support a path to lower nominal yields, across outcomes. A Republican sweep is complicated, with potential for a higher term premium and divergence between lower real yields and higher breakevens as investors try to parse through the uncertainty of combined policy impacts on the US economic outlook. We see marginally higher yields, at least initially, under a Democrat sweep.

To detail our thinking, there are five major policy prongs on which US rates, and broadly macro markets, will turn: (1) immigration, (2) tariff and trade policy, (3) tax cuts and TCJA 2017, (4) composition and independence of the Fed, and (5) deregulation.

Last time we covered the presidential elections, a united vs. divided government framework was enough to think about the election impact on US rates and broadly macro markets. A united government came with higher prospects of fiscal spending and higher rates, and vice versa (see Exhibit 29 and 30 ). Not anymore.

The upcoming election will have significant dependence on executive power (i.e., the president) for many of the policies above — especially tariffs and immigration — making a united vs. divided framework insufficient. The role of the president could be possibly even more influential on the macro picture than the prospect of a united government, especially given the role of immigration in setting the macro narrative (see supply side curve ball).

Overall, as we detail below, it will be difficult to disentangle myriad combinations of fiscal policy paths and their resultant and complicated economic impacts for markets. We think the most straightforward impacts on market perception will be (1) risk of higher inflation and (2) higher volatility. We will consider trading for wider breakevens and long volatility expressions as we get closer to the elections.

## How rates investors may think about election — piece by piece:

Given the five policy prongs we identified above, below we summarize the range of outcomes markets will contend with, and how could markets react to each individual policy prong.

On immigration, while our public policy strategists expect some curb from the recent high levels of immigration in almost all scenarios — the range of possibilities remains wide spanning from a total stop on net immigration, i.e., deportation, to a continued significant expansion of the population base via continued inflows at the same high pace as recent years (see Exhibit 9 ).

Our economics team sees the deportation scenario resulting in a clear deceleration of growth while also having some effect in raising inflation. The downward effect on growth is more clearly, while the effect on inflation would depend on companies' ability to pass on higher labor costs to final products.

We think markets could react to this outcome by pricing in wider breakevens and lower real yields, with the net effect being lower nominal yields — assuming inflation has cooled by the time election results are announced (our base case), and the market is more sensitive to growth by then.

On the other extreme, if immigration continues at its recent pace we could see a continuation of cooling inflation and optically strong growth — we think markets could react to this scenario with a shallow path for insurance cuts — similar to what's priced currently.

Ultimately, how monetary policy shapes up will depend in large part on how immigration, tariffs and tax policy will shape up — and Fed policy is likely to provide a commensurate response.

It remains challenging to suss whether markets will also assume the Fed acts independently or build in some political bias to the Fed's actions. Our views in this piece assume that the Fed acts independently, and only considers economic outcomes.

If the combined mix in policy of lets say immigration, tariffs and tax cuts has stoked inflation concerns in markets — the Fed could be the offsetting force by being more hawkish. On the other hand, if the downside growth impacts are seen dominant, the Fed would be expected to be more dovish. Market pricing would, of course, be in anticipation of what an independent Fed would do.

For markets this would be a challenge to price — not only because markets will need to assess the offsetting growth and inflation impacts, but because they will also have to price the timing of the Fed's response — i.e., whether the Fed responds to these scenarios pro-actively or waits to see these effects play out.

On deregulation, investors could assume that President Trump will provide a deregulation tailwind to growth, another aspect of policy that is more dependent on executive power than Congress. ## Political Considerations and the Federal Reserve

We expect the Fed's independence to continue under either a Trump or Biden presidency. However, markets may anticipate that the Trump campaign's public criticism of the Fed may raise the likelihood of a more dovish FOMC chair under a potential second Trump administration, weighing on US yields and weakening USD on the margin depending on polling.

We think that it is useful to draw a distinction between 1) government actions that could have dovish implications for Fed policy (the implications of which are potentially limited to a weaker USD), and 2) government actions that could undermine Fed independence.

On Fed policy, the president can appoint (with Senate confirmation) committee members that support the independence of the Fed but also may favor lower interest rates.

While there may be challenges in convincing the entirety of the FOMC of the appropriateness of embarking on a more dovish monetary policy path, markets could reasonably assume a heightened risk of it occurring, alongside more dissent and communication challenges for the chair.

Higher inflation expectations, lower real rates, and a weaker USD would be a market response consistent with this outcome, at least initially. Our analysis here suggests that presidential tweets between 2018 and 2020 criticizing the Federal Reserve weakened the DXY index by a cumulative 0.6-1%.

On Fed independence, we expect the Fed to remain a credible, independent agency — as we argue here. Legislative reform of the Fed is unlikely to be part of a plausible policy agenda, even if there is a Republican sweep. It would also be challenging for a president to appoint enough people to the Fed Board that would simply follow political orders in possible contradiction of the Fed's statutory mandate. ## Fiscal Policy

Our economics and public policy colleagues note that a Republican sweep would likely be the most fiscally expansionary outcome, while a Democratic sweep would likely be the least fiscally expansionary outcome. As a result, the growth and spending implications of a Trump administration fiscal agenda might support the USD, while a Democratic sweep might weigh on the USD.

Investors have increasingly focused on risks that a significant fiscal expansion (which could put upward pressure on the US debt-to-GDP and debt service-to-tax revenue ratio) may act as a USD-negative catalyst (as was the case for GBP during October 2022). This thesis argues that rising debt-to-GDP could negatively impact market confidence in the US government's credit profile, leading foreign investors (including central bank reserve managers) to increasingly diversify away from USD and UST.

However, we do not expect investors to seriously question the US government's credit profile, even in a potentially fiscally expansionary administration. As we discuss here, debt-to-GDP fell from 130% in 2020 to around 122% in 2023 as nominal growth accelerated while fiscal deficits narrowed. Our debt sustainability analysis suggests that nominal growth is likely to remain above debt service costs for several years. And relative to other developed economies, the US debt situation is not an outlier. In our view, there are no realistic alternatives to a highly influential international role for USD.

## FX Volatility

Realized FX volatility rose between November 3, 2020 (the day the presidential election was held) and November 9 (the day Biden was projected to win the election). During that period, the possibility of a contested election outcome clouded the US political, regulatory, and trade policy outlook. Elevated currency volatility reflected this uncertainty. We think policy uncertainty (and therefore FX volatility) may return during this cycle in a contested outcome or days-long delay in result delivery later this year.

## From a Cross-Asset Perspective, the 'Election Playbook' Looks a Lot Like Our Mid-Year Outlook

With eventual election outcomes being mostly noise, we believe investors should just stick to the macro and fundamental drivers. Our economists see US real growth at a robust 2.1% this year and next; inflation slows to near the target after the noisy data of 1Q24, and Fed rate cuts begin in September, with fed funds 75bps lower by year-end. Good macro, fair fundamentals, valuations, and technicals, rate cut cycles… they all make for a great environment for risk assets, a reason we went overweight in global equities in our asset allocation recommendations in our latest strategy mid-year outlook.

We are bullish, but cautiously bullish. Because there's unusually low visibility in 1H25. Yes, the US general elections is a key risk event that could lead to a very wide range of asset performance beyond year-end 2024, but it is not the only one. How inflation trends and the Fed path remains uncertain. China recovery remains uncertain. Geopolitical risks remain uncertain. This explains why our strategists' latest forecasts have an unusually wide gap between their bull and bear cases for bond and equity markets. And also why even if this were not an election year, our advice would be the same — look for assets which tick the "triple C" boxes of carry, convexity, and cheap optionality. Be long European and Japanese equities. Go overweight in spread products in fixed income. Ignore the noise going into the US general elections.

## Pass the SALT?

One of the most politically contentious provisions within the original TCJA, we expect a debate to return to Capitol Hill regarding the future of the SALT cap deduction.

The background: Placing a cap on the total deduction a taxpayer could claim between state & local taxes (SALT) & federal taxes proved to be a helpful revenue raiser in the initial TCJA back in 2017. However, since its inception, moderate lawmakers in high-tax states like New York and California have pushed back against the cap's introduction, arguing that it disproportionately hurts their constituents.

The options: lawmakers have several options for the SALT cap (which expires in 2026) — among them, letting it expire, extending the cap as is (\$10,000), modifying it higher (\$15,000) or changing income requirements (i.e., increasing the threshold for those subject to the cap. Each one yields a different amount of savings/revenue to offset potential tax cut extensions.

The path forward: our Republican sweep scenario includes an extension of the SALT cap only assuming a relatively large margin of control in both houses of Congress. In that scenario, we expect that the need for offsets to limit the deficit impact of the bill will outweigh blue state Republicans from states like New York who might oppose the cap. We expect the cap comes back in line with the 2017 model (i.e., \$10,000, and at the same income levels) but acknowledge this assumption could be flexed to assume slightly lower revenues. In a slimmer Republican control scenario, we expect that the SALT caucus (those lawmakers that oppose the cap) will prevail, meaning that the cap will roll off. We anticipate that because the majority will likely be built on wins in traditionally Democratic seats (i.e., seats in New York flipping to Republican control), meaning that for those vulnerable lawmakers to buy in to the whole bill it likely will have to be without a SALT cap extension.

• No consensus on post-election market impacts

• Little consensus on post-election policy outcomes

## 1) There doesn't appear to be consensus on answers to any of our meaningful questions

Especially in sweep scenarios, market uncertainty is evident on the various outcomes. Expectations for various market targets (S&P, USD, UST10y) are relatively evenly split among categories for sweep scenarios, indicating that there is no investor consensus on how markets should/will trade in any given outcome.

Investors expect equities will see the largest upside in a Trump win + Republican government, but in general lack consensus in either sweep scenario. 13% of our respondents expect S&P >+10%, while investors expect equities sell off most in a Democratic sweep outcome. The connection between policy expectations and market expectations is clear here: investors are less worried about taxes & fiscal in a Trump win but more concerned on those two topics in a Biden win and therefore expect equities slightly down/flat.

What the election could mean for immigration: Although immigration tends to be one of the most difficult policy areas to enact legislative change, we expect that these significant levels of voter dissatisfaction with the current state of play as well as both candidates' emphasis on the importance of the issue could provide an incentive for lawmakers to seek policy reform. Similarly, we believe it's plausible that both candidates would seek to utilize executive authorities in this area to specifically address the southern border of the United States, which has accounted for a large share of the increase in FY2023.

We note that there is scope for executive authority with respect to immigration policy, although several executive actions that were attempted during former President Trump's first term were challenged or blocked by the courts, including limiting entry points for asylum seekers and revoking temporary protected status for certain classes of migrants. Media reports indicate the former president is seeking to employ other avenues as well, which could include actions like Sec. 1182f of US code, which allows the president to "suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or non-immigrants" if entry would be "detrimental to the interests of the United States." President Trump utilized this authority in his first term, and although challenged, it was ultimately upheld by the Supreme Court, which interpreted the Section 1182f authority as granting the president broad power to suspend entry of migrants into the country.

For that reason, we expect there are potential avenues for executive action that could result in lower rates of immigration in a potential second Trump term, even if challenged later by courts. Given substantial policy uncertainty, the magnitude or impact of these policies is difficult to assess, but we expect that overall flows would be limited by the implementation of executive actions taken in conjunction with possible concurrent legislative routes.

We believe a more significant and durable change to immigration levels would have to be legislated through Congress, noting that potential policy in this area could resemble the bipartisan border compromise negotiated earlier this year. This includes several provisions in the list below, including asylum changes, border emergency authority, and changes to the visa application system. Other possible policy routes are also listed below:

• Asylum overhaul: The bipartisan bill includes a proposal to 1) create a new, stricter standard for asylum seekers and 2) establish a new process for migrants claiming asylum. We note that the president could also — through the attorney general and DHS — place limits on asylum.

• Border emergency authority: The bipartisan compromise establishes a threshold based on the average number of migrant encounters, above which most new migrants entering the country illegally will automatically be removed (more detail below).

• Visa changes: Lawmakers could increase or decrease the number of visas per year for five years.

• Agencies (executive branch) could issue rules and regulations that impact the way immigration law is administered or not carried out, and courts can uphold this power (U.S. v. Texas, 2023).

• The president can issue executive orders that instruct the agencies, and therefore can result in significant policy shifts, for example, the Deferred Action of Childhood Arrivals (DACA) during former President Obama's term. Note that given the authority underpinning executive orders, they can be reversed in subsequent administrations.

Directionally, in line with the CBO, we expect lower net immigration over the next few years in either election outcome, but we expect lower net migration numbers in a Trump win scenario than a Biden win scenario, with key uncertainties depending on the makeup of Congress. In the normalized scenario immigration is in line with the pre-COVID average of 800k, with 0.6%Y population growth and breakevens similar to pre-Covid at 87k/ month. In the deportation scenario net immigration is at zero, caused by low immigration and a rise in emigration (likely due to stricter deportation policies). The population growth is entirely driven by the native-born population, at 0.2%Y, and breakeven payrolls at 45k.

In any post-election outcome, we see stricter immigration policy as highly likely but the extent of changes to immigration policies and enforcement is open to question. ## Trade policy/tariff takeaways

• Our survey results suggest it may be difficult for Biden to "out-hawk" Trump on China policy … 52% of respondents to our survey think Trump is more likely to increase non-tariff barriers vs. 12% for Biden. While the evidence shows that both have increased non-tariff barriers, only 27% of respondents said both were equally likely. Biden has increased tariffs and non tariff barriers over the past fou years, but these actions do not appear to be reflected in the survey data. Interestingly, nearly half of respondents do not expect Biden to roll back China tariffs, meaning that "competitive confrontation" is well understood.

• But a greater share expect Biden to enact tariffs on China relative to other geographies: Respondents indicated that Trump was more likely across the board to increase tariffs (in particular, we asked about the EU, Mexico, and China). However, a greater share of investors believe both are equally likely to enact tariffs on China relative to other geographies.
