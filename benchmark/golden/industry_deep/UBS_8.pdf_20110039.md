# China Economic Perspectives Home Destocking Debate and Property Forecast Downgrade

## Where are we now in the property downturn?

China’s property activities have not bottomed since the unprecedented sharp downturn in 2021. The decline in urban housing prices has accelerated since H2 2023 amid elevated inventory pressure, weighing on household home purchase intention, consumption confidence and local government finances. China has eased property policies since end-2022 and rolled out coordinated easing measures in mid-May 2024. However, traditional easing measures have had a limited impact, as supply-demand fundamentals have changed notably and property policy easing has been piecemeal. In particular, the progress of property inventory purchase has been slow so far.

## How large is the inventory pressure and potential destocking cost?

We use 'available for sale but unsold' residential projects as an indicator of inventory levels. As of July 2024, this figure was estimated at 19mn units, including 3.8mn units 'completed but unsold' and another 15.1mn units 'uncompleted but available for presales' homes. Both our top-down estimate and bottom-up data for 80 major cities show the seasonally adjusted inventory to sales ratio has jumped notably to 29-30 months lately, mainly on sharply lower property sales. We think the destocking priority should focus on higher-tier cities, and estimate the total destocking cost required to normalise the inventory to sales ratio in 80 major cities to 13-14 months at around Rmb3trn.

## Why the progress of home destocking has been slow?

There is a substantial gap between the market housing price and social housing price (or cost-based price cap), the latter significantly related to the proposed inventory purchase price in many cities. In addition, the PBC's relending facility faces a variety of bottlenecks, including high interest cost, short duration and small funding size. The price gap and these bottlenecks make the current destocking programme less feasible and more difficult to implement. We think the PBC needs to expand the scale, lower the interest rate, and extend the duration of its social housing relending. An aggressive option could be using special CGB, special LGB or policy banks bonds to buy inventory.

## What more can be done to stabilise housing market and facilitate destocking?

We think China may need to implement much stronger property policy support to stabilize the housing market, such as establishing a much larger scale destocking program (with low-cost fundings), more aggressive mortgage rate cuts, more credit support to developers, and government-coordinated property debt restructuring. Nevertheless, our baseline forecast assumes additional modest property support, including further cuts in mortgage rates and the downpayment requirement, expansion of whitelist loans, and stronger support of developers’ financing. We also expect China to raise the size of effective funding support for destocking to Rmb1.5-2trn, which could contribute 1.2-1.7mn units of inventory destocking in higher-tier cities.

## Lower 2024-2025 forecast of property activities

Our baseline forecasts a continued but 'less bad' property downturn in 2025E compared with 2024E, with property sales, new starts and investment falling another 5-10%, 10- 15% and 5-10%, respectively, all weaker than our previous projections. Overall residential sale volume may fall further in 2026E with lingering inventory pressure. We think bigger policy actions are needed but may only be triggered by much deeper property downturn and a significant external shock such as a 60% US tariff hike.

## Where are we now in the property downturn?

Property activities have not bottomed after a multi-year sharp decline. Since 2021, China has experienced the sharpest property downturn in history. Overall property sales volume declined by 51% in Q2 2024 from H1 2021, while new starts have contracted by a much steeper 69% from H220, and real estate development investment has fallen by 34% from H2 2020. That said, even after such a sharp decline and recent acceleration in property policy easing, especially those announced in mid-May (see more details), property activities have not bottomed. Overall property sales, new starts and investment in July 2024 all continued to register deep YoY decline of 15%, 20% and 11%, respectively, with their seasonally adjusted absolute levels all below Q2 average (see July data comment). High frequency data also shows that new residential sales in 30 major cities only saw a short-lived rebound in late June and early July, but slid again afterwards, recording a deeper contraction of over 20% YoY in August (vs a 16% YoY decline in July, see UBS China Activity Tracker).

Importance of property sector to China’s economy has fallen notably. Based on China’s Input-Output Table, we estimate that the property sector accounted for more than 26% of China’s GDP around 2020, including its direct impact (real estate service and property construction) and spillover effects via supply chain links (see more discussion). With the sharp property downturn since 2021, we estimate that the property sector's importance to GDP may fall to around 20-21% in 2024E. Moreover, we estimate that the contribution of the property sector to GDP growth dropped sharply from 3.6ppt per year during 2001-10 and 1.5ppt per year during 2011-2020, to a significant drag of more than 3.5ppt in 2022 and 1.5-2.0ppt in 2023. Given the property downturn has deepened so far this year, we think this drag may increase to more than 2.5ppt in 2024. We think this remains the main headwind to China’s GDP growth.

… weighing on household consumption and local government finances. Weaker property activities directly translate into weaker employment and household income growth, and lead to softer sales of home appliances, furniture and related goods. Moreover, we estimate that around 60% of China’s household wealth is in property, and falling property prices have weighed on household wealth and consumer confidence significantly, which has been a major drag on China’s post-pandemic consumption recovery. In addition, the continued property downturn and housing price decline have also depressed local government land sales revenue from Rmb8.5trn in 2021 to Rmb5.8trn in 2023, with another 22% YoY decline posted in Jan-Jul 2024. Local government financing vehicles (LGFVs) are facing balance sheet problems as well, as they relied on property and land as collateral and/or revenue. This gives homeowners in China more 'holding power' on their property than those in other countries, with less risk of foreclosures and fire sales.

## How has China eased policies to support housing market?

China rolled out coordinated property policy easing in mid-May. China has eased property policies since end-2022, including cutting the minimum downpayment requirement and mortgage rates on a differentiated basis, relaxing home purchase restrictions, rolling out a whitelist scheme to support construction of stalled projects. Given the continued sharp property downturn in early 2024, the government announced further coordinated property easing measures on 17 May (see Figure 5 and our report), indicating the top leadership’s determination to stabilise the housing market. Many high-tier cities, including all four tier-1 cities (see Figure 6), rolled out follow-up easing measures. There are three key pillars to ongoing property policy easing: boosting home purchase demand, supporting home delivery and developer financing, and destocking property inventory.

Cut downpayment and mortgage rates and ease HPR to boost housing demand. The government announced on May 17 to lower the minimum downpayment requirement nationwide by another 5ppt to 15% for first-home buyers and 25% for second-home buyers, both record low. Most Chinese cities have brought down the downpayment ratio to 15% and 25%, respectively, while most tier-1 cities have set a slightly higher ratio. The minimum mortgage rate requirements were removed nationwide for both first home (previously LPR-20bp) and second home purchase (previously LPR+20bp). We expect the average new mortgage rate to drop further from 3.69%/3.45% in Q1/Q224 to 3.0-3.2% in coming quarters. Three tier-1 cities continued to retain the requirements but at lower levels. In addition, most tier-2 and lower cities have already removed all home purchase restrictions (HPR). All tier-1 cities have eased HPR somewhat in the past several months (eg, lowering the requirements of qualified homebuyers), although some form of restrictions still exist.

The impact of traditional property policy easing measures has been limited. One important reason why policies have had a limited impact is that China’s fundamental property demand has weakened notably in the past decade, as urbanisation (annual increase of urban population) slowed sharply from 23mn during 2011-20 to only 10mn during 2021-23, new baby birth declined from 16mn to 10mn, and new marriage registration dropped from 11mn to 7mn. Although upgrading and replacement demand remains meaningful in the long run, given the large scale of existing urban housing stock (29.5bn sqm in the 2020 census), the related home purchase intention has been dragged by soft post-Covid income growth, prolonged housing price decline amid elevated inventory pressure, and concerns about home delivery (see China housing survey). Meanwhile, China’s urban home ownership is already high (96% in 2019 PBC household survey, 80-90% in UBS China housing surveys), urban household debt has increased notably with the debt to disposable income ratio rising to over 100%, and China has already provided 64mn units of various types of social housing as of 2023 (including 34mn units in shanty town renovations), which means there is now less room for China to revive the housing market and home sales. Moreover, property policy easing measures in the past two years have been rolled out in a piecemeal way that has been unable to stabilise market sentiment and housing price expectations.

'Whitelist' scheme to support stalled projects and home delivery. The government has prioritised ensuring home delivery of stalled projects as the top task of property policy easing since end-2022. UBS estimated that stalled projects may have jumped to 745mn sqm in December 2023 (see more details), which requires Rmb1.5trn for project completion (to fund 50% of overall construction cost). Regulators introduced the 'whitelist' scheme in January 2024 to encourage banks to issue loans to support qualified stalled projects (only for those already pre-sold but uncompleted). Official data shows that banks have approved loan credit line of Rmb1.4trn as of August 2024 (vs Rmb935bn as of mid-May and Rmb520bn as of March). That said, given the continued property downturn in 2024, we think the size of stalled projects and required credit support may have increased further from end-2023, as evidenced by the sharp 22%YoY decline in residential completions YTD. The actual issuance of whitelist loans may have been slow relative to the approved credit line, as developer loan outstanding increased by Rmb400bn from early 2024 and loan issuance has reportedly favoured more SOE developers’ projects on banks’ risk control considerations. Also those uncompleted and unsold projects are not covered by the whitelist scheme. We foresee further expansion of whitelist scheme to cover more stalled projects and faster issuance of whitelist loans over the rest of 2024. More concrete progress in the implementation of whitelist scheme should help support the construction of stalled projects and stabilize homebuyers’ confidence in the coming year.

Support qualified developer financing through 'operating loans'. The government has been reluctant to rescue developers during this property downturn, given its concerns about moral hazard risk and commitment to the deleveraging of developers. But given massive defaults among private developers in the past 2-3 years and the significant spillover effects, the government started to call for reasonable funding support for all types of developers since end-2023. Regulators introduced an 'operating loans' scheme in January 2024, allowing qualified developers to borrow from banks using their commercial assets as collaterals. In May, Vanke, a much-watched major developer with mounting debt repayment pressures, finally secured the first batch of Rmb20bn operating loans to repay its maturing debt (total Rmb60-70bn in 2024), making it the first and only known leading non-SOE developer to have received largescale bank credit support. This may be a meaningful break from previous policy setting of supporting stalled projects only, although it also requires developers to engage in proactive asset disposals and ultimately property sales stabiliation.

Local governments started to buy existing home inventory. The State Council urged local governments in May to withdraw or buy back unused idle residential land to ease developers’ financing condition. More importantly, it asked local governments (in areas with high inventory pressure) to buy existing home inventory for social housing purposes. Some local governments have announced their pilot plans of home inventory purchase before and after 17 May. As a start, the PBC set up a Rmb300bn relending facility to support banks providing Rmb500bn credit support to local SOEs, in order to purchase completed but unsold commodity housing, which will be converted to social housing (for sales or for rental).

However, the progress of inventory purchasing has been slow so far. Macro data and on-the-ground evidence both suggest the home inventory purchasing programme has made slow progress in the past three months, relative to the government's repeated emphasis of the policy. As a result, inventory pressure has remained elevated and continues to weigh on housing prices in most Chinese cities. As at end Q2 2024, only Rmb12bn or 4% of the PBC’s total Rmb300bn relending facility for social housing has been utilised. Over 13 cities have announced details of their inventory purchase programmes, but implementation has been slow (see UBS property sector report). Meanwhile, in some cities, such as Zhuhai, the majority of completed inventory purchases have been from SOE developers rather than private developers, so the latter has not seen much alleviation of inventory pressure.

In the following sections, we will discuss how large is the property inventory pressure, what are the key bottlenecks restricting the progress of inventory purchasing, and what more can be done to facilitate inventory purchasing and stabilise housing market.

## How large is the property inventory pressure?

Three measures of property inventory. There are no perfect property inventory statistical measures in China. 1) The first available indicator is a narrowly defined official statistics of 'completed but unsold' projects, which does not include those projects are already available for pre-sale but not completed. This indicator is exactly the government’s key focus in its ongoing campaign of property destocking. 2) The second indicator is a more representative one of 'available for sale but unsold' projects, including both the first indicator (completed but unsold projects) and those available for pre-sale but not completed projects. However, there is no official statistics for the second indicator. We need to either use city-level market data as a proxy or make our own estimate from available official statistics. 3) The third indicator is a more broadly defined one, covering all 'under-construction but unsold' projects, including both the second indicator and those are under construction but not available for pre-sale.

China’s existing residential inventory – how large is it in absolute terms? Firstly, official statistics show that 'completed but unsold' residential inventory totalled 382m sqm as July 2024 (or around 3.8m units), a 22% increase from a year ago. Secondly, based on official statistics of property activities and our assumptions of residential project lifecycle, we estimate that China’s overall 'available for sale but unsold' residential inventory likely reached 1.9bn sqm as July 2024 (or around 19mn units), including the above 3.8m units of “completed but unsold” projects and another 15.1mn units of 'uncompleted but available for pre-sales'. It is worth noting the pre-sale share of total residential sales has declined substantially from its peak of over 90% in 2020-21 to 70-75% in recent months, given the homebuyers’ deep concern over stalled projects and home delivery. More broadly speaking, we estimate there are another 1.8bn sqm (or around 18mn units) of 'uncompleted and unavailable for pre-sales projects under construction, which will potentially become residential supply in the coming one or two years.

How large is the property inventory destocking pressure? Although the government’s inventory purchase program mainly focused on the “completed but unsold” residential inventory, we think the 'available for sale but unsold' projects is a more representative indicator reflecting the overall situation of China’s residential inventory pressure, given still-high recent pre-sales ratio of 70-75%. There are two sets of data in the 'available for sales but unsold' projects.

One set of data is city-level housing market data across China’s 80 major cities (including 35 higher-tier and 45 lower-tier cities), which accounted for 25- 30% of China’s residential sales volume in the past two years. The latest data shows that total 'available for sale but unsold' projects in 80 cities reached 476mn sqm (or 4.8mn units) in July 2024. While the figure is marginally lower compared with 2023, the inventory-to-sales ratio (seasonally adjusted) has jumped sharply to around 29 months due to sliding property sales, more than double a 'normal' level of 13-14 months (the 2010-2021 average).

The other set is our own estimate above for China’s overall inventory of 1.9bn sqm (19mn units) in July 2024, which also edged down in the past several months from its peak in H2 2023. However, a sharp decline in property sales pushed up the inventory to sales ratio notably to 29-30 months (seasonally adjusted), much higher than a 'normal' of 16-17 months (the 2010-2021 average). Our top-down estimate of the nationwide property inventory to sales ratio shows a strong correlation with the bottom-up 80-city market data, with lower-tier cities usually suffering a higher inventory-to-sales ratio.

And how much may destocking cost? Here we assume that: 1) China needs to bring down the residential inventory-to-sales ratio of those 'available for sales but unsold' projects from the current elevated level to the 2010-2021 average 'normal' level achieved before the property downturn; 2) future underlying property sales volume may stay largely stable at the current pace; and 3) local government may purchase residential inventory at around a 20% discount to the current market price for future social housing purposes. The total cost of inventory purchases will be Rmb3trn in 80 major cities. We also estimate that the theoretically overall nationwide destocking cost at around Rmb7trn (including Rmb3trn cost for 80 major cities), with the same assumptions as above.

Nevertheless, the priority of property inventory destocking should focus on higher-tier cities in the early stage (for instance here 80 major cities) in our view, given their relatively less bad fundamental and more sizable social housing demand than lower-tier cities. If the housing market in higher-tier cities could stabilise gradually, it may have a positive spillover effect on housing price expectations and market confidence to lower tier cities, which may eventually help inventory destocking there. In addition, some existing inventory in lower-tier cities may have been obsolete and need to be written-off, which cannot be effectively and easily digested.

## Why has property inventory destocking been slow?

What are the key factors affecting inventory purchase? As we discussed earlier, the progress of property inventory purchases has been slow in the past three months, relative to the government's repeated emphasis of the policy and previous market expectations. There are several key concerns or obstacles from local governments that may have been dragged the progress of property destocking. Inventory purchase programmes are set to be implemented on a market-oriented basis, while local governments have no intention or capability to hold large-scale property inventory for a long time. Instead, property inventory purchase is designed mainly for social housing purposes, either for sale or for rental. Local officials will likely try hard to avoid paper losses during the inventory purchase and management.

Overall, there is substantial gap between the market housing price and social housing price (or the cost-based price cap), the latter of which is significantly related to the proposed inventory purchase price in many cities. In addition, the current funding mechanism – the PBC relending facility – faces various bottlenecks, including high interest costs, short duration, and small funding size, relative to what are really needed from the property inventory purchase programmes. The price gap and these bottlenecks make the inventory purchase programme less feasible and harder to implement, given local officials are accountable for implementing property destocking without losses.

Substantial gap between inventory purchase price and housing market prices. Inventory purchase prices proposed by various local governments are usually linked with comparable social housing prices (resettlement cost) or capped at a cost-based low price (such as: basic land cost + construction cost + 5% profit), which are usually 30-50% lower than commodity housing market prices. In addition, some large cities explicitly set the ceiling of social housing rental at 60%/75% (if managed by public entities, eg Shenzhen and Chengdu) or 90% (if managed by commercial entities) of comparable rental market prices. Therefore, the inventory purchase price for social rental housing purpose should also be lower than market prices to justify lower public rental prices. This large price gap likely reduces many developers’ intention to participate in the destocking program, as accepting such large price discounts could imply that their balance sheets are severely distressed and cash flows are extremely tight.

PBC relending facility is hampered by high interest cost, short duration and small size. The planned Rmb500bn bank loans (backed by PBC’s Rmb300bn relending) is too small relative to the total funding needed to address property inventory issue (for instance, Rmb3trn for destocking in 80 major cities), as we have discussed above.

Meanwhile, the PBC’s relending facility has a one-year tenor and can be rolled over four times by up to five years, which is also much shorter than the regular lifecycle of social housing rental projects. Therefore, local governments may need to repay their bank loans early either by selling the purchased inventory rapidly, using government revenue, or through the securitisation of social housing rental programmes. In addition, the actual lending cost for most local SOE borrowers may be around 3%, which is 1.75% interest rate of PBC relending facility plus a mark-up to cover banks’ operation cost and credit risk. Compared with an 2.3% average market rental yield in 50 major cities (1.7% in tier-1 cities) and even lower rental yield for social housing, a 3% borrowing cost looks too high to justify long-term operation of social housing rental projects (see more).

## What more can be done?

Further lower downpayment requirement and mortgage rates. China can lower minimum downpayment requirements and mortgage rates more from the already record-low levels, especially in tier-1 cities where minimum downpayments and mortgage rates are still higher than other cities. In addition, interest rates on existing mortgage continue to be much higher (by 50-100bp) than on new mortgages (3.45% in Q2 2024), even after the 73bp cut on Rmb22trn of existing mortgage loans in Q3 2023 (leading to a saving of Rmb160-170bn in annual interest expenses for households). The government could make another round of re-pricing of existing mortgage rate to alleviate homebuyers’ interest burden. It should help improve households’ cash flow and consumption power, but its impact on the housing market may be marginal.

Expand the 'whitelist' scheme and support more developer financing. Banks have already granted a Rmb1.4trn credit line for qualified whitelist projects as of August 2024. That said, the actual issuance of whitelist loan seems slow, and the coverage of the whitelist scheme is still insufficient. We think the government could urge banks to issue whitelist loans more rapidly from the already approved credit line, further expand the coverage of the whitelist scheme to more qualified non-SOE projects, increase the approved credit line significantly, and grant some regulatory forbearance to whitelistrelated loans. Moreover, the government may consider providing additional credit support to those stalled projects outside of the whitelist scheme, namely uncompleted and unsold projects that have already started construction substantially but face cash flow difficulties. The government could also send a stronger and clearer signal of credit support to developers to stabilise market sentiment and contain the negative spillover effect. In addition, the government can play a more active role in coordinating property debt restructuring involving developers, suppliers, banks and local governments.

Expand scale, lower interest rate, and extend duration of PBC social housing relending … We think the PBC could triple or quadruple the Rmb300bn relending facility, which may help underpin more than Rmb1.5-2trn of bank lending to support inventory purchasing programmes. Meanwhile, the PBC could allow banks to roll over the relending facility from the current four years to 8-10 years or longer. More importantly, the actual interest cost of bank loans for inventory purchases could be lowered by 100-150bp or more from the current level of around 3% to make the destocking for social housing rental purposes more feasible and implementable. Although the PBC may be reluctant to lower its relending rate directly, we think the Ministry of Finance could help bring down the actual lending cost by subsidising banks’ interest payments to the PBC, as the it did with policy banks’ special construction bond issuance during 2015-2016.

… to facilitate a meaningful destocking. If the bank loans of Rmb1.5-2trn is fully utilised to purchase 'completed but unsold' projects only (at a decent discount to market housing prices) as the government now plans, 48-64% (or 1.8-2.4m units) of all existing 'completed but unsold' inventory in China could be digested. Alternatively, for destocking of 'available for sales but unsold' projects in higher-tier cities only, Rmb1.5- 2trn bank loans could purchase 1.2-1.7mn units of inventory in 80 major cities, which is equivalent to 16-21% of China’s overall residential sales volume in 2024 or 26-35% of existing inventory in 80 major cities. This could help bring down inventory to sales in higher-tier cities from 29 months now to around 20 months, assuming largely stable overall property sales. That said, these policy changes and destocking programmes may take several years to proceed.

An aggressive option: using special CGB, special LGB or policy banks bonds to buy inventory. Ongoing market-oriented destocking programmes are required to be implemented without incurring losses at the local government level, which should have substantial discounts on inventory purchase prices and be supported by low-cost longterm stable funding. All of this, in our view, needs quite a lot more policy breakthroughs. If property activities and housing prices come in much weaker than expected, an aggressive option to achieve rapid and efficient property destocking could be that the government use its balance sheet or policy banks’ credit to buy property inventory directly, both of which could benefit from longer duration and lower interest rates than the current mechanism. Bloomberg reported recently that China is mulling using special LGB to purchase inventory, while we think special CGB and policy banks’ bonds could also be potential sources of fundings if needed. The government could roll out a much large destocking program, perhaps establishing special entities for doing so to normalize property / sales ratio in 80 major cities. The main challenges to using this option are how to contain moral hazard risk and manage government debt risk. The government could issue top-down guidance to clarify the details and principals of the inventory purchasing programmes.

## Downgrade 2024-2025 forecast of property activities

Our baseline forecast assumes that the government will roll out modest additional property support to stabilise the housing market. We expect further lowering of new mortgage rates by up to 50bp, another 5ppt cut of downpayment requirement, more relaxation of HPR in tier-1 cities, wider coverage and faster issuance of whitelist loans, some additional support on qualified developers’ financing.

More importantly, we expect the government to increase the size of effective funding support for property inventory purchase to around Rmb1.5-2trn, either via the PBC’s existing relending facilities with longer duration and lower interest rates (with direct rate cut or subsidies on interest), or via funding from government bonds or policy bank bonds, or both. We think the government may focus on destocking in higher-tier cities. Then the full utilisation of Rmb1.5-2trn funding could contribute 1.2-1.7mn units of inventory destocking and home sales in higher-tier cities. If we assume such destocking is implemented in the coming two years (2025-2026), it could contribute to 8-11% per year of China’s overall residential sales volume in 2025-2026. This could help stabilise overall residential sales volume at low levels in the coming quarters.

Lower 2024-2025 forecast of property activities. As we highlighted in our July data report, given the year-to-date weakness of property activities in 2024 and the still subdued momentum in the property market, we revised down our full-year 2024 forecast of property sales to a decline of 15-20%, new starts to a decline of 20-25%, and property investment to a decline of 8-10%. For 2025, we think property new starts may continue to decline by another 10-15%. Property sales may decline by a further 5- 10% in 2025E. Real-estate investment may decline by another 5-10% in 2025E. Our new forecast of property activities in 2025 are weaker than our previous forecast but less bad than our forecasts for 2024. If future property easing and destocking progress come in weaker than expected, the decline in property activities in 2025 may be deeper than our baseline forecast.

What could trigger bigger policy actions? Given our baseline forecast of property activities in 2025 and inventory purchase progress, overall residential sale volume may continue to decline in 2026 from 2025, mainly due to previous sharp drop in new starts. Property inventory / sales ratio may not fall to historical normal level by end-2025. To facilitate a faster rebalancing of the housing market, we think China may need to implement much strong property policy support, such as a much larger scale destocking program (with a fund size of Rmb3-4 trillion, which could be funded by the long-term low-cost issuance of government bonds or policy banks’ bonds), more aggressively cut of mortgage rates and coordinated property debt restructuring. However, we think those may only be triggered by deeper downturns in the property market and a significant external shock such as a 60% US tariff hike.
