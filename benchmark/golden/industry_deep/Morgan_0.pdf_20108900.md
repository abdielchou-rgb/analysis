## Executive Summary

![](images/bc8f56e34d3f109c50e4889ba453fa4b2838e4d4b0004c22ab8c5f088e0e1041.jpg)

Leveraging the expertise of our global analysts across asset classes and regions, our mid-year report summarizes the macro and market outlook for the second half of the year and beyond. We highlight key points from the outlook below:

## Economics

Global: Global growth is moderating from its robust 2023 pace but the expansion is moving onto firmer ground. The world is becoming less dependent on US demand as global capex and European consumers pick up. These developments are generating a modest recovery in global manufacturing. Although an early year inflation acceleration is fading, global core inflation is expected to remain at close to 3% this year as tight labor markets and elevated wage gains limit service sector disinflation. These developments limit space for policy easing and our forecast incorporates high-for-long policy stances. The implications of these developments 12 to 24 months ahead are uncertain but we remain skeptical that inflation and rate normalization can be achieved without weakening demand.

US: We expect growth will continue to moderate in 2H24, with a 1.0% average growth pace for the remainder of the year. Fundamental drivers such as slowing wage inflation and anchored consumer and business inflation expectations are aligning with inflation in the ballpark of the Fed’s 2% inflation target. We expect the Fed will cut rates at the November FOMC meeting and continue at a quarterly cadence in 2025. We see risk of an earlier ease at the September meeting if any of the next three labor market reports show material weakening in hiring.

China: Our forecast for 2024 GDP growth stands at 5.2%yoy, assuming the quarterly growth will return to 4.5%q/q saar in 2H. Risks to our view include housing, which remains the largest drag and uncertainty around policy implementation still. Reluctance of monetary easing (constrained by concerns about currency and banking stability) may compromise the effort to return to a growth-supportive policy stance.

## Equities

• US: “Higher for longer” has favored size and quality spreads relative to an average company that is more cyclical and rate sensitive. These beneficiaries were amplified by the mania in AI/LLM stocks to a point where Momentum Crowding and Stock Concentration are now at multi-decade extremes.

• For this trend to continue in 2H, S&P 500 Mega Caps will need to keep revising estimates higher and maintain price momentum. In our view, this will be a challenge with the hurdle for consensus growth high while valuation and investor positioning are at or near highs of this cycle.

International: Unlike the beginning of the year, when equities benefited from improving economic activity and the anticipation of multiple Fed rate cuts, the growth-policy tradeoff is expected to worsen in 2H24. There is a risk of disappointments, with the Fed likely staying “higher for longer,” US activity momentum decelerating and a softening of pricing and top-line growth, hurting earnings delivery. Additionally, valuations look stretched, especially vs. real yields, creating risk that the robust earnings growth needed to justify current elevated multiples might not come through.

• Regionally, Japan will likely perform well in 2H24 on the likely stabilization in FX. In Q1 we closed the UW on Eurozone vs. the US, noting it is too early to move the Eurozone to an outright OW vs. the US, with the potential for a better entry

point in 2H, once there is increased clarity on French politics. For EM, there is less excitement, given Fed expectations and a likely strong USD, but forecast EM equities in general will trade better this year than they did in 2023.

Market Volatility: The surprisingly low equity volatility YTD is driven by fundamental and technical factors, including rising markets, risk complacency, low realized correlation, and large volatility selling flows. As long as a benign market environment persists, these factors are likely to keep volatility low. However, this is not a permanent feature, in our view, and the volatility cycle should eventually turn, perhaps rapidly, as the factors currently depressing volatility unwind.

## Rates

US Rates: The market’s Fed expectations remain the dominant driver of Treasury yields, and are likely to be the key factor in determining the direction of travel over 2H24. We forecast a 25bp cut in November, followed by a quarterly cadence of cuts. It remains likely this easing cycle will be shallower and perhaps more closely resemble the 1995 and 2019 easing cycles rather than the aggressive easing cycles during the 2001 recession and the GFC in 2007-2008. We believe there is still room for yields to decline. We forecast yields biased somewhat higher through the summer as markets push back on the expected timing of the first cut, but then declining again in the fall as the first ease approaches, with the curve bullishly steepening in the process.

International: In 2H24, most DM central banks will start (BoE, RBNZ and Norges) or continue (ECB and Riksbank) to reduce the degree of monetary policy restriction with the notable exceptions of the RBA and the BoJ, which will likely start a policy rate normalization away from ZIRP with two hikes by the end of this year. Overall, resilient growth and sticky inflation have slowed down the broad process of policy normalization since the beginning of the year. In the Euro area, the recent data dynamic reinforces our view of a slow disinflation story, indicating less need for “sufficiently restrictive” policy rates.

## Credit

US High Grade: We believe spreads can stay tight with still-strong technicals driven by high yields, a slowdown in supply, an expected improvement in corporate earnings and the start of Fed policy easing in 2H24. Credit metrics have been deteriorating with leverage near the higher end of its 5-year range and interest coverage near the lower end. That said, we do not believe credit fundamentals will be a key driver for spreads unless the economy slows meaningfully. We remain constructive on HG credit and believe that the asset class offers relatively safe carry.

US High Yield & Leveraged Loans: We believe leveraged credit will provide carry-esque returns over the next six months and spreads will finish 2024 comfortably inside the long-term average with balance sheets in a good position ahead of a forecasted economic slowdown. We continue to forecast high-yield bond spreads to widen slightly by YE2024. We continue to forecast high-yield bond and leveraged loan default rates to end 2024 at 2.00% and 3.75%, respectively.

Securitized Products: We look for home prices to be up \~5% in 2024, marginally lower than last year. While affordability continues to be a challenge, limited supply and slow new construction keep homes well bid but volumes extremely low. For CMBS, risks will likely continue to bend more towards extensions rather than writedowns. However, since time alone does not always resolve issues, particularly for bonds with significant office exposures, we remain neutral on CMBS valuations.

## FX

The bullish USD view remains intact in the medium term, driven by two key factors. First, the dollar's considerable yield advantage persists, with 40% of global central banks already cutting rates. Second, US growth exceptionalism still lingers, despite moderated US growth and an improving outlook in Europe and stabilizing growth in China.

## Commodities

With concerns about economic growth diminishing, we believe the recent decline in commodities is only temporary. Underpinning our constructive BCOM Index and sectoral recommendations is the expectation that in addition to supportive fundamentals over the next few months, commodities would be likely exposed to a confluence of forces, among them weather-related volatility, China’s Third Plenum, and the onset of the Fed’s rate cutting cycle.

## Economics

## Global: Normal is as normal does

Buffeted by unprecedented shocks and dramatic policy actions, the current expansion has delivered extreme and unusual business cycle dynamics. Global inflation was pushed to multi-decade highs through the early stages of a recovery in which the level of global GDP has remained depressed relative to its pre-pandemic potential path. Central banks engaged in a synchronized tightening of roughly 450bp (outside China) to contain inflation. While this tightening has not, to date, produced sub-potential global GDP growth or rising unemployment rates, inflation has made a substantial but incomplete step down.

Against the backdrop of the unusual and unprecedented, it is surprising to see a strong consensus build about the path ahead. Last year’s concern that monetary policy tightening would tip the US economy into recession has given way to a rising tide of optimism for a quick and relatively painless return to pre-pandemic norms. Both central bank and private sector 2024-25 forecasts coalesce around a narrative in which inflation moves back to 2% without damage to labor markets. This immaculate disinflation outcome, in turn, is expected to open the door for policy rates to approach their pre-pandemic estimates of neutral. A market embrace of this narrative has supported risk appetite, pushing equity valuations to record highs, alongside market pricing of roughly 200bp of US and Western Europe policy rate cuts over the coming two years.

Our economic outlook has been built on an alternative narrative in which the pandemic shock and the aggressive policy response have more lasting reverberations. Last year’s supports from US fiscal stimulus and the unwinding of the Russia invasion commodity price shock are fading from the scene, pointing to a moderation in global growth closer to a trend-like pace. But the improvement in private sector balance sheets engendered by pandemic policy supports has remained in place, as large nominal GDP gains have supported solid income increases for both households and corporates. This environment points to continued resilience in the face of elevated interest rates and a broadening in the sectoral and regional base of global growth (Figure 1).

## US

After expanding at a surprisingly fast 3.1% (4Q/4Q) pace in 2023, GDP growth has downshifted to what appears to be closer to a 1.5% rate in 1H24. We expect activity growth will continue to moderate in 2H24, and we are penciling in a 1.0% average growth pace for the remainder of the year (Figure 4).

Consumer spending growth has moderated recently, as the post-pandemic tailwinds supporting last year’s exceptional growth have begun to normalize. While there are mixed signals from the labor market, the broad thrust of the data indicates that nominal labor income growth should continue coming back down to earth in 2H24, providing a further tempering influence on consumers.

The government sector delivered another large boost to growth last year. Spending growth at all levels of government has slowed at the start of this year and we look for that to continue in the second half.

The interest-sensitive sectors of the economy have been tepid over the last two years, which stands to reason. Housing saw a modest recovery in 1Q24, though recent leading indicators point to renewed weakness in 2H24.

Business capital spending has been supported by IRA and CHIPS Act-related spending. The more recent data suggest that while this buildout is continuing, the growth in this policy-supported capex is starting to peter out. Outside of this category, business capital spending growth has been soft, and business surveys indicate that this should remain the case in coming months.

Even as GDP growth slowed markedly in 1Q24, consumer price inflation reaccelerated. The recent data suggest the disinflation process that took hold in 2H23 is getting back on track. Perhaps more significantly, fundamental drivers such as slowing wage inflation and anchored consumer and business inflation expectations are aligning with inflation in the ballpark of the Fed’s 2% inflation target (Figure 5Core PIandth fus).

Much of the pickup in core inflation in 1Q24 occurred in the so-called supercore category—core services excluding rental inflation. While we don’t expect a repeat of the outright decline in supercore prices delivered in the most recent CPI report, we do think the worst here is likely behind us.

Rental inflation—both tenants’ rent and owners’ equivalent rent—has remained elevated. Industry data suggest these categories should cool, though these data have been sending that signal for some time now. Timing the arrival of further rental disinflation is far from a precise science, but when it occurs it should provide more confidence that we are on track for a more normal pricing environment.

Against this backdrop, we expect the Fed will cut rates at the November FOMC meeting and continue at a quarterly cadence in 2025. We see risk of an earlier ease at the September meeting if any of the next three labor market reports show material weakening in hiring.

The November FOMC meeting concludes the day after the election. The outcome of that election will have significant impact on the economic outlook for 2025. Trade, immigration, and tax policy could all be affected by the presidential and congressional elections.

While these are all topics for next year, uncertainty about the fate of these policies could affect business activity and risktaking behavior over the remainder of this year. Historically it is hard to detect these uncertainty effects in US election years, though history may be a poor guide for what to expect in this election year.

## Euro area: Better growth, ECB cuts rates

Euro area growth has picked up in 1H24. After a difficult 2023, which saw only modest growth, the Euro area economy has gathered pace again in 1H24. GDP posted a gain of 1.3%q/q, saar in 1Q24, which was ahead of the signal of the PMI. But, the PMI improved further into 2Q24, suggesting an underlying pickup to around a 1.5%ar.

In terms of drivers, the consumer is a compelling candidate for explaining the pickup given the constellation of stillstrong wage growth and much lower headline inflation. This is giving strong real income gains at present. However, the data are not validating this fully with consumer spending up at less than a 1%ar recently, and indicators of goods spending not yet picking up up strongly even in 2Q24. Hence, other factors may also be contributing, such as improvements in trade, manufacturing and the business sector more generally. This has led to some lift in Germany as well.

Our forecast assumes that this above-potential momentum is maintained through mid-year, when we have a modest slowing to trend to reflect that household real income gains become less intense. But, to the extent that the recent pickup reflects other factors as well, near-term growth risks are likely skewed to the upside.

A key support has been that corporates remain in decent shape. Having increased their profit margins sharply, they are maintaining them at high levels. This has allowed them to increase employment last year, despite the weakness in growth, and to absorb the resulting weakness in output per worker.

Disinflation proceeds with pressure points. Despite the pickup in GDP growth, we think that the Euro area will see further disinflation, with core inflation falling back to the ECB’s target in 2H25. A key call is that current strength in wage growth still reflects an adjustment to the high inflation of the past two years and that it will ease now that headline inflation has already slowed substantially. It also assumes that companies will hold their high profit margins, rather than expand them much further in the near-term. This may be a harder call when growth is improving, but has some support in the latest data.

In testing these views, the ECB is very much focused on services inflation and on wages in particular. On wages, the ECB has taken some comfort from its forward wage tracker and the details of pay settlements, which should show some moderation outside of the delayed pay deals in Germany. But, the failure of labor productivity to pick up biases unit labor costs higher and the unemployment rate lower, relative to the ECB’s forecasts. Hence, there are clear pressure points.

Progress has also been more difficult on services inflation, which jumped back to 4.1%oya in May. Services prices have on averaged increased at a 5%ar since the start of the year. The German VAT increase on restaurant meals explains a part of this and large increases in insurance prices explain a bit more. The latter could reflect lagged effects from the pandemic. But, even if these effects are discounted, services inflation has looked firm.

ECB begins to cut rates. Despite the pickup in growth and the pressure points in the disinflation process, the ECB delivered a first rate cut in June. Its forecasts imply that further cuts are on the cards, although there is now much less forward guidance of this and greater data-dependence. Hence, further cuts require reassurance on wages/productivity and services inflation. We assume that this will gradually materialize, keeping the ECB on track for further cuts in September and December. We then expect further cuts next year down to a neutral policy rate level of 2%. Risks may be skewed to the upside, but we do think that the Euro area still has a decent chance of disinflating without a material weakening in the labor market. In parallel, the ECB is also further scaling back its balance sheet support, with PEPP reinvestments slowing in 2H24 and ceasing altogether at the end of this year.

Political risks come back into focus. The EU election did not result in any dramatic shift in the composition of the European parliament with only modest gains of non-centrist parties. But, French president Macron took more drastic action, calling for snap elections for the end of June. The situation is still fluid, but could result in Macron having to “cohabit” with Le Pen’s RN for the final two years of his presidency.

We note however that RN has dropped some of its most drastic policies, such as Frexit, and toned down other policies, e.g. on taxation and fiscal policy in general. There are also domestic and European constraints. The latter include the impact of bond markets expressing any concern through spreads and the new fiscal rules that will require a certain amount of fiscal consolidation. However, the situation is evolving and has the potential to create challenges. Our baseline forecast assumes that any impact remains confined to politics and does not have broader market or macro implications.

## China: Back to trend growth in 2H

China’s economic activity has been choppy and uneven in 1H. Activity started strong in 1Q, with real GDP growth rising 7.4%q/q saar (or 5.3%oya). With the notable exception of housing activity and housing-related consumption, most economic activity recovered strongly in 1Q led by industrial production, manufacturing investment and an increase in export volume. However, with the fading of Lunar New Year effect and withdrawal of policy support (as reflected in the net decline in government bond issuance and broad credit data in April), activity cooled off in 2Q (JPM forecast: 3.5%q/q saar). Meanwhile, the divergence of economic activity continued, with out-performance in advanced manufacturing, green sectors and exports, under-performance in housing market activity and in between, steady yet modest growth in infra-FAI, consumption and services. The economic imbalances, especially weaker domestic demand vs. supply, led to prolonged deflation pressure: GDP deflator has been negative for four consecutive quarters (-1.1%oya in 1Q), and most likely will continue to be in 2Q and 3Q.

Policy swing has contributed to the volatility in economic activity. The strong 1Q performance was in part due to improved global demand, and in part the effect of policy support in 2H23. However, it appeared that the strong start in 1Q led to some degree of complacency among policymakers, which contributed to the activity slowdown in March and April. This was similar to the episode one year ago. Encouragingly, policy adjustment happened earlier this time. Instead of waiting until the July politburo meeting (the policy turning point last year), the politburo meeting in late April emphasized the importance of policy continuity, explicitly calling for an accelerated pace of government bond issuance and use of rate cuts and RRR cuts by the PBOC. Following that, fiscal policy was normalized, and a new round of housing policy relaxation was announced on May 17 that not only included additional demand-side easing measures (lower downpayment and lower mortgage rates) but also a new initiative to reduce housing inventory (300bn yuan re-lending to support government purchase of unsold homes). While we think these policy adjustments are unlikely to drive another strong rebound in economic activity, they could control downside risks and support a return to trend-growth in 2H.

Our forecast for 2024 GDP growth stands at 5.2%yoy, assuming quarterly growth will return to 4.5%q/q saar in 2H. We do not expect the government will bolster fiscal stimulus beyond what was approved at the March NPC meeting. However, fiscal contraction should not repeat this year, given the fiscal package implies a shift from significant fiscal contraction last year to marginal fiscal easing this year, and the pace of fiscal utilization has lagged behind normal years. On the monetary policy front, the PBOC appears to stay cautious. We now forecast a 10bp policy rate cut and a 25bp RRR cut for the rest of the year (both in 3Q), and credit growth is revised down to 8.5% amid a tightened rule to ban the idle credit phenomenon (bank lending to corporates not to support capex but to deposit back into banks). In the housing market, housing demand remains weak despite the latest round of demand-side easing measures, as demand is dragged by weak income

growth, weak house price expectations and concerns about home delivery. The new initiative to reduce housing inventory is in the right direction, in our view, but the pace of take-up remains uncertain at this stage.

Risks to our 2H growth forecast can come from both domestic and external fronts. First, housing remains the biggest drag. Full-year real estate FAI will likely come in weaker than expected, possibly falling by about 9.5%oya. This implies a larger macro drag than we expected at the beginning of the year, though it can partly be offset by positive surprise from manufacturing investment. Second, policy measures to boost consumption remain muted, even for the announced measure to support trade-in consumption for NEVs and home appliance. This raises the question on the sustainability of consumption and service recovery into 2H. Third, export has been an important lifting factor (net exports contributed 14.5% of 1Q GDP growth), but the trade tension seems to have increased as reflected in recent tariff increases by the Biden administration and EU on selected Chinese goods, US government criticism of China’s excess supply, and Trump’s threat to hike tariffs on Chinese goods to 60%. Lastly, but most importantly, policy implementation is still full of uncertainty. Reluctance for monetary easing (constrained by concerns about currency stability and banking stability amid net interest margin compression) may compromise the effort to return to a growth-supportive policy stance. More importantly, it is unclear whether important directional changes in economic policy (to address both cyclical and structural issues in China) can be adopted, e.g. removing the policy bias in favor of production over boosting domestic demand, equal support for manufacturing upgrades and service upgrades, and equal support for SOE and non-SOE sectors.

## Equities

## Global and US Overview

Since last year, we have argued that a soft-landing outcome would be difficult to engineer. Instead, a no-landing would be more likely with higher-for-longer rates until growth surrenders to restrictive monetary policy and softening macro backdrop. For this reason, we have remained in favor of size and quality spreads favoring larger and higher quality companies that are less cyclical and rate sensitive. This view has mostly played out with the Largest 20 US Stocks (+27% YTD) and High Quality (+10%, equal weight, top quintile) significantly outperforming S&P 500 Equal Weight (+5%), S&P 500 ex-Largest 20 Stocks (+6.6%), and Russell 2000 (+0.7%). Globally, we are seeing a similar return attribution with MSCI ACWI ex US (+6.3%) outperforming its equal-weight index (+0.2%). On the other hand, we underappreciated the resiliency of Mag-6 in terms of price momentum and earnings revisions. The Higher for Longer beneficiaries were further amplified by AI / LLM stocks to a point where Momentum Crowding and Stock Concentration are now at multi-decade extremes. The persistence of winners winning resulted in the Largest 20 Stocks explaining 65% of S&P 500 gains over the past year and 75% YTD. Excluding the outsized impact of these 20 companies, S&P 500 would be at \~4,700 index level (vs current 5,465 level). For this trend to continue in 2H, the Largest 20 will likely need to keep revising estimates higher to maintain price momentum. In our view, this will be a challenge with a high consensus earnings growth hurdle (2H EPS growth of 12% and 2025 of 14% are more in line with early-to-mid cycle recovery) while valuation and investor positioning are near cycle extremes.

On the earnings front, we expect consensus estimates to be revised down with easy comps in rear view after 2Q24. Similar to stock performance, earnings revisions and growth are concentrated and not supportive of broadening growth. In our view, this will be a challenge after 2Q24 reporting season when earnings lap easy comps (base effect and earnings surprises from AI / LLM companies). Also, the cumulative effect of higher interest expense remains a headwind for earnings with every passing quarter along with stronger trade-weighted USD (every \~2% upside in USD is \~1% downside in EPS). Consensus estimates imply doubledigit growth rate for the foreseeable period despite an aging business cycle with diminishing demand, peak margins, and fading buyback effect. As for price momentum, it is already at an extreme with Momentum Crowding at a 35-year high, Stock Concentration at a 60-year high and market cap % GDP ratio near a 70-year high. Similarly, investor risk appetite is very high based on low equity risk premia, rich equity valuations on an absolute and cross-asset basis, compressed vol / VIX, tight credit spreads, and a broadly bullish investor survey at our Macro Quant conference.

While timing reversals and rotations is difficult, we are in the camp that hyperbolic moves in price and sentiment are more often violently corrected than not when the exuberance runs its course, and the largest institutional investors are done chasing. Given robust investor sentiment and elevated positioning, we recommend diversifying away from momentum tail risk by adding allocation to anti-momentum Defensive Value plays (i.e., Utilities, Staples, Healthcare, Telecom, Dividend Aristocrats) and away from pro-cyclical exposures (Industrials, Consumer Discretionary, Financials, and unprofitable Small Caps). Also, Defensives are a hedge against the unknowns, trade at a cheaper relative multiple (Defensive vs. Cyclicals valuation spread at lows), and offer a higher shareholder carry than most Tech and Cyclical industries. Our equity outlook does not account for tail events even though there are plenty that could be a catalyst for higher volatility, rapid deleveraging, and a sharp break in global markets (e.g., rates and FX risks due to unsynchronized inflation and growth cycles such as between US and Japan, tightening global liquidity, elections, and geopolitical risk).

Business cycle moving sideways, low-income consumer showing stress, and rapid rate cuts have been tabled. The 4Q23 market rebound started on a hopeful expectation that inflation normalization would allow the Fed to cut rates (\~7 cuts by Dec 2024) and engineer an immaculate soft landing for the economy. Instead, inflation drivers have surprised to the upside for much of the year and growth has been uneven, forcing expectations down to only \~2 cuts for this year. The leading real growth indicators of QMI (e.g., initial claims, PMIs, freight traffic, etc.) are moving sideways at best (e.g., new orders PMI barely around 50, and sluggish recovery in credit and weak money supply growth) and consumers are showing some signs of distress (e.g., retail sales, consumer sentiment, auto, and credit card delinquencies). Growth trajectory relative to sentiment markers (narrow credit spreads, earnings surprise, consumer surveys, etc.) has not yet shown the vigor that accompanied the well-known soft landings in 1984 and 1995.

There is a clear disconnect in the huge run-up in US equity valuations and the business cycle. While JPM QMIs are not teetering at the edge of a slowdown, the amount of improvement in financial conditions (a proxy to predict future GDP growth) does not justify the gain in S&P 500 price index (an overvaluation of at least 15% based on data as of May). With inflation running above the Fed’s target and the economy not broken, the market has been treating “bad data is good.” In our view, however, we are nearing the point where “bad data will be bad” for risk assets with potentially softening macro, and inflation could stay sticky and above the Fed’s target. Even if the Fed were to cut rates, the long-term rate (discount rate input for risky assets) does not need to move down in lockstep given tightening liquidity and potentially higher term premia. There is a risk that an opposite of the hopeful expectation could play out in coming quarters where growth decelerates, inflation remains firm, and long-term rates don’t move sharply lower (a risk of stagflation).

Diminishing liquidity. Since the Fed began the latest attempt two years ago to normalize its balance sheet, total assets have declined to 26% from 36% of GDP or \~\$1.7tr from peak. Meanwhile, major central banks outside the US face even more contractionary balance sheets that have been reduced to 43% from 59% of GDP or \~\$5.5tr from peak. However, despite quantitative tightening, the Fed has been able to prop up the monetary base thus far by reducing the size of reverse repo facility (i.e., replacing RRP on its liability account with reserve balances). However, the room for additional reduction in reverse repo is limited. The path to further normalization of the balance sheet would require decline in reserve balances, a source of funding liquidity for Main Street and Wall Street. In this sense, liquidity is likely about as good as it gets, and risks mostly lie to the downside. That poses a threat to equities where the valuations relative to GDP are near 70- year high.

Narrow leadership is masking weakness, and the breadth is awful. The outsize return of the largest stocks is masking weakness in other areas of the market, in our view. For example, S&P 500 equal-weighted is largely unchanged over the last 2.5 years and Russell 2000 has been moving sideways for even longer even though we have seen 25% nominal GDP growth over the last 3 years. Similarly, only a small percentage of S&P 500 companies are behind the earnings revisions for 2024 (Top 20 is up 18% vs. Rest of S&P 500 down 6%) and 2025 EPS (up 22% and -3%, respectively) over the past 12 months. In fact, the return and earnings revision are almost perfectly symmetric with size, which in our view is due to the higher-for-longer regime driving extreme divergence between winners and losers combined with rapid increase in AI related investment spending. Largest 20 companies are global behemoths supported by secular demand trends, asset light and less labor-intensive businesses with strong pricing power and low interest rate sensitivity, and steady cash flow generation. Precisely for the opposite reasons, Russell 2000 has struggled to grow in recent quarters with revenue growth negative every quarter since 2Q23 and earnings growth negative since 1Q23 based on Refinitiv data. In our view, mid-single-digit growth rate earnings

delivery is more sustainable at this phase of the business cycle relative to robust double-digit EPS growth expected for 2024 (11%) and 2025 (14%). Two consecutive years of double-digit earnings growth is more in line with early cycle when there is pent-up demand with margin expansion, and falling interest expense. Also, at this point in the earnings cycle, the incremental buybacks remain largely concentrated in mega caps due to still strong cash flow generation and are less effective due to high valuation and cost of debt.

Momentum factor remains the key driver of equity gains. The long-leg of Momentum is up another 12% YTD and 21% cumulatively since early 2022 while the short-leg has significantly underperformed at -0.2% and +2.6% over the same period. This makes Momentum the best performing factor since 2022, followed by Quality (+19%) and Low Vol (+14%). Momentum remains concentrated in Mega Caps (median Momentum decile market cap current: \$68b vs. \$40b last year) and High Quality (P/E spread relative to market at 99%ile vs 35-year history). While Quality outperformed Value since 2022, there has been a noticeable rotation into Value YTD in the US and more so abroad where the AI theme is not as dominant. In the US, Momentum P/E has fallen over the past three months with Momentum exposures slowly rotating into Cheap Defensive (Value) stocks. Since Nvidia’s earnings surprise in May 2023, six S&P 500 LLM stocks have driven 65% of the index gains over this period and 75% YTD. To put these gains in perspective, these LLM stocks are worth \$15 trillion, which is equal to the combined market caps of Japan, Germany, India, and Brazil.

US election risk. Unlike past election cycles, when the debate focused on which set of policies likely would be more positive for equity markets, the current scenarios seem to be more neutral, with the potential for meaningful negative outcomes. An incumbent presidential victory would likely be more of the same given a continued gridlocked Congress limiting the passage of new transformative policies. However, a loss for the incumbent could drive substantial changes in areas such as trade policy (e.g., US-China), foreign policy (e.g., Middle East/Israel, Russia/Ukraine, etc.), regulation (e.g. environment/energy, anti-trust) and fiscal/tax policy (e.g., TCJA sunset on 12/31/25), see report for full breakdown. In terms of betting odds, the challenger has improved his lead to the widest since February of this year. Swing state polling also shows favorable expectations for the challenger though they remain very narrow in Michigan, Wisconsin and Pennsylvania. Lastly, general election polling still shows the challenger ahead but by a much narrower margin than betting odds and swing state polling. In our view, gridlock is the most neutral outcome in the short/medium term, as it would limit changes to existing policies passed under the current administration and limit the implementation of new, and possibly volatile ones, though risks remain around unilateral executive and/or agency action. Key catalysts include the presidential debate (6/27), which could clarify key policy platforms for both candidates. See report for more detailed discussion on key scenarios and 10 policy baskets.

## Market Volatility

US: The VIX has averaged just \~14 YTD, and recently fell to post-COVID lows. The surprisingly low volatility is driven by fundamental and technical factors, and we maintain that

volatility is disconnected from the high interest rates and macro data. On the fundamental side, rising markets, risk complacency, and low realized correlation drove index volatility lower. In particular, the low correlation was caused by divergences between small and large caps and sectors like Tech, and the impact of individual stocks like NVDA that drove most of the US stock market returns. On the technical side, volatility is being suppressed by large selling flows from option-based ETFs, short-dated (e.g., 0DTE) options, and various risk premia strategies (e.g., QIS, dispersion). This technical supply creates a virtuous cycle by leaving option dealers long gamma and causing them to suppress realized volatility through their hedging flows, which in turn spurs systematic strategies (e.g., Volatility Targeting funds) to re-lever.

As long as the benign market environment persists, this virtuous cycle is likely to remain intact and keep volatility low. However, in our view this is not a permanent feature and the volatility cycle will turn, perhaps rapidly – but it is difficult to predict when. In the case of a market shock, systematic strategies (which are currently highly levered) could become forced sellers, volume sellers could be at risk of a disorderly unwind, and the volatility suppressing force from optionbased ETFs would quickly disappear on a material sell-off.

Europe: The announcement of snap elections in France led to a spike in short-term European volatility. The VSTOXX, which had traded broadly in-line with the VIX for most of the year, reached a high of \~20, while the VIX was unaffected and continued to trade at \~13. We find this surge in European risk premium excessive and recommend fading it (see H2 European Equity Derivatives Outlook). While the short-term excess volatility premium for European equities appears excessive and should moderate, we expect part of it to linger on for the foreseeable future, as we are unlikely to have clarity on French fiscal policy for months after the elections and as frictions with the EU will become more likely.

Japan: The VNKY Index has averaged 19.5 points year-todate, which is 1.5 pts below its 10-year average. We observed heightened volatility in 1Q24 as the Nikkei climbed above the 40,000 mark. Dealer gamma positioning significantly influenced market realized volatility. Initially, this was driven by investors purchasing calls to chase upside momentum, which later expanded to include investors buying puts to hedge cash stock positions. We anticipate these dynamics to persist as the market regains upward momentum. Structured product issuance remains subdued, and we do not expect it to be a primary driver in 2H24.

HK/China: The VHSCEI was elevated in 1Q24, averaging 28 points before stabilizing at an average of 25.3 points in 2Q24. At the beginning of the year, structured product

dynamics were in focus as major China indices approached key levels with high concentrations of knock-in barriers. The risk from structured product hedging has decreased significantly following a large volume of product expirations in 1H24. The return of market dispersion was another factor containing index volatility, as index options are typically sold to fund the purchase of single-stock options in dispersion strategies. Warrant flows remain lackluster as the recovery in Chinese equities was not sustained. We believe sustained momentum is necessary for warrant flows to pick up.

## EM Sprint Turned into a Marathon

In our Year Ahead report, we thought 2024 would start bumpy for EM equities driven by a challenging global macro picture of high rates, geopolitical developments, and strong competition from US exceptionalism. We also predicted that positive optionality might build throughout 2024 as conditions materialized for US monetary easing, slowing activity, and reversal of USD strength. We believe this view still holds but the upside will come later than initially forecasted.

What is better? J.P. Morgan revised GDP growth upward across the board. EM equities returns are the strongest when global manufacturing PMI is rebounding.

What is worse? There is little confidence that the US exceptionalism phaseout is near: (1) we revised 2024 US GDP to 2.4% from 1.6%; (2) global core inflation should settle closer to 3% than 2%; and (3) the US neutral rate, r\*, could be higher than the Fed’s assumption.

What remains? J.P. Morgan continues to forecast US monetary easing and economic slowing into 2H24. Under such conditions, EM positives might become appreciated: (1) a favorable EM-DM growth differential; (2) diversification away from US equities; (3) low investor positioning; and (4) EM trading at a deep discount to DM on both forward P/E and P/BV metrics.

What to OW in EM equities? Global forces antagonizing – strong PMI yet exceptional US – lead us to navigate ambiguities via a barbell: (1) Structural / Endogenous Investment Cases – OW India, Mexico, and Saudi Arabia; (2) Tactical / Cyclical Exposure to Global GDP Growth – OW Brazil, China, and Indonesia.

Value+Commodity=EM. The global expansion is on firm footing through 1H24 with broad-based GDP upgrades and improving manufacturing PMI. And that has prompted the question: should equity portfolios rebalance toward more Value and commodities – energy, consensus materials (gold and copper), and non-consensus materials (iron ore and steel)? EM equities can offer both value and commodity exposure.

But the US election is on the horizon: Neither Trump nor Biden administrations have changed EM underperformance versus US equities. The long macroeconomic cycle of US exceptionalism continues to dominate. The qualitative debate with EM investors remains bear biased on the risks of “America First,” higher tariffs, and the potential collateral damage to the 2025 inflation outlook. The US election could become an obstacle for EM equity fund inflows that conflicts with the view that the US will slow and cut rates into year-end, historically favoring EM equities.

## Treasuries

Treasury yields have risen 40-60bp over 1H24, as a sharp pendulum swing has occurred. At the start of the year, market participants had fully embraced the “immaculate disinflation:” core PCE had returned to 2% on a 6-month annualized basis and Chair Powell’s dovish tone at the December 2023 FOMC meeting emboldened thinking the Fed would be easing earlier and more aggressively than other DM central banks. However, this thesis was derailed by a series of strong inflation and labor market reports throughout 1Q24, and markets aren’t pricing a full 25bp ease until the November meeting. However, yields have retreated from their local peaks in recent weeks, as the latest data have assuaged the worst fears that inflation is accelerating, retail sales indicated consumption is slowing, and both JOLTS as well as initial claims give further sense the labor market continues to slowly rebalance.

As a result, the combination of rangebound markets and negative carry will make it challenging to be structurally long duration. Therefore, we prefer to add duration when valuations are attractive, unless we see structural changes emerge which give the sense the Fed can cut more aggressively. Along the curve, given how little is priced into 2026, we think there is value in being long in the 3- to 5-year sector of the Treasury curve. Nonetheless, with yields near the bottom end of their recent ranges, it’s tough to be long at current levels, even though Treasuries appear modestly cheap on a fair-value basis.

As we have discussed for the last few months, there are ways to thoughtfully construct long-duration proxies with better carry profiles. Equi-notional flatteners synthetically replicate exposure to Treasury forward yields, and offer bullish duration exposure with better carry profiles than outright longs (see Treasuries, US Fixed Income Markets Weekly, 3/22/24). By maintaining equal notionals, these flatteners have a smaller bearish exposure in the short-duration leg of the trade when compared to a duration-neutral flattener, resulting in a bullish duration exposure. Spot Treasuries in the short- to intermediate sector explain a high share of the variation in these equinotional flatteners, with relatively high betas as well. Along the curve, synthetic 1y1y and 3y2y forward Treasury yields are sitting in the middle of their 6-month ranges, exhibit high correlation with changes in intermediate Treasury yields and exhibit relatively neutral carry profiles. Thus, we would use any bearish move to add synthetic front-end forward exposure via one of these equi-notional flatteners.

Turning to the curve, an easing Fed would certainly support a steeper curve, as the long end tends to steepen first in the period between the last hike and the first ease, while the front end tends to react only once the Fed begins easing (see Treasuries 2023 Outlook, 11/22/22). For this reason, we have preferred long-end steepeners as a more strategic representation of our Fed easing view, but clearly with limited success thus far. Moreover, our work has also shown that the curve does tend to steepen into even more shallow easing cycles like we described above. Given these traditional pre-easing dynamics, we favor 5s/30s steepeners as the long end of the curve appears somewhat flat now adjusting for the market’s medium-term Fed policy and inflation expectations, and the Fed’s ownership share of the Treasury market. However, we recognize that these steepeners do not tend to consistently perform until the first Fed cut, and steepeners may not become consistently profitable until later in the summer.

## Short-Term Fixed Income

A key call in our money markets outlook this year is that MMFs will remain an attractive asset class, with overall AUMs staying flat year-over-year. In the event, flows into MMFs have proven even stronger than anticipated, up \$182bn YTD to \$6.35tn, thanks to stickier inflation and stronger-than-expected economic growth that have led to a significant delay in Fed easing expectations.

These inflows underscore the abundance of liquidity still in the financial system, most of which seemed to be sitting in the front-end. To that end, concerns that the banking system would be rapidly approaching reserve scarcity in 1H24 ultimately proved unfounded. EFFR was steadfastly stable at 5.33%, while SOFR traded in a 1bp range around 5.31%, outside of quarter-ends and tax-related payment dates. In fact, even when bank reserve balances dropped by over \$300bn to \$3.27tn in the second half of April, SOFR barely made a blip. At the same time, bank CP/CD spreads narrowed to nearly a post-QT low at the start of the year, cheapened slightly around March quarter-end and April tax day, before spreads began to tighten again. In 2H24, we believe funding conditions will continue to be relatively benign, though acknowledge there are a variety of risks that liquidity investors will have to grapple with in the coming months. Even with QT taper, the Fed’s balance sheet is still shrinking, albeit at a slower pace, which means reserves are still increasingly approaching scarcity. As of the time of writing, reserve balances registered \$3.4tn. Market expectations have largely coalesced around \$3.0-\$3.1tn as the lowest comfortable level of reserves (LCLoR), and while our Fed balance sheet expectations call for reserve balances to remain comfortably above \$3tn during 2H24, signs of reserve scarcity could start to emerge towards year-end as we come closer to approaching LCLoR. The good news is that we also project ON RRP balances to remain near \$400bn for at least a few months and over \$300bn for the remainder of the year, which will help to offset any funding pressures arising from balance sheet normalization.

Treasury’s funding needs also remain a concern given expectations of wider deficits over the coming years. In light of CBO’s latest 10-year projections, our Treasury strategists now expect that any additional financing needs over the balance of 2024 will be met primarily with T-bills. Additionally, there is a risk that Treasury could begin another round of coupon auction size increases sometime in mid-2025, slightly earlier than anticipated (see CBO’s 10-year budget projections: less than meets the eye?, J. Barry, 6/18/24). Upcoming US elections could further widen the deficit.

Against this backdrop, with Treasury debt outstanding and primary dealer inventories already at record highs, we have already seen markets taking a bit more time to digest Treasury supply, particularly when the supply settles on statement dates. That said, given the abundance of liquidity still in the front-end (indeed, MMFs’ AUMs, dealer repo, and T-bill balances have grown by \$1.3tn, \$1.0tn, and \$1.45tn, respectively since YE22, Figure 26) and the potential demand growth for Treasuries from other structural buyers, this should help alleviate any funding pressures over the near-to-medium term (see Yes, T-bill demand should remain robust, T. Ho, 5/30/24).

With respect to the credit side of the money markets, MMF reform is top of mind, though the impact on credit spreads should be minimal, unlike what we saw in 2016 (see MMF reform spotlight: Not the same as 2016, T. Ho, 5/3/24). What happens to credit spreads in July will be telling to the extent remaining institutional prime funds (i.e., those that have not announced conversions or liquidations) turn even more defensive to shore up liquidity heading into October. Even if spreads do cheapen however, we suspect other liquidity buyers will jump in to take advantage of the spread backup, thus limiting its widening.

Net, despite some emerging risks in 2H, we see liquidity remaining abundant in the front-end. Market expectations have coalesced around a more moderate path of easing, to the tune of somewhere between zero and three cuts by YE24, which should continue to attract cash into MMFs and the front-end more broadly, all else equal. Thus, this should limit any impact on EFFR/SOFR, T-bills/OIS, and CP/OIS spreads over the coming months.

## Interest Rate Derivatives

The first half has seen yields trade in a considerably wide range, thanks to dramatic shifts in Fed expectations. At the start of the year, expectations for policy rates over the course of the year were pretty much concentrated in scenarios involving deep cuts (5 or more by year end). As inflation data proved stickier than expected early on, these expectations reversed course, to the extent where, as of April and May, a not-insignificant weighting was being placed by markets on scenarios involving additional hikes by year end. But a softer inflation print in June, followed by Fed Chair Powell’s comments that additional hikes were “not in anyone’s base case,” helped to mitigate the tail risk of further hikes. More recently, market expectations have coalesced around a moderate easing path that involves somewhere between zero and three cuts by year end (Figure 27Folwing asfter p JudCh P’ cm, kx v  ).

Through it all, the range in yields has been significant, with front end yields trading in a \~100bp range and long end yields trading in a \~75bp range. The range in maturity matched swap spreads has also been significant, but the wide ranges in swap spreads are not because of correlation with yield levels - indeed, the beta between 5-year maturity matched swap spreads and 5-year Treasury yields has been essentially zero over the past three months. Rather, it reflects the swings in macro factors that impact the entire term structure of swap spreads.

As we have highlighted in recent weeks, the term structure of swap spreads is best understood by examining the slope of a cross-sectional plot versus modified duration, which can be interpreted as the negative of term funding premium, and the intercept, which can be interpreted as zero duration swap spreads. Over the course of 1H24, zero-duration spreads have traded in an especially wide range, varying from -15bp at the start of the year to near zero in late April - it now stands near -7bp. Term funding premium, which can be defined as the incremental compensation demanded by Treasury investors for terming out principal per each additional year of duration extension after hedging interest rate risk, has also varied from as low as 3.5bp/year to a high of nearly 5bp/year - this now stands near 4.5bp/year.

## International Rates

In 2H24, most DM central banks will start (BoE, RBNZ and Norges) or continue (ECB and Riksbank) to reduce the degree of monetary policy restriction with the notable exceptions of the RBA, which is expected to remain on hold with cuts only in 1Q25, and the BoJ, which will likely start a policy rate normalisation away from ZIRP with two hikes by the end of this year. Resilient growth and sticky inflation have slowed down the broad process of policy normalisation since the beginning of the year. However, the recent data dynamic reinforces our view of a slow disinflation story, with stronger conviction in the Euro area, and indicates less need for “sufficiently restrictive” policy rates. In our baseline, such a slow reduction of monetary policy restriction will leave policy rates “restrictive for longer” eventually contributing to taking inflation closer to target in a soft-landing baseline scenario. The narrative of “higher for longer” with a possibility of no landing has lost traction lately given the loss of growth momentum in the latest surveys readings. On the other side, the fragility in the macro outlook seems limited and not enough to justify a hard landing, although pricing close to zero probability for that scenario seems complacent. This macro outlook leaves us with a broad medium-term bullish duration view on intermediate (first) and short end rates (later), with an eventual bull steepening of the yield curve. Tactically, we see valuations not particularly attractive amidst a broad short-term range trading environment, but recommend investors to trade DM

rates ex-Japan with a broad bullish bias, using back up in yields to add positions with more macro validation expected later this year (Table 2).

The calibration of such bullish stance is more medium-term at present while, near-term, we expect intermediate German yields to stay range-bound (5-10Y in 2.30-2.60% range) until confidence around the disinflation process increases. Over 2H24, our bias is to trade duration tactically from the long side given our medium-term bullish stance and we would consider adding outright longs if 5-10Y Germany moves closer to 2.60%. We target 10Y Bund yields at 2.20% by 4Q24. We expect the German curve to broadly bull steepen/bear flatten going forward, especially in 5s/30s, and maintain a strategic steepening bias. ## Global Inflation Markets

At a global level, despite the ongoing decline in DM headline inflation rates, core inflation over the first half of 2024 has been stickier than expected, particularly in services. We think global core CPI will start to slow over the second half of the year as core service price inflation moderates, but the decline will likely be sticky and uneven across countries. Core goods inflation has stabilized at pre-pandemic levels following the sharp decline seen over 2H23 however the drivers of core goods inflation are becoming more mixed. Global supply chain pressure has eased but output prices remain firm and container shipping costs have moved higher.

In the Euro area, headline and core inflation are running at 2.6%oya and 2.9%oya, respectively, and we expect headline HICP to fall to around 2.2% by the end of 2024 with core HICP remaining elevated at an average of 2.8%oya over 2H24 (Figure 31). Core services inflation has been hovering around 4% in oya terms reflecting a combination of holidayrelated items, the impact of higher German VAT on restaurant meals and the large increases seen in insurance prices. We expect core HICP to fall to around 3% in 1H25 as negotiated pay and wage effects moderate but the path remains sticky and near-term risks to inflation are to the upside. 5Yx5Y HICP has traded in a broad 2.20-2.40% range over the first half of this year and we expect this range-trading dynamic to persist over 2H24. The backdrop of declining headline inflation and an ECB easing cycle that will be slow and steady will likely keep 5Yx5Y HICP capped whilst our expectation for a modest rally in intermediate yields will put limited downward pressure on intermediate breakevens. However, sticky core and risk premia will likely keep 5Yx5Y HICP above the 2% level. We look to tactically trade a 2.25 – 2.40% range in 5Yx5Y HICP over 2H24.

In the UK, headline inflation has fallen to 2.0%oya recently but the decline in core has been much slower and stickier. Core CPI is at 3.5%oya with services inflation running at a still elevated 5.7%oya with volatile transport costs adding recent pressure. The decline in headline CPI to 2% will likely prove temporary as energy related base effects dissipate over the coming months and we expect headline inflation to average 2.2% over 2H24 and rise to 2.6%oya by the end of this year. The decline in core CPI will continue to be sticky and we forecast core inflation to average 3.3% over the second half of this year as domestic wage pressures take time to moderate. We expect the BoE to cut rates 25bp in August and November but visibility on the pace and timing of cuts over 2025 is limited. We expect RPI to average 3.8% over the second half of this year. 5Yx5 RPI has been steadily declining over the past few months and we think 5Yx5Y will trade in a narrow 3.30-3.45% range as BoE easing will be cautious and slow and we project only a limited rally in nominal yields. We hold a strategic short further out the inflation curve in 10Yx10Y RPI given relative valuations and the background RPI-CPIH alignment should drag RPI forwards in the 5Y+ sector lower.

In the US, headline and core CPI inflation are running 3.3% and 3.4% oya, respectively, as of May. ## Global Spread Markets

As we approach mid-year, the starting point and market narrative are not so different from they’ve been most of this year. That is, optically thin credit spreads remain counter-balanced by elevated all-in yield levels which mean the asset class offers a decent, though not stellar return. Away from this opening gambit, we think the credit ecosystem remains very well-balanced: meaningful asset-liability mismatches are hard to identify; ‘tourists’ or non-traditional holders of risk feel far from the ascendency; and, while we’ve some new-issue fatigue creep into in US High Grade, average-ish cash balances elsewhere mean it’s hard to make a topdown case that investors are ‘over their skis’. Bad credits have been ringfenced and good credits have been refinanced against the backdrop of record capital markets activity. If pushed to identify a crowded trade, it would be the up-inquality trade, which has been a bedrock of portfolio construction for quite some time now.

If there one thing which is likely a little different for credit markets today and going forward, at least for developed credit markets, it is the intersection between politics, budget deficits and bond market volatility. We have been given a recent preview of what this might look like given all that has ensued post the calling of snap parliamentary elections in France. The main parties’ tax and spending promises have been a major part of the political debate ahead of the UK general election and the first US presidential debate means fiscal policy will never be far from the headlines. While we don’t think we’ll ultimately see any extreme, Truss-like outcomes, the point for creditors as we’ve seen of late in France is that this intersection between politics, budget deficits and bond markets can turn credit-rates correlation positive.

## Currencies

## G10

Our view on the dollar remains medium-term USD-bullish (expressed through EUR and CNH puts vs. USD). The bullish USD view had been motivated by two pillars which continue to stay intact to varying degrees. First, the dollar’s considerable yield advantage, especially considering that it is a defensive currency. Even though the Fed has been on hold, in the global context the dollar’s yield advantage is actually improving as other central banks have been cutting rates. Specifically, the dollar is currently yielding more than 45% of the global universe (Figure 50) and 40% of the global central banks are already cutting rates (Figure 51).

Second, US growth exceptionalism was the additional pillar to our USD bullish view. This pillar has de-intensified vs. start of the year as US growth has moderated (from 3.4%ar in 4Q23 to 1.7% in 1H24) while the outlook for Europe has improved and that for China has stabilized. However, elements of US exceptionalism still linger. Eurozone June PMIs showed that the momentum is already running out of steam, while China growth continues to be feeble. Moreover the monetary policy divergence has also started to unfold with the ECB already cutting rates and PBoC still easing. Finally, our commodity strategists are bullish, which if realized would benefit the dollar more than commodity importers. EUR/USD target near-term target is 1.05 while USD/CNY 7.30 with upside risks.

## Fixed Income

EM assets to be held back in 2H24 by the looming US elections, while macro uncertainties remain unresolved. The first half of 2024 saw EM markets continue to be driven mainly by the US and global cyclical environment, as the interaction of growth and inflation cycles with their impacts on the US and EM monetary policies have been at the fore. The growth outlook is better despite a recent stall in momentum, while central bank rate forecasts have had to be revised higher again. Equity and commodity prices are higher in H1, with credit spreads tighter, giving a mid-cycle feel where risk has outperformed rates-sensitive assets. EM’s own recovery from distressed pricing, along with this global trend, has led EM credit to outperform local markets YTD, as EM sovereign spreads tightened 34bp (when adjusted for Venezuela’s index inclusion) and EM corporates tightened 54bp, for total returns of 2.8% and 3.9% respectively. Local markets have been dragged by FX spot moves (-4.8% vs the USD) and higher yields (+39bp), while carry has helped limit the total return loss to -3.7% on local bonds in USD terms.

Keep MW across EM local rates, FX, Sovereigns & Corporates, while hiding out in idiosyncratic EM carry. Trading a strong global macro trend for EM does not currently look like a great risk/reward strategy to us. First, there still remains a degree of ‘observational equivalence’ in the US and global data: on the one hand there is still a tight US labor market which can generate sticky inflation, with few signs of corporate or household imbalances, so rates may stay highfor-long. On the other hand, growth looks to be slowing with our economists forecasting this to continue in 2H24 and the cumulative impact of rate hikes may still be lagging through the economy. EM assets have become tired of trading these macro variations, as each scenario has looked plausible. The bar for further Fed hikes is currently high but the rates landscape may again change post-US election. This brings us to the second issue for EM markets, which is a US election cycle in which some of the policy proposals may significantly impact specific EM countries (see Emerging Markets Outlook & Strategy for 2H24 and also 2024 US Election Watch). Given a lack of risk-premia in EM assets ahead of this, there has been an element of complacency. Balancing this headwind, we still think EM has good idiosyncratic carry in places where two cycles of distress have abated: an EM default cycle and an EM inflation cycle. Both were precipitated by a generational US hiking cycle and there is still some excess risk premia we think in some high-yielding local and hard currency markets to exploit. Harvesting this idiosyncratic carry while remaining hedged against market beta is how we are currently recommending to position within EM into H2.

## FX

2H24 is likely to see a shift in market focus away from incessant handwringing around the Fed cycle towards US elections and their impact on EM, especially centering on the odds of a Republican presidential and/or Congressional victory. The latter is likely to impact EM currencies via four key channels – tariff policy, geopolitics, currency policy, and domestic fiscal policy.

On the direct impact of tariffs, we believe countries with a large share in US imports and material gains from rebalancing away from China’s imports since 2017 are most at risk; the

European Union, Mexico and Vietnam are the most exposed, while EMEA EM and Latam ex Mexico should be relatively insulated. However, this narrow focus likely underestimates the indirect impact of trade tensions and uncertainty on global manufacturing confidence, which can materially impact small open economies like CE3 even in the absence of direct trade with the US, given their large shares of goods trade/ GDP and overvalued exchange rates.

Regardless of China’s state of bilateral trade with the US, bearish CNY risk is likely to persist given only limited geopolitical risk premium in the price. Given the NEER targeting nature of FX policy regimes in EM Asia, significant RMB weakness is also likely to spill over onto regional currencies, particularly amongst the low-yielding, trade-reliant cluster of small open economies (TW, KR, SG, TH, MY).

On FX policy, the currency manipulator report could also again rise to prominence under a Trump presidency, but only a few EM currencies such as Vietnam, Singapore, Taiwan and Israel appear at risk presently. However, (geo)political considerations may motivate the Treasury to avoid labelling some of these.

Finally, domestic US fiscal policy has the ability to impact EM through changes it causes to the US yield curve, monetary policy and the USD. There may be partial cushion against bearish risks from valuations: the recent sell-off following election cycles in Mexico, South Africa and India has improved EM FX valuations and positioning remains roughly flat, with EM FX having a positioning score of +0.02 in our EM Client Survey.

In terms of model portfolio positioning, we enter H2 with an overall MW stance, which subsumes regional OWs in Latam and EMEA EM, vs. UWs in EM Asia, with a preference for currencies with high carry and/or attractive valuations. We hold OW/bullish trades in Latam (MXN, PEN, UYU), EMEA EM (RON, TRY, ILS, HUF) and EM Asia (SGD). We hold UW/ bearish trades in CNH, COP, CZK and THB.

We have been MW in our EMBIGD Model Portfolio all year as tight spreads, especially in the IG-BB space, have not provided compelling valuations in our view. However, pre-GFC history indicates that the resumption of a grind tighter could still leave room for the EMBIGD ex-CCC spread to compress even more toward US IG, despite the current apparently very tight levels. We therefore only expect a strong widening of spreads under a scenario where US recession risks come back into the fold. The EMBIGD has returned 2.4%ytd: after seeing strong returns in 1Q24, led by the high-yield segment, 2Q returns have stalled a bit, even as UST have recovered since end-April. ## Commodities

Commodity prices surged to new highs at the end of May, with copper, gold, cocoa, and frozen orange juice reaching all-time-highs, not to mention US natural gas price rallying 74% over the past month and a half, but pulled back in June on profit taking across the complex amid growing concerns that the US economy was slowing abruptly.

Soft patches are slowdowns which occur within growth cycles and after a month of economic data tilting softer, recent news reaffirmed that the US economy is cooling, not collapsing, a “Goldilocks” scenario in which US growth is neither too hot, nor too cold.

With fears on economic growth fading, we believe the recent pullback in commodities is just that: a pullback.From current levels, we continue to see a 10% appreciation in the broader BCOM Commodities index by year-end (2024 Commodities Outlook: Structurally bullish gold for second consecutive year, bullish opportunities in oil and gas more tactical, 30 November 2023; Global Commodities: Not a deflationary force any more, 25 April 2024).
