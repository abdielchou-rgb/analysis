## US Oil Supply and Demand

US crude production reached a new high of 13.3 mb/d in November, with annual growth remaining at +0.9 mb/d YoY. Demand was broadly flat MoM, with weakness in clean products demand. Crude imports were boosted as refinery maintenance season ended and run rates picked up significantly.

US crude production reached a new high of 13.3 mb/d, enabled by a boost in production from the traditional growth engine of shale - the Permian basin.

Crude production in November inched up to a new production record of 13.3 mb/d. The largest MoM increases were seen in PAD Districts 2, 3 and 4, led by the Permian basin of Texas and New Mexico. The weekly data implies production levels remaining at this level through December then falling off into January as a result of the extreme cold period that hit the US and shut in large areas of crude production, particularly in North Dakota.

## Supply Continued…

Baker Hughes data saw the oil rig count drop at the start of November, but rig additions in the latter half of the month left the rig count flat at 450 MoM. The steady rig count recovery seen in October seems to have lost steam in November. Of the major basins, the Permian added 2 rigs over November, while Eagle Ford and the Bakken were flat. Looking forward, the rig count rises going into December before maintaining at a steady level of \~455 rigs through till mid January. The rig count then begins to roll off week-on-week into February.

Despite a flat rig count, operators continue to be able to reach new production highs. This is partly due to increased well efficiency, with S&P Global estimating that overall industry rig efficient has increased by nearly 25% since Q1 18. Additionally, any rig count decline is coming from the price operators that run the least efficient rigs. Meanwhile public operators have been adding rigs back since early November, increasing the share of wells that are more efficient

Since the May PSM data release at end of August, the EIA has begun including a new line item in its reports called 'Transfers to Crude Oil Supply'. This line item has been in the region of 600-750 kb/d since it was introduced, and consensus is growing that this should be incorporated into the US crude supply figure. In the chart below, we track US supply with the transfers included, which gives a supply figure of 13.8 mb/d for November, significantly higher than the 13.3 mb/d 'field production' only figure. On a transfer-adjusted basis, crude production is down 0.2 mb/d MoM, from 14.0 mb/d in October to 13.8 mb/d in November. This contrasts with the production increase seen in the direct 'field production' figure.

Crude imports rose significantly in November, increasing by 800 kb/d MoM. This was led by increases in imports from Canada and Latin America, in particular Brazil. Refiners were incentivised to step up crude imports in November, as prices moderated off October highs and the heavy autumn refinery maintenance season ended. Crude exports declined marginally in November. Greater volumes to Taiwan, Germany and Spain were countered by a sharp decrease in volumes to China and the Netherlands.

## Demand

Oil demand was broadly flat in November maintaining at 20.7 mb/d. This corresponds to 2.5% YoY growth. Seasonal weakness in clean products and asphalt kept demand muted, despite large jumps up in fuel oil and naphtha demand. Jet-kero demand continued to drop in November, as flight numbers continued their gradual downward trend from the August peak. As year-end approaches, US oil demand for FY23 is averaging \~20.2 mb/d, up 1.1% on FY22.

Jet fuel demand declined by a further 4% in November to reach 1.6 mb/d. This was roughly in-line with the 2% drop MoM in number of US domestic and international flight numbers, according to BloombergNEF data.

Despite the MoM decline in air traffic, flight levels have recovered strongly during 2023. In terms of passenger traffic, a strong Thanksgiving demand helped push US domestic air traffic 9.1% above November 2019 levels. On a regional level, international air traffic out of North America was 7.4% higher than in November 2019 (IATA). The North America region was one of the first to recover in terms of air travel, hence why annual jet demand growth is slowing as the strong rebound in demand post-COVID had already begun last year.

## Inventories

US total crude and product inventories at the end of November were effectively flat on end-October levels, rising by only 4 mln bbls. The main drivers of the small inventory increase were crude and middle distillates. Despite a pick up in refinery runs, a build in crude stocks was possible due to elevated crude import levels and further domestic crude production increases. Looking forward into December, further pick up in refinery run rates will likely limit crude stock builds going forward. A large jump up in middle distillate runs in November, while demand turned down, enabled a boost in both net exports and stocks of middle distillates.

## Crude Adjustment Factor

The crude adjustment number is a figure the EIA reports on a monthly (and weekly) basis that acts as a balancing item for when the supply and demand data they have collected does not align. Prior to 2019, the crude adjustment factor was relatively minor, averaging \~50 mln bbls annually. However, the adjustment factor has attracted more notice in recent years when annual adjustments have exceeded 150 mln bbls.

There was a negative crude adjustment for the fourth consecutive month in November, equalling -315 kb/d. This used to be relatively rare, as the adjustment factor is usually positive, however this year has been marked by a large number of negative adjustments. This corresponds to a 9.5 mln bbls downward revision in crude supply over the course of November and takes the sum of adjustments for the year well into negative territory.
