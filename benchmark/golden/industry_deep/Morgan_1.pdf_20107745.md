# The Viewpoint: Trade – Improving fundamentals, rising tensions

The acceleration of Asia’s real exports in the past three months has lifted them above the 2021 peak. Forward-looking indicators suggest the export recovery will be sustained in the coming months. But at the same time, we are mindful that rising tensions may yet curtail this improvement.

## Key Takeaways

Asia’s exports had been on a path of gradual improvement since Aug-23, but the pace has picked up significantly in the past three months.

The recovery has been fairly broad-based across tech and non-tech exports.

This strength in exports is helping the region manage the potential growth downside from central banks having to maintain higher real rates.

■ The strength in global new orders relative to inventory and a better US growth outlook suggest to us that the export recovery will continue.

If trade tensions re-emerge in a meaningful manner, it would weigh on corporate confidence and capex, and consequently trade.

## Fundamentals are improving

Asia’s exports are rising again post-adjustment: After rising sharply in 2020-21 on the back of strong global goods demand, Asia’s exports peaked in Dec-21 before declining thereafter as goods demand began to normalize. Since reaching a trough in Dec-22, Asia’s exports had been modestly recovering since 2H23 but have now taken another leg higher since the turn of the year. This has meant real exports have now risen above the Dec-21 peak in Jan-Feb. The recovery in trade is playing out somewhat better than we initially expected. Importantly, the improvement has been relatively broad-based across the region and by product categories.

This trade cycle has been stronger than the pre-covid cycle: Taking a step back, this trade cycle has been stronger for Asia as compared to the post-GFC and pre-covid periods. Asia’s exports have been growing at a monthly annualized 4.4% since 2019, compared with 3.5% in 2008-19, though it remains slower than the 2000-08 average of 9.6%. Post GFC, the DM private sector had been deleveraging and this constrained private sector demand and consequently Asia’s exports. Today, the DM private sector balance sheet is no longer deleveraging and this has helped moved Asia’s exports onto a different trajectory post covid. To be sure, the initial rebound in goods was much sharper as there was a shift in consumption share towards goods spending between 2020 and 1H21. As economies reopened, the nature of the spending moved back towards services, which led to a period of digestion. But on the whole, trade growth has still been stronger so far in this cycle.

## What has been driving the improvement?

We delve into Asia’s exports by product and destination to gauge how broad-based the improvement has been. Both tech and non-tech exports have been doing better, and exports to most regions, excluding the Euro Area, are improving.

Tech exports are on an improving trend: For tech exports, which constitute about a quarter of the region’s exports, the stronger performance in the initial covid years meant the magnitude of adjustment on the way down was larger than non-tech exports. Indeed, on an indexed level basis, tech exports have largely lagged non-tech exports since late 2022. However, tech exports are now improving alongside non-tech exports. For the early reporter economies of China, India, Korea, Taiwan, and Japan (which account for 71% of the region’s exports) where March trade data is available, it is the case that tech exports continued to improve in March. Additionally, Korea’s first 20-days daily semiconductor exports suggest the strength in tech exports has largely held up in April.

Non-tech exports are faring better too: To be sure, Asia’s external demand recovery has not been limited to tech. From an end-use perspective, capital goods exports – which account for about a third of the region’s exports – have steadily improved after a prolonged period of weakness, benefiting from stronger US capex trends (see more details on this in later paragraphs). Commodity exports too (accounting for 20% of the region's exports) have stabilized as commodity prices have risen in recent months. In China, auto exports have continued to be strong as its manufacturers gain share in the global EV market.

By destination, the improvement has been broad-based, with the exception of exports to the Euro Area (which accounts for 12% of Asia’s exports). In particular, Asia’s exports to the US have led overall exports and have seen a strong uptick over the past three months.

## Will this recovery in trade be sustained?

The trade recovery has more room to run: We are drawing confidence from a few forward-looking indicators that suggest the recovery in trade will continue:

1) New orders to inventory ratio is improving: At an aggregate level, the new orders to inventory order ratio for G4 and select large EM economies – which tend to lead Asia’s real exports by six months – have risen to a 25-month high. Initially this improvement had been driven by a correction in inventories, but now an upturn in new orders is driving this ratio higher. At the same time, 80% of the economies are reporting a higher new orders to inventory ratio, up from just 20% a few months ago. The breadth of this recovery is providing us with comfort that it can be sustained.

2) A stronger outlook for the US economy: Our US economics team have lifted their US GDP growth forecast by a full percentage point – from a forecast of 1.9% for 2024 (on an annual average basis) during the last outlook round in Nov-23 to 2.9% currently as they take on board the impact of increased immigration and its effects on output and faster payroll growth (see Friday Finish – US Economics: What the New Immigration Means for Consumption, Apr 19). This means that instead of a deceleration in growth, our team is now forecasting that the US economy will accelerate this year.

Against this backdrop, both consumption and capex are doing better than was initially expected. Indeed, the trailing data on US imports bear this out. Auto imports have continued to trend upwards, while capital goods imports have picked up and have become the marginal driver of strength for imports. Excluding autos, consumer goods imports have kept steady in a range.

3) Tech spending to modestly recover: Our tech analysts foresee a modest recovery in tech spending, and so the strength that we have seen in tech exports should continue. At the moment, it is the case that tech demand, especially related to AI, is still very strong, while it could be the case that there is a slightly weaker than expected recovery in non-AI tech demand (see TSMC: Price hike to come with AI growth; reiterate OW, Apr 18). The latest read from our CIO survey suggests there will be a moderate recovery in spending expectations in 2024 (see Technology – Software & Services: 1Q24 CIO Survey – Budget tightness remains, Apr 9).

Tensions are rising

The risk of trade tensions appears to be materializing: Earlier in the year, we had written about the risks of trade tensions returning (see The Viewpoint: Trade Tensions, Supply Chain Diversification, and China, Mar 19). Since then, trade tensions seem to have gained momentum, especially in recent weeks. From the perspective of US-China trade tensions, the visit of Treasury Secretary Yellen in early April, when she warned about China’s overcapacity issues, and subsequently President Biden’s call to triple tariffs on steel and aluminum imports from China, amongst others, have raised the temperature. From a Europe-China perspective, German Chancellor Scholz also recently visited China and raised similar concerns as regards dumping and overproduction, though he did express some reservations about enacting trade protectionist measures when asked about this issue. Given this backdrop, and as we enter into US election season, we expect trade tensions to continue to rise. In this regard, the outcome of the US elections will be key in determining the outlook for trade tensions.

Mind the drag on corporate confidence and capex: If trade tensions rise meaningfully, we think the indirect effects arising from damage to corporate confidence and capex will matter more, as the effects will likely be quicker and more pervasive. This has the scope to weigh on corporate confidence and animal spirits, reducing the incentive for the corporate sector to invest, which would then be a drag on the trade cycle in the near term. While the re-emergence of trade tensions will likely accelerate supply-chain diversification efforts, this will only provide a partial offset for select economies and that too over the medium term, and the resulting lower demand would weigh on overall growth and be a net negative.

Summary

Trade matters: In sum, the improving fundamentals of the trade cycle are being met with rising trade tensions. The recent move up in exports across the region has been beneficial for the region.

Exports helping offset the downside from higher real rates: Specifically, exports have helped China to manage its growth dynamics at a time when domestic demand remains relatively weak. Strong exports so far this year is a key reason growth has been strong. Indeed, our China economics team estimates that net exports contributed 0.2pp to real GDP growth in 1Q24, a reversal of the 0.2pp drag on real GDP growth in 4Q23. For Asia ex China, stronger exports have also helped the region to be in a better position to deal with higher real rates. With the delay in Fed cuts, we have also pushed back our expectations for rate cuts across the region but have surmised that the support from exports and its attendant spillover effects to investment will mitigate the downside to the region’s growth.

Watch out for trade tension risks: We would be especially mindful of the potential downside risks to the region’s growth outlook as we enter the second half of the year, as the factor of rising trade tensions may outweigh improving fundamentals then.
