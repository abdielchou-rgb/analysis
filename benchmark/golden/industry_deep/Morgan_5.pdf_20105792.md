Dealers held a net short gamma position ahead of the January 2024 options expiry. Notably, the adjustment in dealers' gamma exposure exhibits an asymmetrical response to market movements. A downward shift of 10% in spot levels would intensify the short gamma position to an estimated USD 1400 million, accounting for about 7% of futures turnover. Conversely, an upward movement of the same magnitude would substantially reduce the gamma exposure to minimal levels. Such shifts in positioning amplify the market's sensitivity to unforeseen events, indicating that technical factors are likely contributing to heightened market volatility, particularly in the event of market corrections.

## Incorporating a low P/B ratio tilt into buyback strategy can enhance its

performance. While the TSE reforms encourage companies with low P/B ratios to take action for improvement, the government<sup>’</sup>s goal is still far away. The government targets to have 80% of TSE prime market trades trading above a 1x P/B ratio, while the current level is at 59%, indicating room for further growth. Following the strong TSE reform measures and relatively elevated Japanese stock prices, companies with low P/B will likely be more willing to engage in share buybacks announcement and execution. Our systematic backtest with monthly rebalance frequency (see more details here) demonstrates that buyback factor with a low P/B tilt outperformed both the buyback strategy without factor overlay and the broader market. Specifically, the strategy shows strong outperformance during the past 6 months when Japanese stock market remains range-bound (Figure 11).

## Buy Nikkei 2024 dividend futures

We recommend investors consider taking long positions in the Nikkei 2024 dividend futures. Our top-down estimates indicate potential upside of approximately 5%. For longer term dividend futures, we advise a more prudent approach due to possible selling pressure once the issuance of structured products resumes. This cautious stance should be understood within a broader context where we are fundamentally optimistic about Nikkei dividends. If the longer-dated dividend term structure shows an inversion (i.e., longer-term dividend futures trading at a discount to shorter-term futures), we recommend increasing long positions in dividend futures to take advantage of such weakness.

The anticipation of higher dividends in 2024 has escalated following the announcement of a special dividend by Trend Micro. At present, the 2024 Nikkei dividend futures are being traded at a rate that is 2% higher than our most conservative estimates, indicating a positive market outlook towards the potential growth of Nikkei dividends. What could fuel upside to 2024 dividend estimates? We foresee a number of potential catalysts. Firstly, the anticipated exit from deflation could bolster domestic consumption, subsequently enhancing earnings in sectors reliant on domestic demand, such as retail, food, and transportation (see here). This is further bolstered by the improvement in real wages, a result of the government<sup>’</sup>s policy to raise the minimum wage and successful cost pass-through efforts by companies. Similarly, a recovery in capital expenditure could benefit sectors like machinery and semi-conductors, leading to improved earnings (see here). Secondly, with the TSE reform gaining momentum in the coming year, companies have an added incentive to increase their payout ratio (see here). The government<sup>’</sup>s goal of having 80% of the TSE prime market trading higher than 1x Price to Book ratio (the ratio is currently 59%) is still a long way off, suggesting more room for growth. The special dividend announced by Trend Micro indicates the potential for more companies to follow suit, further boosting our dividend estimates, which currently factor in zero in our bottom-up estimates.

Our top-down model forecasts a level of 688 pts for Nikkei 2024 dividend futures. In response to the latency often found in analysts<sup>’</sup> estimate revisions, we have developed a top-down model to provide a more timely alternative for analyzing the fair value of dividend futures. This model is grounded in the historical interplay between three key factors: GDP growth, earnings growth, and the dividend payout ratio. The underlying rationale is that earnings growth is intrinsically linked to economic growth, and corporate dividend policy is partly driven by expected growth in earnings. A significant risk for long dividend futures investors is the potential resumption of structured product issuance. These products constitute a source of dividend supply risks, frequently triggering selling pressure in longer-dated tenors. Japan<sup>’</sup>s financial regulator has been scrutinizing this type of product since September 2022 (see here). With the FSA wrapping up its review of the structured product market, the likelihood of structured product issuance resuming in 2024 appears high. However, the exact timing remain uncertain. Consequently, we are adopting a more cautious stance on dividend futures in tenors from 2025 onward. While we recognize that longer-dated dividend

futures can benefit from our structurally bullish dividend thesis, we caution that a potential revival of structured products could depress longer-dated dividend futures, inflicting mark-to-market pains on long dividend future investors.

## Risks of Common Option Strategies

Risks to Strategies: Not all option strategies are suitable for investors; certain strategies may expose investors to significant potential losses. We have summarized the risks of selected derivative strategies. For additional risk information, please call your sales representative for a copy of “Characteristics and Risks of Standardized Options.<sup>”</sup> We advise investors to consult their tax advisors and legal counsel about the tax implications of these strategies. Please also refer to option risk disclosure documents.

Put Sale: Investors who sell put options will own the underlying asset if the asset<sup>’</sup>s price falls below the strike price of the put option. Investors, therefore, will be exposed to any decline in the underlying asset<sup>’</sup>s price below the strike potentially to zero, and they will not participate in any price appreciation in the underlying asset if the option expires unexercised.

Call Sale: Investors who sell uncovered call options have exposure on the upside that is theoretically unlimited.

Call Overwrite or Buywrite: Investors who sell call options against a long position in the underlying asset give up any appreciation in the underlying asset<sup>’</sup>s price above the strike price of the call option, and they remain exposed to the downside of the underlying asset in the return for the receipt of the option premium.

Booster : In a sell-off, the maximum realized downside potential of a double-up booster is the net premium paid. In a rally, option losses are potentially unlimited as the investor is net short a call. When overlaid onto a long position in the underlying asset, upside losses are capped (as for a covered call), but downside losses are not.

Collar: Locks in the amount that can be realized at maturity to a range defined by the put and call strike. If the collar is not costless, investors risk losing 100% of the premium paid. Since investors are selling a call option, they give up any price appreciation in the underlying asset above the strike price of the call option.

Call Purchase: Options are a decaying asset, and investors risk losing 100% of the premium paid if the underlying asset<sup>’</sup>s price is below the strike price of the call option.

Put Purchase: Options are a decaying asset, and investors risk losing 100% of the premium paid if the underlying asset<sup>’</sup>s price is above the strike price of the put option.

Straddle or Strangle: The seller of a straddle or strangle is exposed to increases in the underlying asset<sup>’</sup>s price above the call strike and declines in the underlying asset<sup>’</sup>s price below the put strike. Since exposure on the upside is theoretically unlimited, investors who also own the underlying asset would have limited losses should the underlying asset rally. Covered writers are exposed to declines in the underlying asset position as well as any additional exposure should the underlying asset decline below the strike price of the put option. Having sold a covered call option, the investor gives up all appreciation in the underlying asset above the strike price of the call option.

Put Spread: The buyer of a put spread risks losing 100% of the premium paid. The buyer of higher-ratio put spread has unlimited downside below the lower strike (down to zero), dependent on the number of lower-struck puts sold. The maximum gain is limited to the spread between the two put strikes, when the underlying is at the lower strike. Investors who own the underlying asset will have downside protection between the higher-strike put and the lower-strike put. However, should the underlying asset<sup>’</sup>s price fall below the strike price of the lower-strike put, investors regain exposure to the underlying asset, and this exposure is multiplied by the number of puts sold.

Call Spread: The buyer risks losing 100% of the premium paid. The gain is limited to the spread between the two strike prices. The seller of a call spread risks losing an amount equal to the spread between the two call strikes less the net premium received. By selling a covered call spread, the investor remains exposed to the downside of the underlying asset and gives up the spread between the two call strikes should the underlying asset rally.

Butterfly Spread: A butterfly spread consists of two spreads established simultaneously – one a bull spread and the other a bear spread. The resulting position is neutral, that is, the investor will profit if the underlying is stable. Butterfly spreads are established at a net debit. The maximum profit will occur at the middle strike price; the maximum loss is the net debit.
