## Will 1Q24 be 3Q23 Déjà Vu? Very Unlikely

In the third quarter of 2023, real GDP rocketed higher. The unexpected growth spurt led to hawkish Fedspeak that sent Treasury yields dramatically higher. The spotlight shone brightly on the Atlanta Fed's GDPNow tracker, which provided the earliest indication of the quarter's strength.

This week, the first estimate of 4Q23 GDP kicked off 1Q24 GDP tracking from the Atlanta Fed: 3.0%, which compares to the Blue Chip consensus at 1% and Morgan Stanley economists at 2.0%. Does an economy growing at 2 or 3% in real terms – near or far above potential growth, which the median FOMC participant projects at 1.8% – need a rate cut in March? Seems unlikely.

At the same time, is the economy really growing at a 2 or 3% pace in January? Also seems unlikely. As we discussed last week, frigid temperatures to start January don't seem conducive to growth. Indeed, in its quarterly earnings release, Visa Inc. acknowledged slower domestic spending through January 21, 2024, on extreme weather impacting inperson transactions.

We also continue to see a clear disconnect between how small businesses across the US think about sales and how the Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) accounts for them; note the divergence between the BEA's measure of real final sales in the 4Q23 GDP report and the NFIB's account of actual sales changes (see Exhibit 1 and Exhibit 2 ).

## 3 Reasons to Expect USD strength; 3 Reasons to Stay Neutral

We continue to forecast that the USD will rise against G10 currencies by the end of 2024 for several reasons, including these three:

First, our economics colleagues expect US growth to be significantly stronger than the average sell-side economist.

We expect the US economy to grow by 2.1% this year – faster than the pace at which it has grown since 2000 and well above the 1.5% median forecast among economists polled by Bloomberg. Conversely, we expect the UK and China to grow more slowly than most other economists do (see Exhibit 13 and Exhibit 14 ).

Our expectation for faster-than-expected US growth and slower-than-expected growth abroad means that data prints are likely to surprise to the upside in the US and to the downside abroad.

Second, our economics colleagues forecast that European inflation will remain subdued in the coming months relative to inflation in the US. While we expect core US CPI inflation pressures to decelerate, we expect HICP to remain at or below a 2% annualized rate in the euro area for several months.

Furthermore, recent declines in European gas prices raise "an increased risk to undershooting the 2%, in particular in 2025" in the ECB's projections.

As a result, market pricing for ECB policy may continue to reflect the possibility of one or more 50bp cuts from the ECB later this year, with risks that the market-implied possibility of 50bp cuts increases. Such an outcome would likely move front-end yield differentials in the USD's favor.

Third, in recent months the USD has traded closely in line with the price of stocks of firms that derive a significant proportion of their revenues from China ( Exhibit 15 ). This "China exposure" equity basket is available on Bloomberg as MSXXCHNA Index.

As sentiment on the Chinese growth outlook (as measured by this equity basket) has declined, the US dollar has gained, and vice versa.

Notably, the broad USD has recently traded more closely in line with the prices of the China-exposed stocks than with the broader S&P 500, outright Treasury yields, or yield differentials ( Exhibit 16 ).

Investor sentiment on the China growth outlook is likely to remain downbeat, given our expectation that policy easing so far has been reactive and insufficient, keeping the risks of a debt-deflation loop alive and that policymakers will maintain a modest pace of reactive easing. Our equity strategy colleagues recently adjusted their targets for China equity indexes and continue to recommend underweighting China equities.

Despite these factors, we are not currently recommending long USD positions for several reasons, including these three:

First, our rates strategy team expects that 5y Treasury yields will decline in the coming weeks (see United States | Making growth great again ), limiting the extent to which yield differentials are likely to move in the USD’s favor.

Second, our equity research team notes that strong returns for the S&P market cap weighted index often tend to come between the last Fed hike and the first Fed cut. Potential further S&P 500 gains may reduce upside risks for the USD. That said, equity market gains so far in January (S&P +3%) have not prevented the DXY from rising over 2% month to date.

Third, our high-frequency fair value model (see discussion here) suggests that a number of G10 currencies are undervalued relative to the USD.

# Interest Rate Strategy

## United States

Making growth great again: Have we seen this movie before? It was just 3 months ago that yields were rising based on strong GDP growth (Atlanta Fed GDPNow hovered around 5%), and the Fed was taking notice. The the Fed suggested that high growth wasn't as concerning, given the supply side improvements, and the yield rise reversed substantially. This week's market reaction to PMIs and GDPNow suggests that markets are making growth great again.

Are supply-side improvements 'largely complete'? Markets either do not appreciate the role of supply, or think that the supply-side improvement is "largely complete" as "several" FOMC participants noted in the FOMC minutes. However, markets might be missing that (1) labor supply, driven by immigration, could continue until the current administration stays, i.e., until the election, and (2) weakness in China PPI and China import price indices – part of global supply chains – augurs well lower core goods inflation in the US.

January FOMC: Rate cuts on the Fed's mind? We stay biased long duration with 5y notes into the FOMC meeting, but do not see a major move happening on the back of the FOMC meeting itself, especially as the Fed waits for the CPI annual revisions on Feb 9. Even if the FOMC is neutral for duration, it sees potential for yields to move lower amid a cooling inflation backdrop.

Improved prospects for curve steepeners: We examine historical curve moves and classify curve moves as normal (bull steepening. and bear flattening), or abnormal (bear steepening or bull flattening); we also identify normal and abnormal regimes. We see that the market had been in the abnormal regime for some time but has exited that recently, opening the door for a cleaner bull steepening of the curve, in line with our bullish call on duration.

## Euro area

On duration, we note how we will enter into a week full of macro risks, predominantly stemming from the Jan flash inflation print, the Fed FOMC, and the NFP payroll number. We study market reactions during the last 3 January inflation prints, where the volatility around the print created meaningful surprises vs consensus, with core surprising to the upside in all instances over the last 3 years. We note that usually the market starts to build up shorts heading into the print, while duration tends to cheapen on a median basis by \~20bp in the 15 working days following an upside surprise.

Nevertheless, with the information at our disposal, we think that overall, there could be a potential asymmetry given the observed positioning. With the risks around all the events listed for next week, we lean into a long-duration position, trying to limiting overall directionality giving the uncertainties previously mentioned. We like buying the RXH4 136/137/138.5 broken call fly for 6 cents and a max payout at expiry being worth 18x the premium paid.

On ASW, we reiterate that most factors call for a cheapening of ASW in 2024, however the path will likely not be a straight line, and ASW could be trading sideways and widening in sympathy with seasonal patterns. Until now, BTPs have performed well relative to core and should this continue, we see downside risks to our forecast for 1Q24 of 59bp on the Bund ASW (which pencilled in a BTP/Bund at 220bp). To quantify the risk, we assume a 10y BTP/Bund at 160bp and V2X at 14%, i.e., close to current levels, and get a March fair value of \~46bp (using the monthly model), which would bring the potential target range to 35-45bp, accounting for a discount.

In the near-term, the core ASW dislocations relative to our models' fair values suggest the recent tightening might be overstretched. The Bobl ASW cheapened \~6bp since the beginning of the year, diverging significantly from historical price action. The discounts on the Bund and Buxl ASW versus our model plead for the seasonal widening to occur this year. A widening of the Bobl ASW should support our 32s53s OAT ASW box (short 53s).

## United Kingdom

Next week's focus will shift to the February MPC, which offers a compelling opportunity to position for a dovish tilt from the BoE, with our favoured expression being receiving May'24 MPC. From a valuation standpoint, front-end MPCs look quite attractive to receive as the repricing of lower BoE's cuts expectations in recent weeks limits the downside risk in our view.

Meanwhile, the market likely underestimates how quickly the BoE could turn dovish with the upcoming MPC being an opportunity for the BoE to shift tone, and we believe a dovish market reaction would be the most likely scenario. First, the BoE's projections will likely bring near-term inflation forecasts meaningfully lower, and second, a change in the voting split could also trigger a dovish market reaction by signalling the end of the tightening bias.

The demand/supply dynamics will also turn supportive in the very short term, with limited gross supply while coupon flows, index extension, and redemptions support the demand backdrop. Having said that, we remain cautious on UK duration as the upcoming budget poses downside risks for gilts valuations; hence, we keep our medium-term steepening and short ASW bias.

## Japan

We discuss the market implications of the January BoJ MPM. It might be the first time that the BoJ has officially communicated the future monetary policy path after removing NIRP. BoJ Governor Ueda explained that there should be little need for particularly abrupt or “discontinuous” policy adjustments if the economy continues to perform in line with the central bank’s envisaged scenario.

In other words, the policy rate would be hiked only very gradually provided that goods inflation keeps slowing and services prices maintain a gradual uptrend with backing from wage growth. With overall inflation continuing to slow down and USD/JPY and overseas interest rates now at least a little “calmer” than they were last October, there would appear to be little reason for the market to price in a particularly aggressive succession of rate hikes.

We see the front end of the curve already pricing in a gradual rate hike path, and thus see it as attractive to receive on an outright basis. We thus suggest switching from previously recommended 2s5s steepeners (which were overweighting the short end via 1.5 vs. 1 DV01 weights) to receive an outright 2y OIS position. We

suggest sticking with 10y OIS receiver vs. 30y JGB short (DV01 1 vs. 1) and 30y ASW short positions in expectation of supply/demand conditions remaining comparatively unfavorable beyond the 30y sector of the cash bond market.

## The tussle between growth and inflation reignites

Have we heard this story before? Growth is strong, while inflation is cooling. Despite that fact that inflation is the Fed's mandate, and growth is not, markets choose to focus on growth, and yields rise rapidly. The Fed follows suit and shows concern about growth (September 2023 FOMC meeting). And eventually the Fed sounds less concerned about growth (November and December FOMC meetings) and discusses the role of improving supply in lifting potential GDP growth, as well as cooling inflation. In turn, yields retrace most of the rise.

The price action in the last week was a reminder of the same story and a surprise to us. The upside surprises in the S&P PMIs and then ultimately US GDP growth for December and 4Q23 led markets to price higher yields and lower probability of rate cuts. Markets

are making growth great again.

The market's reaction could be based on two things (1) either markets have not fully appreciated the idea that based on supply-side improvements, high growth can exist without threatening the pursuit of high inflation (see Powell's quote from December below), or (2) markets think that all supply-side gains have played out, suggesting rising growth is a threat to inflation.

Powell on supply improvements, December 2023: So, this was the year when labor force participation picked up, where immigration picked up, where the distortions to supply-and-demand from the pandemic, you know, the shortages and the bottlenecks really began to unwind. So, we had significant supply-side gains with strong demand, and we got what looks like a 2.5%-plus or a little bit more than that, growth year, at a time when potential growth this year might even have been higher than that just because of the healing on the supply side. So, that was a surprise to just about everybody.

December FOMC minutes on the viability of supply side gains: Several participants assessed that healing in supply chains and labor supply was largely complete, and therefore that continued progress in reducing inflation may need to come mainly from further softening in product and labor demand, with restrictive monetary policy continuing to play a central role. A few others saw potential for further improvements in supply.

Why would the market think that supply-side improvements may have played out? This idea first showed up in the December FOMC minutes themselves (see grey box above). Several FOMC participants saw the improvements from the supply side as largely complete, while "a few others" saw more supply improvements. If markets make the same assumption, it can help explain why markets are focusing on growth-side surprises and ignoring the continued progress in inflation (see Exhibit 19 and Exhibit 20 ).

But should markets feel so confident that supply-side improvement is complete? After all, the FOMC minutes do not specify why it sees the supply-side improvement as complete. There are two threads here: whether the labor supply increase is complete, and whether the improvement in global supply chains is complete.

Starting with the labor side, it is pretty clear that the rise in labor supply comes down to a rise in inflows from immigration ("born outside the US" in the BLS data) – see Exhibit 21 . What is not clear is why this increase in foreign-born labor will stop. The increase in foreign-born labor force can be linked to the immigration policy of the current administration, one which is expected to continue at least until the next election - i.e., the next 10-11 months, especially if there is no immigration deal passed in the current session of Congress.

The second type of supply-side improvement mentioned by Powell was the improvement in the global supply chains. According to our economists' global supply chain index, supply chains were near their optimal levels in the last few years (see Exhibit 25 ), a major improvement that has contributed to cooling of core goods CPI in the last few months.

Some investors point out that rising tensions and the blockage in the Red Sea has increased supply chains distortions again. Our global economics team noted that the Red Sea disruptions are not yet sufficient to affect their US inflation forecasts.

Can disinflation risks from China rise again? Another feature of global supply chains that has influenced US inflation in the last few years has been the deflation and disinflation that has emanated from low-cost manufacturing in China, which has had a palpable role in lowering core goods costs in the US.

What is notable is that our China economists see not only a very weak non-commodities PPI for now, but also see the risk of stimulus in China being directed more towards the supply side and less towards consumer-driven reflation policies. This risk of continued disinflation from China could extend the supply-side improvement that has helped US inflation cooling so far.

Overall, we see continued downside risks to inflation while we suggest fading the market's growth-driven upside in yields. We suggest staying long duration in long 5s.

## Improving prospects for curve steepeners

Changes in the yield curve since last fall have been characterized by large movements in the long end, particularly as the curve bear steepened between August and October. In Exhibit 27 , we show that much of the cumulative change in 2s30s since August has been driven by bear steepening and bull flattening.

Exhibit 27: Cumulative change in 2s30s and cumulative change resulting from bear steepening and bull flattening since August 1, 2023
![](images/4f51cf40bd2a4161ce6ccd5651292672609691e5c822acbc2397a0ec8c255e23.jpg)
Source: Morgan Stanley Research, Bloomberg

Exhibit 28: Percentage of days with 2s30s bull steepening and bear flattening in the trailing 3 months, 2000-2024
![](images/e7dc152fbfff06546c83c4fc91a16f8e725c64d77979fc8a905b92b1d8466a30.jpg)
Source: Morgan Stanley Research, Bloomberg

However, long-end driven curve changes are not necessarily predictable by changes in fundamental variables such as Fed expectations, which typically correspond to bear flattening or bull steepening. As a result, trading these "abnormal" curve movements can be difficult unless there is a clear theme around Treasury demand or supply. Furthermore, once long-end led movements start, they tend to persist: bear steepening and bull flattening occur in sequence, just as bull steepening and bear flattening do.

We can think of curve movements as falling into two regimes: (1) "normal", front-end led movements (bear flattening and bull steepening, as in Exhibit 28 ) and (2) "abnormal", long-end led movements (bear steepening and bull flattening, as in Exhibit 29 ). As Exhibit 28 and Exhibit 29 show, the frequencies of bull steepening and bear flattening over 3-month windows tend to align, just as the frequencies of bear steepening and bull flattening do.

Exhibit 30 makes clear the persistence of abnormal movements: the prevalence of abnormal movements (bull steepening and bear flattening) is a strong predictor of the prevalence of abnormal movements 3 months in the future, with a positive linear relationship carrying an R-squared of 52%.

Exhibit 29: Percentage of days with 2s30s bear steepening and bull flattening in the trailing 3 months, 2000-2024
![](images/3431417bdab466584208bd7b1d619cb29680dd18fe9b87784f8cfedf4d4757eb.jpg)
Source: Morgan Stanley Research, Bloomberg

Exhibit 30: Frequency of 2s30s bear steepening and bull flattening vs. frequency of 2s30s bear steepening and bull flattening 3m prior

% days with bear steepening or bull flattening

![](images/1e4e06f5f80aad0f0d5d18662e8d222c0e4da7e785119652fceb216bc930afad.jpg)
Source: Morgan Stanley Research, Bloomberg

Given the existence of two different regimes for curve movements, we want to identify a way to determine which regime we are in. To do so, we expand our definition of "abnormal" movements to include all curve changes led by the long end. These movements include bear steepening, bull flattening, and curve twists in which the larger movement occurred in the long end.

We measure the percentage of day-over-day changes in 2s30s that are led by the long end and use the following rule to distinguish between a normal and abnormal regime:

1. If long-end led curve movements represent over 50% of the combined magnitudes of daily changes in 2s30s over the past 3 months, then we are in an abnormal regime.

2. Otherwise, we are in a normal regime.

Furthermore, to switch between regimes, we require the percentage of curve movements to stay above or below 50% for at least 5 days.

In Exhibit 31 , we show the percentage of curve changes led by the long end, with abnormal regimes highlighted. Exhibit 32 focuses on the last year, showing that the curve entered an abnormal regime in both early and late 2023.

Our indicator for curve regimes, by construction, is lagging: we require 3 months of lagged data to determine whether we are in a normal or abnormal regime. However, the regimes are persistent: outside of the two periods when curve movements were led overwhelmingly by the long end due to the Fed being at the effective lower bound, abnormal regimes last for 116 days on average, and normal regimes last for 196 days. Once we enter a regime, we can be somewhat certain that we will stay there for some time.

Exhibit 31: Percentage of curve movements led by the long end with regime indicators, 2000-2024
![](images/4298934062973465c9bf9597b7886b4c66ccf409ed5a50852386c697d76fdbd4.jpg)
Source: Morgan Stanley Research, Bloomberg

Exhibit 32: Percentage of curve movements led by the long end with regime indicators, since 2023
![](images/928ff6d7b0eb5f77e5a9f89bc56e60c61ba205889bb65c619b202cb52449672d.jpg)
Source: Morgan Stanley Research, Bloomberg

Based on our regime indicator, we entered an abnormal regime on September 29, 2023 and exited it on January 16, 2024. As a result, we are now in a normal regime in which we can expect more predictable curve movements, led by the front end. Given these improving prospects for the curve to move normally, we think being long the belly of the curve – aka long 5s – seems an optimal point to express duration longs.

Supply and seasonal cheapening. Supply in itself isn't a factor that usually drives the trend of ASW, in our opinion. For instance in 2022, supply that had to be absorbed by markets (which we measure with gross supply - gross QE) increased significantly (see Exhibit 51 ), while the Bund ASW reached new highs in the same year, peaking at 100bp+, on the back of increased excess liquidity and wider BTP/Bund.

Supply is nonetheless not entirely irrelevant for ASW price action as seasonality analysis suggests; ASW tend to tighten ahead of supply and richen in the aftermath. This year again, the compression in ASW was likely fuelled by the heavy supply in the first three weeks of January.

In the coming months, we estimate gross supply-gross QE will remain elevated. As Exhibit 51 illustrates, the six-month rolling average of gross supply - gross QE (in DV01) should be high relative to history, with a local peak in June. In a context where volatility is rangy and the BTP/Bund no longer drives ASW (see below), such dynamics on supply can keep repo cheap, alongside the decline in excess liquidity, and so might represent a further tightening risk.

## What did the BoJ have to say about the subsequent trajectory of its policy rate?

When it comes to the impact on interest rate markets, the precise timing of the NIRP exit will probably be less important than the subsequent trajectory of BoJ policy rate hikes. Governor Kazuo Ueda’s post-meeting press conference on January 23 marked the first time that he had alluded to this trajectory.

## (our translation of Japanese text)

[In response to a reporter] Given our current views on the outlook for inflation, the economy, and financial conditions, our intention is to manage monetary policy so as to prevent any major discontinuities.

My understanding of your question is that you’re asking me whether we’ll be taking the subsequent trajectory of interest rates into consideration when deciding on a NIRP exit, and my answer is “of course”… Given the current state of the economy we believe that we should be able to manage policy in a way that avoids any particularly significant discontinuities.

One thing we can probably say is that we expect monetary conditions to remain extremely accommodative for some time to come even after achievement of the price stability target comes into sight and NIRP is exited.

Ueda’s basic message was that if the economy continues to perform in line with the central bank’s envisaged scenario, then there should be little need for particularly abrupt or “discontinuous” policy adjustments. In other words, the policy rate would be hiked only very gradually provided that goods inflation keeps slowing and services prices maintain a gradual uptrend with backing from wage growth.

The official minutes for the December meeting also indicate that the BoJ has already started to discuss the post-NIRP trajectory of its policy rate: “Members also discussed policy conduct from a somewhat long-term perspective. A few members expressed the view that the Bank was likely to continue with substantial monetary easing for the time being, even after termination of the negative interest rate policy and the yield curve control framework.”

Ueda did of course stop short of hinting as to what might be meant by “gradual”, with much obviously ultimately likely to depend on what happens to the economy and prices after NIRP is actually ended. Markets have thus been left with little to work with as of now.

That said, with overall inflation continuing to slow down and USD/JPY and overseas interest rates now at least a little “calmer” than they were last October, there would as of now appear to be little reason for the market to price in a particularly aggressive succession of rate hikes.

Markets are currently fully pricing in a BoJ rate increase to 0.25% within the next year and are also looking for subsequent hikes at a pace of around 25bp per year (see Exhibit 68 ). We consider it “fair” to be pricing in such a gradual trajectory at this juncture and see little prospect of a dramatic upward repricing unless inflation should start to accelerate once again.

That said, it is conceivable that markets could start to envisage the BoJ reaching a higher terminal rate (however gradually), in which case we would expect the belly zone of the curve (rather than the short end) to be at particular risk of underperformance.

## Suggested positioning

It will likely be difficult for markets to price in a much sharper BoJ rate hike trajectory for at least the time being given Governor Ueda’s aforementioned indication that – based on the central bank’s current economic outlook (gradually higher services inflation but lower goods inflation) – he expects “extremely accommodative” conditions to be maintained even after a NIRP exit. In this regard, January's Tokyo CPI print already showed a decent deceleration (see Exhibit 66 ).

Moreover, our US rates strategists are now starting to see greater downside risk to US inflation that should be supportive for long-duration positions in the UST space.

Downside risk should be comparatively limited at the short end, in our view, given that it is already priced for the BoJ’s policy rate to be hiked to 0.25% within the next year and by a further 25bp each year thereafter (see Exhibit 68 ), which makes outright longs look like a reasonable prospect to enjoy the attractive carry + rolldown (2y: 11.6bp/6m).

## EUR/GBP | Better call vol

Governor Bailey and the BoE's communiqués may not formally confirm that the cutting cycle is starting soon. But the implication of a dovish forecast revision could make this clear to the market anyway, in line with our economics team's base case for the first cut in May.

EUR/GBP is uniquely sensitive to rate differentials and insensitive to risk appetite. Meanwhile, implied vols for the pair are near post-Brexit lows despite the ECB and BoE meetings and important economic data.

Investors are short EUR/GBP to an elevated degree, reflecting the broader dominance of 'carry' as an FX theme and EUR/GBP's high carry/vol ratio. This creates risks that the market may be wrong-footed in the event of a dovish BoE surprise.

We caution investors not to keep EUR/GBP topside expressions on for too long. Market focus post-BoE could quickly pivot away from monetary policy and back to fiscal policy. The March 6 budget could bring additional fiscal expansion, bolstering growth and rate expectations and GBP along with it.

## NOK & SEK | "Takk" or "tack", what's the difference?

In G10 FX, the term "nordics" is often used to refer to Norway and Sweden in one breath. While they do exhibit similarities, at least geographically, they also have their own idiosyncracies.

In flow terms, both NOK and SEK are traded against dollars and euros in much more balanced way than other G10 currencies. This results in much more balanced correlations to US and EU factors (for the respective CCY/USD and CCY/EUR

## pairs).

However, differences become slightly more pronounced when looking at trade. While trade between these two nations is significant, and this economic interdependence is likely responsible for the correlation between the two, their export partners differ. As a result, the impact of this can be found in the price action of the respective currencies and the sensitivity to various equity markets.

Finally, differences are at their greatest when considering energy-related commodities. With significant differences in quantities exported, NOK stands out with a higher sensitivity. Nevertheless, the sensitivity of NOK varies greatly across commodities and market conditions. The currency exhibits outsized sensitivity to oil, in particular during market rallies.

We continue to recommend long NOK/SEK (and short GBP/NOK) positions, flagging the one-sided sensitivity of NOK to oil as an additional support.

CAD | BoC reaction

We maintain short EUR/JPY positions. A deeper rate cut trajectory could end up being priced in if European data continue to come in on the weak side, in which case EUR/JPY could start to be seen as having greater downside potential.

In G10 FX, the term "nordics" is often used to refer to Norway and Sweden in one breath. While they do exhibit similarities, such as how they express thanks ("takk" in Norway and "tack" in Sweden), they also have their own idiosyncracies.

We examine various similarities and differences of the two currencies below, starting with flow and ending with the current economic outlook.

## Flow

An interesting characteristic that both NOK and SEK display compared to the rest of G10 currencies is how evenly distributed their flows are between USD and EUR.

According to the Bank of International Settlements (BIS) Triennial Survey, the clearlyfavored counterpart for G10 currencies is the US dollar ( Exhibit 92 ).

However, for NOK and SEK, the USD dominance is much less evident, making up less than 50% of average daily turnover. For these currencies, the EUR is responsible for the majority of remaining turnover ( Exhibit 93 ).

One of these differences is found in their exports. Whereas Sweden's second-greatest exporting partner is the US, Norway's is the United Kingdom.

Moreover, with pension funds currently likely to be a little overweight domestic bonds (as discussed in Japan | Back to duration, and back to carry ), it is possible that (remaining) monthly portfolio rebalancing flows may have been boosting USD/JPY to at least some extent. This hypothesis is supported by the fact that foreign bonds have been seeing quite significant inflows (see Exhibit 111 ).

## So…all up to the Fed?

This week’s BoJ monetary policy meeting and Governor Ueda’s subsequent press conference appear to have been interpreted in a generally hawkish light, with the quarterly Outlook for Economic Activity and Prices update expressing optimism about the prospect of a “virtuous cycle between wages and prices” and Ueda offering some thoughts as to what might happen to the policy rate beyond an exit from NIRP. USD/JPY did show somewhat of a downward bias while Ueda was speaking, but actual declines proved shortlived.

While Ueda did hint that “normalization” is set to commence in the relatively near future, he also suggested that monetary conditions will remain “extremely accommodative” even beyond a NIRP exit provided that the economy and inflation do not end up deviating too dramatically from the BoJ’s currently envisaged scenario.

The underlying implication – given the BoJ’s current inflation forecasts – is that rapid tightening is unlikely (as argued by our economists in Measuring Japan's Neutral Yield Curve: Shifting to Normal Economy, October 16, 2023) if inflation stabilizes at around +2%.

This suggests that FX market participants have little reason to position for a significant (Japan-versus-the-world) convergence of monetary policy stances and may thus help to explain why JPY did not end up strengthening more dramatically.

JPY cross rates may thus continue to be driven in significant part by the policy paths of other central banks. A “soft landing” currently appears to be the main scenario in the US as growth-related data stay strong and risk sentiment remains buoyant, with pricing of Fed rate cuts remaining commensurately subdued and expected carry prospects as such still looking supportive for USD/JPY (see Exhibit 115 ).

That said, our US rates team expects Fed rate cut pricing to start being ramped up once again given the risk of worsening data on the back of recent bad weather as well as a sharp slowdown in leading indicators for rent inflation, and is accordingly bullish on duration. The natural corollary is that USD/JPY could end up facing at least some downward pressure via the policy convergence narrative.

Any convergence of monetary policy stances is perhaps likely to be more significant for Europe than for the US. The ECB matched the expectations of most by leaving monetary policy unchanged this month, but President Lagarde hinted at her post-meeting press conference that the possible timing of a rate cut might have been discussed (albeit with Governing Council members ultimately reaching a consensus that such a discussion was “premature”).

The overall tone appeared to be dovish, with greater weight placed on downside risks to economic growth and inflation than on upside risks.

A deeper rate cut trajectory could end up being priced in if European data continue to come in on the weak side (see Exhibit 116 ), in which case EUR/JPY could start to be seen as having greater downside potential. We thus continue to recommend sticking with EUR/ JPY shorts.

## Bills to absorb swings in the Treasury's financing need for 2024

As discussed previously, we expect bills to reflect any changes with the timing of QT’s taper. It’s important to highlight that the Fed is currently re-investing UST paydowns above its caps via auction add-ons. This means that the purchases are in addition to the announced auctions and do not impact how much the market must absorb at the time of auction.

For example, take a hypothetical month in which we have \$300bn of coupon auctions and there are \$200bn of coupons maturing (\$100bn owned by the Fed and \$100 owned by the market). If the Fed did QT at \$60bn, it would then reinvest \$40bn via add-ons. The Treasury would then raise \$100bn (300bn – 200bn) + \$40bn (via add-ons) in cash for a total \$140bn cash increase. If taper was at \$30bn, then the Fed would reinvest \$70bn and the Treasury would raise \$170bn in cash for that month.

Hence, the total financing need would be reduced by \$30bn and the TGA would be \$30bn higher for this hypothetical month. At the same time, the market would see \$300bn of coupon auctions and only \$100bn of privately owned maturities for a \$200bn increase in market-owned supply in both scenarios. The impact to supply would then be over time and be dependent on what Treasury decides to do on the back of this extra cash.

To recap, if taper starts sooner (June), the size of these total add-ons for 2024 will be larger by \$110bn relative to our base case and by about \$180bn if you compare it to a full 2024 of QT at its current pace. The reason for reducing issuance by the \$110bn in bills is that that the alternative (reducing coupons) could complicate things and lead Treasury to:

• Push even further when bills stay within 15-20% of total debt. If you reduce coupons, then bills would turn out to be an even higher portion of total debt over the coming months.

• Continue relying on bills at a time in which liquidity in the front-end is starting to come into balance as the RRP drains to zero and QT starts to bite in 2H24.

• Reverse all the progress the Treasury has made in increasing coupons since the August refunding. Looking ahead beyond 2024, we think the Treasury would like to eventually keep increasing coupon issuance as rates come down. Reducing coupons in 2024 adds complications as Treasury would have to eventually reverse course again towards even larger coupon increases, potentially causing an unwarranted market impact.

Given attractive yields and still-ample liquidity in the front-end, we expect demand for bills to remain strong. As shown in Exhibit 144 , 3m bills vs. OIS have remained tight despite a significant surge in net supply. At the same time, a depleted RRP in 2H24 could create some upwards pressure to the bills – OIS spread. On the other hand, a moderation in net bills supply and steady inflows into MMFs should limit how much bills – OIS widens.

In short, this strategy buys futures when the Simple Moving Average Crossover (SMAX) generates both a long and a short signal, given the historical outperformance of long signals traded long and underperformance of short signals traded short. Given that the SMAX could generate both a long and a short signal within the predefined holding period, an investor may have a 200% long position since each of the two signals would be traded in separate portfolio sleeves.
