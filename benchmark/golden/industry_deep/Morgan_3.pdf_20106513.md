# US bond futures rollover outlook

March 2024 / June 2024

This roll cycle is different from recent experience, with several different factors at play in influencing calendar spreads in different sectors. The macro backdrop is interesting as well. Although Fed expectations – and therefore financing rates – should remain stable, tail risks continue to linger and could bias repo rates lower if realized.

Carry remains negative, and early delivery is a significant risk in all sectors. Longs who wish to avoid delivery should roll before first notice day. Also, calendar spreads are directional with yield levels in all sectors, and we recommend rolling positions on a bpv-weighted basis.

CTD switch optionality is significant in the classic bond contract, and also – more unusually – in the back-month ultra-long bond contract. In the classic bond contract, option values are overpriced and basis convergence in the front points to calendar spread widening. In the June WN sector, a switch to the high coupon Nov 53s is likely and would result in much lower wildcard optionality if that were to occur. This interplay between wildcard and CTD switch optionality, in addition to CTD relative value considerations, leaves us bearish on the WN weighted calendar spread.

Net commercial longs are elevated in the TU, FV, TY, and UXY contracts, and we expect weighted calendar spreads in these sectors to be biased narrower due to the resulting technicals. In the UXY sector, a potential flattening of the curve between CTDs will likely add to the narrowing bias.

• Lastly, should tail risks escalate causing market expectations of aggressive Fed easing, forward financing rates would fall and bias calendar spreads wider. This effect would also be amplified in all but the TU and WN sectors, because of the fact that back-month CTDs have high coupons, meaning carry could turn positive and futures could price to last delivery day. While not our baseline view, this remains a tail risk that is important to consider during this roll period.

## Overview

The March-June Treasury futures roll cycle comes yet again at a volatile time in the markets. Stronger than expected economic data (NFP, Claims, ISM, and most recently Friday<sup>’</sup>s CPI) have pushed yields steadily higher by 30-40bp in recent weeks. At the same time, concerns around regional banks<sup>’</sup> CRE exposure continues to linger and is weighing on markets. It is worth highlighting that there aren<sup>’</sup>t signs of a broader stress in markets, and Fedspeak over the past week indicates that the Fed likely also views such concerns as localized to a few banks. As seen in Figure 1, last week<sup>’</sup>s messaging from Fed speakers suggests that the Fed (i) is not reading too much into the recent strength in labor markets, (ii) continues to anticipate cutting rates at some point, but remains patient in wanting to see sustained and broadening disinflation, and (iii) sees regional banking sector issues as limited to smaller banks and unlikely to derail the broader policy path.

All of this is of course relevant for Treasury financing rates, which must price in nearterm Fed expectations and which have sometimes been volatile in recent roll cycles. In particular, a key determinant of calendar spreads involving H4 and M4 contracts is the forward repo rate between early March and early June. Currently, forward repo between 3/1/2024 (optimal delivery date for March contracts) and 6/1/2024 (optimal delivery date for June contracts) is priced squarely between levels consistent with a hold in both March and May meetings and a 25bp ease in May (Figure 2). This is in line with our economists<sup>’</sup> view where they look for the first ease to occur in June, with a reasonable chance for May (see Maybe May, but March less likely, M. Feroli, 1/31/2024), and is in line with the Fed commentary. As such, financing rates are not obviously biased in any direction and will likely remain stable in the next few weeks. Of course, one tail risk to this view is that regional bank fears continue to worsen and become more widespread, which could cause markets to price in aggressive Fed cuts and bias financing rates lower. To be clear, this is not our modal expectation, and we expect financing rates to be mostly stable during the roll period and not impact calendar spreads, while being mindful of this tail risk.

Carry continues to be negative across all contracts thanks to the elevated level of financing rates. With CTD carry negative, futures contracts are priced to first delivery day, and early delivery was a common occurrence in the last quarter, even in the contracts where delivery optionality was high (Figure 3). Early delivery is once again highly likely in many sectors and a significant risk in all sectors during this roll cycle; therefore, we recommend that longs who would like to avoid taking delivery roll their positions before first notice day.

At the risk of sounding repetitive, investor positioning will continue to play an important role in this cycle as open interest in the futures complex is once again at record highs (Figure 4) and market depth is yet to recover to 2022 levels (Figure 5). What this means is that the impact of any technical position imbalance between commercial longs and shorts could be exacerbated due to the sheer volume of contracts that need to be rolled during a compacted time frame in a relatively poor liquidity environment (see Investor Positioning).

Delivery optionality and basis convergence will be a relevant factor in this roll cycle, both in the ultra-long bond and the classic bond contracts. In the classic bond contract, steepening of the back end of the curve and an increase in yields have led to increased traditional switch optionality in both the front and back contracts. However, the net bases appear to be overpricing this optionality. Given that the back net basis tends to remain stable through the roll cycle, we expect the front net basis to converge in coming weeks and exert a widening pressure on the weighted US calendar spreads (see CTD switch option values and basis convergence). In addition, any significant rise in yields and/or curve steepening would increase back-month delivery optionality by more than the front, again supporting a calendar spread widening bias. In the ultra-long bond contract, the wildcard continues to play an important role in both the front and back contracts, but traditional CTD switch optionality is now a feature of the back contract as well. The front contract net basis is likely to remain wide thanks to a significant wildcard option, but in the back contract there is an interplay between the wildcard and the traditional switch optionality. The CTD could switch to the Nov 53s in a relatively modest rally, which could significantly impact wildcard option value because the Nov 52s have a much higher conversion factor and a smaller tail. As a result of this interplay, we expect the wildcard option to be worth less in the back contract, which only implies additional narrowing bias on the calendar spread from this dynamic (see Wildcard and switch optionality in the ultra-long bond contract).

At the time of this writing, most contracts (except the US) have different CTD bonds for front and back contracts, which creates the potential for relative value impacts. In the WN and UXY contracts, we see the relative valuation of front and back CTDs versus each other playing a role in driving calendar spreads in this cycle. In both contracts, the front CTD has richened with respect to the back CTD, and any correction in the curve between CTDs would pressure calendar spreads narrower. Additionally, given the different CTDs we recommend a BPV-weighted hedge ratio when rolling positions forward to mitigate directional exposure. Our recommended hedge ratios and other details pertaining to the contracts can be found in the contract-specific tables (see

Contract Tables).

Lastly, we note that there is some chance for tail risks to escalate and cause forward financing rates to drop, which would bias calendar spreads wider. This impact would be amplified in sectors with high coupon CTDs in the back month as a drop in financing rates could shift optimal delivery to the end of the month. Back contract CTDs into the classic bond, ultra 10-year note, 10-year note, and five-year note all currently have coupons that are at or above 4%, and in the ultra bond contract a switch to the high coupon bond (4.75%) is quite likely. Despite these high coupons, carry is currently negative (since financing rates are higher still) and futures are priced to early delivery. But should forward financing rates drop sharply, as could happen in a tail risk scenario, carry on these high coupon bonds would turn positive and futures would shift to pricing in late delivery, triggering an additional cheapening to the tune of one month<sup>’</sup>s (now positive) carry. Therefore, a long CTD basis position with repo termed out to first delivery day and unwound on first delivery day can be thought of as containing an embedded call option on repo rates (see US bond futures rollover outlook: June 2023 / September 2023). This feature is desirable in the June contracts in the sectors noted above due to the high coupons of the back month CTDs. This also increases the willingness of shorts to roll early this cycle and could play some role in moderating the typical narrrowing pressure from the rolling of net commercial longs.

In summary, (Figure 6), we are bearish on the weighted WN calendar spread given that (i) the back basis is likely to converge more than normal because of increased switch optionality and decreased wildcard optionality, and (ii) a potential flattening of the CTD yield curve between the front and back CTDs would also bias the calendar narrower. We are bullish on the weighted US calendar spread as the front basis is more likely to converge than the back basis, and also because a potential rise in rates would increase switch option values by more in the back month than the front. We are bearish on the weighted UXY calendar spread due to elevated net commercial longs as well as a potential flattening of the curve between CTDs. We are bearish on the weighted TU calendar spread on the back of extremely elevated net commercial long futures positions. We are also bearish on the weighted TY and FV calendar spreads, but somewhat more mildly. This is because elevated net commercial longs are likely to bias calendar spreads narrower, but there is some offset from the fact that long basis positions in the back-month CTDs are attractive as a way to position for an unexpected Fed rate cut and a sharp drop in financing rates.

## Investor Positioning

As we noted earlier, investor positioning will continue to play an important role in this roll cycle as the aggregate open interest in the futures complex has only continued to increase over time and now stands at over 1.6 trillion 10-year equivalents (see Figure 4). At the same time, market depth remains low, broadly similar to the last roll cycle. Given the sheer scale of the Treasury futures footprint, any technical imbalance between commercial longs and shorts could result in pressuring the calendar spread going into first notice day, given the tendency of commercial accounts to roll ahead of the delivery month.

Currently, net commercial futures open interest is considerably elevated in the 2-year and 5-year note contracts as well as the classic and ultra 10-year note contracts (Figure 7 and Figure 8). In the long-end contracts (i.e., the classic and ultra long bond contracts), commercial futures positions are net long but by amounts that appear much closer to historical average levels. Thus, these sectors appear somewhat more balanced. Therefore, from a positioning standpoint, we expect narrowing pressure from a preponderance of net longs in all sectors except the long-end contracts, although this effect is likely to be most dominant in the TU sector (given other offsetting factors in the other contracts, discussed later in this piece). The impact of investor positioning is likely to be much more muted in the classic and ultra long bond contracts.

## CTD Relative Value

As of the time of this writing, most contracts (except for the US) have different CTD bonds for front and back contracts, which creates the potential for relative value considerations between front and back CTDs to impact the calendar spread during the roll cycle.

In the WN contract, the yield differential between the front and back contract appears too steep relative to history and could flatten as the roll period approaches. Of course, as we discuss in the next section, the back contract CTD in this sector could switch in a relatively modest rally to the Nov <sup>’</sup>53s. However, even if such a switch occurs, the frontversus-back CTD curve would still appear too steep as the curve between Nov <sup>’</sup>53s and the May <sup>’</sup>49s also appears steep (Figure 9). Given that this steepening has mostly come after the late November roll into March contracts, this steepening would appear to be the result of futures technicals and could thus flatten back as we head toward the next roll. A flattening in this yield spread would pressure the calendar spread narrower. As a rule of thumb, every 1 bp richening of the back contract CTD versus the front should drive the calendar spread narrower by 6.75/32nds.

In a similar vein, the yield differential between the front and back contract CTDs in the UXY sector has also steepened in the past few months and appears too steep relative to recent history, again likely the result of futures technicals that could be poised to reverse (Figure 10). As a rule of thumb, every 1 bp richening of the back contract CTD versus the front should drive the calendar spread narrower by \~3/32nds.

In all the other contracts, front and back CTDs appear fairly priced with respect to each other, and relative value considerations are unlikely to impact the calendar spreads in those sectors. In the classic bond contract, the front and back CTDs are identical, causing relative value considerations between CTDs to be moot. Moreover, while CTD switches are possible in the classic bond contract, they are likely to be somewhat symmetric across both front and back contracts, thus making it unlikely that frontversus-back CTD considerations will play a role in driving that calendar spread.

## CTD switch option values and basis convergence

In the TU, FV, and UXY sectors, there is little chance of CTD switches because of yield and curve movements. Both front and back bases are trading near zero, making basis convergence a non-issue for calendar spreads in these sectors. In the TY sector, the CTD into the front contract is the 4.875% Oct 2030 and the CTD into the back contract is the 4% Jan 2031. There is some switch optionality in the back contract, which we estimate to be worth around 1- to 1.5/32nds, stemming in part from the potential for WI 7s to become CTD. That said, the market has priced the back month net basis fairly steadily at around 0.5/32nds, and this is unlikely to change materially during the horizon of this roll. Therefore, switch options and basis convergence are unlikely to drive calendar spreads in the TU, FV, TY, as well as UXY sectors, in our view.

In the bond contract, the 4.625% Feb 40 is currently the CTD into both the front and back contracts. But switches are possible, with bonds ranging from the 4.5% Aug 39 to the 4.75% Feb 41 being potential candidates for CTD, into both the front and back contracts. All in all, we project 0.25/32nds of switch option value in the front and about 2.5/32nds in the back – markets are currently pricing in 2/32nds and 6/32nds, respectively. This makes net bases in the front and back contracts about 2/32nds and 3.5/32nds wide, respectively. On the margin, this is a source of widening pressure on the calendar spread since back-month net bases typically remain stable during rolls and only begin to converge much later in the life cycle of a contract, while the front contract net basis typically converges heading into the roll.

This leaves the ultra-long bond contract, where historically (in recent years) net bases have been considerably wide but almost exclusively because of significant wildcard optionality. This time, the March contract is very much in the same mold – the WNH4 net basis is considerably wide, because of wildcard optionality, and there is relatively negligible CTD switch optionality. But the WNM4 contract has a lot more interesting characteristics – we discuss this in a separate section below.

## Wildcard and switch optionality in the ultra-long bond contract

As readers may recall, the wildcard option stems from two aspects of the futures delivery process. One, the market-neutral hedge ratio between the CTD and futures in a basis position (which is rather close to the conversion factor of the CTD) is not necessarily the same as the delivery ratio (which is simply \$100K notional per contract, or \$200K notional per contract in the TU sector). The differences between these two ratios is often referred to as the “tail.<sup>”</sup> This tail, in the case of the WN contract, is positive, meaning that a residual CTD bond position will need to be sold after making delivery by a long basis investor. Two, while invoice prices are determined at futures market close (2pm Chicago time), notification of delivery can be made several hours after that. This creates a time window after futures close in which yield declines, if sufficiently large, can create a significant benefit from selling the tail and making delivery of the required amount of bonds.

The ultra-long bond contract is well known in recent years for being a sector dominated by the wildcard option. And is the case for the WNH4 contract. Traditional CTD switches, on the other hand, have thus far been far less probable in the WN contract. That appears set to change as we move forward from the March contract to the June contract in a few weeks. This makes the WNM4 contract quite interesting in somewhat counter-intuitive ways. We discuss both contracts below.

In the WN contract front and back net bases are 9.5/32nds and 13/32nds wide, respectively. In the front contract, there is very little traditional switch optionality (i.e., the potential for CTDs to change because of rate and/or curve movement), and the wide net basis is mostly a reflection of the significant wildcard optionality in this contract. Using the recent averages for post-futures-close volatility, we estimate this wildcard

option as being currently worth 5/32nds in the WNH4 contract<sup>’</sup>s net CTD basis. Of course, the wildcard option value depends on this particular assumption on post-futuresclose volatility. Given the considerably elevated policy uncertainty currently (see When it rains, it pours, 2/2/2024), there is some upside risk to this estimate, and we think wildcard option value will likely remain elevated in the front contract going into the roll cycle. This underpins our view that we expect the front net basis to remain somewhat stable going into the roll cycle. History supports this expectation – the front net basis, while wide with respect to our current estimate of the wildcard value, is at the past 4- cycle averages and has widened or remained stable in the past 3 out of 4 rolls even when above our wildcard model estimate. Therefore, we expect the front net basis to remain elevated and stable throughout the roll cycle.

The back contract is much more interesting, due to unusually elevated traditional CTD switch optionality in the WNM4 contract. The CTD into WNM4 is currently the 2.25% Aug '49 issue, with the Nov '49 being a close second. But somewhat interestingly, in a relatively modest rally, the CTD is projected to switch to the 4.75% Nov '53s, all the way to the long end of the basket. Even more interestingly, such a lengthening in the CTD maturity would not lead to a lengthening in duration risk, but a shortening.

All this seems counter-intuitive at first glance. To make sense of it all, we begin by noting that futures prices are determined not by the prices of bonds in the basket but by bond prices divided by their conversion factors. In other words, if we imagine plotting the factor-scaled price (defined as price divided by conversion factor) of all the bonds in the basket versus yields, the futures price-versus-yield profile would track the lower envelope of all the bonds' individual profiles. Thus, what matters for futures pricing is not bond prices but factor-scaled prices. In a similar vein, the futures contract<sup>’</sup>s BPV is determined (to a decent first approximation) not by the CTD<sup>’</sup>s BPV but by the factor-scaled BPV (i.e., BPV divided by conversion factor). In Figure 11, we include the bonds in the WNM4 basket sorted not by maturity date but by factor-scaled BPVs. As can be seen, the Aug '49 is no longer at the short end of the basket but rather in the middle, and the Nov '53s are at the short end of the basket, not the long end. Thus, it is logical that a decline in yields could cause the CTD to shift toward the Nov '53s. In a very similar vein, it is also worth noting that when conversion factors differ widely, comparing BNOCs conveys a misleading picture of the impact of a CTD shift. Instead, a more accurate picture can be obtained by looking at factor-scaled BNOCs, i.e., BNOC divided by conversion factor. Here again, we can see that a switch from the Aug '49s to the Nov '53s would be noticeable but modest in terms of impacting the futures price, given the fact that the two bonds' factor-scaled BNOCs are less than 3/32nds apart, while the unscaled BNOCs differed greatly.

There is one important difference, however, between having the Aug '49s as CTD versus the Nov '53s, and that has to do with the wildcard option. The Aug '49 has a low coupon and a low conversion factor, which makes the wildcard option quite valuable. In contrast, the Nov '53s have a high coupon and a high conversion factor, which causes the wildcard option to be worth less than a third as much as it is for the Aug '49s in futures price terms (Figure 12). This dynamic makes it much more difficult to monetize the wildcard option and makes it likely that the WNM basis will trade closer to levels that would be consistent with simply pricing in traditional switch optionality. Currently, the Aug '49's WNM4 net basis is about 4.5/32nds wider than our estimate of fair value for that basis based on traditional switch optionality. This suggests there is about 4.5/32nds of wildcard optionality priced into the contract (or 7+/32nds, in futures price terms). This appears fair given current probabilities of CTD shifts. However, given the above-mentioned risks associated with the wildcard option this time, we would expect the basis to converge more than has typically been the case in this sector.

Thus, it would appear that both commercial accounts (who remain net long, albeit by a more modest margin than usual) as well as spec accounts could emerge as better buyers of the WNM4 contract during the roll period. Therefore, we expect the WN weighted calendar spreads to be biased narrower in this roll cycle.

Lastly, it is also worth noting that the wildcard option is only in play in the ultra-long bond contract, because of the combination of a low CTD conversion factor and high CTD duration. All other contracts are not similarly impacted by the wildcard option, and we do not foresee any impact on those calendar spreads.

## Appendix

Calendar spread relative value. For contracts with negligible embedded option values, the relative value of the calendar spread can be determined by comparing the implied repo rate (IRP) of the calendar spread to the forward three-month funding rate. The calendar spread IRP is the breakeven funding rate for going long the calendar spread, taking delivery in the front month and making delivery in the back month contract. If the calendar IRP is historically low compared to the forward financing rate for a particular market, the calendar spread may be considered rich.

For contracts where delivery options in the futures are valuable, such as U.S. Treasury bond futures, we think a simple IRP analysis is inadequate because it overlooks the potential impact of switches in the cheapest-to-deliver bond. In these cases, the option-adjusted relative values of the front and back contracts should be compared to determine the relative value of the calendar spread. If the Option-Adjusted Basis Net of Carry (OABNOC) or Risk-Adjusted Yield Spread (RAYS) is lower for the front month than for the back month contract, the calendar spread is considered rich.

2. Changes in short-term funding rates. An increase in funding rates generally causes futures prices to rise relative to cash prices, since it decreases the carry basis. Thus, the calendar spread reflects changes in the shape of the short end of the yield curve: for example, if four-month funding rates rise while onemonth rates remain the same, the calendar spread would be expected to fall. Changes in funding rates affect back-month futures more than front-month futures, since the back-month contract has three months more of carry in its basis. As a rule of thumb, for a parallel change in funding rates:

-1 x (change in funding rate) / 4 = change in calendar spread.

For example, if short-term rates increase by 25 basis points, the calendar spread is expected to decrease by approximately .0625 or 2/32nds:

-1 x 0.25 / 4 = -0.0625 (in decimal form, or -2/32nds).

3. Supply and demand pressures. The calendar spread may become richer or cheaper depending on the balance of long and short hedgers and investors who are rolling positions.
