# Global Economics & Strategy Mapping market structure: identifying outliers

## This is a new publication that we plan to put out more or less monthly

We employ unsupervised machine learning and some new visualization techniques to show how assets relate to each other within as well as across different markets. This allows us to describe how the world is evolving, identify anomalies or emerging themes and isolate which financial instruments are more central or peripheral versus their peers. We used this approach in investigating the dominance of AI—Nvidia and the Magnificent Seven specifically—in the global macro universe several weeks ago. Here we apply it to the topic of identifying outliers, defined broadly as assets that are behaving unconventionally, be they bonds, stocks, currencies or commodities.

## About 15% of the 140 instruments we look at screen as 'anomalous

We start by taking two-day returns to compute correlation matrices and then focus on (z-scored) changes in correlation structures over time. Some of the assets flagged are barely moving while their peers have broken away. But 10 currently exhibit Sharpe ratios in excess of two on a trailing two-month window, and four exceed four (Figure 1). Mostly these are agricultural commodities, which often behave idiosyncratically. But we also find a number of European and Asian stock markets moving up our list, alongside EU emissions, Israel (stocks and the currency) and aluminium. Some of the reasons are fairly obvious or can easily be traced back via news searches. Others should simply be borne in mind or serve as a flag for further investigation if deemed interesting.

## Today's market structure displays marked divergence

European bonds are behaving as a distinct block from USD-denominated ones, with only Japan trading totally differently. The same bifurcation goes for currencies, where KRW, TWD, MYR, INR, PHP and bitcoin constitute a clearly separate grouping. Stock markets are more disparate, though the S&P500 screens almost as idiosyncratic as the Shanghai Composite. Finally, commodity-wise, precious and base metals have grouped together and outperformed everything else, except cocoa, which continues to do its own thing.

## Anomalous behaviour is not so easy to define or measure

Financial analysts typically identify outliers by measuring returns and volatility in one time series and comparing its behaviour to others, ie, A spikes, outperforms B and is more/less correlated to C. But that wastes a lot of granular information, especially as one's universe of assets under consideration expands. We can recoup that and exploit it by conducting the same process multivariately, which means asking how correlated is A to B and C and comparing that answer to the same for B and C, etc. It's a small if computationally intense step that when run iteratively sheds a lot more light on how the entirety of relationships for each instrument (and the market in aggregate) evolve.

For this piece, we selected 140 financial instruments that we deemed broadly representative of four major asset categories—national stock markets, sovereign and corporate bonds, currencies, commodities—in roughly equal proportions. We then took 2-day returns and used these to calculate all possible pairwise correlations over two time horizons, the last two months and a historical window spanning 3.5 years. By comparing each asset's correlation patterns—its set of relationships with every other asset—from both periods, we get a measure of how much it has diverged from the status quo.

## We rank assets by how much their steady-state correlations have changed

Figure 1 is a synopsis of the assets screening as most anomalous today, ranked by how much their correlation patterns have shifted relative to the 3½ years prior. The next column, by contrast, shows how much things are shifting on the margin, so the last two months compared to the previous two, and a complementary list ranking assets by such short-term shifts is provided below in Figure 2. Note, however, that what ranks highly here could just as well be in the process of mean reverting from some recent extreme, and thereby be unwinding some anomaly rather than establishing one. In both cases, we focus on standard deviations of such shifts to mitigate overemphasis on instruments (mostly commodities) that exhibit more volatile correlation patterns over time.

The last three columns warrants mention, too, highlighting another characteristic of this framework: Many assets screen as standouts due to what they haven't done performance wise rather than what they have. Thus, Poland's equity index (WIG20), for example, ranks high even though the price has barely moved, implicitly because the other European indices it normally trades with have left it behind. One can see this more clearly by turning to the charts in Figure 3 to 6, which map the multidimensional relations in 2D. In Figure 3, WIG20 is a plain dot clustering with south-east Asia stocks. Most of its natural peers—PX, BUX, DAX—are located elsewhere, more south and are mostly surrounded by blue rings, darker signifying greater vol-adjusted price gains. Figure 5 shows where WIG20 stood in our post-COVID reference period:up north and closely tied to EU and CEMEA stocks. A similar reasoning applies to S.Africa stocks (TOP40), which in the current period have detached from their natural CEMEA peers to join the LATAM stocks cluster. Conversely, VND gets highlighted with a ring of the deepest red in the current period, but since correlation-wise it has remained relatively close to its usual South Asian peers it is not flagged as anomalous here.

Of the 22 assets that screen as anomalous in our framework, aluminium and cocoa stand out as having the strongest positive risk-adjusted returns. A quick news search for the former relates its performance to fresh US and UK sanctions on Russia, while the latter reflects unprecedented speculation interest driven by poor crops in West Africa and the impact of recent EU rules to curb deforestation. The EUA outperformance is newer, smaller and probably less durable (see here for one explanation why). Corn has posted strong gains following Russian missile strikes on Ukranian infrastructure, while cotton and sugar are giving back a lot of ground on expectations of strong crops in Texas and Brazil, respectively. Several European equity markets are also behaving uniquely better, led by Spain, the UK and Italy, and with them China that our colleagues have recently upgraded. The Israeli currency and stocks are retreating again amidst the latest escalations, while the underperformance of Indonesian stocks likely relates to foreign outflows as a weak currency in the face of a resolutely on-hold Fed impedes the start of rate cutting cycles.

## How are the anomaly scores derived?

To identify instruments behaving anomalously, our method focuses on changes in correlation structures over time rather than fluctuations in individual returns and/or volatility.

## Correlation matrices computation

We start by calculating two-day log returns (to account for time differences in closing prices) for each instrument, which we then segment over two distinct periods: a longterm reference window of 3.5 years and a more immediate two-month window. This comparison helps identify deviations from a fairly stable recent norm. We run the same analysis also using the preceding two months as the reference period, offering a more granular, yet potentially noisier, view of market shifts. For each time window, historical and current, we compute correlation matrices that contain all possible pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients between our chosen set of instruments. These matrices form the basis for monitoring the evolution of market relationships.

## Comparison of correlation patterns across time windows

Next, we extract vectors of the correlation coefficients for each instrument from both historical and current matrices. Each vector represents the correlation of one instrument to all others, providing a snapshot of its market relationships during a given period. We measure the extent of change in each asset’s market relationship by calculating the correlation distance (quantifying dissimilarity on a scale from 0 to 2) between corresponding vectors from different windows. We can then run our analysis in rolling iterations from 2016 to the present, which allows us to attribute notable deviations to specific events or emerging trends, providing ex post validation. In Figure 7and Figure 8 we plot these dissimilarity scores for a set of commodities and more ‘traditional’ equity and fixed-income instruments, respectively. Sudden spikes tend to coincide with systemic or asset class-specific shocks (e.g., COVID and the early war in Ukraine) as well as more idiosyncratic events.

## Z-Score calculation for anomaly detection

The final step involves quantifying the degree to which the observed dissimilarity in an asset’s correlation structure constitutes an anomaly. We z-score the rolling absolute dissimilarity scores and flag as anomalies instruments deviating beyond one standard deviation from their historical average. This normalization process adjusts for the inherent volatility in correlation patterns that characterizes certain instruments (typically commodities—See the higher levels and variability in the absolute scores in Figure 7 vs. Figure 8Equites' andbo rml, ywcp   below). Hereby, we can capture not only the most prominent shifts but also identify instruments that, while showing minor absolute changes, reflect significant deviations from their historical correlation patterns.

## How are the market maps built?

In our analysis, each instrument's correlation vector can be represented geometrically as coordinates in a multidimensional space defined by its correlations with every other metric (in this case, by simply computing 1-correlation) each element of the vector represents the distance of that instrument from every other instrument based on their returns behaviour. This effectively creates a spatial representation of an instrument’ position relative to others in the market. Then, by comparing these vectors from two different periods, we are essentially trying to capture how each instrument’ ‘coordinates’ within this multidimensional correlation space have moved, with prominent shifts indicating changes in how the instrument is influenced by or influences broader markets dynamics.

To visualize this, we apply t-SNE which is a dimensionality reduction technique (similar to PCA, but non linear) to map complex relations between items in 2D, while trying to preserve the original topological and geometric relations as much as possible. Changes in these maps are relatively simple to interpret, as the literal 'movement' of instruments across time windows can help quickly grasp significant shifts in market structure and identify new clusters of similar behaving instruments. At the same time, individual assets that have moved significantly according to our anomaly scoring would presumably be in quite different positions across the different windows. But a caveat to keep in mind is that not all shifts may be immediately obvious in a 2D representation due to some inevitable loss of information inherent in reducing data from a higher-dimensional space (like the full set of pairwise correlations) to just two dimensions. And t-SNE is particularly geared to preserving 'local' neighborhoods, which is ideal for identifying emerging clusters but can sometimes downplay the 'global' context as part of the trade-offs. This means that, while closely correlated instruments will likely remain close in the visual map, their position relative to less correlated assets can get distorted. Moreover, very subtle shifts, which could be quite significant for one instrument due to its history of minimal movement and thus be flagged by our Z-scores, may not stand out as clearly in the maps when arranged alongside instruments that exhibit larger absolute shifts.

Having secured our spatial layout, we add other attributes to convey more information about changing market structure. Each instrument is color-coded discretionarily to reflect its asset class and outlined with colors indicative of its risk-adjusted returns over the period: intensifying blue and red for positive and negative, respectively, centered around zero in white. Additionally, the size of each point is proportional to the typicality of an instrument's return profile in a given market landscape; smaller implying greater deviations from the market centroid, i.e., the average return profile. We compute this metric using Mahalanobis distance, which gives us a measure of abnormality that’s adjusted for the market’s inherent volatility and correlation patterns. This can contribute to our understanding of what constitutes anomalous behaviour not only relative to past patterns (the main focus of our analysis) but also relative to a central tendency within the current market context.

## Is this a predictive framework?

Our approach is not designed to predict market movements in the way traditional models do. By flagging instruments that start to behave differently in the market space, we aim to provide a focused list of ‘top movers’ that may warrant further research. But these indicators should serve only as starting points, as our process does not readily allow for isolating which specific dimensions are responsible for the changes we report. This aligns with other unsupervised machine learning techniques, where emphasis is placed on uncovering patterns that might not otherwise be apparent rather than on prediction. Nevertheless, while our present focus is on pattern discovery, we are exploring ways to incorporate supervised machine learning to validate and backtest the sensitivity and specificity of our scores against actual market outcomes.
