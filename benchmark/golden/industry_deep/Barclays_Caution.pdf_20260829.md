# Alternative Data View on US Consumer

## Narrative is changing, but is the data?

Renate Marold, BCI, US

Excerpted from  Alternative Data View on US Consumer: Narrative is changing, but is the data?, published on August 15, 2024

Language used by S&P1500 executives about the US consumer is changing, with frequent chatter of a cautious consumer and references to continuing general softness. However, apart from the ongoing shift to more regular discount store shopping, signals from credit card data do not seem worrying.

Earnings call language in the current reporting season suggests that the narrative around the US consumer is changing: corporate executives talk more frequently about a cautious US consumer, and less about consumer strength. Chatter on consumer weakness and references to a "cautious" consumer are most prominent among consumer discretionary companies, while continuing general softness seems to be of concern to materials, industrials, and IT companies.

However, we wonder if the pessimism in language is aligned with consumers' current economic reality.

Consumers are continuing a slow move toward shopping more at discount retailers, as derived from US Barclays proprietary credit card data, suggesting US consumers continue to trade down. In addition, trends in monthly median spending amounts in various prominent consumption categories seem in line with some slowing in consumption.

However, we think the credit card data suggest that the consumer's state of mind is far from signaling concerns of a recession. We do not see any sharp pullbacks, and positive growth in some discretionary categories suggest that the consumer is not overly cautious in spending either.

## Japan Economics Focus

## Our forecast for BoJ's next hike frontloaded to January

Naohiko Baba, BSJL, Japan | Ryuichiro Hashimoto, BSJL, Japan

Excerpted from Japan Economics Focus: Our forecast for BoJ's next hike frontloaded to January, published on August 14, 2024

We frontload our call for the next hike to Jan (from Apr) with a risk of an earlier move in Dec. Labor cost pass-through to service prices should be evident by year end, while the new year brings a clearer outlook for FY25 wage hikes. We forecast a further hike in Jul 25, taking our outlook for the terminal rate to 0.75%.

The BoJ raised its policy rate to around 0.25% at the 30-31 July monetary policy meeting (MPM). Comparing its policy stance in April and July, it added language saying “given that real interest rates are at significantly low levels […], the Bank will continue to raise the policy rate” (if economic and price conditions remain on track) while removing the phrase “accommodative financial conditions will be maintained for the time being.” In terms of the former, however, Governor Ueda has repeated a message to that efect since the March MPM.

In our view, the real driver of these revisions to the BoJ’s language was not a fundamental change in logic, but rather a tactical shift of communications in step with the government and ruling Liberal Democratic Party (LDP) aimed at correcting excessive JPY weakness – ie, this was the BoJ displaying a fighting stance to the FX market as a result of calls from the government and ruling party.

Looking at the summary of opinions from the July MPM, there was only one view that might be interpreted as unambiguously hawkish. Otherwise, there were some opinions biased against rate hikes, and even among the comments supporting them, cautious remarks stood out. In the speech by Deputy Governor Uchida as well, a cautious stance on next hikes was emphasized following the market upheaval in the wake of the July MPM. “In contrast to the process of policy interest rate hikes in Europe and the United States,” he said, “Japan's economy is not in a situation where the Bank may fall behind the curve if it does not raise the policy interest rate at a certain pace,” suggesting the BoJ can choose the timing of future hikes without rushing.

We believe the market upheaval that followed the latest MPM significantly raises the hurdle to a hike at the September and October MPMs, which come amid major political events including the LDP presidential election, a potential lower house dissolution and general election (Nikkei newspaper, 18 July) and the US presidential election.

To date, the BoJ has prioritized correcting JPY depreciation over the prevailing weakness in consumption data given its outlook for real wages to improve as annually negotiated wage hikes (“shunto”) are reflected on macro wage data. Now with the respite from excessive JPY weakness, economic conditions, including recession risk in the US, likely figure more prominently again in the BoJ’s policy assessment.

We believe the next checkpoint around the BoJ's scenario of a “virtuous cycle between wages and prices” will be the pass-through of labor costs to CPI services inflation, in addition to the maintenance of elevated inflation expectations. Our analysis suggests that the pass-through of labor costs to service prices is non-linear and that the pass-through rate will continue to rise with a lag. Since this outlook is highly uncertain, however, the BoJ will need to gather enough evidence before making any judgments.

We expect the BoJ will need to wait until at least the December MPM to confirm that the next wave of service price markups is reflected on the nationwide CPI data. However, we revise our base case for the next hike narrowly in favor of a move at the January 2025 MPM (our previous base case was April 2025) as the outlook for the FY25 “shunto” wage hikes will gradually become clearer at that point through hearings by the BoJ branch managers. In addition, we forecast a further hike at the July MPM, revising up our forecast for the terminal rate from 0.5% to 0.75%.

## Alphabet Inc.

Google vs DoJ: Monopoly Remedies, Updated Impact Zones & Scenarios Ross Sandler, BCI, US | Trevor Young, CFA, BCI, US | Alex Hughes, BCI, US | Joseph Petroline, BCI, US | Michael DiSanto, BCI, US

Excerpted from Alphabet Inc.: Google vs DoJ: Monopoly Remedies, Updated Impact Zones & Scenarios, published on August 15, 2024

The investment community has started to wake up to the reality that GOOGL finds itself in a challenging situation. We’ve long held that investors shouldn’t focus on what they think is “right” here, all that matters is the court's decision. Now we must consider scenarios and consequences of GOOGL breaking US antitrust laws.

The Key Take-Away: The potential winners of this federal court’s decision (link) several decades down the road are likely to be little tech, consumers who benefit from more innovation as a result of competition, and the integrity of US laws. That’s likely all that the court is focused on, not what investors care about. There are few winners from the perspective of public market investors here (perhaps big SEM advertisers). And unfortunately, GOOGL and AAPL (covered by Tim Long) are both in a very precarious situation, in our view, as many roads lead to the unwind of the ISA contract and all of its elements that have benefited both companies for the past decade. Using new data from last week's court decision document, we can better unpack the potential impact zones that may change as remedies are implemented (we’ve run all numbers on 2026, which is likely when these changes start occurring; we assume that Google's appeal will ultimately be unsuccessful). We see a range of outcomes between 2%-41% of Alphabet gross profit being eliminated by remedies. These could involve structural changes like divesting Chrome (20% of search queries), Android (19% of queries) or AdWords (80% of search revenue), as has been reported in the press. We also explore behavioral remedies like choice screens w/ capped revenue shares (41% hit to GOOGL GP), and choice screens with uncapped revenue shares (2% hit). We'd note that in the remedies trial just yesterday (link) around Epic vs. Google (Play Store) the plaintif's attorneys made a strong argument for fixed dollar revenue share and importantly NOT a % of revenue, as it removes the incentive alignment between companies (which we would expect from the DoJ remedies as well). This is potentially a strong precursor toward revising the search partnership agreements with OEMs, OS's and browser companies, which could lead to real query share impacts if partners decide to change their approach.

Stepping back, despite this challenging backdrop for GOOGL shares, our math suggests that we are already discounting a few of the less favorable outcomes, but not likely the worst case scenario. We think the next few months should bring us out of the current fog and into a place where we can better quantify exactly where we will be in 2026 and beyond. For now, this is our best take.

## Slash and Burn

Venu Krishna, CFA, BCI, US | Japinder Chawla, CFA, BCI, US | Rex Feng, BCI, US | Madhuresh Rai, CFA, BCI, US | Young Chen, BCI, US

Excerpted from Long & Short of It: Slash and Burn, published on August 13, 2024

LOs cut equity exposure to 2021 levels; systematic funds deleveraged sharply but further selling pressure likely constrained; options flow more nuanced than protection buying; vol normalization is halfway there; retail equity buyers unfazed for now; Tech is now a smaller OW but still the heavy favorite among LOs.

Macro jitters and vol spike clear out an "overgrown" equity rally. After a summer rally fueled mostly by multiple expansion, recession fears are back and motivated LOs to cut equity exposure to 2021 lows. At the same time, Treasury futures positions have risen to extremes. Conversely, global macro HFs continue to add opportunistically to long equity exposure. Net speculative SPX futures positioning is close to neutral, indicating divergence in positioning among discretionary accounts. We think flushed out positioning likely leaves room for equities to establish a stronger fundamental footing.

Systematic funds deleveraged sharply; are they done? Given the sharp rise in volatility, Vol Control (VC), CTAs and Risk Parity (RP) funds have all delevered meaningfully. Indeed, VC equity allocation has fallen from c.110% to \~50%, while RP equity exposure is now near its bottom quartile. Similarly, CTAs have embraced the risk-of by cutting equity/commodity longs, while adding to bonds. Going forward, CTAs will likely continue to sell NKY & buy JPY under most market paths. However, within US equities, where they are neutrally positioned, long-term trends remain favourable, creating a higher bar for CTAs to turn meaningfully short. Overall, given the extent of deleveraging, further selling pressure from systematic funds (esp. Vol Control) will likely be constrained. Instead, should markets stabilise and data prints turn more benign, the funds are likely to add to the buying pressure more meaningfully.

Options flows tell a more nuanced story than mere protection buying: The once-in-ageneration spike in vol across global markets broke, among others, the record for the largest number of S&P options changing hands ever, driven disproportionately by the puts side. Coincidentally, skew steepened dramatically from depressed levels. The steepening, however, is not just simply the result of indiscriminate protection buying, as a more granular options flow analysis suggests a more nuanced story of two-way flow/fast hedge monetization. Rather, volatility reactivity to the equity sell-of was outsized, both historically and vs what was priced by options. This compelled a recalibration higher of the market's estimate of future vol reactivity, resulting in a steeper skew.

Normalization is halfway through, as volatility retraces at record speed: Historically, following an initial spike, volatility tends to re-set higher vs pre-shock levels. However, the VIX has already normalized at the fastest pace in 20+yrs, as well as across other major global vol indices, lending credence to the argument that the latest surge could have been a head fake.

Retail equity buyers unfazed, but bonds preferred over the last month and cash over the last two weeks. Retail flows to equities slowed in the last two weeks after a strong July, but we're not seeing outflows yet. Regionally, US and EM were preferred. A pickup in inflows to

bonds and cash over the last two weeks points to some caution, even though money market flows have been slowing for the last few months as rate cuts get closer.

Rotation hits Tech, but it's still the favorite among LOs. Modestly reduced Tech exposure funded smaller underweights in several sectors, most notably Financials and Industrials. This aligns with "broadening" indicators we've seen over the past month. Discretionary remains the most heavily-shorted sector and Financials the least, but Tech has seen a substantial jump in short selling over the last three months.

Emmanuel Cau, CFA, Barclays, UK | Matthew Joyce, CFA, BBI, Paris | Magesh Kumar Chandrasekaran, CFA, Barclays, UK | Emmanuel Makonga, Barclays, UK | Arihanth Bohra Jain, Barclays, UK

Reassuring consumer and employment data brought calm this week, leading the VIX back to subdued levels. Meanwhile, US and UK CPI data confirmed inflation risks have eased, further paving the way for Fed to cut rates and assuage hard landing fears. Finally, post systematic degrossing, positioning risk has become more balanced.

Calming down. After the turbulence earlier in the month, equity markets enjoyed relative calmness this week. The sharp pick up in volatility (highest since the pandemic) earlier was indicative of panic setting in, as systematic investors de-grossed, likely amplified by the unwind of yen carry positions. Weaker payrolls also exacerbated the selling as fears of an imminent US recession rose. However, we have been arguing that growth is undergoing a soft patch rather than an outright collapse highlighting that the private sector is still in good shape and companies were not too bearish during latest Q2 results. Other indicators of recession such as credit markets were also relatively subdued. (See Hold the line, 7 Aug). Incoming data since then (US ISM services, retail sales, jobless claims, EU GDP) has been reassuring, confirming our view that the sell of was overdone. Naturally, this has helped calm market jitters and the VIX quickly reverted back to below 20x levels. At the same time, PPI and CPI data this week in the US and UK provided more evidence that inflation risks have eased further. This should allow central banks to shift their attention more towards the full employment/growth side of their mandate. Upside inflation surprises would have complicated the next steps for the Fed but the pathway to start easing policy is clear now and they can get ahead of the curve to alleviate fears of a hard landing. This should provide a welcome backstop to the economy, and confirm the mid-cycle scenario is still on for equities. The Jackson Hole symposium next week provides an opportunity for the Fed to guide the markets in that direction although with OIS curves pricing c.4x cuts already for the year and another set of payrolls and inflation data to digest before the next FOMC meeting, its unlikely that they will reveal their cards fully.

## Economic Outlook

## A change in narrative

Christian Keller, Barclays, UK | Mark Cus Babic, BCI, US | Akash Utsav, Barclays, UK

Excerpted from Global Economics Weekly, published on August 16, 2024

As central bankers gather in Jackson Hole next week, the narrative for policy easing is building. Inflation is in retreat, evidenced by another benign US CPI print, and growth is slowing, albeit unevenly across regions. In the US, robust retail sales eased fears of collapsing demand, pointing to gradual rate cuts.

## Inflation is no longer the threat it was

With central bankers from around the world set to gather for the annual Fed symposium in Jackson Hole (22-24 August), the global inflation narrative has shifted in their favour. Although it may be too early to declare victory—and central bankers will certainly be prudent to avoid this in their oficial rhetoric—the inflation scare that had dominated the policy debate since prices started to soar during the pandemic has now largely vanished. Inflation may not be quite at the 2% target yet, but it is close and going in the right direction.

## US CPI signals moderation

This optimism on inflation was validated this week by the US CPI print, with core surprising slightly to the downside, printing at 0.17% m/m. While this is an acceleration from the unusually weak June print, driven by core services, particularly rents, we see this as broadly confirming the disinflationary path. Looking ahead, we continue to expect some disinflation in core services on the back of softening in shelter inflation, as well as gradually moderating supercore, and forecast core CPI to decline to 2.9% y/y (-0.1pp revision) by year-end and 2.6% y/y by end-2025.

## UK labour market and inflation are easing

Indicators in other major economies were also encouraging for disinflation this week. In the UK, July inflation surprised to the downside, as core inflation eased 0.2pp to 3.3% y/y, led by services, which unwound an inflationary boost from Taylor Swift's UK concerts. Beyond hotel prices, momentum also continued to ease across most metrics of underlying services inflation, albeit slowly and from a higher level. We continue to project UK core CPI to ease between now and year-end, as well as next year, falling slightly below 2% y/y by Q4 25. This should be supported by loosening labour market conditions, as seen in this week's labour market report, which showed continued deceleration in wage growth and falling vacancies. The unemployment rate surprised to the downside, but this could well reflect noise, given the known issues with the Labour Force Survey in the UK.

## Euro area inflation in the 2% handle, though services remain stubborn

In Europe, the July HICP details for the largest euro area countries were slightly firmer, showing no deceleration in underlying services momentum and a slight pick-up in core goods. However, both headline and core inflation in the euro area are already contained (in the 2% handle), and our outlook remains for gradual further easing; the incoming final HICPs could, at most, lead us to fine-tune the pace of this disinflation. In addition to euro area final HICP, next week also brings Q2 ECB negotiated wage growth, which should step notably lower to 3.9-4.2% y/y, from 4.7% y/y in Q1.

## China still showing deflationary tendencies

Overall, the global inflation picture has become more benign. Pockets of persistent price pressures remain, but a meaningful broader re-acceleration of inflation seems a limited risk

across the major economies. Indeed, the deflationary tendencies in China could further lower global price pressures.

## Focus shifts towards growth

## US is cooling, not collapsing

As concerns about inflation recede, the focus is shifting towards growth. In the US, this week's robust retail sales continued to alleviate concerns about demand deterioration, as the headline jumped 1.0% m/m, well above the consensus and roughly in line with our forecast. This partly reflected a rebound from the motor vehicle disruptions in the prior month, but the control group also printed at a firm 0.3% m/m on the heels of June's robust 0.9% m/m gain. Along with positive carry-over efects from May and June, these position consumer spending for another solid gain in the current quarter (we currently track 2.5% q/q, similar to 2.3% q/q in Q2). Combined with a downside surprise in initial jobless claims, the narrative of an imminent collapse in US demand is thus again proving to be inaccurate. Growth is moderating from elevated levels, but activity remains solid and is not contracting. Moreover, other data, such as industrial production and housing starts, add to the evidence that bad weather skewed the July data, likely including the payrolls.

## Underlying growth in Europe and UK remains lacklustre

The global growth story is more complex. For instance, underlying activity in the euro area remains lacklustre. This week, Q2 GDP growth rounded to one decimal place was unrevised at 0.3% q/q, which confirms some pick-up in growth in H1 after a year and a half of stagnation, but there are signs that this was driven by the volatile expenditure components rather than domestic demand. The dataflow this week also included the first look at Q2 employment, which decelerated slightly to 0.2% q/q, and June industrial production, which contracted 0.1% m/m. Smoothing through some volatility, production declined 0.4% q/q in Q2, marking the fifth consecutive quarterly contraction. In addition, we saw a large drop in the ZEW survey; we normally discount this indicator, as it surveys financial sector analysts, but deteriorating sentiment could ultimately become a self-fulfilling prophecy. In the UK, GDP growth eased to 0.6% in Q2 (-0.1pp), which is elevated, but consumption and business investment were both weak. Next week, August flash PMIs will provide further signals on growth in the euro area and the UK; we forecast EA PMI to decelerate to 49.7 from 50.2, and UK PMI to remain at 52.8.

## China's economic situation is not improving

Even more concerning is the situation is China, where this week's remaining batch of July data did not change what earlier reports had indicated: an ailing economy, sufering from the ongoing property sector downturn, with little demand for credit and a tendency of higher savings and lower wages and prices. More specifically, bank loans and total social financing growth (8.2% y/y) again disappointed expectations, with loans to the real economy contracting for the first time in 19 years. Meanwhile, industrial production slowed, the contraction in property investment deepened and retail sales growth remained subdued at below 3% y/y. Overall, this confirmed a weak start to Q3, and we see downside risks to our slightly belowconsensus GDP growth forecast of 4.8%.

## All this points to some policy easing

## Carefully calibrated messages at Jackson Hole

This is where policy comes in. With inflation broadly under control and growth slowing, some easing of the monetary policy stance seems in order. Especially as quantitative tightening (ie, the gradual reversal of the enormous balance sheet expansion over the past decade) continues, a reduction of policy interest rates—sharply raised over the past two years—now seems to be the clear path forward. Next week's Jackson Hole symposium, entitled "Reassessing the efectiveness and transmission of monetary policy", will probably not give us any immediate answers to the question of what to expect in the coming quarters. However, speeches by Fed Chair Powell and other prominent policymakers and academics will be carefully followed for the tone and mood they provide.

## Chair Powell unlikely to signal 50bp increments

In our view, Chair Powell will mention that he expects growth in activity to moderate slightly and the labour market to cool but remain close to full employment. Given this, and with inflation subdued, we expect him to express more confidence that conditions are in place for a 25bp rate cut at the September meeting, provided inflation remains moderate and labour market conditions remain little changed. However, we think he will be careful to avoid making any policy commitments, in September or thereafter, and instead reiterate data-dependence (including that the FOMC would prepared to respond forcefully if the labour market were to unexpectedly weaken). We continue to expect a 25bp Fed cut in September, followed by 25bp cuts at the remaining two meetings this year, before switching to a quarterly pace of cuts in 2025 until it reaches a terminal rate of 3.75-4.00%.

Most likely path remains a 25bp ECB cut in September and 25bp BoE cut in November We also still expect the ECB to resume cutting in September. In contrast, the bar for a September BoE cut remains high, despite the softer inflation release, and we instead pencil in a move in November.

## China's PBoC still scrambling

The PBoC continues to fight an uphill battle to lift longer-term government bond yields. After multiple interventions last week, the PBoC this week took further steps to prevent CGB yields from falling, including: 1) state banks selling CGBs outright; 2) ceasing to provide cash through open-market operations, which limits the ability to buy bonds with borrowed funds; 3) asking some rural commercial banks to suspend CGB trading; 4) introducing a new mechanism by narrowing the interest rate corridor for short-term rates; and 5) the securities regulator slowing the approval process for new bond funds.

The PBoC is arguably trying to avoid financial stability risks of lower yields and maintain an upward-sloping yield curve to provide incentives for longer-term investment. However, while interventions may work in the short term—the 10y CGB yield rose 10bp from the record low of 2.124% on 2 August to a three-week high of 2.247% on 12 August—the PBoC's policy seems at odd with the economy's weak growth and persistently very low inflation (which leave real rates elevated). It would seem more consistent for the PBoC to address financial stability concerns directly through regulatory and macro-prudential measures, while allowing rates to adjust lower.

## Japan remains an exception

One exception to these dynamics is Japan, where inflation is firming and has become more persistent. But this is being driven partly by currency weakness and is a step towards normalisation after the country's long battle against deflation. Next week, Japan's July nationwide CPI will provide more insights into the status of this battle.

In addition, Japan stands out in the growth cycle, with activity returning to growth for the first time in four quarters in Q2. In fact, Q2 GDP notably beat our and consensus expectations, led by a strong rebound in private consumption and an upturn in investment. As real household purchasing power improves further due to wage hikes and investment remains robust on the back of strong corporate earnings, we expect private consumption to continue to recover and

GDP growth to gradually converge towards the potential. After incorporating this week's data, we raise our FY24 GDP forecast to 0.5% y/y (+0.2pp).

Given diverging macro conditions, Japan's monetary policy is also on a diferent trajectory, with the BoJ conducting a hiking cycle while most other central banks are easing. Following the surprise July move, we now expect the BoJ to hike twice more, to a terminal rate of 0.75% (+25bp versus our previous forecast) as it aims to normalise policy from the current "significantly low levels" of real interest rates. That said, given the market upheaval after the last hike, dovish comments by Deputy Governor Uchida, and concurrent political events (such as the LDP presidential election, a potential lower house dissolution, and a general election), we think hikes are unlikely at the September and October meetings. Instead, we expect the BoJ to hike again in January, with the risk of an earlier move in December depending on the passthrough of labour costs to services inflation, followed by another move in July 2025.
