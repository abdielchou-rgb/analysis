# China Equity Strategy

# Strategic importance of A-shares, SOE valuation re-ratings and state-owned capital transfers

## The 'people's' A-share market

China's state-owned entities and retail investors currently hold more than 63% of total A-share market capitalisation. We think capital appreciation aids common prosperity, what the central government's 2024 'nine-point' guidelines call the "people's nature of the capital market". The A-share market could serve as a new household wealth reservoir, especially in a property downturn, and the market now has increased strategic importance for the economy (report). We believe 'new quality productive forces' and self-reliance cannot be fully developed without stronger financing support from the equity market. The A-share market recently skewed towards shareholder returns, tighter access to IPOs, rising dividends and share buybacks. A number of government agencies have since mid-January 2024 flagged their resolve to "safeguard" the capital market.

## SOE valuation dilemma and potential solutions

The share prices of SOEs have outperformed the broader market since 2023 on increased defensiveness/dividend yields and some concrete SOE reform progress—a 'valuation system with Chinese characteristics'. But from a long-term perspective there still exists a large valuation gap between SOEs and non-SOEs due to sector distribution, operating efficiency and negative investor perception. Our analysis shows non-financial SOE valuation discounts were not justified under 'classic theory' (report). We think SOE valuations could be enhanced through: 1) improving operating efficiency and ROE, 2) increasing dividend payouts or share buybacks, 3) restructurings and M&A, 4) aligning the interests of SOE management teams (eg, career goals and incentive schemes) with profitability and improving corporate governance and transparency.

## How might capital market development support an ageing China?

As China's population ages, the country faces a pension shortfall. Apart from fiscal support, raising the retirement age and diversifying pension fund investments, we think state-owned capital transfers may help mitigate the problem (report). We estimate Rmb400bn of cash dividends from transferred state-owned capital (9% of China's current annual basic pension income) could be used to supplement pension payments in 2030. As the pension deficit widens, reinvestment and capital operations could create additional value. In fact, 'market value' has recently been added to SOE performance evaluation. SOE re-ratings could long-term create substantial resources for capital operation, and we see some investment opportunities in high-quality SOEs (Figure 31).

## Is the A-share market financing- or investment-oriented?

There has long been a debate among investors over the function of the A-share market —should capital raising be prioritised or are investment returns in the secondary market more important? The debate gets more intense when the market is navigating choppy waters. On one hand, company owners and primary-market investors insist the capital market should serve the development of the real economy. On the other, some secondary-market investors argue stock markets cannot sustain the number of private companies going public year after year.

After reaching almost Rmb2trn in 2016, total funds raised hovered around Rmb1.38trn on an annual basis between 2015-23. IPO and secondary offerings combined took the lion's share of total capital raised, while the former ballooned to Rmb588bn in 2022 from Rmb138bn in 2018, a CAGR of 44% and far outpacing the growth in dividend payouts. The debate was muted when the A-share market was on an upward trend over 2019-21 and quickly exacerbated when A-shares entered a prolonged slump in 2022.

## A brief history

The A-share market was established in the 1990s to serve large state-owned enterprises' (SOEs) financing needs. To meet this need in a targeted manner, a quota system for Ashares was implemented and each province was allocated a predesignated quota.

Since 2010, A-shares have taken on a new role of providing financing to start-ups and non-SOEs as the private sector started to boom. Annual capital raised via IPOs and the number of firms listed on the A-share market since then has consistently topped global rankings. While more than 3,000 companies have gone public since 2014, coinciding with a boom in the primary market and high numbers of PE/VC portfolio companies listing, only 163 companies have been delisted over the same period. We believe the financing-oriented market has helped boost China's economic growth to some degree.

## Why do we need an investor-oriented equity market?

As the macro environment evolves, we believe a more investor-oriented A-share market is needed. In other words, the A-share market would have greater strategic importance. We note the prolonged property slump has been a drag on China's economic recovery over recent years. Based on our economics team's estimates, property and propertyrelated companies accounted for 25% of total GDP. In addition, residential real estate makes up the largest portion by far of Chinese households' wealth, at around 60% in 2020, per People's Bank of China (PBoC) estimates. A continued downturn would inevitably have a negative wealth effect and further dampen consumer confidence. We believe the A-share market is the best long-term wealth reservoir. A rising secondary market may create a positive wealth effect and potentially boost consumption.

We also believe a bullish A-share market helps achieve common prosperity. If we break down A-share market cap by investor type, the state is the largest shareholder. We categorised the top-10 shareholders of every A-share company into either state (staterelated entities, central SOEs, local SOEs, etc) or non-state (individuals, non-SOEs, foreign institutions, etc) and then calculated their disclosed positions to gauge how large a presence the state has in China's equity market. We estimate the state holds at least Rmb27trn, over a third of the total A-share market cap. Moreover, according to the CSRC, there are more than 220m retail investors in China's stock market and an even larger number of mutual fund (MF) investors in China. If we total the state and mass market retail investment, MFs, insurance funds and national social security fund (NCSSF) and label them the stake of the 'ordinary people' within the A-share market, the total amount reaches Rmb49trn, more than 63% of total A-share market capitalisation. This amount dwarfs non-SOE strategic investors, entrepreneurs, high net worth individuals (HNWIs) and hedge funds combined. Therefore, stock market rallies would complement common prosperity. The 2024 nine-point guidelines call this the "people's nature of the capital market". We believe that letting everyone receive some capital returns is the easiest way to enhance a sense of gain among investors.

For the real economy, efforts to foster new growth drivers could be accelerated to promote high-quality development, and developing 'new quality productive forces' is a key priority at the moment. We think a functioning and successful secondary market should not only provide abundant liquidity to primary market investors so that they can exit, but also correctly price a company looking to list so that the candidate has sufficient resources for development. If secondary-market investors continue to make financial losses, start-ups could find it more difficult to access finance. Allowing secondarymarket investors to make money should lead to an increase in financing activities. Hence, we believe self-reliance cannot be fully developed without stronger financing support from the equity market.

From a long-term perspective, capital market development and the establishment of a shareholder-return mechanism would help address an ageing population, in our view. Postponing the retirement age would be the most obvious way to ease the pension shortfall, but such a policy change is destined to face some headwinds. Employers in China already bear a heavy burden with regard to benefit contributions, so it would be hard to ask them to further increase pension contributions. As a result, state-owned capital transfers appear to be another approach, and have in fact already been implemented. In 2017, China began to transfer capital to the NCSSF. As of 2020, these capital transfers had amounted to Rmb3.3trn in total. In 2020-23, the market cap of corresponding stocks rose further to exceed Rmb4trn. We estimate roughly Rmb400bn of cash dividends per year—equal to 9% of the total current annual basic pension income—paid by both listed and unlisted transferred state-owned capital could go towards pension payments in 2030. As the pension shortfall widens further, we think increasing state-owned capital transfers is inevitable. We think A-share market rallies would create a substantial amount of resources for capital operations.

## What has been achieved, and what might happen next?

The A-share market has been subdued this year and, notably, a number of government agencies have commented since mid-January on their resolve to safeguard the capital market. In addition to a number of positive signals and detailed policies from the CSRC, we note: 1) RRR and interest rate cuts by the PBoC; 2) Central Huijin Investment's announcement stating its full recognition of the allocation value of the A-share market (report); 3) the State-Owned Asset Supervision and Administration Commission's (SASAC) incorporation of market cap management in its assessment of the heads of central SOEs; 4) a government work report highlighting the need to strengthen the intrinsic stability of the capital market; 5) the 2024 nine-point guidelines calling for tighter IPO access, continuous supervision of listed companies, and mid-/long-term capital inflow; 6) the Third Plenum proposing to improve capital market functions to coordinate investment and financing. We think the coordinated actions provide strong support for the A-share market over the medium term.

From the perspective of shareholder returns, rapidly increasing dividends and buybacks are pushing for a structural transition in the A-share market. A-share companies have made substantial progress in terms of dividend payouts in the last 10 years. Total dividend payouts more than tripled from Rmb526bn in 2013 to above Rmb1.7trn in 2023, a CAGR of 12.5%. Although A-share earnings have been under pressure in recent years, the absolute amount of dividend payouts has been increasing, indicating listed companies' resolution to enhance investor returns. Share buybacks by listed Chinese companies lag those of other major markets. If we calculate the total amount of share buybacks carried out as a percentage of total market cap, the percentage for the CSI 300 in 2023 was 0.19%, less than a quarter of the Nikkei 225 and a tenth of the S&P 500. However, share buybacks are becoming increasingly common in the A-share market. The total amount of share buybacks carried out by listed companies grew from a mere Rmb8bn in 2013 to more than Rmb154bn in 2023, a CAGR over 34%. A total of 2,083 share buyback plans were announced by listed companies in H124, 2.6x the average level in the past few years. As a result, total A-share shareholder returns in 2023 and H124 exceeded the aggregate financing amounts in the corresponding periods.

## Are SOEs undervalued by investors?

The aggregate earnings of A-share listed companies rose 97% during 2014-23 alongside China's economic growth, but A-share benchmark indices lagged far behind. This is due to some structural restraints, including imbalanced investment and financing functions in the A-share market and issues in the compilation of key stock indices. Noteworthily, in recent years, a decline in listed SOEs' average valuations weighed on key A-share stock indices. In the A-share market, current SOE valuations are only half those of non-SOEs in both PE and P/BV terms, and the gap has widened in recent years. Market participants generally believe this valuation difference is due to three major reasons—sector distribution, operating efficiency and negative investor perceptions.

## SOEs' sharp valuation discount unjustified

We believe the significant valuation discount of SOEs as a whole against non-SOE peers seem unjustified from the perspective of both PE to earnings growth, and P/BV to ROE.

From a PE-multiple perspective, non-financial SOEs have been trading at an increasingly steep discount to their non-SOE peers to the extent that since 2021 non-SOE PE multiples have been more than double those of SOEs. However, this valuation gap can not be explained by differences in earnings growth. To some extent, SOE earnings growth is indeed slower. When we compare the earnings results of non-financial Ashare SOEs with non-SOEs, we can clearly see non-SOE annual profit growth outstripped SOEs in six of the past 10 years. However, if we make an aggregate comparison, the lead is not as alarmingly wide with average annual profit growth of 11% for non-SOEs compared with 9% for SOEs over a span of 10 years (2014-23). In recent years, it has been more difficult for non-SOEs to sustain their lead in earnings growth. SOEs, whose profit has grown much more rapidly since 2021, are trading at a steeper discount to non-SOEs. The discount only narrowed in 2023 as VCC gathered popularity.

PE valuations aside, we note a big chunk of the difference in SOE and non-SOE P/BV valuations cannot be easily explained by differences in ROE. We note non-SOEs have not had a clear advantage over SOEs in ROE terms since 2018, though they did command a significant P/BV premium over SOEs.

Hence, we have coined the term 'non-ROE factors' to try to capture that portion of valuations unexplained by ROE differences. Non-ROE factors are the difference between non-SOEs' P/BV premium over SOEs and non-SOEs' ROE advantage over SOEs. We have found that out of 25 non-financial sectors, only 14 have P/BV ratios that can be explained by their ROE. In other words, for 11 out of 25 sectors, non-ROE factors are simply too significant to ignore.

## Bridging the gap—a sector perspective

Looking deeper at the sector level, we note SOEs in different sectors face different obstacles. We have grouped A-share level-1 industries (excluding conglomerates, banks and non-bank financials) into three categories based on the different measures available to SOEs to bridge the valuation gap with non-SOE peers.

In category A, we identify sectors where enhancing ROE should be sufficient for SOEs to catch up with non-SOEs in terms of valuation. The five sectors in this first category are steel, machinery, non-ferrous metals, electronics and real estate. They share a trait whereby most of their non-SOE peers' valuation premium can be explained to some extent by their greater operating efficiency. In other words, we believe share prices in these five industries are dictated by ROE. We also believe that if the SOEs in these five industries were able to gradually catch up with their non-SOE peers in terms of operating efficiency, the valuation gap may narrow.

Figure 17: Enhancing ROE should bridge the valuation gap in these sectors
![](images/ba39f8ecaa550fa5f707f09dc8db9e1d7a8b289c82c8a52a97bec5c6125c01ef.jpg)
Source: Wind, UBS-S estimates. Note: P/BV premium & ROE advantage calculated using avg. 2010 -23 numbers

In category B, we identify sectors where enhancing ROE would only be able to lift SOE valuations modestly. The five sectors in this second category are home appliances, electrical equipment, textiles, agriculture and light manufacturing. Like the SOEs in category A, SOEs in the second category lag non-SOEs in terms of ROE—however, the non-SOE valuation premium does not fully reflect their advantage in business operation terms. Although SOEs in this category's five sectors could enhance ROE, their valuations may only catch up to a certain extent as in our view they are already overpriced.

Most sectors fall into category C, where SOEs can use a combination of new business development and focus on growth to boost their valuations. In this case, our view is that more investor education may help the investing community form an unbiased, if not more justifiably positive perception, of SOEs.

## Pension shortfall—the why, where, and potential solutions

As discussed above, capital market development and the establishment of a shareholder-return mechanism help address an ageing population, while state-owned capital transfers kicked off in 2017 to ease the pension shortfall in the long-term.

According to the three-pillar pension system as defined by the World Bank, China has already essentially established a multi-pillar pension system. However, in 2022 the total balance of China's first pillar stood at Rmb8.3trn, or 6.9% of China's GDP at the time; in 2023 the total balance of China's second pillar stood at Rmb5.8trn, or 4.6% of GDP; the third pillar remains nascent. In China, total pension assets are roughly 12% of GDP, much lower than the average level of 113.8% in OECD countries, as of 2020.

The Chinese Academy of Social Sciences estimates the annual balance (income minus expenses) of China's basic pension fund could turn negative in 2028, and that the deficit could keep widening (even taking fiscal subsidies into account). The cumulative balance for the first pillar therefore looks set to peak in 2027F and be depleted in 2035F. Zhou

Xiaochuan, former governor of the PBoC, said in February 2023 that China's pension system faces serious challenges and there is no room for delay in addressing them. In December 2023, he further commented that postponing retirement is not about postponing it for as long as you want. The problem of a potential pension shortfall is even worse at the regional level. The number of provinces that are net contributors to the central pension scheme shrank from 21 in 2022 to 18 in 2023.

When China established its national pension system in 1997, it was decided that people who had already retired would still receive pensions despite having made no contribution to the system. As SOE employees who retired before 1997 had made substantial contributions to the country's economic development over their careers, when little progress had been made in market reform, it was deemed fair to provide them with retirement welfare. Those who retired pre-1997 have received pensions as if their personal pension accounts were fully paid over their periods of employment.

But with no personal account contributions from pre-1997 retirees or fiscal support for the pension system, China's working population is effectively financing the pensions of current retirees rather than their own. So, since 1997, the reformed pension system has functioned as a pay-as-you-go model. As the size of the retired population without fully paid personal accounts grows and life expectancy increases, the private account deficit looks set to expand and may lead to a severe pension shortfall in years to come.

Figure 20: Reform of China's pension system in 1997
![](images/790beb095c5d8e762281645823982d95e1829e70f8bcc09c13366bc90ce7df8c.jpg)

Employers in China already bear a heavy burden with regard to social benefit contributions, so we think it would be hard to ask them to further increase pension contributions to bridge the shortfall.

For example, employers in Shanghai must pay 32-40% of nominal wages as a social benefit contribution. Employees must also pay roughly 20% of their wages. Applying the lowest housing fund rate of 5%, a typical Shanghai employee who makes Rmb12,183 a month would take home Rmb9,786 after taxes and other contributions while the employer would pay Rmb16,040 in total.

In 2019, employers’ mandatory contributions to pension plans were reduced by 4ppts to 16% to ease the corporate burden and support economic growth.

Apart from fiscal support, postponing the retirement age would be the most obvious way China could ease the pension shortfall, in our view. However, such a policy change is destined to be controversial and unpopular. As early as November 2008, the head of the Ministry of Human Resources and Social Security's Social Security Research Institute said the relevant departments were considering extending the retirement age. But 15 years later, even though China's population has reached the status of 'aged', the debate is ongoing. In March 2023, State Council Premier Li Qiang said the government would carefully study the raising of the retirement age and would safely launch a policy change when the time was right. The Third Plenum, concluded in July 2024, proposed "in line with the principle of voluntary participation with appropriate flexibility, we will advance reform to gradually raise the statutory retirement age in a prudent and orderly manner." We note the nationwide average retirement age is 55.

Meanwhile, the diversification of pension fund investments could help improve returns. The basic pension fund is 23% invested, with an average yield of 6.49% pa since December 2016 when entrusted operations kicked off. Returns are realised through both direct and entrusted investment. Direct investment is conducted by the NCSSF, with investments mainly in bank deposits and equities. Entrusted investments are conducted by trustees and mainly include onshore stocks, bonds and other financial products. The combined share of onshore stocks, equity funds, hybrid funds and equity pension products is capped at 30% of pension fund's NAV. We think further diversification of pension fund's portfolio could help enhance long-term returns.

Besides the above-mentioned potential solutions to a pension shortfall, below we highlight two other major potential solutions—developing China's third-pillar personal pension scheme (PPS) and state-owned capital transfers.

## How large could personal pension scheme inflows into A-shares be?

Starting mid-2021, China's authorities began issuing notices and policies to support the development of the PPS. A list of 36 pilot cities and the first batch of PPS products were announced in 2022 and we expect the full implementation of PPS to happen rapidly over the coming years. UBS-S financial analyst Haifeng Cao published a deep dive into China's PPS ("APAC Focus: Personal pension scheme—an industry growth driver for decades to come?"). With attractive tax incentives, we expect China's PPS to steadily accumulate assets (contributions greater than withdrawals) until breaking even (contributions equal to withdrawals) around 2060, when we forecast the PPS to reach a cumulative size of Rmb172trn, or 37% of China's GDP. We think that leading brokers with advantages in asset management and investment advisory, as well as MFs, are likely to benefit more from PPS development.

## State-owned capital transfers— dividend payouts and capital operation

While China's pre-1997 retirees did not pay into their personal pension accounts, these former SOE employees are considered to have made important contributions to China's economic development at a time when the country had made little progress in market reforms. Thus, it has been deemed only fair that they should receive retirement welfare. As many of these retirees dedicated their entire career to helping build SOEs, we think it would be appropriate for state-owned capital to be deployed to supplement basic retirement benefits.

In November 2017, the State Council unveiled an implementation plan to transfer a portion of state-owned capital to enrich the social security fund. With the goal of making up for the pension shortfall, 10% of state-owned capital (ie, shareholder equity belonging to the Chinese government) of SOEs would be transferred. At the provincial level, a number of local governments have announced similar plans—eg, the Shanghai government in 2020 issued an implementation plan to transfer part of Shanghai's stateowned capital to the social security fund with a transfer ratio set at 10%.

As of end-2020, a total of Rmb1.68trn of state-owned capital of both central SOEs and financial institutions had been transferred to the social security fund. The total capital transferred amounted to Rmb3.3trn by 2020, when including some local SOEs. The market value of the equities further increased to more than Rmb4trn over 2020-23. As at the end of 2022, the total assets of industrial SOEs amounted to Rmb339.5trn. Total state-owned capital in industrial companies held by the central and local governments delivered a 15% CAGR, from Rmb18.5trn in 2010 to Rmb95trn in 2022, while total state-owned capital in financial companies held by the central and local governments increased from Rmb16.2trn to Rmb27.6trn during the same period. Thus, the total value of the central and local governments' SOE holdings (state-owned capital) came to Rmb122.3trn by end-2022.

In our base case, we expect total state-owned capital held by the central and local governments to reach Rmb200trn by 2030. Assuming a 10% transfer ratio, we estimate Rmb20trn of state-owned capital (though not all the equities are listed on stock exchanges) could be transferred to help fill the pension shortfall, and the ratio could be increased further if necessary.

Thus, we think it is crucial China obtains additional income through the operation of transferred state-owned capital. The government released a policy paper entitled "Measures for the operation and management of state-owned equity and cash income of the transferred capital for enhancing social security funds" in March 2024, setting out the operational and investment management rules.

## SOE reform set to accelerate

Considering the market generally assigns higher valuations to assets with high growth prospects and strong profitability, we think investors would likely need to see evidence of SOEs' improving operating efficiency. In our view, SOEs are unlikely to see a material re-rating if there is no concrete evidence of an improvement in profitability. Meanwhile, dividend payouts rely heavily on profitability. Higher ROE could lay the foundations for higher dividend payouts, which could be used to supplement pension payments over the long run.

The government has rolled out a series of policies and guidance on SOE reform. Related topics have been well researched in recent years; mergers and consolidation, mixed ownership reform, equity incentive plans, asset injections and deleveraging have been

## widely discussed.

As China's population status became 'aged' in 2021, we believe state-owned capital likely needs to serve as a supplement to support the retirement system for all generations. Japan's experience suggests numerous measures and major reforms could be needed. China's pension system faces many challenges and the time to solve them looks increasingly limited. We therefore think SOE reform should accelerate.

The total stake of the central and local governments in SOEs reached Rmb122.3trn by end-2022. From the perspective of cash flow/dividends, considering the substantial size of SOEs' assets and state-owned capital, we estimate a 1ppt increase in ROE would bring Rmb1.2trn in additional earnings attributed to the Chinese government and billions of renminbi in extra dividend payments each year.

From the perspective of valuations and market caps, higher ROE brought by reform would naturally lead to higher valuations for SOEs. We estimate that raising the P/BV ratio for SOEs as a group by 0.5x would boost the collective market cap of SOEs to Rmb61trn, assuming all of them are listed. This could create a substantial amount of resources for capital operation.

Many SOEs' assets are still unlisted. But as the asset securitisation rate increases along with the development of direct financing and a registration-based IPO system, we think more state-owned assets could be listed on exchanges.

## Ideological underpinning—valuation system with Chinese characteristics

To improve investors' perceptions of SOEs, on 21 November 2022 the CSRC proposed “we will build a capital market and explore a valuation system with Chinese characteristics (VCC), so the market can allocate resources effectively.” On 23 January 2024 the CSRC revisited the VCC concept in its 2024 work conference. Just a day later, the State-owned Assets Supervision and Administration Commission of the State Council (SASAC) announced it would include the market value management results in the assessment of SOE executives.

In our view, the VCC not only adds market value through SOEs performance review but is likely aimed at boosting the valuation of SOEs and may help investors better understand the big picture of state-owned capital transfers.

In Japan, the re-rating of listcos has also been connected to the country's pension scheme. NISA, which is Japan's PPS, was established by the government in a low interest rate environment in an effort to channel personal savings into the capital market. Documents from Tokyo Stock Exchange suggest the re-rating of Japanese stocks may help supplement the pensions of Japanese households and drive NISA's development.
