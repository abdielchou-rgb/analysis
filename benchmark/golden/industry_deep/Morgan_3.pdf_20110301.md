# FOMC Preview: September Meeting

The cycle begins with a 25bp cut and a stress on both sides of the dual mandate. The dot plot will have to change, but we do not expect Chair Powell to be firm on the size of future cuts. Our strategists maintain 2s20s steepeners.

## Key expectations

• The FOMC cuts the fed funds rate by 25bp to 5.125%. The FOMC statement acknowledges further progress on inflation and risks to the labor market. The Summary of Economic Projections (SEP) will likely show a shift to three cuts this year instead of one, marking-tomarket for softer inflation and labor market data.

• In the press conference, we do not expect Chair Powell to commit to a cadence for cuts, but to indicate that future decisions will be based on the data.

• We look for three 25bp cuts total this year.

• Our rates strategists suggest investors maintain UST 2s20s steepeners and maintain flatteners between the September FOMC OIS rate and November FOMC OIS rates.

• Our FX strategists recommend short USD/JPY positions as the Fed starts its cutting cycle.

• On the agency MBS side, our strategists remain neutral on the mortgage basis and prefer buying up-in-coupon conventionals.

## September meeting

We expect the Federal Reserve to move ahead with its first 25bp cut at the September FOMC meeting. We expect the FOMC statement to acknowledge further progress in inflation and rising risks to the labor market. In the press conference, we do not expect Chair Powell to go so far as committing to the size or cadence of future cuts. Instead, we think he will reiterate that the Fed remains data dependent and that future decisions will be based on the available data. We expect to see upward revisions to the unemployment rate and downward revisions to core PCE inflation in 2024 in the SEP. We also expect the median dot to shift from one to three cuts this year.

## FOMC policy statement

Current conditions – we look for an important change to both the characterization of the labor market and inflation that is an acknowledgement of the considerable rebalancing in demand/supply factors and further disinflation over the past few months.

GDP continues to grow at a "solid pace." We forecast GDP growth at 2.1% 4Q/4Q this year and next. The economy expanded by 3.0% Q/Q annualized in the second quarter, and we are tracking 3Q GDP growth at a solid 2.1% annualized pace (as of September 9) vs Atlanta Fed's GDPNow at 2.1% and NY Fed Nowcast at 2.6%.

The labor market remains healthy "but it has cooled." As of August, the 3-month average of monthly net new jobs was 116k, down from 243k at the beginning of the year. Since the start of the year the unemployment rate has moved up to 4.2%. Judging from Chair Powell's comments at Jackson Hole, we think the current unemployment rate level will not be characterized as low anymore.

Core PCE inflation has eased considerably, and August CPI data point to a continuing downtrend in core PCE. The downward path is consistent with our long-standing view that the Fed will cut three times this year, starting next week. On a 4Q/4Q basis, we forecast core PCE inflation slows from 3.2% last year to 2.6% this year and 2.1% in 2025.

Chair Powell and the FOMC have highlighted that risks to the outlook – on both growth and the labor market – are two-sided now. Even though inflation is still a consideration, labor markets have become more important to the Fed's reaction function as the economy and inflation cool. Signs of further deceleration in payroll prints (more prints below 100k) will put 50bp cuts on the table.

## Press conference

We think Chair Powell will acknowledge that the Fed should not take anything for granted when it comes to inflation, but still mention that the recent inflation readings add to the Fed’s confidence that inflation is on a sustainable path towards the 2% target. We expect he will note that the labor market is roughly in equilibrium and that he does not want meaningful further deterioration. We do not expect the Chair to give specific guidance on the pace of the cutting cycle. He will likely remain data dependent, indicating that future decisions will be a function of the available data.

## What to price, or not to price, that is the question

Market-implied Fed policy changed dramatically since we last previewed an FOMC meeting in July. Markets had priced the fed funds rate to trough at 3.45% back then. Today, the market prices the trough rate at 2.75% – a 70bp decline since before the July meeting.

In reaction to the July FOMC meeting, we had suggested investors maintain UST 2s20s steepeners and enter flatteners between the September FOMC OIS rate and November FOMC OIS rates (see Stay in UST steepeners, enter Sep/Nov FOMC OIS flatteners). We suggest investors stay in those positions today. But why?

Our economists expect the median 2025 dot to fall 100bp to 3.125% from 4.125% in June ( Exhibit 3 ). That decline compares to the market-implied trough rate falling 90bp over the same period, i.e., between the June FOMC meeting and today. In turn, that implies a small tightening of the risk premium embedded into current market prices.

The downward adjustment to appropriate policy rates probably reflects the additional slowing in the US labor market and inflation since June – both factors that affected market prices. As such, it makes sense that the risk premium embedded into market prices looks similar to June ( Exhibit 4 ).

The economic data between the September and November meetings should remain the most consequential driver of market-implied policy rates. But other markets – equity markets, in particular – may also play an important role.

We discussed this idea in You Can't Always Get What You Want When You Need It. If investors want the Fed to respond more forcefully to weaker economic data, i.e., 50bp rate cuts, but the Fed offers a more demure response, i.e., 25bp rate cuts, then these investors may shun risky assets.

Put differently, if investor growth expectations – generally forward-looking – deteriorate faster than FOMC participant plans to address them – more recently backward-looking – risky asset prices will come under renewed pressure, in our view. This framework comes from the Gordon Growth Model.<sup>1</sup>

• As such, the reaction of investors in risky assets to the FOMC meeting may be the most influential driver of rates in the near term, i.e., until the August JOLTS report on October 1 and the September nonfarm payroll report on October 4.

We don't think that investors will take comfort in how our economists think Chair Powell is likely to communicate in the press conference – especially after having delivered a 25bp rate cut instead of a 50bp cut. Many investors with whom we speak think a larger cut seems appropriate, given the starting point, i.e., the restrictive stance of policy.

These investors, many of whom think the economy is slowing more noticeably than the Fed appears to think, may shun risky assets – especially after their recent recovery.

• The S&P 500 index price sits above that which existed after the July and August nonfarm payroll reports – both of which disappointed.

A deterioration in the price of risky assets should help 2y Treasury yields decline from current levels. In addition, a steepening of the yield curve should accompany the decline in 2y yields ( Exhibit 5 ).

As for the 20y Treasury, we think it should continue to underperform as yields fall, even though that underperformance moderated more recently ( Exhibit 5 ).

With a 25bp Fed cut and some yield curve steepening expected to materialize next week (at least from the perspective of fed funds versus mortgage rates), the technical backdrop for mortgages has shifted positively over the last few weeks. Mortgages have been trading in anticipation of Fed cuts since last summer ( Exhibit 13 ), as cuts are expected to improve NIM for banks and REITs, lower FX hedging costs for overseas investors, and support money manager inflows as retail money gradually shifts into more fixed income.

This week also saw a few points of high-level regulatory clarity from Michael Barr's Basel III Endgame preview that, in combination with improved NIM, will likely support some bank demand in the near future. The re-proposal is estimated to increase the G-SIBs' CET1 capital requirements by 9% rather than the original 19%, and non-G-SIBS are estimated to see a 3-4% increase instead of 6%. While positive for securities demand, we note that lighter capital requirements for banks have been expected for some time now, and additional information around RWA treatment for securities and liquidity treatment for non-deliverable mortgages are still needed. Furthermore, yields on current coupon mortgages don't look particularly compelling for banks ( Exhibit 14 ), in our view. ## Disclosure Section

Mortgage Backed Securities (MBS) and Collateralized Mortgage Obligations (CMO)

Principal is returned on a monthly basis over the life of the security. Principal prepayment can significantly affect the monthly income stream and the maturity of any type of MBS, including standard MBS, CMOs and Lottery Bonds. Yields and average lives are estimated based on prepayment assumptions and are subject to change based on actual prepayment of the mortgages in the underlying pools. The level of predictability of an MBS/CMO's average life, and its market price, depends on the type of MBS/CMO class purchased and interest rate movements. In general, as interest rates fall, prepayment speeds are likely to increase, thus shortening the MBS/CMO's average life and likely causing its market price to rise. Conversely, as interest rates rise, prepayment speeds are likely to decrease, thus lengthening average life and likely causing the MBS/CMO's market price to fall. Some MBS/CMOs may have "original issue discount" (OID). OID occurs if the MBS/CMO’s original issue price is below its stated redemption price at maturity, and results in "imputed interest" that must be reported annually for tax purposes, resulting in a tax liability even though interest was not received. Investors are urged to consult their tax advisors for more information. Government agency backing applies only to the face value of the CMO and not to any premium paid
