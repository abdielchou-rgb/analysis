Recent discussion in these pages has focused on the shifting risk bias around our global narrative of sustained growth resilience, sticky inflation, and shallow central bank rate normalization. 1H24 performance hewed closely to these views: global GDP and core CPI inflation both exceeded expectations as DM central banks generally remained on hold. But momentum dipped around midyear, removing an earlier upside risk bias to our forecasts. This cycle has been replete with short-term momentum swings and there are good reasons to place a high noise-to-signal ratio on this year’s front-loaded growth and inflation profile. Through this noise, however, we see reasons to be concerned that weakening global manufacturing and US labor demand may be signaling a shift toward private sector caution.

These developments have not altered our baseline views on any major economy recently—with the notable exceptions of growth revisions to China (down) and Japan (up). In our updated scenario tree, we reflect recent news, modestly raising the assessed risk of a 2H24 US/global recession to 35% (Figure 1). A more substantial reassessment of the interest rate outlook has been concentrated in the US. We now expect the Fed to speed up rate normalization, delivering roughly 100bp of easing by year-end. Changes elsewhere are thus far limited to EM. Reflecting this shift, the cumulative probability we assign to our two high-for-long rate scenarios (too darn hot and high for real long) has been lowered to 30%, down from 50% as recently as two months ago.

Three underlying elements are worth highlighting to explain our focus on US interest rate risk.

Correlated risk should shake gradualism. Across the globe, central banks have guided toward a more cautious rate normalization than growth-inflation dynamics would warrant. We see this gradualist guidance through the lens of a risk management strategy in which optimism about growth resilience has remained high despite perceived restrictive stances and disinflation confidence has been tempered by tight labor markets and elevated wage gains. Against this backdrop, moderate but correlated shifts downward in growth and inflation risk can have a material impact on central bank behavior.

US at a different cyclical juncture. As we move through 2024, US (and China) policymakers are managing a demand downshift while most other major economies are tracking an anticipated lift from last year’s cyclical weakness (Figure 2). Against that backdrop, the Fed is likely to be particularly sensitive to any indication that the normalization in US labor demand amplifies downward dynamics in the broader economy.

US supply side delivers dividends. Global core inflation is still tracking our forecast for an elevated 3% 2024 rise as we look through the noisy 1Q24 upside and 2Q24 downside surprise. However, there has been a material positive shift in the risk profile on the US inflation backdrop as strong supply side performance combines with moderating labor demand to produce a significant easing in labor market conditions. US wage inflation is now slowing in a manner not seen in other DM economies. Combined with sustained solid productivity gains, US unit labor costs have realigned to a level broadly consistent with the Fed’s inflation target.

We believe these developments warrant a pickup in the cadence of Fed easing, but our forecast for a 50bp cut in September will likely require reinforcement of this risk bias shift in upcoming releases. Central in this discussion is that a more front-loaded Fed easing that responds to recession risks not realized will improve the 2025 growth outlook. While recognizing several uncertainties related to geopolitics and the US election, we continue to forecast both US and global GDP to rise at a modestly above-potential pace next year.

## July core inflation firms outside the US

After a volatile first two quarters in which global core CPI (ex China and Türkiye) rose 3.4%ar, we look for a limited moderation to 2.8%ar in 2H24. Our expectation for continued sticky inflation rests on the view that an incomplete decline in global service price inflation is tempered by modest firming in core goods inflation. At the same time, a regional rotation is anticipated as an unwind of an early year spike and more

easing in labor cost pressures is expected to produce a relatively large slide in 2H24 US core inflation. Signs of a cooling in US services price inflation was evident in June and we expect it to be reinforced in next week’s July release. July reports from nearly half of our GDW universe are already available and show core CPI gains tracking a 0.3%m/m rise. Last week’s Euro area print showed core HICP rose at a 3.7%ar in the three months to July, and core inflation firmed across much of EM although the source of upside varies across regions (Figure 3).

## European gradualism remains in place

The boost to US supply contrasts with weak performance in Europe, where labor markets are tight and wage inflation remains elevated. There is therefore no reason to shift views about inflation risk and central bank behavior. Experience shows that the pass-through of Fed policy changes is limited in the absence of a synchronized shift in growth and/or financial market conditions.

We thus stick to our forecast for a quarterly pace of 25bp cuts for the ECB and BoE, expecting them to ease next in September and November, respectively. The main risk to an earlier move comes from domestic downside growth surprises—with risks related to the Euro area PMI drop and UK riots worth monitoring. Norges Bank, meanwhile, is widely expected to be on hold next week despite a substantial downside surprise in this week’s July core inflation reading. Currency weakness is a focal point for the Committee, and the recent sell-off biases near-term risk in the direction of a hike. We still expect the next move to be a December ease but recognize Norges Bank as a Western Europe hawkish outlier.

## More than an EM ripple, less than a tide

By most benchmarks, EM monetary policy stances are sufficiently restrictive to warrant easing if our macroeconomic forecasts materialize. This is true even in economies that have eased markedly to date. Financial market volatility is an additional near-term constraint on EM easing. The unwind of carry trades has hit EM asset prices, portfolio flows, and exchange rates. While credit and inter-bank markets have remained calm, several central banks have expended significant reserves in FX intervention. On Thursday, Mexico’s Banxico delivered the expected 25bp rate cut, setting aside concerns over exchange rate pressures with the rise in downside risks to near-term growth. The peso, which had been appreciating this week in response to greater assurance of a tight 2025 budget and amendments to the proposed legislative changes, continued to strengthen after the cut. This could be a signal that EM financial pressures are abating. However, we think it’s unlikely that any material action will be forthcoming from EM central bankers before the September FOMC decision.

## Inflation, politics, key to 2025 EM easing

While policy rates are moving lower in EM, the path of real rates is expected to remain elevated vis à vis our assessment of macroeconomic fundamentals. The caution embedded in our forecast partly reflects inflation uncertainty, which remains a dominant concern across Latam and CEE. Measures of slack suggest inflation should move lower, but concern about poor supply-side performance and sticky inflation outcomes does not provide confidence on the path ahead. With this uncertainty elevated, central banks (and our forecasts) put less weight on indicators of slack and more on inflation, including as a lens for assessing capacity.

In addition, EM growth and financial conditions can be severely affected by changes in US trade, immigration, and foreign policies. The US election risk is likely to weigh heavily in the considerations of EM central banks for some time to come. Against this backdrop, it is not a surprise that the forecasted EM rate differential to the Fed has widened, awaiting clarity on these two key sources of risk (Figure 4).

## Good news for Japan’s consumers

Incoming data bolster our confidence that Japan’s economy is set to lift, and we see GDP rising at a roughly 2%ar through the middle quarters of this year. Absent a realization of downside growth risk elsewhere, the path ahead looks good for sustained above-potential growth. A key driver of this lift is the restoration of household purchasing power following a long period in which wage gains lagged inflation. The June wage data showed the largest rise since the mid-1990s, supported by strong summer bonuses and a trend increase in scheduled earnings. Real wage gains turned positive for the first time in over two years. With these gains, combined with temporary tax cuts, households should see a material boost to labor incomes. Further good news came from the BoJ CAI, which confirmed a 2Q consumption rebound after the 1Q slump, while SME sentiment also moved higher. Reflecting these improvements, we look for a 2%ar gain in next week’s 2Q GDP report. The unfolding virtuous cycle should keep the BoJ on a tightening path that takes the policy rate to 1% in 2025. However, it is likely to remain patient—we look for the next hike near year-end—as it awaits a period of financial market stability.

## Downside risks to China growth

The first batch of July data raise downside risks for a Chinese economy already expected to grow at a modest 4.5%ar in 2H24. Exports fell sharply in July. The breadth of the decline across trading partners raises larger questions on global growth but is a clear concern for an economy currently dependent on external sources of demand. Next week’s July data dump should reinforce this point as an expected softening in factory output gains is expected to be accompanied by sub-par retail and FAI growth. The government is stepping up its policy response, but the adjustment remains gradual. The State Council this week announced 20 measures to boost consumption and an earlier ban on online tutoring seemed to be relaxed. Monetary policy is also easing, but the overall policy response looks insufficient to offset downside risks to nearterm growth.
