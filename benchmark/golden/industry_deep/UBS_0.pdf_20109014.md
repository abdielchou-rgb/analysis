# Estimating the earnings impact from potential tariff hike

## Key takeaways from UBS analysts survey on tariff impact

We have surveyed UBS analysts on c.400 China stocks under our coverage for their estimate on the potential impact of a 60% US tariff on their companies' earnings, as a scenario analysis. Overall, we estimate that the US market contributes c.3% of the listed companies' revenue with c.20% of the US exposure currently subject to tariffs (at an average rate of 14%). For many listed companies, it would seem that they've already diversified their supply chains such that the direct earnings impact from a 60% US tariff would be quite limited. Our analysts estimate that roughly one third of costs could be passed down to the end customers and the aggregate earnings impact would be c.1% for those affected (or less than 0.5% of listed company earnings). Machinery, pet food, sportswear OEMs, biotech and tech hardware seemed to be the sectors most exposed. While it would appear that the direct tariff impact is limited for listed companies, the potential secondary effect on the equity market (via investor sentiment, lower domestic employment, more domestic competition if export goods are sold domestically and higher global inflation and rates) could be more meaningful.

## Sectors most at risk

Our survey suggests that the sectors whose earnings appear to be most at risk (at 3% or more) include machinery, pet food, sportswear OEM's, biotech and tech hardware. For tech hardware, the earnings impact could be above analyst estimates as for the component makers, it is difficult to ascertain the final destination of the goods produced or the level of tariffs that will be passed down by the customers. As such, for this exercise we have assumed the earnings impact to be zero for some of these tech companies. As a sensitivity, every 10% decline in tech hardware earnings would reduce MSCI China earnings by 0.2%. In terms of switching costs for US importers (Figure 10), healthcare suppliers appear to be the most costly while machinery, pet food and sportswear OEMs are the cheapest. Should there be a 100% tariff on Chinese companies operating out of Mexico, the earnings impact would be c.1% for the market with machinery, sportswear OEMs and pet food being the most affected sectors.

## What the market is pricing in on tariff?

For companies with considerable tariff exposure, if we compare the current valuation with trough levels reached during 2018 (i.e. the worst of the trade war), they are now trading only 8% above the trough even though the supply chain has now been relocated. Furthermore, export-oriented sectors have performed broadly inline with the broader market YTD though the trend differs significantly by sector with strong performances in home appliances offset by weaknesses in auto parts and solar. It would seem therefore that the market is pricing in some pessimism regarding the tariff outcomes. That said, our conversations with investors suggest that investors are not fully considering the secondary impacts on the economy, e.g. the potential loss of jobs if export businesses are shut down or if overseas goods are sold domestically.

## Which sectors are more vulnerable from a share price perspective?

Comparing the sector YTD performances, valuation vs the earnings impact from tariffs and overall overseas exposure, our conclusion is that machinery, tech hardware and home appliances are potentially the more exposed sector (from a share price and valuation perspective) given their positive YTD performance, the premium vs the trough valuation reached in 2018.

## A recap of major geopolitical events since start of trade war

 Apr 2018, President Trump unveiled his plans of applying 25% tariffs on US\$50bn of Chinese goods and the details of US\$34bn of goods in the first batch;

 Apr 2018, US firms were restricted from exporting to ZTE for 7 years, the first major sanction using export control towards Chinese companies;

 Jul 2018, the US announced plans to apply 10% tariff on another US\$200bn of Chinese goods

 Aug 2018, President Trump intended to hike the tariff from 10% to 25% on US \$200bn of Chinese exports;

 May 2019, the US included Huawei in the Entity List, restricting it from receiving US technology and exports;

 Nov 2020, US persons are prohibited from investing in any of the Chinese companies listed in the Non-SDN Chinese Military-Industrial Companies List;

 Jan 2021, three Chinese telecom ADRs were delisted from US Exchanges;

 Dec 2021, HFCAA finalized the rules to delist ADRs where PCAOB is denied access to conduct audit oversight;

 Feb 2022, CRRC and Wuxi Bio among other companies were put on US's unverified list which were subject to trade restrictions;

 Jun 2022, Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act (UFLPA) in effect to restrict Xinjiang originated goods from entering US market;

 Aug 2022, IRA and CHIPS Act signed into law aiming to counter China's influence in tech and renewables sector;

 Aug 2023, President Biden issued Executive Order restricting US Investments in Chinese tech companies in areas of AI and quantum computing;

 Jan 2024, Biosecure Act was first introduced at US Congress to restrict Chinese Biotech companies from operating in the US.

## Valuation Method and Risk Statement

For various stocks across the industries we cover in the Hong Kong and mainland China stock markets, we use a variety of valuation approaches, including DCF models, Gordon growth model analysis and relative valuation analysis using various multiples such as PE, EV/EBITDA and P/BV.

We think the risks facing China's equities include a hard landing for the property market, a capital exodus associated with currency depreciation and slow structural reform progress. In our view, any government policies that do not adequately address these risks could result in a shock to the market. For example, an excess of stimulus policies could pose a risk to the transition from an investment-driven to a consumption-driven economy and increase the debt of government and state-owned enterprises.
