## EM Fixed Income Focus

## Are we all hiding in the same place?

EM market moves (beta) remain at the mercy of US data developments and we have not viewed there to be good risk/reward in chasing these, which keeps us MW across EM local markets and sovereigns.

One response to this - and one we have taken - is to focus on EM idiosyncratic opportunities in both local and hard currency, to take <sup>‘</sup>idiosyncratic carry<sup>’</sup> while avoiding market beta.

EM local frontier and idiosyncratic positions have increased but do not look too large. For EM Sovereigns, <sup>‘</sup>non-defaulted<sup>’</sup> high spread countries have lost a lot of their value, although <sup>‘</sup>defaulted<sup>’</sup> bond prices are still more truly idiosyncratic.

• We also highlight recent work on opportunities in EM cross-currency basis to enhance carry, as well as the structural changes driving the long-term MXN trends ahead of a US (and Mexican) election.

## US data uncertainty keeps our views more in alpha than beta

The latest US data took some pressure off EM markets but we still see more opportunities in alpha than beta. EM fixed income market moves are at the mercy of US data developments, which have been exhibiting mini-cycles with regular switches and narrative revisions. EM central bank reaction functions have become increasingly dependent on US rates pricing given the disinflation process in EM has slowed and rate cutting cycles are advanced. We do not see these as great risk/reward markets for top down EM directional trades given uncertainty around the next US data prints, market levels that are not overly attractive and positioning that has shifted back closer to neutral. Given this data volatility, we think markets will become less reactive to individual US data prints, and instead will wait for greater accumulation of evidence to assess the Fed<sup>’</sup>s next steps. All-told we are staying MW across EM fixed income, but with focus on picking up good idiosyncratic carry opportunities.

This begs the question of whether the EM ‘idiosyncratic carry trades’ are already saturated: we find this is generally not the case. One response - that we have taken - to the macro uncertainty is to focus recommendations on EM idiosyncratic opportunities, but we are increasingly finding that many others also like them and some market levels have already seen large corrections. So are we all hiding in the same place? Overall, we find that is generally not the case. The positioning increase has been most in local markets idiosyncratic carry opportunities but positioning levels are not too concerning. Local yields are lower in these idiosyncratic opportunities but still not stretched. For sovereign credit, <sup>‘</sup>defaulted<sup>’</sup> countries can still have upside depending on how restructurings work out, but in <sup>‘</sup>non-distressed<sup>’</sup> EM sovereigns entry levels look least attractive and we have generally been moving slowly in the direction of reducing these recommendations once they have performed.

## Fed Fatigue and a Fear of Fading

Fed fatigue and an upcoming US election cycle could ultimately diminish the sensitivity of EM fixed income to near-term data prints. After being highly sensitive to the shifting narrative on the US rates outlook in recent months, the relatively subdued rally in EM fixed income post the benign April US CPI inflation release earlier this month was noteworthy. In part, it can be explained by the reluctance of investors to extrapolate and trade the latest data point after having been caught out by the narrative see-saw multiple times already. In retrospect, this reluctance was validated by the release of the latest FOMC minutes, which had a hawkish skew, as well as May PMIs. With key dates for the US presidential campaign approaching, we think it will become increasingly difficult to take views in emerging markets that are not contingent on the election outturn. With no imminent catalyst to resolve this regime, we think volatility could, counter-intuitively, be under downward pressure in the coming period. This would mean that, in the near term, EM rates and currencies are less sensitive to incoming data, unless there is convincing data opening the door for rate cuts (or even hikes). And as the US elections approach, macro data will anyway take a back seat to US election scenarios.

Carry opportunities in local markets. A prospective backdrop of lower datadependency should, in theory, prolong the already-favourable environment for idiosyncratic and carry opportunities in local markets. So far this year, investors have flocked to EM frontier and <sup>‘</sup>turn-around<sup>’</sup> opportunities in markets such as Egypt and Turkiye, as well as Latin American frontier markets. We continue to recommend long positions in these markets, although we have been adjusting some of our recommendations, such as taking profits in DomRep. Positioning is already consensus in markets such as Egypt, but Turkiye is one market that has significant capacity to attract further inflows should favourable inflation and de-dollarization trends continue. In addition to frontier and idiosyncratic stories, we also think there are compelling opportunities in EM cross-currency basis markets to boost carry without taking undue risk (see our recently published EM Spotlight: Back to Basis). For example, buying short-dated bills in markets such as Mexico, or swapping hard-currency sovereign bonds into local currency in markets such as Colombia or South Africa, can yield a signifcant carry pick-up (Figure 1).

EM currencies lag the rally in commodity prices. The prices of commodities have been rallying recently, a trend which EM commodity currencies have generally been overlooking. The Bloomberg Commodities index is up by 11% since February, with gains most pronounced in metals, although energy and agriculture prices have also risen over that period. This is driving a significant boost to terms of trade for EM commodity exporters. With most commodity sub-group prices at or above year-to-date highs, including gold, one may be tempted to conclude that there is a degree of <sup>‘</sup>shadow<sup>’</sup> dollar weakness lurking in the background, which is not being represented in EM currencies due to the factors we described above (Figure 2). There is unfortunately no straightforward way to express this view in liquid EM currencies. BRL, a currency which we are bullish on, has struggled to rally due to fiscal concerns, while elections later this month in South Africa could dominate the outlook for ZAR depending on the outturn. CLP and PEN stand most to gain from the rally in copper prices, but so far their response has been fairly muted, with copper also seeing some retracement in recent trading.

Structural factors reshaping the Mexican peso. MXN is the best performing EM currency in total returns over the past two and five years. We think the Mexican peso is in the midst of a structural transformation (see MXN: Not your padre<sup>’</sup>s peso II). It is no longer the high-beta risk proxy currency of old, but a more stable currency on a secular appreciation trend. This transformation is being driven by three forces: (1) The structural rise of remittances; (2) The availability of alternative portfolio hedging instruments, dampening the peso<sup>’</sup>s volatility; and (3) The geopolitical dividends from nearshoring. Mexico has overtaken China as the top exporter to the United States last year (Figure 3). Mexico<sup>’</sup>s presidential elections in June are unlikely to generate much market volatility. The US Presidential election, however, could generate far more volatility which is worth hedging against – even though the currency<sup>’</sup>s fundamentals will likely remain unchanged regardless of election result – leading us to add a new sell 3M USD/MXN 18.50 digital call to finance long 6M 18.50 digital call trade recommendation (see here). More structurally, we are OW MXN and MW local bonds in the GBI-EM Model Portfolio.

Are we all hiding in the same place? A look at how saturated the ‘idiosyncratic carry trade<sup>’</sup> is. One response - that we have taken - to the uncertainty about the path of upcoming US data prints and how influential they have been for EM overall market moves, is to focus recommendations and effort on EM opportunities that are as far away from beta as possible and much more driven by idiosyncratic or country-specific developments. EM has had plenty of these, as rising rates since early 2021 led to rolling crises in economies with vulnerabilities, which are now coming out of that distressed period. The better valuations and economic rebalancing that has been necessary to deal with the stress of higher rates has given some EM-specific opportunities, not driven by the next 20bp move in US rates. While we have liked these opportunities for a while, we find them becoming more consensus. If small markets become crowded at unattractive levels, there is the risk of costly unwinds.

Spreads for most EM hard currency‘distressed’ opportunities have already normalised, while local currency idiosyncratic opportunities remain. We have been OW the distressed part of the EM hard currency sovereigns for over a year, but many of these countries have already normalised and are no longer distressed. Looking at the <sup>‘</sup>non-defaulted<sup>’</sup> distressed countries (Figure 7), we can see that spread levels for many are already back to the lows of last 2.5 years since US rates started rising. So upside on these is more limited and we are only OW Ecuador and Mozambique (as well as recommending Argentina \$35s outright) in this group, which are ones will some room still to normalise. The better news on these countries is on positioning, as, while positioning has increased in some of the highest spread countries in the last 6 months, it is by no means uniform as others have seen reductions, which some lower spread countries also seeing high position addition according to our EM Client Survey (see Figure 4Fronties amg hcu wp d  EM). We view this as showing the HY theme is generally well-subscribed but with so many to choose from in sovereign credit then countries are themselves not overly crowded. The <sup>‘</sup>defaulted<sup>’</sup> distressed Sovereign credit is a different story. Here the drivers are not even macroeconomic rebalancings, but rather the likely upside due to the parameters of upcoming debt restructurings. Prices in this part of the market are not at their highs and show reasonable divergence (see Figure 8), with positioning also not showing concerning signs as these are really restructuring plays at this point.

In summary, the positioning increase has been most in local markets idiosyncratic carry opportunities, but positioning levels are not too concerning. Local yields are lower in these idiosyncratic opportunities but still not stretched, with FX spot near the weakest levels (as is to be expected). For sovereign credit, <sup>‘</sup>defaulted<sup>’</sup> countries can still have upside depending on how restructurings work out, but in <sup>‘</sup>non-distressed<sup>’</sup> EM sovereigns entry levels look least attractive and we have generally been moving slowly in the direction of reducing these recommendations once they have performed.

## Explanation of Emerging Markets Sovereign Research Ratings System and Valuation & Methodology:

Ratings System: J.P. Morgan uses the following issuer portfolio weightings for Emerging Markets Sovereign Research: Overweight (over the next three months, the recommended risk position is expected to outperform the relevant index, sector, or benchmark credit returns); Marketweight (over the next three months, the recommended risk position is expected to perform in line with the relevant index, sector, or benchmark credit returns); and Underweight (over the next three months, the recommended risk position is expected to underperform the relevant index, sector, or benchmark credit returns). NR is Not Rated. In this case, J.P. Morgan has removed the rating for this security because of either legal, regulatory or policy reasons or because of lack of a sufficient fundamental basis. The previous rating no longer should be relied upon. An NR designation is not a recommendation or a rating. NC is Not Covered. An NC designation is not a rating or a recommendation. Recommendations will be at the issuer level, and an issuer recommendation applies to all of the index-eligible bonds at the same level for the issuer. When we change the issuer-level rating, we are changing the rating for all of the issues covered, unless otherwise specified. Ratings for quasi-sovereign issuers in the EMBIG may differ from the ratings provided in EM corporate coverage.

Valuation & Methodology: For J.P. Morgan's Emerging Markets Sovereign Research, we assign a rating to each sovereign issuer (Overweight, Marketweight or Underweight) based on our view of whether the combination of the issuer<sup>’</sup>s fundamentals, market technicals, and the relative value of its securities will cause it to outperform, perform in line with, or underperform the credit returns of the EMBIGD index over the next three months. Our view of an issuer<sup>’</sup>s fundamentals includes our opinion of whether the issuer is becoming more or less able to service its debt obligations when they become due and payable, as well as whether its willingness to service debt obligations is increasing or decreasing.
