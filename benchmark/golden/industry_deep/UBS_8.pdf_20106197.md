# Global Equity Derivatives Strategy 2024 Outlook: Vol systems go

## Our models suggest volatility will rise

We have consolidated our multitude of new models for directional relative value and macro/market/stock-specific volatility forecasting. These are well-tested and in some cases use innovative approaches to model probability distributions that we compare to the option-implied (market) distribution across regions/sectors. Our conclusions include:

Modestly higher volatility: Low index volatility in 2023 (SPX 13%) is over as macro trends (rates-driven) begin to normalize slowly and forecasts indicate rising risk premia (15-25% expected volatility).

Macro, not micro volatility: Economic/policy uncertainty is rising at the same time that yields stabilize and Covid disruptions/divergences are waning. These suggest modestly higher correlations leading to modestly higher equity volatility. Growth is expected to slow, inflation fall, policy rates cut, and unemployment rise. Credit spreads may widen a little, but the macro growth slowdown is the key driver of the uptick in volatility in our model outputs. Some idiosyncratic stock level divergences continue to be a headwind to correlations and volatility – particularly relating to thematic exposures like AI and renewable transition.

## An idea generation platform

Our frameworks help us identify directional, relative value, volatility, and correlation opportunities. There is more information in our model output than we present as trade ideas, but we highlight the seven following opportunities we think appealing right now:

1. US elections: VIX Sep/Oct put calendars or Nov/Oct straddle swaps

2. Regional RV: Sell calls on US banks / buy calls on EU banks; US/Japan relative vol

3. Dispersion opportunities: US sector dispersion in selected sectors; Single stock volatility RV

4. Higher for longer hedge: Short profitless vs. long profitable Tech (via ARKK/ QQQ put switch)

5. Credit volatility RV: HY vs. IG credit (via LQD/HYG call switch)

## Executive summary

## The uniqueness of this piece

In building out frameworks for this Outlook, we think we have established something quite special. We combine a series of innovative models to present an increasingly integrated idea generation platform for directional, relative value, volatility, and dispersion opportunities.

Some of our models incorporate macroeconomic as well as cross-asset market forecasts. In others, we use stock fundamentals and market pricing behaviors. We use innovative approaches to analyzing probability distributions and have backtested our models to increase conviction in our regional, sector, and stock ideas.

This Outlook presents all these frameworks, the conclusions we draw from them right now and we highlight a range of appealing ideas.

## The micro outlook for volatility: Stock volatility RVs and custom vol dispersion framework

 We find that volatility is higher for companies with more expected growth opportunities, higher systematic and default risk and higher chances of extreme returns. Financial information about these outcomes explains stock price volatility. We call this our Single Stock Fundamental Volatility Cookbook (SSFV).

 Options market tend to underestimate volatility of stocks with greater (1) cash flow volatility, (2) research and development expenditure, and (3) earnings opacity relative to their sector medians; and overestimate volatility of stocks with greater (4) leverage, (5) return on assets, (6) size, (7) book-to-market value, and (8) sales growth.

 We present the results of this model that forecast stock level realized volatility (relative to index). When compared to implied vols, we can find a range of opportunities in directional volatility at the stock/basket level and in custom dispersion.

## Trade ideas

## 1) With liberty and justice for vol: Trading the VIX term structure ahead of US elections

Presidential election years tend to produce positive US equity returns both pre- and postelections. US equity volatility also tends to rise in tandem ahead of elections, before compressing post-elections into year-end (Figures 2-3).

Using the 2016 and 2020 elections as a playbook, we expect risk premia to be baked in well ahead of elections in November – We are already starting to see this play out. We present two ideas to trade the VIX term structure for US elections.

US elections this year could again provide opportunities across the volatility term structure. Using the 2016 and 2020 elections as a playbook, we expect risk premia to be baked in well ahead of elections in November – We already see \~a half point of volatility in the SPX options term structure (Nov vs. Oct regular expiries, respectively), and a more notable >3pt. premium in the VIX Oct future recently listed (vs. VIX Sep). The VIX futures curve typically provides a more effective way to capture event risk, in our view (Figures 4-5).

The VIX Oct contract technically captures US election event risk via the 30-day forward SPX options strip, but expires a few weeks prior to the actual election date. We would thus expect the VIX Oct future to remain relatively bid into expiry. Following Oct expiry, the Nov contract will be the front-month contract most reactive to any potential event risk coming to fruition post-event (Figures 6-7).

Investors may consider capturing rolldown prior to elections via selling VIX Oct puts to fund VIX Sep puts. Additionally, investors looking to hedge any 'fireworks' subsequent to actual elections may consider selling VIX Oct straddles to buy VIX Nov straddles, either delta-hedged or unhedged (Figures 8-9).

## 2) US vs. European banks call switch: Receive premium or leveraged EU bank upside exposure

Our Market Cookbook highlights that there is perhaps 8% downside to large US banks but modest upside to European banks, totalling a 10% potential return differential. However, the implied volatility of US banks might be too high to where we think it might realize in the coming months and European bank implied volatility too low – a 10% implied volatility differential.

Our Market Cookbook highlights that there is a 10% potential return differential between US and EU banks (EU over), but a 10% implied vol differential (US over). This combination suggests there is value in selling US bank index call options to fund European bank index call options (OTC).

## 6) Cuts like a knife: Hedging a fade in rate cuts pricing via ARKK/QQQ put switches

Our US Economists' forecast is for seven rate cuts this year (totaling 275bps), with the first 25bp cut still projected for March. We suggested late last year that US equity investors had clearly been positioning for a Fed pivot, first via a rush towards TLT call optionality (Figure 25), followed by exuberance in IWM call optionality. Both positioning extremes have since abated, in line with the FF futures-implied probability for a 25bp rate cut in March declining to <50% currently vs. >80% at the start of the year.

We flag our preferred US ETF options relative value pairs most geared to the momentum trade garnered by the Fed pivot (Quality vs. Beta vs. Size). Further uncertainty surrounding a March FOMC cut could provide a floor for bond volatility, a near-term risk to our conviction call for bond vs. equity volatility compression.

The chase to price in (and more recently, price out) rate cuts has garnered a notable momentum trade in US equities over the last several months. This trade has been characterized by Quality, Size, and Beta factors all trading in lockstep with rates and US equity volatility – i.e. Yields higher = VIX higher + outperformance in high/low Quality + low/high beta + large/small cap; Figure 26). This is a relationship we see persisting in the near-term, particularly as (1) direction of rates continues to drive the narrative, and (2) the impact from higher yields continues to weigh on lower quality pockets of the market. Short profitless Tech vs. long profitable Tech (proxied via ARKK/ QQQ) flags as one of the top relative value pairs most geared to this dynamic (Figure 27). Investors may consider trading put switches in this pair. Other pairs that exhibit high correlation/beta to this theme include: IWM/QQQ, KRE or KBE/XLF, JETS/USO, and HYG/ LQD (more details on next page).

## 7) Give credit where credit is due: HY vs. IG credit decompression via LQD/HYG call switches

Using our Cross-Asset Volatility Carry model, we find selling credit volatility has offered the best carry over the last five years (Figure 29). Top-down, this can be attributed to (1) the explicit Fed put post-Covid, and (2) tightening credit spreads offsetting the impact from higher rates (Figure 30). Bottom-up, (1) lack of HY defaults/bankruptcies as the effects of tightening have yet to fully be felt by lower quality corporates (Figure 31), and (2) sector dispersion, similar to US equities.

HY over IG outperformance has largely been duration-driven as rates have moved higher. We see this script flipping with rates expected to move lower in 2024, and a larger widening in HY credit spreads relative to IG.

Within credit itself, we see opportunity in trading credit spread decompression via HYG/ LQD relative. LQD has underperformed HYG by \~6% in price return terms (\~10% total return) over the last two years, though this has largely been a duration story as rates have shot higher (\~4y duration for HYG vs. LQD \~9y; Figure 32).

Rates moving lower in 2024 could drive a bid to credit, particularly in longer-duration IG vs. shorter-duration HY. HY upside could be relatively more limited, given our Credit Strategists' view for greater credit spread widening in HY vs. IG. This negative correlation between credit spreads and bond yields has historically fostered HY underperformance vs. IG, and higher HY volatility vs. IG in turn (Figures 33-34). Investors may consider buying LQD calls funded via HYG calls as a long duration, long Quality-biased RV trade.

## The macro outlook for volatility

Higher macro volatility but a weaker trend in bond yields is expected for 2024. This could increase index correlations modestly and spur larger moves for key macro data points.

We suggested in our 2024 Outlook that we could see a higher macroeconomic-driven US equity volatility environment, partially offset by lower corporate and positioningdriven ('market') volatility (here). These offsetting factors could continue to keep the absolute level and reactivity of volatility capped.

As our Chief US Economist Jonathan Pingle suggests here, the volatility we have witnessed in the underlying macro data has been historically elevated. He primarily flags: (1) forward downward revisions in labor market data, and (2) the gap between the growth in real GDP and GPI having been substantial (Figure 35). Widening budget deficits amidst US elections could cause macroeconomic-driven volatility to persist.

"For example, the cumulative downward revisions to monthly nonfarm payroll employment year-to-date have totaled -372K (the monthly employment reports from January through November). Labor income estimates saw another thwack lower. What the data says one month, can look a lot different a month later...Gross Domestic Product, measured by tracking spending in the economy, and Gross Domestic Income, measured by tracking income in the economy, are supposed to equal. However, over the most recent four quarters of data, the gap between the two, also known as the the statistical discrepancy, has swung by more than at any time in the current data series back to 1947 (when deflated by GDP). The gap is supposed to be zero, and stay zero."

Despite higher macroeconomic volatility through 2022/2023, option-market implied moves ahead of key macro data releases (e.g. CPI, NFP, FOMC) have become more muted of late – This due in part to the lower US equity volatility backdrop (Figure 36). We observe that actual moves in US equities (absolute close-close and intraday) relative to implied have been larger when data has surprised to the downside, and have been more muted for upside surprises (Figure 37). Consistent with the view from our US Economists, we expect relative realized moves for key macro data releases to be larger in 2024 – A near-term return to a 'good is good'/'bad is bad' narrative amidst a lower volatility backdrop keeping implied moves at bay further support this argument, in our view (Figure 38).

## Macro, Market, and Single Stock Fundamental Cookbooks

## The fundamentals of volatility

Yes, volatility is mean reverting. So, percentiles and time-series analysis of volatility have their place. However, volatility has fundamental causes, rooted in macroeconomics and corporate fundamentals. Our goal in UBS Derivative Strategy is to connect these fundamentals with volatility in a way that illuminates opportunities in flow derivative products, as well as the curation of QIS strategies that target alternative risk premia.

## Uncertainty and probability

Markets price a fundamentally uncertain future and prices of derivative payoffs reflect probabilities. Our analysis of the fundamental drivers of volatility must therefore allow for uncertainty and non-linear relationships in ways that traditional statistical methods cannot. More of our research will take this approach, with techniques that allow probability distributions to reveal themselves from the historical data.

## The Cookbooks

Our new suite of models include:

The Macro Cookbook: This balances trade-offs among GDP growth, inflation, employment, and policy rates with S&P 500 volatility regimes. It uses a history of levels and changes in these key economic variables back to the 1960's to find similarities across 256 possible regime combinations. We assess historical weekly returns in these regimes to build an estimate of realized volatility that would reflect our economic forecasts.

The Market Cookbook: In this model, we advance our distributional analysis across the US and European markets to the sector/industry level. We produce return distributions for given shifts in market-based macro variables that reflect components of the "dividend discount model" drivers of equities - real yields, inflation breakevens, credit spreads and equity growth indicators. The multinomial lattice approach allows for rich distributional sampling covering a huge range of market possibilities around baseline forecasts in these key market metrics.

The Single Stock Fundamental Cookbook: Differing levels of growth, profitability, leverage, and other fundamental metrics are consistent with differing levels of single stock volatility relative to the market. Our analysis captures this baseline volatility difference between stocks based on their reported fundamentals.

## Macro Cookbook

## Measuring out the ingredients

The market is driven by a complex mixture of business cycle drivers. Output growth,unemployment, inflation, and the Fed funds rates are four of the most important and are forecast by our economists. We have developed a model - Macro Cookbook, using data since 1967, that gives us great clarity into market behaviour at the cross sections of these factors at varying levels and for varying changes - that's 256 potential market regimes (recipes?), although far fewer are ever realized.

## A rising souffle

Our economists forecast that as real rates peak, output will slide into recession, unemployment will rise and inflation will fall. Our model shows that from current levels and with these forecast changes, this combination usually delivers more volatile returns. That's not surprising, but the bulk of this risk is concentrated in our H2 2024 forecasts, which we think lead to 90-day realized volatility back up above 20%. Before that we see a period of rising volatility with a lower bound of 15%.

## Have cake and eat it

Now that we have fully baked the metaphors, what are the opportunities from this analysis? Firstly, implied volatility looks to have too little premium to our ex-ante realized volatility forecast for the next few quarters. This premium needs to cover the likely negative skew of returns over this period as well as the potential for vol to be higher. Volatility looks moderately underpriced for the risk of a disinflationary recession that we expect in the middle of this year. After that, rate cuts may struggle to deliver lower volatility if the unemployment gap continues to widen. In short, the implied volatility term structure is looking straight through what we expect will be a less linear business cycle trajectory.

## Market Cookbook

The Market Cookbook is a multi-faceted framework that not only focuses on the average returns, but also analyses several aspects of the market's performance distribution. The goal is to estimate US and European stock index, sector and industry market risk metrics for the next three months in indices/ETFs that are tradable in option markets and reflect our strategists' views. These include return expectations, volatility and skewness, as well as probabilities of drawdowns and rallies.

## Conditional return distributions

We forecast important market factors like real rates, breakevens, credit spreads, and growth for the next three months. In addition, we randomise weekly factor outcomes around their expected paths via a multinomial lattice. Finally, we simulate weekly returns three months forward, following factor probability-weighted conditional distributions. In the process, we account for serial correlations. Sounds complex? It is. But it is a fascinatingly intuitive but rigourous way to incorporate economics and finance into plausible market return distributions.

Macro-financial outlook: The analysis focuses on US and European market-based macro drivers of equity returns - weekly changes in 10-year real rates, breakeven inflation, corporate high yield credit spreads and our equity growth indicators. They capture the main variables of the "dividend discount model" - risk-free discount rates, risk premia, and growth expectations.

The most prominent factors in both regions for the next three months are the expectations of moderate growth slowdowns. The key difference is that growth slows more in the US (-0.5 std. dev.) than in Europe (-0.25 std. dev.), while the credit spreads widen +25bps, the yield curve steepens 40bps and breakeven inflation remains roughly at the same levels. Also present in our assumptions are elevated factor volatilities, especially in real rates. The growth slowdowns in both regions and wider credit spreads generally weigh on returns and raise volatility in the next three months. However, not all segments of the market react the same and market implied volatility levels matter.

Figure 56: The model input variables: 3-month US & European macro-financial projections
![](images/c07d24adf27629744ee99dc81c26ba61eed5f2483e5d517b96161eea5b0b70ca.jpg)
Source: UBS, Bloomberg

1) Return distribution building blocks: First, we obtain a weekly return probability density for each index, sector, and industry, as a function of the macro-financial variables. We further differentiate the densities, based on whether recent realized returns are positive or negative. The densities are estimated by means of kernel methods, trained on weekly historical data since 2001. These are the building blocks for sequences of weekly return distributions, projected thirteen weeks forward.

2) Introducing forecast uncertainty: The density functions produce return distributions for given inputs. We can generate distributions along our forecast paths. However, that would miss the impact of uncertainty. Therefore, next, we design alternative weekly macro-financial variable outcomes, each with a given probability, that on average follow their forecasts. This is accomplished by means of multinomial lattices. These outcomes shape alternative return distributions that could unfold each week with varying probabilities. We probability-weight the alternative return distributions to create two weekly sequences of distribution mixes, given positive or negative recent returns.

3) Simulating returns: Finally, we simulate a million return sequences over thirteen weeks, following the weekly distributions. At this stage, we incorporate the impact of serial correlation - the direction of past returns impacting current returns. This produces market risk views from two perspectives. Firstly, we obtain a three-month return distribution and compute the expected return, standard deviation, skewness, and probabilities of drawdowns and rallies. Secondly, we obtain the distribution of threemonth return series volatility. We compare it to three-month option price implied volatility. We also compute volatility distributional properties - its own volatility, skew, and probabilities of alternative outcomes.

We compare the simulated return distributions to the distributions implied by threemonth option market prices (Appendix). More specifically, we compare our three-month volatility and return expectations to corresponding market implied volatility and returns.

## Single Stock Fundamental Volatility Cookbook

## A framework connecting fundamentals to stock volatility

Our Macro and Market Cookbooks relate stock index volatility to macro fundamentals. However, individual stock performance can diverge significantly from market trends. Therefore, in single stock volatility analysis we incorporate both market-wide and stockspecific drivers in a dynamic panel model of S&P 1500 stocks, spanning intra-earnings reporting periods from 1Q 2005 to 3Q 2023.

We find that financial statement disclosures reveal important fundamental information that correlates with future realized stock volatility, such as growth opportunities, operations uncertainty, credit and systematic risks.

 Growth opportunities: (1) R&D expenditure and (2) sales growth;

 Operations uncertainty: (3) size and (4) cash flow volatility; and

 Systematic risk: (5) book-to-market ratio and (6) earnings opacity;

 Default risk: (7) leverage and (8) return on assets.

Realized volatility tends to be higher for companies with more expected growth opportunities, higher systematic and default risk and higher chances of extreme returns. Our single stock volatility framework relates stock-specific and systematic volatility to these financial statement metrics. This provides a more granular perspective, aiding in the identification of undervalued and overvalued stock options, based on intrinsic company characteristics and market implied stock volatility.

We believe that the stock option market does not always reflect information relevant to stock volatility. For example, the market tends to underestimate volatility of stocks with greater cash flow volatility, research and development expenditure, and earnings opacity, and overestimate volatility of stocks with greater leverage, return on assets, size, book-to-market value, and sales growth. We identify 30 option selling and buying candidates among S&P 500 stocks, with the highest and lowest expected two-month implied volatility premia, based on company fundamentals ad their index betas.

## A Leading Index approach to forecasting market distributions

## Summary:

 The OECD CLI has been rising and is potentially heading toward an 'expansion phase

 Historically, the current regime probability mix suggests the SPX should trade between 4000 and 4800

The OECD CLI leading index distribution is a way of calibrating the business cycle with a plausible range for the market.

 This range is rising 50-100 points per month recently as the leading index continues to improve

## Detail:

The strength and momentum of leading indices has historically been a valuable tool when forecasting the strength and momentum of equity markets. We consider four macro regimes: Strong and improving (expansion), strong and weakening (slowdown), weak and weakening (downturn), and weak and improving (recovery). Returns, volatility, and skew can vary significantly through the business cycle and understanding these differences gives us the tools to forecast a plausible distribution for equity markets that is calibrated to history.

The market prices a balance of probabilities. We put this at the core of our analysis.

Figure 68: Business cycle regime probabilities can be calculated based on leading index strength and momentum. Illustrative example from 2015-2019
![](images/a83d237a84250878a77e8ee9f78c03a274db34bb74cfe2e707790f54f448e39e.jpg)
Source: UBS, OECD

We use the OECD Composite Leading Indicators index (amplitude and seasonallyadjusted) as our basis for business cycle specification. This index takes leading economic indicators and financial market data from most G-20 countries and seeks to calibrate the series to GDP outcomes in the coming 6-9 months. The index is subject to revisions but research suggests these are negligible at the global level, with no directional bias, and the direction of travel is not revised in the following month 90% of the time. To calibrate the CLI to the US equity market, we take use 80% of the US CLI and 20% of the EU CLI to better reflect where S&P 500 revenues are generated.

We began 2023 with the CLI entering a recovery phase (low and rising). We doubted the sustainability of this but we now enter 2024 with the CLI on the cusp of an expansion phase (high and rising). Recoveries and expansions are the best environments for equity returns historically. The improvement in the CLI is in contrast to tighter monetary policy and weakness in other leading indicators like the PMIs. We believe the divergence reflects the high starting point for nominal GDP growth, a resilient consumer, and supportive fiscal policies. Given the ongoing slowdown in economic growth and the transition toward tighter fiscal policies, that there is still a chance that the macro regime rolls back over into a downturn. Nonetheless, our equity views in this outlook consider the need to hold positions that will perform well if we are wrong on that. This includes a desire to hold some value and cyclical positions in addition to balancing exposures in structural growth sectors that benefit from lower bond yields.

A key extension to our work on regime modelling is that we acknowledge the uncertainty in leading indicators. They are, after all, merely an indication of the future and so we extract the distribution of probabilities for all regimes that the current level of change of leading indicators present.

The chart below shows the dominant recovery probability in 2023 is giving way to a rising probability of expansion. When we utilise historic average returns, volatility, and skew, we can create a forecast distribution for the equity market based on these probabilities and historical metrics. These indicate that the S&P 500 could plausibly trade between 4000 and 4800 through the first half of this year. The range is rising around 50- 100 points per month recently, however, and the leading index improves and the market stays well above the 200-day moving average.

## REVS: A framework for stock/sector/correlation ideas

## Summary:

 Our REVS framework is a well backtested model-of-models supporting relative value investing. It looks at stock behavior relating to the macro regime, earnings, valuation and sentiment measures. We publish the results of our analysis every month with the latest publication here.

 The framework allows for the generation of ideas for directional, relative value and correlation related trades across sectors and stocks.

REVS currently favors cyclical sectors over defensives across the US, Europe, Japan, China and Australia. Technology is generally favored across regions except China where it is amongst the least favored sectors. This suggests value in a US tech over China tech position. This is a relative value that has been performing well for years and continues to since the January 10 publication of this analysis.

## Detail:

Our Market REVS framework combines macro and micro elements to identify shorterterm relative preferences across countries, industry groups and styles. We believe the key drivers for equities are the:

1. Macro regime (R)

2. Earnings trends and surprises (E)

3. What's priced into valuations already (V) and

4. Changes in the sentiment of investors (S)

Using some proprietary and public data, we have created a structure that gives us useful indicators in each of these areas and we can adapt our prioritization of each component as market conditions change. Taking each in turn, we can explain our decision to use these in our framework.

Start your engines! Market REVS incorporates signals from key market drivers.

Regime - Based on the current regime probability framework explained in the previous section, we can calculate a measure of potential performance for each country, industry group, and style based on their performance in a similar regime distribution in the past. We do this at the macro and the sector level using OECD and PMI leading indicators.

R is for Regime. The macro economy can be significant or dominant in equity market returns.

Macro Regime Probability model: Stock prices are greatly influenced by economic regimes. We calculate regime probability weighted historic returns to give us an indication of relative performance in the current environment.

Sector PMI (New Orders) model: We utilise sector level PMI New Orders data to calculate a probability weighted sector regime score.

Earnings - We think expectation changes, particularly surprises, are key drivers of relative returns between countries and industry groups. The two metrics we currently utilise in this area are:

E is for Earnings. The micro economy can also be a source of
differentiation within and through regimes.

Machine learning EPS forecast vs. consensus model: Our quant team have done impressive work in creating a model for 12-month forward single stock EPS growth. They have shown that this is more accurate than consensus estimates and particularly during periods of earnings volatility. We take the deviation of this machine learning estimate from consensus as a measure of potential earnings surprise. (link)

EPS revision strength model: Analyst revisions to earnings estimates often coincide with share price performance. Many use simply the number of analyst upgrades versus downgrades but we think this misses too much information about the scale of upgrades and downgrades. We therefore look at the recent changes in the actual EPS estimate.

Valuations - Whatever is happening in the macro regime or earnings, there is always a good chance this information is 'in the price'. A volatile and poorly performing sector might already be incredibly cheap and have less downside risk without further macro or earnings deterioration. Our valuations measures are designed to calibrate for the relative valuation already reflected in markets.

V is for Valuations. We look for differentiation in what is priced in to markets already.

Adjusted Forward PE Ratio model: Value is a popular strategy for patient investors. We enhance one of the most popular valuation indictors, the fPE Ratio, by adjusting the numerator (i.e. prices) by the quality of the companies balance sheets.

(Extreme) Rates Momentum model: The speed and magnitude of bond moves are ones of the key factors of investors' risk appetite and valuation changes. Increasing equity exposure after a strong rally or sell-off in rates has historically been rewarded with rising valuations.

Sentiment - Other factors not captured in our regime, earnings or valuation metrics relate to market structure. Specifically, we look to whether recent trends in investor activity give us an indication of momentum or exhaustion. The metrics we consider in this area are:

S is for Sentiment. We think shorterterm measures of market positioning and flows further enhance our understanding.

Crowding model: Our quant team have utilised a variety of sources including market wide prime brokerage data to generate a crowding score at the stock level. We have aggregated this to the country and industry level taking into consideration both the level of crowding and the change in crowding. At an extreme, positioning can be a good contrarian signal. (link)

Up&Down Volatility model: This is a price-based indicator of positioning that gauges the probability of a trend reversal based on the recent balance of upside versus downside volatility.

CTA Momentum models: These aim to benefit from the persistence in asset prices. Our CTA trend-following algorithm is applied on 1) stock, 2) industry, 3) sector, and 4) country index prices.

We believe our REVS framework pulls together some key variables that will help us determine relative preferences for countries and industry groups. We can be adaptive in our weighting of each measure as market conditions change.
