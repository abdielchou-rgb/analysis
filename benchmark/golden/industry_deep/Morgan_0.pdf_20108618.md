## Market commentary

The rally in front-end Treasury yields ultimately proved short-lived when Friday<sup>’</sup>s payrolls report showed job gains rebounded significantly in May. Two-year Treasury yields traded to a local low of 4.72%, only to move back to 4.87% on Friday, down only 2bp week over week. Soft data initially led the rally. The ISM composite index came in weaker than expectations, declining by 0.5-pts to 48.7, despite the output PMI improving to 53.0 from 51.1 in April (see US: Mixed messages in May manufacturing surveys, M. Hanson, 6/3/24). Job openings also dropped by 296k to 8.06mn in April, a larger decline than expected, and the lowest level of vacancies since February 2021. The prior month<sup>’</sup>s level was also revised down from 8.49mn to 8.36mn (see US: JOLTS data show further softening in April, M. Tasci, 6/4/24). Combining that with a weaker-than-expected ADP print, where private payrolls rose 152K vs. consensus of 175K, and claims rising by 8K on the week, slightly above consensus, the data seemed to be foreshadowing a labor market that is coming into better balance.

Then, the closely-watched nonfarm employment figure came in meaningfully above expectations, increasing by 272k in May. Pay growth also increased by 0.4% during the month, with the year-ago figure now at 4.1%. However, the unemployment rate increased to 4.0%, the highest level since January 2022. Over the past 12 months, the unemployment rate is up 0.4%-pts, while nonfarm employment grew by an average of 230k per month. The confluence of these two factors point to the importance of immigration in boosting trend, or breakeven, job growth, and the fact that the monthly establishment survey is likely overstating the pace of job growth.

With the strong job growth in May and persistent strength in wage growth still very far from meeting the Fed<sup>’</sup>s broader test, our economists now see a July ease as highly unlikely. They now expect the first ease in November, followed by quarterly cuts next year (see Strong jobs overshadow rising unemployment, M. Feroli, 6/7/24). Against this backdrop, we have revised our forecasts to reflect a later start to a Fed rate cut and a shallower path of a Fed easing cycle. We now expect SOFR and EFFR to end the year at 5.10% and 2y Treasury yields at 4.60% (Figure 1).

Looking ahead to next week, the FOMC will likely leave the target range unchanged. Regarding the SEP, our economists expect the median dot for <sup>’</sup>24 to show two eases (previously three). Moreover, the median dot for next year could reflect three eases, and the longerrun median dot possibly moved up to 2.75%. For this year<sup>’</sup>s economic projections, our economists expect GDP growth will be revised down from 2.1% to 1.5% and core PCE revised up from 2.4% to 2.8%, and year-end unemployment revised up a tick to 4.1%. We don<sup>’</sup>t anticipate material changes to the <sup>’</sup>25, <sup>’</sup>26, or longer-run projections (see FOMC preview, M. Feroli, 6/7/24).

Separately, this week, the Fed released the results of its March Senior Financial Officer Survey, which surveyed 98 domestic and foreign banks holding over 75% of the total reserve balances in the banking system. Of the respondents, 70% expected the size of their bank<sup>’</sup>s balance sheet to remain roughly unchanged over the subsequent six months, with less than 10% expecting theirs to decrease. Importantly, a plurality of respondents reported that their bank expects to take actions to maintain its level of reserve balances and HQLA. Furthermore, over 75% of the respondents noted that they prefer to keep “additional reserves<sup>”</sup> above their LCLOR estimates. Importantly, among that group, over half reported that they prefer to keep “additional reserves<sup>”</sup> of 11-50% of LCLOR and another 40% preferred greater than 50% of LCLOR. Interestingly, when asked about the smallest spread to IORB at which their bank would consider substituting reserves for other assets, respondents generally reported a 10-20bp spread needed to rotate into overnight repo, fed funds, dollar deposits, FX swaps, and money market Treasury securities, though this differed across domestic banks (10-30bp) and foreign banks (5-10bp). Finally, with regards to funding needs, banks expressed a preference for FHLB advances, repo, fed funds, and Eurodollars for overnight funding, and FHLB advances, CP/CDs, and repo for term funding needs.

## A deep dive into non-financial CP

Compared to last year, non-financial corporations have played a slightly more active role in raising money market credit supply thus far this year. Collectively, this issuer base has tapped the CP market by an additional \$58bn (or 22%), up to nearly \$325bn year-to-date. This is the highest level of outstandings since the onset of the pandemic in March 2020. When looking at the spilt of net supply by rating cohort, Tier 1 issuers make up the bulk of this year<sup>’</sup>s increase, up an additional \$43bn, while Tier 2 corporates added an additional \$12bn to outstandings during the same period (Figure 2).

Interestingly, the increase to overall supply has been broad-based across different sectors, albeit still primarily concentrated in Health Care companies. Based on DTCC data, Health Care companies increased their CP outstandings by \$36bn YTD, followed by Energy \$14bn, Consumer Staples \$10bn, and Communication \$8bn, to name a few (Figure 3). We believe M&A activity has been one of the main factors behind the growth in CP outstandings this year, particularly in the Health Care sector. To that end, Health Care companies have grown to be the largest issuers of corporate CP, and we estimate their aggregate outstandings to be \$68bn, or 21% of total non-financial outstandings (Figure 4Health Crcompnis vgw b uf P,d   \$68 21% - ).

It is perhaps worth noting that some issuers have kept a slightly higher CP maturity profile since early 2023. Indeed, weighted-average maturities for Tier 1 issuers have increased by 8 days to 42 days over that period (Figure 5). Furthermore, total outstandings across 1m and 3m tenors rose by \$14bn and \$13bn, respectively, since Janurary of last year (Figure 6). A flatter CP yield curve and investor appetite to extend within the money markets could be contributing to corporates concentrating their maturities slightly longer on average. Currently, the 1m3m yield curve for Tier 1 non-financial CP is at -4bp and has traded close to flat or negative since May of last year (Figure 7). Meanwhile, Tier 1 and Tier 2 yield spreads to bills have traded mostly in a narrow range over the past year, reflecting continued demand for corporate supply (Figure 8).
