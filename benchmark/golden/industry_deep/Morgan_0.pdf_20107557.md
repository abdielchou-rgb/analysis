With the CPI and PPI data in hand, we think that core PCE inflation is tracking a 0.27% gain for the month, which would allow the year-ago reading to tick down from 2.8% to 2.7%. After this week<sup>’</sup>s repricing, rates markets are not priced for a full 25bp rate cut until September, and imply just 45bp of eases for this year (Figure 1). Though the recent strength in the data has dampened our confidence, we still think that inflation is set to moderate over time. The Fed will receive four CPI reports before the July FOMC meeting, and our baseline still calls for the first rate cut at that meeting, followed by a pace of quarterly 25bp cuts at subsequent SEP meetings thereafter (see Economics).

In light of this, our fair value framework suggests that 10-year Treasury yields are now trading about 30bp too high, after controlling for near-term Fed expectations, and medium-term inflation and growth expectations (Figure 2). The gap between Treasury yields and their model-implied fair value has increased to more than 2 standard deviations, a level that was only seen for about a month last fall, and before that in the fall of 2022. However, we are reticent to add duration at current levels, because it will take multiple softer inflation and labor market prints for market participants to feel confident the Fed can begin to normalize monetary policy. Moreover, absent a catalyst, we think it<sup>’</sup>s challenging to recommend duration longs in a world in which the curve remains inverted and carry in these positions remain negative. Instead, we prefer to hold 5s/30s curve steepeners: even though we may not see a material decline in front-end yields in the near term, structural factors argue for higher term premium at the long end of the curve (see Treasuries).

Additionally, we recommend holding longs in 5-year TIPS. Despite the repricing in Fed policy expectations, the front end of the inflation curve continues to imply a significant step down in the pace of core CPI inflation over coming months, and 5-year TIPS breakevens appear roughly 16bp too narrow versus our fair value estimate. Moreover, in contrast to nominal Treasuries, 5-year TIPS offer 15bp of positive carry over the next two months and provide a better hedge against the risk that inflation proves stickier than expected (see TIPS).

In derivatives, a nuanced look at implied probability distributions from December SOFR options shows that markets are assigning significant weights to a disparate set of Fed scenarios, and the dominant scenario with the largest weight has now become the 1-hike scenario (Figure 3). To be sure, it is possible that markets will price in larger weights on hiking scenarios and pressure forwards even higher. But it is more likely the case that sticky inflation data could delay the onset of Fed easing rather than force the Fed to hike further, and this analysis furthers our conviction that the repricing in the Fed path has gone a bit too far. Elsewhere in options markets, implied vols rose on the week, led by the upper left, which has mostly converged to fair value. Thus, we recommend switching bullish exposure to longer expiries in short-to-intermediate tails, which remain cheap, and where rising policy uncertainty in the Reds should be supportive. We also maintain a bearish view on longer-expiry swaption vol in 30-year tails. In swap spreads, we maintain a widening view in the 2-year sector, which is likely to be supported by negative T-bill issuance over coming weeks, but we turn neutral on spreads in the 5- and 30-year sectors (see Interest Rate Derivatives).

Attention also shifted towards balance sheet policy this week, as the March FOMC minutes offered a little more information about how Fed officials are thinking about the QT taper process. The Committee appears broadly inclined to cut in half the monthly cap on Treasury runoff (currently \$60 billion), but to leave the \$35 billion monthly cap on MBS unchanged. The minutes weren<sup>’</sup>t specific on the timing of this action but did indicate they would like to do this “sooner rather than later.<sup>”</sup> Longer-run balance sheet issues such as the composition of assets were largely left for future meetings. We continue to believe that the Committee will announce a halving of the Treasury cap at the next FOMC meeting in early May, with implementation occurring in mid-May (see Fed balance sheet plans coming into better focus, Michael Feroli, 4/10/24). Notably, members discussed “initial perspectives on longer-term aspects of balance sheet policy<sup>”</sup> including “options to achieve in the longer run a portfolio that consists primarily of Treasury securities.<sup>”</sup> While the first option would be to reinvest MBS paydowns into Treasuries at some point, another would be outright MBS sales, which were debated in 2018. While the Chair noted in his latest presser that it<sup>’</sup>s too early to have this debate in earnest, markets should be ready for more discussion on sales as there are already operational steps being taken to ensure that it is a viable option.

In the near term, Fed balance sheet dynamics bear watching, as we expect the TGA should rise sharply in coming days due to the concentration of tax receipts in the days around the April tax filing deadline. Indeed, in the last two years, more than 50% of total non-withheld individual income taxes were concentrated in the four days around the April tax filing deadline. We could see the TGA rise over the next two weeks, which should allow Treasury to maintain smaller T-bill auction sizes until the cash balance declines to more normalized levels, and expect an additional -\$116bn in net T-bill issuance over the coming three weeks. There are downside risks to these forecasts, as we might be underestimating the strength of upcoming tax receipts amid tight labor markets and strong asset performance last year (see US Treasury Market Daily, Jay Barry, 4/11/24).

To that end, we will monitor the money markets closely next week. So far this month, liquidity has remained abundant, as reflected in SOFR and TGCR setting at 5.31 and 5.30, respectively, even as MMF AUMs declined slightly by \$43bn (over 4/2 to 4/11). We continue to think that outflows related to tax payments should not result in a funding crisis for a variety of reasons. First, there is still a positive balance at the ON RRP facility, which can take advantage of any dislocations in the funding markets. Second, the SRF is available for eligible counterparties to source liquidity, which should provide a soft ceiling for overnight rates. Third, the recent stickiness of reserves perhaps suggests that banks might already be preparing for outflows, thus minimizing the shock to the system. Overall, while we could see some funding pressures in the coming weeks, they should be relatively contained (see Short-Term Fixed Income).

Turning to spread markets, mortgages underperformed on the week as rates went sharply higher and the curve flattened following CPI. Investor questions centered on what a highfor-long environment means for mortgage demand. We think bank demand may prove somewhat resilient in the face of a prolonged Fed-on-hold backdrop; banks are likely to see steadier deposit growth after choosing to compete more aggressively with money market rates and as a resilient economy generates greater loan growth. Furthermore, recent bank buying has been in the form of hedged fixed-rate PTs or CMO floaters, so the impact of the recent selloff on AOCI is likely less harmful. While money manager inflows have been robust, trailing returns are likely to turn negative as the late 2023 rally moves out of the historic lookback window, and this could stem the tide somewhat (Figure 4). Finally a Fed hold is not as enticing for foreign demand as a Fed cutting cycle, as currency hedged lifers would prefer a steeper US yield curve. We remain broadly neutral on mortgages at these levels but note that higher coupon conventionals can perform well with the Fed on hold (see Agency MBS).

As a reminder, the CARES ACT included a temporary foreclosure moratorium which prevented a material pickup in foreclosures like what we saw in the post-GFC period. In contrast to mortgages, the gap to higher yields corresponded to HG bond spreads setting new tights, as the spread on our JULI broke through 100bp on Wednesday for the first time since 2007, having tightened 6bp MTD. This is a reminder of how higher yields are increasing demand for high grade credit. Indeed dealers were net sellers of a record \$4.5bn of bonds to investors on Wednesday, per TRACE, while weekly inflows into Agg funds rose by\$4.9bn, the highest weekly inflow in two months. With only \$44bn of issuance MTD, light supply also contributed to this outperformance, though we believe issuance will potentially pickup after bank earnings are reported next week. However with the JULI trading considerably tight to other asset classes such as S&P500, CDX.IG, and Munis, there are clearly growing risks of some pullback. This could come from bond funds, as flows tend to shift only once total returns move beyond +/-2.5% over a three-month period (Figure 6). HG bonds have returned -1.8% MTD and -2.0% YTD, clearly testing ranges where there is increased risk investors pull back and spreads widen. Furthermore we are heading into 1Q24 earnings season, and earning growth expectations have been trimmed to 3.2% YoY following a number of companies revising down their EPS guidance (see Corporates).

Similarly, HY bond yields rose 20bp over the week to new YTD highs at 8.18% in the aftermath of the firm CPI report, as spreads tightened 10bp to 324bp, just 5bps above the November 2018 low. Moreover a rising forward curve is boosting loan yields, which rose 34bp to 9.18%, with spreads roughly unchanged at 476bp (see High Yield). Turning to fundamentals, balance sheets for most US leveraged loan issuers are in a good position but we saw further signs of deterioration in 4Q23. EBITDA expanded on a yoy basis at the slowest rate since 1Q21, and stickier inflation on the cost side than the revenue side is leading to some margin deterioration across select sectors. And interest coverage metrics are now 0.22x below the long-term average. That said, a positive offset to the slower growth and higher rate backdrop is that companies are responding conservatively by slowing debt growth (0% y/y). As such leverage for Loan issuers decreased for an 11th consecutive quarter to a post pandemic low. Meanwhile higher-rated companies saw leverage decline q/q, while B3 and lower-rated companies saw leverage increase (see 4Q23 Leveraged Loan Credit Funda mentals, Nelson Jantzen and Tony Linares, 4/10/23).

Based on the relative cheapness of increasingly <sup>‘</sup>sticky<sup>’</sup> CLO new issue AAA financing spreads, we are lowering our US and Euro AAA Primary Tier-1 target to 140bp for MY24 and to 130bp for YE24 (from 150bp and 140bp, prior). Indeed, the US CLO market has shrugged off a record supply pace (\$52bn YTD) as demand has remained strong despite the draining of excess liquidity in the system, while the reduction in market expectations of Fed easing has increased the attractiveness of floating rate products. Even at tight spreads, demand is not slowing for high-quality fixed income, and we note CLO AAA new issue has lagged the spread tightening in neighboring sectors (Figure 7). If proved correct, for the US our call implies -10bp compression from here to MY24 and a cumulative -20bp to YE24. We revise higher our CLOIE Total Index FY24 return forecast to +7% (from 6% prior), implying +4.5% for the rest of the year (see CLOs).

Turning to relative value, and in light of the revisions lower to our Tier 1 US primary BSL CLO AAA spread targets mentioned previously, we reiterate that CRE CLO AAAs look relatively cheap at current levels of 1m term SOFR + 200bp. Our CRE CLO AAA index is trading historically wide to CLOIE AAA despite very little risk of writedowns, with 45%+ credit enhancements. Meanwhile, servicer extensions remain a risk for CRE CLOs given the decline in refi success rates. However, we see risks as low barring revisions to much tighter conditions, with spreads still attractive under very conservative extension scenarios (see CMBS).
