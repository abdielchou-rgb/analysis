## The solution: biomass for "green" energy

Biomass is any type of organic material; biomass used for energy can include forestry products/residues, agricultural products/residues, organic waste streams (including municipal waste, animal fats, used cooking oil), as well as more novel technologies like algae/seaweed. On the one hand, energy from biomass is renewable, biomass from residues & waste (as opposed to biomass grown for the purpose of bioenergy) have little other use of value, and electricity from biomass is considered a dispatchable energy source. However, like many other ESG considerations, there are a number of challenges that include feedstock considerations, regulatory uncertainty, the "food vs fuel" debate, whether emissions from biomass actually are lower than other energy sources, as well as traceability and the potential for deforestation (among other environmental & biodiversity considerations). We note that we could see perverse incentives around biomass that lead to misuse of resources, including misselling fats/oils as waste and deforestation / environmental considerations, when alternate sources of energy may otherwise be preferable.

## Focus on biofuels

Biomass used for energy production needs to be considered through a broad lens, and should be assessed in the context of region- or country-wide land & energy management. We believe there are likely to be better uses of land, nature, and other resources than creating electricity from biomass given there are many different types of low carbon energy. We do, however, understand the need for biofuels as a replacement for fossil fuels where electrification is developing (i.e. biofuel use in the ongoing transition to EVs) or not currently practical (aviation, maritime transport).

## 1. Biomass investment considerations

We view the most interesting opportunities for biomass in biofuels. Renewable diesel, Sustainable Aviation Fuel (SAF), and other biofuels that are "drop in" fuels do not require changes to infrastructure or engines, and as such are able to easily replace fossil fuels. Some biofuels may require upper limits on how much can be blended into regular fuels, while others require adjustments to infrastructure/engines. Key considerations include feedstock and demand, both of which impact margins and the economic viability of the product. We highlight key players and exposures in section 7. Opportunities.

We view the most interesting opportunities for biomass in biofuels. Feedstock is a key consideration.

Renewable diesel (RD) is an attractive alternative because of its chemical similarity to petroleum diesel, meaning that it is a "drop in" fuel that can be used in existing infrastructure & engines. This differs from biodiesel which typically must be blended with petroleum (or RD), and has blending limits of \~20%. See our renewable diesel note here. The RD market has grown significantly in California as producers are able to capture two types of credits (D4 RINs and Low Carbon Fuel Standards (LCFS) credits, our FAQ here). Based on April data, RD volumes sold increased to 547m gallons, from 272m gallons in Q4 2021, and makes up \~63% of total diesel consumption in California, versus 32% in Q421. This represents a carbon intensity reduction of 15.3% versus 2010, leaving a further 5% reduction required by 2030. This could be revised to target a 30% carbon intensity reduction in 2030, 34.5% in 2031, 52.5% in 2035, 75% in 2040 and 90% in 2045, outlined in a California Air Resources Board (CARB) proposal. LCFS credits contribute to the economic viability of RD, and have seen significant increases in RD demand, see Figure 1.

## 2. A need to replace fossil fuels

Low carbon energy sources to produce electricity do exist, though considerations include grid integration and management, as well as large scale electrification (eg switching from combustion engine vehicles to electric vehicles). We note concern around our reliance on fossil fuels has contributed to a renewed focus on nuclear energy, our deep dive here. While electrification can aid in much of the required energy transition, is not practicable for all energy users, for example aviation and maritime transport.

These factors are complicated by the economics of both fossil fuels and alternatives, which can create further challenges when the alternative requires scale & development. Uptake of alternatives can be supported by regulatory measures, which have been seen with low carbon electricity and biomass based energies, for example EU's SAF regulation (details below). Given the complex value chain, including processes, we believe clear, stable regulation is key in helping create a viable market for fossil fuel alternatives, as was the case for low carbon electricity. We have previously highlighted the uncertainty in EU politics, which could be impactful for legislation, including implementation of existing legislation.

## 3. The solution: Biomass?

Biomass is any type of organic material, and has been used as an energy source (i.e. heating & cooking) since prehistoric times. There are a number of ways in which we use biomass for energy today, sources may be burned to create heat, converted into electricity, or processed into biofuel. Bioenergy typically comes from wood, agricultural products & waste, municipal waste, though newer approaches include using algae. We note other uses of biomass (not in scope of this report) include food, high value chemicals, textiles, bioplastics, medicine, and cosmetics.

Biomass is any type of organic material, and has a range of uses, including energy production.

Traditional bioenergy is generally used non-commercially, typically open stove cooking and heating in developing and emerging countries. Traditional bioenergy currently represents a significant proportion (\~40% in 2020, Figure 11) of bioenergy; this type of biomass use should be reduced due to the air particle pollution and associated human health impacts. We note IEA scenarios forecast zero traditional biomass use from 2030 to align with the goal, though we view this as potentially optimistic.

Modern bioenergy is the use of biomass as energy outside of traditional use as outlined above. This may include forestry (including wood) to produce electricity, agricultural and forestry residues to produce electricity or biofuels, and organic waste streams, which may include animal manures or municipal solid waste (MSW), which is typically converted to biofuels. Short rotation woody biomass is often categorised separately, which are tree species (eg hybrid poplars, willows, and eucalyptus) that have fast growth rates and are used for bioenergy.

Wood has been commonly used as a traditional biomass for many years, but is also used in modern biomass to produce electricity. The wood used could be trunks and large branches of any species, but short rotation woody crops (trees such as hybrid poplar, willows, and eucalyptus) are expected to become prominent.

Agricultural waste/residues are waste products from agricultural activities that are not used for human consumption, for example bagasse and husks from sugarcane and rice production.

Municipal Solid Waste (MSW) is any bio-based municipal waste that can be used to produce bioenergy, such as garden waste, paper & cardboard, food waste, as well as used cooking oil. Separating these waste sources may be a hurdle from a consumer behavioural change perspective, and collection may prove difficult. We note definitions differ, and in some jurisdictions any waste (including plastic & textiles) can be considered as biomass feedstock; we would not consider the latter as biomass.

Algae, including seaweed, is another potential biomass feedstock for biofuels, though remains nascent. Algal based biofuels have high potential given their high photosynthetic efficiency, lack of competition for arable land and as a dominant food source, and flexibility with regards to climate. However, significant development of the industry would be required to achieve viability, including achieving efficiencies in water use (this may include the potential use of waste water), nutrient/fertiliser use, and processing more broadly. Cost competitiveness is a key hurdle to development (harvesting is currently a high cost activity), though higher value products (including food & medicinal) may drive development in the shorter term. We note algae was named as a potential biomass source in EU's SAF regulation.

## We think a holistic view of energy and land management should be taken, which may see some land used for energy crops prioritised for food.

Biomass with carbon capture and storage (CCS) at the point of burning is known as BECCS. There is debate around the feasibility of this technology from an economic point of view, as well as a practical one. While it's debated that, theoretically, BECCS could be considered a carbon negative energy source, pricing considerations tend to make this uneconomic given the requirement of carbon capture and sequestration. UK's Climate Change Committee and the BEIS Committee have both highlighted that "the burning of wood pellets for energy production should only continue if BECCS captures and permanently stores the carbon produced under the North Sea". Another point of view is that BECCS isn't able to deliver the scale of negative emissions that is required based on current emissions forecasts, and would therefore only be able to make a limited contribution to meeting climate change targets. We also note that CCS, nor BECCS, is proven at scale. We previously highlighted potential unintended consequences of BECCS where an IPCC report refers to examples of maladaptation including conversion of land into "forest plantations for the sole purpose of increasing carbon sinks through bioenergy with carbon capture and storage (BECCS)...which may actually result in negative carbon sink...and significant loss of overall biodiversity".

## How is bioenergy created?

There are a number of ways to create bioenergy, with varying levels of technologies to capture and use the energy stored in the biomass; the simplest being direct combustion for heat (eg burning wood to heat your home), while more complicated processes may include fermentation or gasification, the resulting product of which is subsequently combusted to use the energy (eg fermenting forestry residues creates the liquid fuel ethanol, which would later be burned for energy). Figure 10 shows the relationships between biomass source, process, and output. Biofuel & biogas production pathways can be complicated and costly, and can require consistent feedstock compositions which can result in supply side constraints.

Most biomass conversion processes require high heat, which contributes to cost and can impact relevant emissions depending on the source of energy. Specific cost breakdowns across biomass sources are difficult to find, though more general levelised cost of energy/electricity show that biomass is not necessarily a cheap form of energy, see Figure 9Levlisd cotfnrgy, bamp US\$/MWh. Given specific inputs and outputs are predetermined for some bioenergy products, growth can be hampered by limited supply of key inputs, for example animal fats & used cooking oil to create renewable diesel. Costs can also be impacted where the biomass is imported, or where biofuels are exported, or otherwise where transport or sorting (eg waste) is required.

Production of biobased fuels can be complicated, and a number of considerations come into play, notably whether the fuel is "drop in" i.e. are functionally equivalent to fossil fuels and are fully compatible with existing infrastructure.

Drop in fuels are typically made from vegetable oils (including used cooking oil) and/or animal fats through a hydrogenation process. This includes renewable diesel and SAF: the fuels have the same chemical makeup as other hydrocarbon fuels, meaning they release CO2 and other emissions when combusted.

Non drop in fuels require some infrastructure changes, including to engines. This includes first-generation biodiesel hydrogen, as well as bio-based ethanol, methanol & ammonia. These fuels generally burn more cleanly than hydrocarbons (fossil fuels, renewable diesel & SAF) as they are created in an esterification process, resulting in a different chemical composition.

Biogas is often created by capturing methane from landfill or food waste through fermentation or anaerobic digestion processes, and can be used as fuel for transport & heating.

We note some challenges in the renewable diesel generation space due in part to increased capacity additions and low voluntary uptake, impacting margins, as well as some reported technical complications. Shell paused its 820k ton biofuel project in Rotterdam following technical difficulties that resulted in cost overruns, as well as weaker than expected biofuel margins. Our European Energy team had anticipated the facility to start in 2027. Shell guided post tax impairments of US\$1.5-2bn coming from its Singapore C&P assets (US\$0.6 - 8bn), where it previously stopped another potential biofuel project, and its Rotterdam HEFA facility (US\$0.6 - 1bn). BP has also scaled back plans for future investments in SAF and biodiesel projects, though took full ownership of a biofuels joint venture in Brazil. Vertex has converted their renewable diesel plant back into a traditional refinery. Neste has also faced challenges around margins, though incoming regulatory shifts, including EU biofuel targets, EU SAF targets, EU's antidumping duties on Chinese biodiesel, and Californian targets "should help reverse the negative momentum".

## IEA forecasts significant growth in bioenergy...

According to the IEA, use of bioenergy is set to almost double between 2010 and 2050; their 2050 estimate is in line with a total annual energy production of 100EJ, which, according to the IEA, is viewed as sustainable. We note that given traditional biomass use declines to zero in IEA forecasts (from 40% of all bioenergy in 2020) modern bioenergy is expected to increase significantly; from 6.6% of total energy supply in 2020 to 18.7% in 2050 (see Figure 12). ## 4. Regulatory support

There are a range of regulatory support mechanisms for bioenergy globally. Recent activity has focused on SAF and biofuels, including blending mandates, though we note subsidies exist for electricity generation via biomass.

Mechanisms of note in the EU are mandated targets outlined in the Renewable Energy Directive (RED) and the ReFuelEU Aviation (SAF) regulation. RED requires Member States to ensure the transport sector either a) sees the share of renewable energy reaches at least 29% by 2030, or b) reduces greenhouse gas intensity by at least 14.5% by 2030, and is broadly supportive of biofuels.

The UK's biomass strategy shows broad support for biomass, including importing biomass from other countries. The strategy paper acknowledges the role that biomass has played in renewable electricity, and suggests the future is less certain. Language in the strategy suggests continued use of biomass for the production of biomethane, transport fuels, industrial decarbonisation.

Brazil has petrol blending mandates that require 27% of petrol fuel to be ethanol, and in 2024 increased blending mandates for diesel to 14% biodiesel. We note the availability of 100% biofuel pumps at service stations in Brazil.

Biomass, SAF & RD definitions differ across regions; some require inputs to be waste, others do not.

## 5. Advantages of biomass

Electricity from biomass is considered dispatchable energy, meaning that it can provide additional energy to the grid as and when it is needed. Similar to coal, natural gas, and hydro facilities, bioenergy facilities are able to quickly ramp up and down energy production. This is important in light of an increasing share of solar & wind in the energy mix, which produce energy at variable rates; bioenergy complements that variability. This dispatchable energy could, in theory, instead be met through batteries storing energy from wind/solar, however we note battery technologies are not yet operating at commercial scale. It would also be worth considering the overall emissions, as it has been found that some sources of biomass produce more emissions than other dispatchable sources coal & natural gas (further details in section below).

Many forms of biomass do not require dedicated land use e.g. agricultural residues, organic municipal waste, forestry industry residues. IEA forecasts that these sources of biomass will represent 60% of global bioenergy supply in 2050, compared to 20% in 2023. These sources of bioenergy are likely to see consistent production without competing for land or food, though logistics, including collection, is generally more challenging and could increase costs (and emissions) significantly. We note greater use of organic municipal waste would likely require considerable consumer behavioural change.

(Organic) waste to fuel does not require land use, though collection can be challenging.

In addition, short-rotation woody crops can be produced on marginal land, which is agricultural land that would not produce value from food crops. This can be due to poor soil quality, pollution from previous activities (or ongoing activities nearby), poor water quality, or the land being too far from transportation. Further, IEA notes that shortrotation woody crops can produce double the amount of bioenergy per hectare than most conventional bioenergy crops. However, it's possible that arguments are brought forward that the quality of marginal lands could be increased and be restored to crop quality, e.g. via rewilding, planting cover crops, improving mineral balances, among other interventions. This may be supported by biodiversity/ecosystem restoration policies like Kunming/Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (our note here) & EU's Nature Restoration Law. We also note that ecosystem considerations will vary in importance and potential impact, as nature, including water, is extremely localised.

Using agricultural residues, organic municipal waste, forestry residues & woody crops grown on marginal land may assuage some concerns around the food vs fuel debate (see below), however, we would highlight other unintended consequences such as security of supply of feedstock, either in the context of alternative uses of residues (agricultural/forestry) or where significant efficiencies may be made that may reduce organic municipal waste streams, as well as impacts from climate change such as drought and wildfires. For organic waste, increased collaboration around collection of key feedstocks like fats & oils could be part of a push towards a more circular economy and potentially increase bioenergy feedstock, though this would likely require behavioural changes.

Further, biomass is a renewable (if not always sustainable, see below) source of energy; though there are concerns around our changing climate, biomass will continue to be able to photosynthesise, creating plant matter out of key minerals and energy from the sun. Bioenergy is also able to contribute to hard-to-electrify sectors that require high temperatures, e.g. paper and cement production, reducing dependence on fossil fuels, and theoretically reducing their carbon footprint (carbon emissions of bioenergy are debated, see discussion below).

There is debate around whether biomass with carbon capture and storage (CCS) at the point of burning (known as bioenergy with carbon capture and storage, or BECCS) can be considered carbon negative. While theoretically, this may be possible, pricing considerations tend to make this uneconomic given the required addition of carbon capture & sequestration. UK's Climate Change Committee and the BEIS Committee have both highlighted that "the burning of wood pellets for energy production should only continue if BECCS captures and permanently stores the carbon produced under the North Sea". We also note that CCS, nor BECCS, is proven at scale.

## 6. Disadvantages of biomass

A key debate around biomass used for energy is the "food vs fuel" debate, which centres on whether agricultural production (and land use more broadly) should be used to feed a growing population or used to create energy. The debate is complicated by supply/demand dynamics, and where energy may be more valued than food, potentially impacted by the provision of subsidies. The food vs fuel consideration also includes resources used to create the biomass, including water. Today, a large proportion of bioenergy comes from conventional bioenergy crops (Figure 11), forecast to increase through to 2030, which could either be used as fuel or as food. As discussed above, marginal land is typically not used for food and could therefore be used for biofuels without consideration for the food vs fuel debate, though as noted above there may be other unintended consequences. Other sources of biomass for energy like organic municipal waste, and residues, would not be considered in this particular debate.

"Food vs fuel" is a key debate in the biomass space. We note definitions differ across regions.

It is debated whether emissions from using bioenergy are lower or higher than other sources, including fossil fuels. The IPCC AR6 WG3 found that, broadly, it depends: "the use of bioenergy can lead to either increased or reduced emissions, depending on the scale of deployment, conversion technology, fuel displaced, and how/where the biomass is produced (high confidence)". The BBC has noted that "burning wood produces more greenhouse gases than burning coal", while a report from Institute of Economic Affairs noted that "burning imported wood chips for electricity often creates more CO2 than burning fossil fuels". Their report also highlighted what is described by some as an "accounting trick" in the international carbon accounting frameworks which allow the emissions from burnt wood to be allocated to the carbon budget of the country in which the trees were grown, rather than the country where the biomass was burnt, arguably resulting in underreporting of emissions in countries using the fuel. Another factor with regards to emissions from woody biomass includes the "carbon payback period": the time it takes for new trees to sequester the carbon that was burned in energy generation, estimated to be between 44 and 104 years, which is viewed as unsustainable. We note other sources of biomass, such as residues, municipal waste, as well as newer sources like algae may be more carbon efficient, depending on a number of factors related to sorting & transport.

Energy from biomass is not necessarily a lower emissions fuel.

There are limited cost analyses across biomass sources, though we note more general levelised cost of energy/electricity show that biomass is not necessarily a cheap form of energy, see Figure 9Levlisd cotfnrgy, bamp US\$/MWh. Other forms of electricity generation are cheaper and may produce fewer emissions (see above), and as such we believe electricity generation should be focused on other sources of energy. In addition, whether used for electricity or fuels, poor harvests of crops intended for bioenergy either through pests, infections, wildfires, or drought (many of which expected to intensify due to the climate crisis) could result in fuel price spikes.

The ecosystem & biodiversity impact of biomass used for energy can be significant, though arguably not more so than large scale agriculture. Monocultures do not encourage biodiversity and typically have low resilience to pests and infections, potentially leading to large scale loss of biomass. This can complicate the food vs fuel debate above, as monocultures are increasingly viewed as harmful to nature & biodiversity. Further, biofuels are associated with land use change and deforestation.

## International Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), AR 6 WG3 noted:

"afforestation or production of biomass crops for BECCS or biochar, when poorly implemented, can have adverse socio-economic and environmental impacts, including on biodiversity, food and water security, local livelihoods and on the rights of Indigenous Peoples, especially if implemented at large scales and where land tenure is insecure (high confidence)"

In our view, traceability is a complicated but key issue for biomass, centring on two main points; 1) the potential for land use change / deforestation, and 2) misselling of "used" product. Both of these are likely driven by the economics of bioenergy, and could be exacerbated by regulatory intervention without adequate traceability measures. A key player in UK electricity production from biomass was the subject of a BBC investigation that alleged the company had been using wood from rare old-growth forests from British Columbia in Canada. Wood pellets are typically made of wood shavings (for this company, 80% of material from Canadian pellets come from sawmill residues); we would view accurately tracing sawmill residues to the point of production/ growth as extremely difficult. Another consideration is the misselling of "used" product, increasingly prevalent with regards to SAF as EU rules state that SAF can be made from agricultural or forestry residues, algae, bio-waste, used cooking oil or certain animal fats. Given the ambitious targets for SAF use (see regulatory section above), economic incentives may see products like unused cooking oil be misrepresented as "used" (see comment on p3 here), and in lieu of residues, fuels may be made of whole crops. We believe implementing traceability would be a considerable challenge in these scenarios.

There is also a question of efficiency with regards to conversion losses. In IEA scenarios, conversion losses from biomass represent 19% of all bioenergy supply in 2030, dropping to 12% in 2050 (14EJ of conversion losses from 73EJ of energy in 2030, and 12EJ of conversion losses from 99EJ of energy in 2050). These are mainly from converting modern solid bioenergy to biofuels. Given conversion losses are not insignificant, this supports the need for integrated land and energy planning to maximise efficiency.

Biofuels also have a lower volumetric energy density than other liquid fuels, meaning you need higher volumes of fuel to achieve the same energy production. This is important for aviation where weight (linked to volume) of fuel is a key factor in ongoing fuel use (it takes fuel to carry fuel on board); potential inefficiencies here would be a consideration for when proportions of SAF become significant.

## 7. Opportunities

While biomass use for energy is not uncommon, it is usually part of a diversified energy production portfolio, therefore there are few pureplays. Bio-based fuels continue to develop, and while recent capacity additions and limited voluntary uptake ahead of regulatory catalysts have created a less attractive market proposition, momentum is expected to reverse from next year, with demand outstripping supply. Regulatory support for bio-based fuels are significant over the long term; scalability remains a challenge, in part due to feedstock considerations.
