# Assessing the cost of supporting ongoing industrial upgrades

Despite expanding global manufacturing market share, concerns are rising about risks from negative PPI and high industrial investment growth in China amid rising trade tensions. We address the key question of the cost and sustainability of supporting the current path of industrial upgrades.

## Key Takeaways

The pace of further industrial upgrades is one of the potential drivers of productivity gains in China...

...and it will be one of the determinants of the outcome of the 3D debate in China, in our view.

Average annual credit costs of \~Rmb2.2tn (2025-27) related to industrial are still manageable if banks start to rationalize industrial credits in 2025...

...particularly because policymakers have notably reduced risks related to other major credit categories, such as property and LGFV.

Prolonged overinvestment – with negative PPI running beyond 2027 and further bank loan yield and NIM decline – will be the biggest risk to this model.

China has unique competitive advantages in its decentralized industrial supply chain that covers all industrial sectors. This allows it to achieve quick product innovation and upgrades, more efficiency gains, and lower cost. We believe China's competitive advantages in industrial sectors have been further strengthened in recent years, aided by more shift in policy and resources to this area. However, prolonged negative PPI and declining profit margins in certain sectors have also raised market concerns and served as a reminder that rapid capacity expansion and upgrades of industrial sectors will not come without costs.

What's the total cost of supporting the current path of industrial upgrades? Is that sustainable? Will this lead to a downward spiral that will be much more costly for China to get out of? We expect effective supply-side reform in 2025 with rationalization of industrial credit growth; our bottom-up approach shows that the total portion of manufacturing credit at risk could be \~10% by then. In this case, banks would need to digest 3.3% of total manufacturing-related credit, or Rmb2.2tn in NPLs p.a. over 2025-2027. This would be Rmb980bn in excess of banks' normal annual risk digestion pace in manufacturing credits, which we view as a manageable level of costs for the level of industrial upgrades achieved. But if overinvestment continues through 2027, persistently low interest rates are another risk in focus.

Introduction

Many unique competitive advantages have the potential to drive further industrial sector upgrades and expansion for China...

...helping China gain further global market share despite trade tensions.

However, this will come at a cost and entail risks, which we seek to identify and quantify.

## What are China's unique competitive advantages?

Driving ongoing industrial upgrades is not easy and requires comprehensive efforts from many aspects. We believe several unique competitive advantages have been supporting industrial upgrades in China – and could continue to do so. These include:

1. A decentralized industrial supply chain covering all industrial sectors,

2. A sophisticated infrastructure system that's being constantly upgraded and which includes strong and stable power supply,

3. A policy framework that favors industrial sectors and industrial supply chain development with strong ability to coordinate resources,

4. A financial system that has notably improved its services to the industrial supply chain via fintech, and

5. Progress already made in AI development in industrial verticals.

The pace of further industrial upgrades – one of the potential drivers of productivity gains in China – will be one of the determinants of the outcome of the 3D debate in China, in our view.

## What's the cost of supporting the current path of industrial upgrades – and is it sustainable?

However, prolonged negative PPI and declining profit margin in certain sectors have also raised market concerns and served as a reminder that rapid capacity expansion and upgrades of industrial sectors will not come without costs. What's the total cost of supporting the current path of industrial upgrades? Is that sustainable? Will this lead to a downward spiral that will be much more costly for China to get out of?

Since the pressure on both PPI and shrinking profit margins in many industrial sectors arise largely from the supply side of the issues, we believe the pressure can be reduced via periodic supply-side reform without triggering a downward spiral of macro risks. However, losses for credits supporting lower-quality capacity built up during rapid industrial investments will be the key cost for the financial system to support the industrial upgrades.

Our analysis shows some signs of rising industrial sector credit risks after four years of quick credit expansion, but high-risk credits are still at levels much more modest than in 2016, before the last round of supply-side reform.

We expect a more rational pace of industrial credit growth combined with some risk digestion starting in 2025, particularly after the drag from property and LGFV de-risking efforts moderates.

We project that the effort to maintain ongoing industrial upgrades will lead to 10-15bps increases in bank NPL formation between 2025 and 2027.

The average annual credit costs of \~Rmb2.2tn between 2025 and 2027 related to industrial investments and upgrades are still manageable, particularly because policymakers have notably reduced risks related to other major credit categories, such as property and LGFV.

## What are the key risks to this development path?

1. Overly low loan yields could erode the financial system's ability to digest risks from industrial investment cycles.

2. If growth in industrial investment continues to outpace production until 2027 and 2028, high-risk credits would rise 3-4ppt per year at the current pace.

3. A major shock in global demand could occur, or demand could shift much more quickly to other parts of the world than it has over the past five years.

4. Policy mistakes in future industrial cycles could arise, given a more cyclical path of growth.

## Key fundamental themes – the backdrop

We believe it has become even more clear that China is likely to remain focused on industrial development in view of its still relatively low GDP per capita and higher potential for productivity gains in the industrial sector vs. the service sector.

As we have highlighted in our report of 19 May 2022 – China Financials: Regulatory Reset + Industrial Policy Support – Implications for Financials – many policies in recent years have been designed to shift credit and resources back to the industrial sectors.

In addition, we believe the focus on regulation and financial risk control has also been designed to create a stable financial environment for industrial upgrades in China, which tend to take a long time and could be very cyclical.

Despite the higher market focus on property and LGFV loan risks, we believe property credit risks are manageable and will just take some time for the financial system to digest. LGFV risks also appear manageable as long as the future pace of credit growth can be contained.

We believe one of the debates on the 3D Journey in China ultimately hinges on whether the country can push for further industrial upgrades, a potential driver of productivity gains.

Our analysis shows that China has built a comprehensive system to support further industrial upgrades, rooted in the competitive advantages that it possesses in many areas. The policy shift to support industrial upgrades is also paying off.

However, given the highly cyclical nature of many of the industrial sectors and rapid product and tech cycles, we believe whether and how China can manage costs and risks related to credits to the industrial sectors will play a critical role in the process. This will determine whether China can maintain credit and capital support for ongoing R&D, investments, and upgrades for its over 3,000 industrial sectors.

Our analysis shows that there are some signs of rising industrial sector credit risks after four years of quick credit expansion and high manufacturing investment growth, but they are still at levels much more modest than in 2016, before the last round of supply side reform.

We expect a more rational pace of industrial credit growth combined with some risk digestion starting in 2025, particularly after the drag from property and LGFV de-risking efforts moderates.

We project that the effort to maintain ongoing industrial upgrades will lead to annual credit costs of around Rmb2.2tn from the broader manufacturing-related areas between 2025 and 2027. We view this as manageable and sustainable.

However, prolonged overinvestment – with negative PPI running beyond 2027 and further bank loan yield and NIM decline – will be the biggest risk to this model.

## We still see ample room for long-term industrial sector expansion despite the near-term pressures

Overall competitive advantages for China's manufacturing sectors appear to be strengthening despite trade tension and multipolar world dynamics. This is evidenced in still expanding global manufacturing market share and ongoing industrial upgrades. Although the market share gain was partly supported by what appears to be less economical investments in some areas, we believe several unique competitive advantages of China's industrial supply chain also played an important role in the upgrades and market share expansion. They include:

1. A decentralized industrial supply chain covering all industrial sectors,

2. A sophisticated infrastructure system that's being constantly upgraded, including strong and stable power supply,

3. A policy framework that favors industrial sectors and industrial supply chain development with strong ability to coordinate resources,

4. A financial system that has notably improved its services to the industrial supply chain via fintech, and

5. Progress already made in AI development in industrial verticals.

These unique factors have helped China continue to achieve notable upgrades in industrial sectors with expanding global market share in the past five years despite global trade and geopolitical friction.

In addition, benefiting from a shift in policy focus to industrial sectors, we have seen an expanding talent pool for the industrial sector after years of resource shift, especially among people with higher education. This is the most critical factor in industrial upgrades, in our view.

## What's the cost of supporting the industrial-led growth model?

Products and the tech landscape in industrial sectors are ever-changing. Also, there are notable cycles in global product demand. Furthermore, the investment model is still capital-heavy and relies on credit support.

In light of these factors, we believe one of the biggest costs of supporting the industrialled growth model will be the credit losses related to the industrial cycles. In addition, the coordinated policy support for the industrial sectors supports quick industrial upgrades and creates global leaders – but it could also equally lead to more lower-quality investments by weaker firms, which in turn could lead to higher credit costs when industrial cycles turn.

The cost also determines whether the financial system can continue to support the ongoing industrial sector expansion, which will be key to watch for the sustainability of the growth model.

Hence, this will be one of the determinants of the 3D issue faced by China, in our view. The balance between cost and rewards of the industrial investment will help determine whether China can push up productivity further, which will help resolve the demographic and debt issues, or be weighed down by the debt issue.

For example, the banking system digested over 25% of industrial loans in aggregate, or a total of nearly Rmb7.2tn of loans, as NPLs between 2016 to 2020 related to risks built during the 10+ years of investment boom before supply-side reform started in 2016. Looking back now, the cost was smoothly digested by the financial system given still higher NIM and profit growth before 2021. In addition, those investments had helped built a sophisticated industrial supply chain that notably strengthened China's competitive edge – a good trade-off, in our view.

## Where are we in the cycle now and what's the rough cost of supporting the current pace of industrial investments?

Investment and credit growth in the industrial sectors has already been high in the past three years, and capacity expansion has started to outpace industrial production demand in the past 12 months. This has been weighing on the profitability of some industrial firms.

In light of this, we believe it is time to assess the related credit risks at this point, particularly because policymakers and local governments are pushing for further pickup in industrial credit growth and industrial investments, which could add further pressures on industrial firm profitability and EBIT interest cover.

Via the assessment of eight key metrics – including EBIT/interest cover, gross margin, pace of debt growth, debt to asset ratio, etc. – for China's 400,000 industrial firms divided into 31 subsectors, we estimate that around 6.8% of overall manufacturing credit could be at risk as of end-2023. This compares to around 18% of industrial credit at risk at the end of

2015, before the last round of supply-side reform. This implies additional credit cost of Rmb209bn for the banking system per year if the risks are digested over the next three years, which is quite manageable.

## When do we expect supply side reform 2.0? The pace of industrial investment should start to rationalize in 2025

However, rather than slowdown in industrial investment and credit growth in 2024, we have actually seen a pickup. Most local governments are pushing industrial investments to cushion the impact from LGFV debt control and continued slowdown in the property market. As a result, we believe high-risk credits will continue to build in 2024, although the better-than-expected export growth could help slow the pace of the buildup.

We believe industrial investment and related credit growth will start to rationalize starting in 2025 for these reasons:

1. Drag on investment growth from property slowdown and LGFV risk control may ease in 2025, which could reduce local governments' incentive to push industrial investments.

2. Already lower rates on industrial credits, vs. the last cycle before 2016, will trigger a quicker slowdown in industrial credits when risks start to surface.

3. More private sector-driven investment in this cycle vs. more SOE-driven industrial investments before 2016 should trigger industrial credit slowdown after two years of negative PPI vs. the four years before 2016.

Our base case is more rational industrial investment growth starting in 2025, with NPL digestion starting in 2H25. In this scenario, we estimate that the total portion of credit at risk could be \~10% within manufacturing credits by 2025. Consider a three-year risk digestion period afterwards, which would also lead to a sharp decline in manufacturing credit support, the annual risk digestion would be equivalent to 3.3% of total

manufacturing-related credit. In absolute numbers, it will be a cost of around Rmb2.2tn in NPLs per year for three years, creating Rmb980bn additional credit cost per year above banks' normal pace of risk digestion.

Over the medium term, we expect slower credit growth and likely more rational loan pricing after the next wave of industrial NPL digestion to support some rebound in bank NIMs – particularly after the rebound in profitability of the industrial sector following what will in effect be supply-side reform 2.0. This should help the financial system to rebuild some reserves and get ready for the next investment and risk digestion cycle.

## What are the key risks to the industrial-led growth model and how will the multipolar world affect the sustainability of the model?

Maintaining and expanding this large and complicated industrial base is not an easy task. Despite the concerns about impact from the multipolar world and shifts in demand and production, we actually see the biggest risks to this industrial-led growth model coming from internal policies in China.

Specifically, we see the following risks to this model, particularly given the highly capital-/ investment-intensive and cyclical nature of the industrial sectors:

## 1) Overly low loan yields that erode the financial system's ability to digest risks from industrial investment cycles

Our analysis shows that the banking system can still digest the industrial NPLs over the upcoming NPL cycle, with current NIM and profitability, benefiting from NPL coverage built up during periods before the past rounds of rate cuts. Even so, we believe the current average loan yields are already too low, and, hence, generate low or no returns over the cycles.

We believe further rate cuts for industrial loans would lead to little or no room for banks to digest risks from future industrial cycles – eventually leading to reduced risk appetite and ability to support credit and capital allocation to the industrial sectors.

2) Overinvestment in industrial sectors and tolerating negative PPI for too long, leading to impairment of the financial system's balance sheet.

We estimate that the total portion of credit at risk could reach \~18% within manufacturing credits by 2027 if China's policy continues to push industrial investments at a higher pace than demand – particularly if this is combined with some demand shock from overseas markets arising from unexpected global slowdown or escalation of geopolitical issues. Given the large amount of NPL risk accumulated through a longer period between 2023 and 2027, we think it could take 4-5 years for the banks to digest these risks.

This will be similar to the risks accumulated right before the last round of digestion in 2016. However, manufacturing-related credit would grow to a much bigger size, at around Rmb77tn by 2027, compared to <Rmb30tn in 2016. At the same time, banks' profit most likely would not be able to grow at a similar pace. In fact, it's already slowed notably in 2022 and 2023: commercial banks' profit at Rmb2.3tn is only 28% higher than the average annual profit of Rmb1.9tn between 2016 and 2020.

In this scenario, we believe total credit growth would fall sharply when the banks start to digest accumulated manufacturing NPLs after 2027, because manufacturing credit would have become a main contributor to TSF by then, replacing the other old drivers such as infrastructure and property.

In addition, we believe systemic risks would also rise as weaker banks with thin margins and modest provision buffers could be dragged down by the mounting NPLs. This could also inflict some permanent damage on the financial system in China – with a notable reduction in the system's capacity to support further industrial investments and upgrades

We'd also expect potential government capital injection to major banks to help maintain reasonable credit supply. However, the financial's system risk appetite and ability to allocate credits efficiently would likely take time to restore.

## 3) Worse-than-expected impact from the multipolar world, with more demand shifting to other parts of the globe

Concerns exist about the impact from higher tariffs, ongoing trade friction, and demand shift away from China in a multipolar world – see our global report, Global Industrials: Manufacturing in the Multipolar World. Yet the actual impact so far has been quite manageable, thanks in part to China's policy shift to focus on the industrial supply chain and upgrades. However, if the scenario laid out in our global report on the multipolar world does materialize, it could lead to higher losses for China's financial system and further pressure both credit demand and supply in China – particularly if the demand shift is even worse than the scenarios laid out in our global report.

Positively, the evidence in the past five years shows that continued investments and innovation in China can help mitigate such risks, and economic forces eventually drive global demand towards products with better quality and price, despite some friction costs and temporary shocks.

## 4) Policy mistakes in future industrial cycles, given a more cyclical path of growth

Policy twists and turns during China's economic transition and property cycles have temporarily reduced policy transparency and hurt market sentiment. The future economic development path could be even more cyclical, so how policymakers coordinate policies to manage the cycles will also be key to ensure more long-term sustainable development.

Specifically, we see risks of overly aggressive policy interference in the industrial cycles, which would tend to lead to belated and harsh policy turns – and be particularly costly from the perspective of credit risk and market confidence.

## When can investors look beyond risk concerns for financials?

We believe risk concerns about banks should continue to moderate into 2025 and 2026...

...particularly if banks start to digest industrial NPLs...

...but cycles will drive stock performance afterwards.

We continue to believe that banks have a competitive advantage in asset sourcing and risk management for credit and capital demand from the manufacturing, infrastructure, SME, and retail segments. This has already allowed banks to gain market share in credit allocation. In addition, we believe the progress on risk containment and the focus shift to manufacturing should drive more sustainable long-term credit demand in China.

However, we continue to expect moderating loan and asset growth as regulators continue to reduce window guidance on loan growth and urge banks to reduce self-circulating loans. In addition, we expect more rational growth in industrial loans starting in 2025 to help reduce overcapacity risks.

Specifically, we expect overall bank loan growth to slow from 9% in 2023 to 6.8% in 2025 and 7.1% in 2026 before some rebound is likely in 2027.

Positively, we expect some NIM rebound starting in 2H25, following some modest pressures in 1H25, to help digest industrial NPLs. Specifically, we believe slower overall credit growth will support loan yields and also reduce competition for deposits, which should also help banks reduce funding costs.

We expect gross NPL formation to pick up to 1.57% in 2025 and 1.59% in 2026; we expect banks to reduce NPL coverage ratio to cushion the earnings impact. We estimate that NPL coverage needs to decline 10-11 percentage points on average in 2025 and 2026 to offset higher formation.

Accordingly, among the banks we could see more volatile earnings with more divergent ROE trends based on their asset sourcing and risk management capabilities. We believe CCB, PSBC, CMB, Industrial, and Ningbo will have more resilient earnings.

Despite the likely modest earnings pressure, we believe more rational credit growth, reduced concerns about risks once the banks start to digest industrial NPLs, and some NIM rebound should support bank valuation over time, particularly if China can continue to show resilient industrial upgrades and further reduce reliance on the property market.

## We see gradual reduction of concerns about low asset return on insurance over time

We've seen much concern about the low interest rate environment, especially since 2H23. This pressures China's insurers a lot considering their mismatch between assets and liabilities and relative rigid liability costs, thus weighing a lot on their valuation.

Insurers are also preparing themselves to deal with this situation from both the liability side (reducing liability cost through optimizing product mix, lowering pricing interest rates, and reducing commission rates) and the asset side (narrowing asset-liability duration gap by investing more in long-term assets). We are pleased to see the progress they have made.

We believe China's life insurers will have less sensitivity to interest rates over time and will benefit from a rebound in rates. In addition, we believe the ongoing industrial upgrades should support resilient income growth in China, which should support steady growth in insurance sales, particularly when households reduce property investments as a means of long-term savings.

## We still see ample room for leading investment banks such as CITICS, CICC, and HTSC to consolidate market share over time

Even though overall IPO volume will be depressed in the near future, we believe China's capital markets will continue to support the most innovative manufacturing firms, while more stringent requirements on quality of listed companies will drive further divergence in the value added by underwriters. Leading institutional franchise will have an edge in deal sourcing, financial advisory, and execution, as well as investments in the primary market to secure client relationships at an early stage.

Separately, we also see rising M&A opportunities as industrial firms will show higher consolidation needs after rapid capacity expansion, especially when much of the capacity growth has been driven by private companies, leading to more market-oriented decisions. This could benefit the top dealmakers.

## We still see ample room for long-term industrial sector expansion despite near-term pressures and the multipolar world dynamic

The competitive advantage for China's manufacturing sector appears to be strengthening, despite trade tensions and a multipolar world dynamic, evidenced by still-expanding global manufacturing market share and ongoing industrial upgrades. While the market share gain was partly supported by what appears to be less economical investments in some areas, we believe several unique competitive advantages of China's industrial supply chain also played an important role, including:

1. Decentralized industrial supply chain, covering all industrial sectors,

2. A sophisticated infrastructure system that's being constantly upgraded, which includes strong and stable power supply,

3. A policy framework that favors industrial sectors and industrial supply chain development,

4. A financial system that has improved its services to the industrial supply chain via fintech, and

5. Progress already made in AI development in industrial verticals.

If China can manage the industrial cycles and related credit costs well, we expect to see continued strong long term credit demand from industrial sector upgrades and related investments.

## Case study: Cigarette lighter production in Shaodong, Hunan

We think this is a good illustration of how China can push the efficiency of the supply chain to the extreme, even for merchandise as small as a lighter.

China accounts for nearly 70% of the global production of cigarette lighters. Shaodong, a county with a population of 1mn in Hunan province, produced roughly 15bn lighters and exported 3.5bn in 2022, equivalent to \~50% of China's lighter exports. The retail price of a typical lighter has been unchanged at \~Rmb1 for 20 years, despite rising labor and materials costs. We believe a range of factors contribute to China's strong global market share in this product:

Industry cluster forming a complex supply chain collaboration: More than 20 parts go into making a simple plastic lighter, while more than 200 parts and 14 processing procedures are needed in total to make various types of lighters. As of 2023, there are 114 companies along the lighter supply chain in Shaodong, including 30 producers of parts, 84 auxiliary factories, and 12 exporters. Consequently, most of the production could be done in Shaodong, with auxiliary factories located within 20 kilometers.

## Industry association created to coordinate resources, competition and

encourage innovation: In 2002, the Lighter Industry Association was founded in China to aggregate and better utilize resources as well as guide rational pricing. It also coordinates product innovation to help producers meet customized requirements for customers across the globe.

## Automation, partly supported by the government, helped save costs:

Automation has helped a local leading producer to reduce its labor cost per lighter from Rmb0.1 to Rmb0.015 in four yeas. In 2017, the Shaodong Municipal Government invested Rmb200mn to found the Research Institute for Smart Manufacturing, which specializes in the industry upgrade of small and mid-sized companies, including lighter companies. This has enabled small enterprises to save costs to gain higher margin.

## Case study: DJI

We think the success of DJI reveals how some of China's manufacturers are climbing up the value chain, leading technology innovation and grabbing global market share.

Headquartered in Shenzhen's Nanshan District, DJI is the world's top producer of camera drones. Despite various US sanctions and restrictions since 2016, the firm has maintained >70% global market share and expanded revenue at a 26% CAGR between 2016 and 2022. We attribute its success to:

Leading core technology development: DJI invests heavily in R&D and has built a competitive edge in flight controllers, stabilizers, and transmission, which are crucial to camera drone applications. Notably, DJI accounts for 31% of global patent applications relating to UAVs (unmanned aerial vehicles).

Efficient supply chain has established a cost advantage: As a former director at MIIT once put it, "you could make a drone without going out of Nanshan District." Since DJI's debut of the first camera drone in 2012, a large number of drone manufacturers have been set up in Nanshan, Shenzhen, which subsequently attracted a comprehensive supply chain to the area. According to the Shenzhen Government, by 2022 Shenzhen had become home to 1,500+ drone companies including DJI, accounting for 70% of the national annual industrial output in drones.

Drone production generally requires 2,000-3,000 components, most of which can be procured within Nanshan or nearby areas, while key materials such as carbon fiber, lithium batteries, or magnetic materials can also be sourced from the Pearl River Delta area thanks to an established electronic industry there. These advantages have helped to improve DJI's cost competitiveness globally and speed up its time to market.

Dedicated local government support: The Shenzhen Government has laid out Five Year Plans for the drone industry since 2013, and encourages businesses through subsidies as well as by building industrial parks. Additionally, the Shenzhen Government has been encouraging top universities nationwide to supply talent to the industry.

Strong brand equity strengthens DJI's market share despite trade tensions: DJI dominates the global camera drone market with top-notch quality and cost efficiency, leading to strong brand equity over the years. This allows the firm to maintain market share despite various US sanctions and restrictions, and to pass on increased tariffs to end customers.

It has become increasingly clear to us that China's financial cleanup, regulatory reset, and the new financial sector reforms have one goal in common: to reallocate financial and social resources to China's supply chain and industrial sector, in particular to high-end manufacturing. A series of initiatives that influence financial, fiscal and local government policies has been rolled out to support this end. Notably, the direction of tax cuts, changes in education policies, policies to break down data monopolies, and dedicated efforts to contain housing and land prices are all partly aimed – we think – at ensuring a better environment for high-end manufacturing and supply-chain development.

Fiscal and tax policies have favored industrial firms in recent years:

## Chengdu Hi-Tech Industrial Development Zone ("CDHT")

The Chengdu Hi-tech Industrial Development Zone was established in 1988, and was approved as one of the first national high-tech zones in 1991. CDHT now covers an area of 237.3 square kilometers, and its GDP reached Rmb301.58bn in 2022. There are three leading industries in CDHT, namely electronic information, biomedicine, and digital economy.

We believe the rapid growth of CDHT is highly correlated with regional support, including subsidies and favorable policies. The CDHT Management Committee has released consultation papers and policies on a weekly basis, covering AI application, tech innovation and talent acquisition.

In terms of industrial upgrades, it announced detailed action plans for industrial technology upgrades on January 30, 2024, listing subsidies for companies meeting certain standards, clear application procedures, and 16 directions of support.

For AI application, it released a consultation paper on 2024-2026 action plans for the development of a high-quality AI industry on August 29, 2023, aiming for Rmb50bn of AI business by 2026 with over 100 companies and 50 leading AI companies. It will be achieved via enhancement of the computation power supply, data sharing and tech innovation capabilities.

On technology innovation, the committee has devised a series of policies to attract leading talent in key areas such as settlement arrangement, subsidy and reward, and to support entrepreneurship in professionals.

By the end of 2023, the company's total assets exceeded Rmb116bn, with 199 investment projects and total investment exceeding Rmb89.5bn.

In 2015, the company and BOE jointly launched the Singularity Power fund with Rmb4bn+, investing in the integrated circuit industry. ## 2) Fintech development shifting to supply chain financing has also notably improved financial market support to the industrial supply chain

Besides traditional financial support to the industrial sector by policymakers (such as a more industrial-friendly capital market, guidance to direct more credit and improving payment processing efficiency), the adoption of and investment in fintech has helped strengthen China’s supply chain. Banks have been investing heavily in IT to better identify, capture and serve industrial financing needs along the supply chain, including big data analytics, blockchain, and IoT backed by satellites (by Ping An Bank). Local government

also cooperates with financial institutions (FIs) by providing access to a large amount of public data, which allows FIs to analyze customers more accurately using big data, especially for SMEs.

## Case study: Ping An Bank's Nebula IoT Program

Since 2019, Ping An Bank embarked on its Nebula IoT Program to upgrade its supply chain finance, and the bank has launched three satellites from 2020 to 2022. These satellites enable Ping An Bank to monitor in real time borrowers' operating activities and collateral: PingAn-1 and PingAn-2 satellites are IoT Satellites, which could improve the IoT data communications on the ground. PingAn-3 is a Remote Sensing Satellite, which could obtain high-resolution image data and provide more timely operation data along the supply chain. This could help Ping An Bank reach out to more lower-tier clients and lower the financing cost for industrial companies.

As of end-2022, more than 30 innovation projects have been implemented, serving over 21,000 customers and connecting more than 20mn IoT end devices. There are six major industrial fields, including smart vehicle connection, smart manufacturing, smart energy, smart agriculture, smart infrastructure and smart logistics. As of 2022, the Nebula IoT Program has enabled more than Rmb650bn in financing.

## Case study: Shanghai big data inclusive financing app

In December 2019, the Shanghai big data inclusive financing app was launched, adding extra functions on top of cloud for government affairs and an open platform for public data. It collects 400+ types of public data from eight departments, including business registration, tax payments, admin penalties, and judicial lawsuits. It is open to banks for creating profiles for SMEs based on big data analysis, which aims to increase the accuracy and scope of loan extensions to SMEs.

The eight departments are Science and Technology Commission of Shanghai Municipality, Shanghai Municipal Human Resources and Social Security Bureau, Shanghai Municipal Planning and Natural Resources Bureau, Shanghai Municipal Bureau of Ecology and Environment, Shanghai Municipal Commission of Housing and Urban-Rural Development, Shanghai Municipal Administration for Market Regulation, Shanghai Municipal Tax Service State Taxation Administration, Shanghai Municipal High Court.

The productivity of inclusive financing business is improving, driven by big data analysis. Notably, inclusive financing loan balance grew 205% from the beginning of 2020 to the end of 2022, and the number of clients increased 298% at ICBC Shanghai branch, while the number of staff for inclusive financing business remained the same.

Another area where fintech development has progressed is the setup of local data centers, which could help SMEs with their financing needs and digital transformation. ## 4) Steady growth in power supply and keeping power price low for industrial firms

Stable and sufficient power supply is a basic requirement for industrial firms to conduct their daily operations.

We think China has three competitive advantages in terms of electricity supply to support the upgrades for various industrial sectors.

1. China has built up its electricity capacity and supply over the last 10+ years: For example, the capacity of power generating equipment has grown notably since 2020, especially in 2023 with newly increased capacity of power generating equipment reaching 370GW, up 82% yoy. Electricity production in China has continued to grow, exceeding the US since 2010 and reaching 8.9tn KWh in 2023 (vs a stable electricity supply in the US with electricity production of 4.2tn KWh in 2023).

2. The construction of ultra-high voltage (UHV) lines in China could ensure timely and efficient power transmission: The success of UHV projects is highly correlated with government support and investment in top talent to undertake tech research to find breakthroughs. For example, the West-to-East Electricity Transmission Project in China, led by the State Grid Corporation of China, involved thousands of experts from 300+ institutions, which not only has made a major breakthrough in terms of China's UHV capability but also has made notable progress on tech upgrades of core equipment (including transformers, circuit breakers and other parts). Our Utilities team expects accelerating construction of UHV projects in 2024.

3. Finally, low tariffs charged on electricity also lower cost for industrial companies: The coal-benchmark tariff for industrial sectors in China varies from Rmb0.3/KWh to Rmb0.45/KWh, which has remained stable since 2017. This is lower than the tariff charged to industrial sectors in the US at \~8cents/kWh, or over Rmb0.5/KWh.

## 5) Expanding infrastructure investment in 5G, IoT, data centers and other emerging areas

To encourage demand for high-end industrial products, policies have been directed at developing New Infrastructure in China. 1. IT infrastructure, (i.e., 5G, IoT, Industrial Internet, Satellite Internet, AI, cloud computing, blockchain, data center, and smart computing center);

2. Integration infrastructure, deepening the application of IoT, big data and AI technology to help upgrade traditional infra; and

3. Innovation infrastructure, supporting research and product innovation.

The development of such infrastructure could leverage and integrate existing resources for higher efficiency, deepening analysis and broader usage of data for industrial upgrade. It could also build a fully-connected system, covering industrial sectors, government, finance and other companies.

In order to meet the challenges of traditional supply chains, the industrial Internet integrates various technologies to construct a digital, decentralized supply chain. The industrial Internet can realize the full connection of all factors in a supply chain through the comprehensive interconnection between equipment, systems, factories, and logistics to build a data-driven industrial manufacturing and service system and reshape the traditional supply chain. The new industrial Internet platform-based supply chain, which uses a new generation of information technologies, such as big data and artificial intelligence, is characterized by comprehensive connection, efficient coordination, and intelligent decision-making. The Industrial Internet promotes the digital transformation of supply chains, transforming traditional supply chains into highly intelligent digital supply chains by making them more lean, flexible, and intelligent.

# What's the cost of supporting the industrial-led growth model, and where do we see risks?

In light of the ever-changing products and tech landscape in the industrial sector, notable cycles in global product demand and a still capital-heavy investment model, which relies on credit support, we believe one of the biggest costs of supporting the industrial led growth model will be the credit losses related to the industrial cycles. Whether the financial system can continue to support the ongoing expansion in the industrial sector and in upgrades will be key to watch for the sustainability of the growth model.

Notably, the banking system digested more than 25% of industrial loans or a total of nearly Rmb7.2tn of loans as NPLs between 2016 to 2020 related to overcapacity built during the 10+ years of investment boom before supply-side reforms started in 2016. In light of already high investment growth in the industrial sectors, supported by strong industrial credit growth in the past 3 years and moderating margin in a number of industrial sectors, we believe it is time to assess the related credit risks at this point. In addition, this will give us a general sense of average annual credit costs needed to support the industrial-led growth model.

## Where are we in the industrial cycle right now?

A number of factors have pushed up industrial investment in recent years, including:

1. Policy shift to support industrial upgrade, particularly given the rising trade tensions globally.

2. A further shift in global production to China during Covid.

3. With the slowdown in property investment and a tightening on LGFV debt and investments, various levels of government are pushing industrial investment to support GDP growth.

The shift in global production to China during Covid, and upgrades in China's various manufacturing sectors supported a strong pickup in demand growth despite the capacity expansion. As a result, we saw industrial production growth (in nominal terms) continue to outpace FAI (fixed asset investments) in 2020 and 2021, sustaining the trend started in 2016 with the onset of supply-side reforms.

Our screening process can be summarized as below:

First, we look at the single most important indicator for potential credit risk: EBIT interest coverage. We would consider a sector to have at least medium/high risk if its EBIT interest coverage is either 1) notably worse than 2015's level; or 2) notably lower than that of other sectors (we see 7x as an average level for EBIT interest coverage now).

If the EBIT interest coverage ratio does not suggest medium/high risk, we then look at the other seven metrics and assume:

• The sectors with no more than three red flags have low credit risk;

• The sectors with over three red flags have medium risk.

On the other hand, if the EBIT interest coverage ratio does suggest medium/high risk, we then look at the other seven metrics and assume:

• The sectors with no more than three red flags have medium risk;

• The sectors with over threered flags have high risk.

• Our consumer analyst, Lillian Lou, points out that agricultural product processing, including animal feed production, is an important sector to ensure stable food supply and CPI in China, and therefore would receive government support. Despite weaker profitability, she doesn't consider the sector to have high credit risk.

Specifically, the sectors we categorize as high credit risks account for 11% of total industrial revenue and 11% of total industrial debt, while the sectors seen as low risk account for 35% of revenue and 37% of debt. ## What level of credit risk is implied by the manufacturing sectors?

Though we believe a higher portion of credits to the medium to high risk industrial subsectors could be at risks in coming years, we certainly do not believe the entire credit base of those sectors will become NPLs. Drawing on experience from the past cycles, even if all the companies in certain industrial sectors are suffering losses due to overcapacity issues, once the capacity in those sectors is cut a reasonable amount, most companies will then return to profitability. Hence, even for credits to the medium to high risk sectors, only a portion of such credits will become NPLs in an industrial downturn.

In order to arrive at a reasonable estimate of potential percentage of risky credits, we draw on experience from the last round of industrial NPL digestion cycle and consider the years it will take before China starts to address industrial risks this time around. A rule of thumb is: the longer it takes, the higher the potential NPL ratio could be. And the sooner the industry starts to cut back on capacity growth, the faster margins can be restored, as well as debt service capability.

China experienced 4.5 years of negative PPI between 2012 and 2016 with industrial investment growth outpacing industrial production growth for over 10 years before 2016. This was followed by banks cumulatively digesting 26% of manufacturing-related loans as NPLs between 2016 and 2020. Approximately, we believe continued quick industrial investment and credit growth when PPI is already negative could increase the potential NPL ratio 4-5pppt per year .

2023 was the first year with negative PPI during the current cycle. As we are in mid-2024 now, manufacturing investment growth is still higher than 2023 and PPI remains negative.

Therefore we consider the potential NPL ratio that will be incurred by two years of continuous negative PPI at 12% for sectors with high credit risks.

For the sectors categorized as low risk, we assume 4% at risk, which is the average NPL ratio for manufacturing loans over the past 10+ years.

For the sectors categorized as medium risk, we assume the potential NPL ratio to be the average of the high risk and low risk categories.

Taking into account each sector's proportion of total manufacturing credits, we estimate that around 6.8% of overall manufacturing credit could have been at risk at the end of 2023. Note that we don't see the 6.8% to be an NPL ratio at a specific point of time in the future. Rather, we see it as a cumulative NPL ratio that could be gradually exposed over several years in the future.

## Current level of higher risk credits is still manageable, but things could get worse before getting better

For the past three years between 2021 and 2023, we estimate that the banks have been digesting 1.9% of manufacturing-related loans as NPLs per year, based on the banks we cover. This is equivalent to around Rmb900-1,000bn of NPL digestion a year, within a total of Rmb3-3.1tn of annual NPL digestion since Covid. This is a more modest pace compared to the last round of supply-side reform between 2016 and 2020, when banks were digesting manufacturing NPLs at \~5% per year. Apparently, rebounding manufacturing profitability and export market share gains, as well as extensive risk digestion before Covid, led to milder NPL pressure in the past three years.

We think the 6.8% in potential NPLs as of 2023, a level that is likely to increase further as manufacturing capex continues, would lead to more annual risk digestion above 1.9% in coming years. Hence, we use 1.9% as the base rate and consider that any incremental risk digestion above this level would have an impact on banks' profit and capital.

If the banking system were to start digesting the higher-risk credit now, we estimate that the banks need to increase the pace of manufacturing NPL digestion to just 2.3%, from 1.9% in the past few years, for 2-3 years. However, it doesn't appear that rationalization of industrial investment and credit will come as early as 2024. In fact, since the current policy focus on dealing with financial risks related to LGFVs and the property sector, policymakers in both central and local government are still pushing industrial investment and credit growth to cushion the macro impact from slower local infra investment growth and property sales and investment. Hence, we believe risks are likely to build up further before the risk digestion process. On risks and outlook for a few key industrial sectors, our analyst teams have outlined their outlook below.

## Views from our industry analysts

## Fuel processing, chemicals, and fibers

Jack Lu

These segments have largely been in a down-cycle since COVID, owing to China's weak macro recovery and property downturn as well as supply-side overcapacity. We think the industry may continue to show lackluster earnings for a while, but credit risk for these segments should be low, in our view, despite high financial leverage and stretched EBIT/ interest coverage.

• State-owned fuel processing capacity accounts for 70% of total capacity in China. PetroChina and Sinopec as integrated oil companies have robust upstream operations to provide strong cash flow. The state-owned energy enterprises have very healthy balance sheets now overall, although their downstream segments have shown stretched financials in the past few years.

• Non-SOE independent refining and petrochemical companies have high leverage ratios as a result of aggressive expansion in the past five years, with financial support from provincial governments. It is difficult for them to make a decent margin during the down-cycle now, but their cash flows are still sufficient to cover interest as their capital spending cycles slow. Unlike property developers, their products – including fuels and chemicals – are consumable goods with more efficient working capital turnover, although their leverage ratio looks high. And also, China has the flexibility to unwind fuel export restrictions depending on fuel inventory levels, which will result in higher pricing and better margins.

• Teapot refiners (very small-scale capacity) account for 15% of total output in China. The central government's strict fuel consumption tax levy in the past few years has burdened their profitability. Over the longer term, we expect them to be further consolidated into larger scale capacity with improved economics.

• Specialty chemical companies are exposed to niche markets, and their products are either replacing imports (high-end polymers, fiber, and healthcare materials), or expanding overseas to gain share in the global market, based on China's supply chain cost advantages. Their financials should remain solid, and they also enjoy tax benefit and favorable funding polices.

• The chemical fiber segment has had high leverage and low margins for a decade, and has been well consolidated. Some of these assets are operated under Sinopec and PetroChina, with low credit risk, and others are largely independent fiber plants that have established a cost advantage and stay low on the global cost curve. Besides, their downstream fabric market is fragmented with weak bargaining power, which allows fiber companies to have a quick cash cycle to cover interest.

## Materials

## Rachel Zhang

## Where we are in the cycle

Depending on their end demand and supply situation, different commodities/materials sit at different stages of their cycle.

• Up-cycle: copper, alumina/aluminum, gold, and uranium.

• Near or at the bottom: lithium, paper, glass fiber, steel, cement, and building materials.

• More downside to go: glass, solar glass.

Those with less exposure to property/infrastructure and more to manufacturing will have a better demand outlook. And if a bit of supply disruption is added (e.g., copper, alumina), pricing will be even firmer.

## Long-term industrial upgrade potential

For industries with high energy intensity such as aluminum, glass, and cement, producers continue to upgrade to try to lower the unit consumption of power or coal. Carbon trading is expected to start in steel, aluminum, and cement in 2024. This will lead to more upgrades and exits of outdated capacity.

In addition, other industries' upgrade to more automation, smart manufacturing, etc. will result in more demand for either equipment demand or more power/power equipment. That will also lead to more demand for metals such as copper, aluminum, and steel which are used in power equipment and machinery.

## Capex outlook

For commodities that are in an up-cycle (e.g., copper, alumina/aluminum, gold, and uranium), there will be more capex on M&A or development/expansion projects.

For materials with a growing long-term outlook (e.g. solar glass, lithium), despite current weak earnings, companies will invest capex hoping to gain market share. .

## Risks

Materials/commodities are often correlated with the macro economy. Changes in monetary policy, property and infrastructure-related policy, and/or EV and renewablerelated policy will affect demand for materials/ commodities.

## Industrials (capital goods)

Sheng Zhong, Chelsea Wang

## Where we are in the cycle

Capital goods cycles mainly depend on the supply-demand balance in the downstream markets. Currently, the segments are in different stages:

• General manufacturing equipment (including industrial automation) is at the bottom of the cycle, stabilizing amid ongoing recovery from traditional industries (such as consumer electronics, food & beverage, etc., mainly supported by strong export demand), offset by the down-cycle in new energy-related downstream markets including solar, lithium batteries, and EVs.

• Construction machinery is at the late stage of a down-cycle, in our view. The weak property market and higher requirements for infrastructure project quality have constrained construction activities. Therefore, the demand in China's market is mainly from replacement, which is at the trough, but the cycle reversal takes time.

• Heavy duty trucks are in an up-cycle, mainly driven by increasing replacement demand, and increasing LNG heavy duty truck penetration.

## Long-term industrial upgrade potential

We believe upgrades of the downstream industries and capital goods themselves have great potential. Industrial upgrade is the long-term target for China's manufacturing, entailing more advanced equipment among other requirements. Together with higher localization targets for lower costs and safer supply chains, this will be the key driver for capital goods.

For capital goods themselves, we see strong upgrade potential for higher technology, efficiency and energy-saving equipment, including but not limited to automation, robotics, high-end special equipment, etc.

## Risks

For general equipment, the demand is related to China's domestic macroeconomy and exports. Stronger/weaker-than-expected Chinese macro conditions and export demand will be the key risks, including but not limited to policies, geopolitics, etc.

## Autos

Tim Hsiao, Stanley Wang, Cindy Huang

## Where we are in the cycle

Domestic passenger vehicle sales (\~22mn units in 2023) have yet to return to the peak of \~24mn units in 2016-17. That said, growing NEV penetration and a benign policy environment supported by stimulus and NEV purchase tax exemption led to a domestic volume recovery of \~3-6% p.a. between 2021 and 2023. Meanwhile, China's latest push in vehicle replacement and easing car loan policies could underpin further domestic volume growth in 2024, in our view, though price competition could stay intense if demand fails to keep up with supply amid macro uncertainties.

## Long-term industrial upgrade potential

Overall vehicle capacity utilization was around 60% in 2023 per our estimate, though utilization for a few JV brands could be below 30%. On one hand, vehicle demand recovery could lead to higher capacity utilization – but more importantly, we think upgrading outdated ICEV capacity to EV capacity would be key to driving higher capacity utilization, where EV players could revitalize brownfield capacity rather than build greenfield capacity. One example would be GAC-Aion taking over Mitsubishi's ICEV plant following the latter's exit from China in 2023.

## Capex outlook

We look for China's automotive sector capex to expand because of 1) capacity upgrades, 2) charging infrastructure deployment, and 3) overseas expansion. We expect most Chinese auto OEMs' and parts suppliers' capex to increase overseas, but it could decelerate in China over the next 2-3 years. China's EV and auto parts makers will look to accelerate their plans to set up overseas production amid growing protectionism from the West, while the domestic auto market could enter a phase of industry consolidation that could eliminate volume laggards.

That said, we expect higher capex per unit of capacity overseas than domestically, since there tend to be 1) lower subsidies overseas than in China, and 2) more capex would be allocated overseas toward localizing supply chains, infrastructure development, and channel expansion.

## Risks

Most EV makers continue to prioritize volume and share gain over profitability in 2024, so intensifying price competition could remain a key industry risk, in our view. While growing EV exports and falling battery costs could partially alleviate OEMs' margin pressure and overcapacity concerns, we think more meaningful industry consolidation would need to take place before the competitive landscape improves.

# When do we expect supply-side reform 2.0? Rational pace of investment and risk digestion to start in 2025

## We believe industrial investment and related credit growth will start to rationalize in 2025

We expect an effective mini-version of supply-side reform will start in 2025 for the below reasons:

1. Drag on investment growth from property slowdown and LGFV risk control may ease in 2025, which could reduce local government's incentive to push industrial investments.

2. General loan rates have declined from \~5.6% in 2016 to 4.3% in 2023, and corporate loan rates declined further. to 3.7%. We think the already lowered rates, vs. the last cycle before 2016, will trigger quicker slowdown in industrial credits when risks start to surface.

3. More private sector-driven investment in this cycle vs. more SOE-driven industrial investments before 2016 should trigger industrial credit slowdown after two years of negative PPI vs. four years before 2016.

This means the overinvestment and negative PPI would probably span 2023 and 2025 – i.e., for 2-3 years. Hence, we believe sectors with higher or medium risks will increase compared to the end of 2023.

## We see credits that will be potentially at risk increasing to 10% of manufacturing loans if investment growth starts to rationalize starting in 2025

Generally, we assume that manufacturers will retain largely stable global market share, as in 2023, which would help support stable margins in the next two years. ## How do things compare to the last round of supply-side reform in 2015?

If China starts to slow down manufacturing investments by 2025, we think in many ways the situation will be more benign compared to 2015:

• The manufacturing capacity issue will be less severe – there would be only 2-3 years of negative PPI this round as compared to 4.5 years of negative PPI before 2016.

• In general manufacturing profitability and interest coverage will still be better than in 2015, thanks to more broad-based upgrades and market share gains globally this time around.

• Banks have more robust balance sheets after financial cleanup, with stricter NPL recognition and NPL coverage up from 181% in 2015 to 205% in 2023.

On the other hand, we also see aspects that could make it more challenging for banks to digest risks in 2025:

• Revenue growth is weaker owing to lower NIM, moderating loan growth, and fee rate pressure. As a comparison, commercial banks' revenue grew an average of >Rmb300bn per year between 2015 and 2019, which provided the first line of defense to absorb rising NPLs. However, banks' revenue in 2021, 2022, and 2023 was basically flat. This effectively reduces banks' capability to digest NPLs by way of growth.

• Bank profitability is also weaker as represented by declining ROE – from \~15% in 2015 to \~9% in 2023. This means that internal capital generation will face more pressure once more NPLs start to surface.

• The size of manufacturing-related credits has also risen notably in the past 3-4 years, to Rmb52tn in 2023 according to our estimate, almost doubled from Rmb28tn in the previous round of digestion.

## NPL cost should face another round of more notable increase but remain manageable if manufacturing credits start to rationalize in 2025

We think the incremental Rmb980bn NPL digestion per year from manufacturing credits could be absorbed in a number of ways without hitting banks' profit and capital. Below is one hypothetical combination:

• By 2025 we see less need from property NPL digestion, which we estimate to be Rmb500-600bn per year since 2021. This could release room of \~Rmb200bn for manufacturing NPLs.

• Usually 30% of NPLs can be collected in cash eventually.

• Existing provision could buffer the digestion: we factor in \~5 percentage points reduction in NPL coverage per year, for three years.

• Corporate financing cost would need to go up 40-50bps from the current level of \~3.7%. This should be a natural result from rationalized credit supply from banks, and we think a modest rise of corporate loan yield to 4.1-4.2% would still not add too much pressure for industrial firms.

We see this as one potential way to spread out the cost and balance the usage of provision buffer and raising interest rates, which could minimize the eventual hit to banks' profit.

In fact, we are already seeing signs YTD in 2024 that policymakers are urging some industries to remain rational in capacity expansion. For instance, China's Ministry of Industry and Information Technology (MIIT) on 8 May issued a document seeking public opinions on curbing inefficient capacities in the domestic lithium-ion battery value chain. MIIT laid out the following requirements covering:

1. R&D expenses: should be higher than 3% of total revenue;

2. Utilization rate: >=50%;

3. Performance metrics: technical requirements for batteries and major components, which includes processing control, energy density, cycle life, etc.

## Overly high credit cost will also push more rational manufacturing credits before 2026

If, however, the rationalization comes one year later, in 2026, we estimate that the cost would be too high. First of all, one more year of rapid capex investment amid declining margin would likely push more firms within the manufacturing sector to the risk of default. Hence, we estimate that potential NPLs could reach 12.7% in 2026, compared to 10% in 2025.

Second, the size of manufacturing credit would expand further, likely reaching Rmb74tn by 2026. This would lead to larger incremental risk digestion need per year, at Rmb1.7tn (which is 73% of commercial banks' profit in 2023), considering a three-year digestion period.

To absorb the cost, we think NPL coverage would potentially need to drop 16 percentage points per year and corporate financing cost would need to rebound 50bps. After three years, the banking sector's NPL coverage ratio would fall to 150-160% – basically exhausting the buffer built in the prior 10 years.

Alternatively, to avoid overconsumption of the provision buffer, the average corporate financing cost would need to rebound more than 110bps (to 4.8-5.0%) in order for the banks to earn enough interest income to digest the risks. However this could represent a meaningful increase of interest cost for companies in the short term, which might not be preferred by policymakers.

# How will total NPL and credit growth trend during the industrial credit risk digestion cycle?

While we expect slower industrial investments starting in 2025, we believe further stabilization in the property market, more progress in LGFV risk control/SOE efficiency improvement, and continued growth in service and rural segments should support reasonable income and GDP growth. As a result, we believe stable infra/LGFV credit quality and further moderation in property loan NPL formation would cushion the pickup in industrial loan NPL formation and some deterioration in retail and SME loans during the industrial loan digestion period.

Specifically, we expect banks to digest 3.3% of manufacturing-related NPLs per year if they start to address the risks in 2025, up from 1.9% in the past several years. On the other hand, we think property NPL formation could moderate from 3.2% in the past three years to 1.7% by 2025, releasing around Rmb200bn per year to absorb the industrial NPLs. We believe more downside support for LGFV debt could also reduce NPL formation in infrastructure loans, which we estimate to be at around 0.7% in 2023. For our LGFV debt resolution roadmap, please refer to: China's 3D Journey: China Financials: What a long-term LGFV solution could look like (15 Sep 2023).

Overall, banking sector gross NPL formation could pick up to 1.57% in 2025, 1.59% in 2026, and 1.6% in 2027, vs. around 1.4% in the past two years, based on our forecast. This means the situation would be similar to the 1.6% NPL formation (and digestion) we saw during the peak of the last NPL cycle in 2018. However, the size of NPL digestion would be bigger at around Rmb4.4tn per year, compared to the average of Rmb3.1tn per year since Covid and Rmb2tn in 2018.

Positively, we think this level of NPL digestion would remain largely manageable given a variety of ways we discussed above to potentially spread the cost. In addition, the NPL digestion could be finished in three years this time around, shorter than the last period, which took five years from 2016 to 2020.

## We believe more rational industrial investment and NPL digestion should weigh further on TSF growth

This was evidenced during 2016 and 2020: when banks were pushed to recognize and digest manufacturing-related NPLs, manufacturing credit growth dropped to 1-2% per year.

Therefore we think slower industrial investment and credit growth is another cost to be borne by the economy during the risk digestion phase.

## If banks start to digest industrial NPLs starting from 2025, we expect slower TSF at around 7%

If the rationalization happens in 2025, which is our base case, we expect total manufacturing-related credit growth to drop from 12-13% in 2023 and 2024 to below 7% in 2025, before rebounding slightly to above 7% in 2026 and 2027. We expect some overshoot in the first year of rationalization because some self-circulating loans, previously extended to satisfy window guidance, would most likely be cleaned up first. Though the drag could be partly offset by some rebound in property, mortgage, and infrastructure credit, we still expect overall credit growth to decline to around 7% during the three years of risk digestion from 2025 to 2027.

With the higher requirements on quality of investment projects, we believe overall infrastructure credit growth, especially that driven by LGFVs, should continue to moderate in 2024 and 2025, as the rationalization of LGFV debt started in late 2023. There will also be divergence across different regions and potentially more new credit for investments by LGFVs in developed regions. We expect the growth of infra-related credit to slow down to 6.1% in 2024 and 4.5% in 2025 from 9.5% in 2023, before rebounding to 5-6% in 2025 and 2026.

As for the property sector, we think credit growth could stabilize at low single digits in 2024 and 2025. We believe the recent relaxation of home purchase restrictions and a rational policy combo (including lower mortgage down payment ratio, rates floor, and Rmb300bn relending quota) should cushion core asset prices and help the sector bottom out over time – see China Financials: New policy combo balancing property market support and risk control (17 May 2024). We factor in some recovery with property credit growth back to mid- to high single digits in 2026 and 2027.

## What are the long-term uncertainties and risks?

Maintaining and expanding this large and complicated industrial base is not an easy task. Despite the concerns about impact from the multipolar world and shifts in demand and production, we actually see the biggest risks to this industrial-led growth model coming from domestic policies in China. Specifically, we see two risks to this model, particularly given the highly capital-/investment-intensive and cyclical nature of the industrial sectors:

1. Overly low loan yields that erode the financial system's ability to digest risks from industrial investment cycles, and eventually lead to reduced risk appetite and ability to support credit and capital allocation to the industrial sectors;

2. Overinvestment in industrial sectors and tolerating negative PPI for too long, which lead to impairment of the financial system's balance sheet;

3. Policy mistakes in future industrial cycles, given the highly cyclical nature of the industrial sectors.

## We believe reduced ability to support the ongoing industrial investments and digest risks from the industrial cycles will be the biggest risk to this model

To support the industrial-led growth model, we believe there will be ongoing needs for new credits and capital to support industrial investments and R&D. In addition, some level of infrastructure upgrades will also be needed to help China maintain a capacity edge in the industrial sectors. Both will be better met by the banking system because banks are better suited to finance the large supply chain with tens of millions of SMEs and to handle the less transparent infrastructure cash flows. The capital market will play a supplementary role that focuses on identifying and financing a few winners in each industry.

Our analysis shows that the banking system can still digest the industrial NPLs over the upcoming NPL cycle with current NIM and profitability, partly benefiting from NPL coverage built up during periods before the past rounds of rate cuts. However, we believe the current average loan yields are already too low, and, hence, generate low or no returns over the cycles.

We believe further rate cuts for industrial loans will lead to little or no room for banks to digest risks from future industrial cycles – eventually leading to reduced risk appetite and ability to support credit and capital allocation to the industrial sectors.

Specifically, we expect through-the-cycle NPL formation of manufacturing credits to range between 1.9% and 3.3% annually, according to the analysis above. Considering 50% final loss content (the portion of NPLs that can't be recovered eventually), this will result in around 1% to 1.7% of credit cost inherited in loan pricing. Overall, we think manufacturingrelated loan rates will probably need to stay no lower than the range between 4.1% and 4.8% to make them economically sustainable for banks through the cycle.

Currently, the average new corporate loan yield stands at around 3.75% with average manufacturing loan yield likely modestly higher at 3.8%-4.0%. As we discussed earlier, with help from the reserves built up when interest rates were higher, we believe the banking system can still digest the next industrial NPL cycle. However, if corporate loan yields continue to fall to \~3% over time, it would certainly reduce support to further industrial investments and innovation. With 3% corporate loan yield, we think:

• Funding cost would probably be further reduced to 1.5%, as compared to \~2% in 2023;

• Credit cost for industrial loans would remain on average \~1.3% through the cycle;

• Operating cost would likely stay the same; and

• Therefore, the implied ROA for banks on industrial loans would be -0.3% on an annualized basis.

## What would a through-the-cycle negative ROA of -0.3% from industrial loans mean for the banks?

1. Banks would not be able to build provision buffer to cushion future industrial down-cycles, owing to losses made in this business.

2. Because industrial lending would effectively need to be subsidized by other business, it would reduce banks' risk appetite and capital allocation to this sector over time.

More importantly, it would impair overall profitability and reduce capital generation, which is necessary to support future industrial credit growth. To put that into context, - 0.3% ROA on Rmb52.9tn in overall industrial-related loans in 2023 means \~Rmb120bn loss after tax a year. It would effectively reduce banks' average ROE 43bps in a year. Considering likely lower yield from other loans in this environment, not just industrial loans, the drag on ROE would actually be more severe.

Over an industrial cycle of five years, banking sector ROE could drop from 9.6% in 2023 to a level around or lower than 7% if loan pricing fails to compensate through-the-cycle costs. At this level of ROE, we think banks could support only around 5% total credit growth after 30% dividend payment. Given the much more limited credit supply capacity and unappealing pricing from industrial credits, we'd expect more capital to be allocated out of industrial sectors, to other areas such as retail.

As a result, we think the risk is that after one or two industrial cycles combined with low interest rates, banks' support to industrial loans will decrease to low-single-digit growth over the longer term, as compared to the mid-teens level in the past three years. This is similar to the low credit growth industrial sectors experienced during 2016 and 2020. It would notably affect China's further industrial upgrade and innovation, in our view.

From another perspective, while the 50bps reduction in funding cost (from 2% to 1.5%) in this case could partially offset lower loan rates, it would also materially reduce interest income earned by depositors across China. Because >54% of household financial assets are allocated in deposits, the interest income is a key source of financial returns that can be earned by a large proportion of ordinary households. Based on the balance of Rmb137tn of retail deposits as of the end of 2023, every 50bps decline in deposit yield (assume lower funding cost will be shared similarly across households, companies and other institutions) will be equivalent to Rmb685bn loss of income to households a year. This effectively allocates part of the burden of supporting industrial upgrades to household income, while the very purpose of industrial upgrade is to drive more sustainable income growth.

## Overinvestment in industrial sectors and tolerating negative PPI for too long, leading to impairment of the financial system's balance sheet

Multipolar world challenges – such as higher tariffs, sanctions, production offshoring, and other decoupling-related risks – are increasing pressure on market share and margins for China's industrial firms. In this tougher environment, we think China's manufacturers and financial system could actually prolong the capex cycle in order to fight for every bit of market share with higher efficiency and lower cost. Potentially, this could cause manufacturing overinvestment to last until 2027.

If this happens, we'd expect manufacturing debt to continue to increase. Manufacturing output would grow, but PPI and margin could further deteriorate owing to global market share loss. This would lead to a widening gap between FAI and industrial production, slower profit growth, and eventually worsening debt servicing capability, as measured in EBIT interest coverage.

This could also inflict some permanent damage on the financial system in China, which could notably reduce the system's ability to support needed credit supports for further industrial investments and upgrades of China's vast industrial supply chain, core elements of China's competitive advantage.

## Rmb2.0-3.5tn capital injection to SOE banks would likely be needed in this scenario

We expect potential government capital injection to major SOE banks in this scenario to help maintain some reasonable credit supply. However, the financial's system ability to efficiently allocate credits and risk appetite would likely take time to restore.

First of all, we estimate that Rmb500bn-Rmb1.5tn capital injection to SOE banks could be needed to offset the profit impact in the NPL digestion cycle after 2027. We take into account a variety of ways to reasonably share the excess industrial NPL digestion burden (similar to the discussion above) – but find that the bottom line would still be hit in the end, given the amount of NPLs accumulated over many years. In addition, if policymakers want to shorten the NPL digestion process (from five years to three years) to minimize drag on industrial credit growth, the profit pressure would be greater each year and require more capital injection. Specifically, we consider:

• Rmb200bn that could be digested with the room freed up from less property NPLs;

• 30% that could be collected back;

• Around 5 percentage points (10 percentage points if digested in three years) reduction in provision coverage each year;

• New corporate loan yield to rebound 40-50bps (rebound 70-80bps if digest in 3 years);

• SOE banks to account for 60% of the total NPL digestion and profit shock, because more industrial credits were supplied by SOE banks in the past several years.

The 1ppt drop in CET1 ratio in four years is essentially the cost for SOE banks to support industrial credit expansion at a low margin. Therefore, Rmb1.5tn capital injection would be needed in 2027 to bring SOE banks' CET1 ratio back to 12%, which would restore the buffer to supply industrial credit at a similar speed in the future.

2. With a similar profit trajectory as above, we think SOE banks' average ROE would drop around 2ppt, from 10.9% in 2023 to 8.8% by 2027. As banks start to digest industrial risks from 2027, and considering capital injection from the government to cushion the profit impact, we expect SOE profit to remain flat during the risk digestion process. Hence, we see ROE dropping another 2ppt, to 6.8% when the SOE banks come out of the five-year risk digestion cycle. At 6.8% ROE, we believe the banks could support only around 5% credit growth without further reducing capital ratio. If policymakers still want the SOE banks to supply around 8% credit growth, they might need to inject Rmb600bn a year in capital to make up for the shortfalls. To sustain the 8% annual credit growth over a threeto four-year cycle, total capital injection could reach around Rmb2tn, according to our estimate.

Overall, this would lead to potential Rmb2.0-3.5tn in total capital injection. Regardless, we believe the financial's system ability to efficiently allocate credits and risk appetite would likely take more time to restore.

## Impact from the multipolar world is worse than expected, with more demand shifting to other parts of the world

Despite the concerns about the impact from higher tariffs, ongoing trade frictions, and demand shifting away from China in a multipolar world – see our global report: Global Industrials: Manufacturing in the Multipolar World (1 May 2023) – the actual impact so far has been quite manageable, partly because of China's policy shift to focus on industrial supply chain and upgrades. However, if the scenario laid out in our global report – potentially 17% of manufacturing output relocating out of China in a decoupling scenario – does materialize, it could lead to higher losses for China's financial system and put further pressure on both credit demand and supply in China.

We think the biggest risk to the financial system could emerge if the loss of production and exports takes place in the relatively near to medium term, say by 2027, which would likely coincide with continued manufacturing investment domestically. As a result, we see the following impacts.

• Notable revenue loss in a relatively short period, at 17% as estimated in our global report, would lead to sharper margin contraction of affected manufacturing supply chains, because cost base takes time to be adjusted, especially considering higher depreciation from capex expansion in recent years.

• Widening gap between investment and production owing to output relocation out of China could exacerbate overcapacity.

• Debt service capability as measured by EBIT interest coverage would face more pressure.

As a result, we think the potential NPL ratio could rise further from 18%, as estimated in the above scenario with overinvestment through 2027. It would lead to notable NPL digestion pressure for banks, potential capital injection needs, and lower credit demand and supply over the longer term. The negative impact would increase if the output relocation exceeds the 17% estimate.

Alternatively, if the output relocation is to happen over a longer period, say by 2030, we'd expect manageable risk to banks' asset quality. However, it would still lead to moderating industrial credit demand and slower overall credit growth in the longer term. This is because we believe banks would slow down credit supply to industrial sectors if the trend of manufacturing output relocation becomes more clear over time, which will help control credit risk exposure. On the other hand, we think a 17% output shift over a period of seven years would still reduce the potential growth of industrial output from the 5-6% level to 2.5-3.5% towards 2030. We'd expect manufacturing-related credit growth to decrease to 3-4% on average from 2025 to 2030, if this happens.

## Policy mistakes in future industrial cycles, given a more cyclical path of growth

Policy twists and turns during China's economic transition and property cycles have temporarily reduced policy transparency and hurt market sentiment. The future economic development path could be even more cyclical, so how policymakers coordinate measures to manage the cycles will also be key to ensure more long-term sustainable development. Specifically, we see risks of overly aggressive policy interference in the industrial cycles, which tend to lead to belated and harsh policy turns, which are particularly costly from the perspective of credit risk and market confidence.

## Implications for China's long-term credit demand and interest rates

We continue to believe that banks will have a competitive advantage in asset sourcing and risk management for credit and capital demand from the manufacturing, infrastructure, SME and retail segments. This has already allowed banks to gain market share in credit allocation. In addition, we believe the progress on risk containment and the shift in focus to manufacturing should drive more sustainable long-term credit demand in China.

However, we continue to expect moderating loan and asset growth as regulators continue to reduce window guidance on loan growth and urge banks to reduce self-circulating loans. In addition, we expect more rational growth in industrial loans starting in 2025 to help reduce overcapacity risks. Specifically, we expect bank loan growth to moderate to around 7% in 2025 and 2026 before some likely rebound beyond 2027.

Positively, we expect some NIM rebound starting in 2H25, although 1H25 could still be negatively affected by the repricing from the 25bps cut in five-year LPR in 1H24. This will help banks to digest industrial NPLs. Specifically, we believe slower overall credit growth would support loan yields and also reduce competition for deposits, which should also help banks reduce funding costs.

We expect banking sector gross NPL formation to pick up in 2025 and 2026 to close to 1.6%, and expect banks to reduce NPL coverage ratio to cushion the earnings impact. We expect on average 10-11 percentage NPL coverage decline per year in 2025 and 2026.

Accordingly, among the banks we could see more volatile earnings with more divergent ROE trends based on their asset sourcing and risk management capabilities. We believe CCB, PSBC, CMB, Industrial, and Ningbo will have more resilient earnings.

Despite the likely modest earnings pressure, we believe more rational credit growth, reduced concerns about risks once the banks started to digest industrial NPLs and some NIM rebound should support bank valuation over time.

## Banks

If banks start to rationalize industrial credit growth and digest related risks in 2025, we could see a more notable slowdown in loan growth and higher NPL formation ratios, but the profit impact should remain manageable, because there will still be room to reduce NPL coverage and improve NIM to offset.

We expect more rational growth in industrial loans starting 2025 to help reduce overcapacity risks, while bank loan growth could moderate to around 7% in 2025 and 2026 before some likely rebound beyond 2027, when banks largely complete risk digestion in this cycle.

As discussed above, we expect gross NPL formation to pick up from around 1.4% in the past several years to close to 1.6% in 2025 and 2026. This will be driven by the following.

• Manufacturing-related NPL formation would rise to \~3.3% in 2025, from 1.9% previously as risk digestion starts in 2025;

• We expect property NPL formation to come down notably by 2025 from >3.2% on average between 2021 and 2023, helped by more downside support from the government. In fact ,banks already noted the inflection of property NPL formation in 2023.

• Infrastructure NPL formation could also moderate given more focus on resolving and rationalizing LGFV debt growth since 2023.

## Insurance

Insurers are preparing themselves for the low interest challenge… We see much concern about the low interest rate environment, especially since 2H23 (please refer to our report How will further interest rate declines affect insurers?). It pressures China's insurers a lot considering their mismatch between assets and liabilities and relatively rigid liability costs, thus overhanging their valuation. Insurers are preparing themselves to deal with this situation better from both the liability side and asset side. We are pleased to see the progress they have made.

• Reducing liability cost: We see insurers accelerating the optimization of their product mix and promoting par products in 1H24 to lower their rigid liability costs. The National Financial Regulatory Administration (NFRA) also asked insurers to lower their pricing interest rate cap from 3.5% to 3.0% in 2H23. The bancassurance channel also underwent commission rearrangement in 3Q23 and margin increased substantially afterwards. We believe some top listed players still have a decent rigid liability cost at <2.5%.

## Brokers

Though IPO volume will be depressed in the near future, we still see ample room for leading investment banks such as CITICS, CICC and HTSC, to consolidate market share over time: We believe capital markets will continue to support the most innovative manufacturing firms, while more stringent requirements on listed company quality will drive further divergence in the value added by underwriters. Leading institutional franchises will have an edge in deal sourcing, financial advisory, and execution, as well as investments in the primary market to secure client relationships at an early stage. Separately, we also see rising M&A opportunities as industrial firms will show higher consolidation needs after rapid capacity expansion, especially when much of the capacity growth has been driven by private companies, leading to more market-oriented decisions. This could benefit the top dealmakers.

## HKEx

We've extensively discussed the cyclical pressure (higher rate environment) and structural pressure (Mainland China PE/VC's investment shift to industrial) on HKEX, we believe these factors have gradually been reflected in HKEx's valuation in the past year or so. Notably, onshore regulator CSRC has notably tightened IPO financing since late 2023 and raised the financial threshold for new IPOs since early 2024. We think this indicates that capital market support for industrial firms in the onshore market will also be more focused on quality rather than quantity, which will in turn translate into more rational PE/ VC investments in industrial areas over time.

As the previous proactive policy shift to favor industrial sectors is largely done, together with more selectivity in the onshore IPO market, we think PE/VC funds could gradually look for more opportunities in the service sectors, to which funding has been underallocated in the past 4-5 years. This will help to build the IPO pipeline for HKEx over time, likely beyond 2026.
