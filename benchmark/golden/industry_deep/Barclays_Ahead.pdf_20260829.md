## French 2024 parliamentary elections

## Shades of grey

Silvia Ardagna, Barclays, UK | Mariano Cena, Barclays, UK | Themistoklis Fiotakis, Barclays, UK | Lefteris Farmakis, Barclays, UK | Marek Raczko, Barclays, UK | Sheryl Dong, Barclays, UK | Rohan Khanna, Barclays, UK | Max Kitson, Barclays, UK | Zoso Davies, Barclays, UK | Soumya Sarkar, Barclays, UK | David Alam, Barclays, UK

Excerpted from French 2024 parliamentary elections: Shades of grey, published on June 12, 2024

Initial polls indicate no outright majority will result from the French legislative elections to be held on 30 June and 7 July, but a strong advantage for Le Pen's party makes 'cohabitation' or a hung Parliament the most likely outcomes, both negative vs the status quo. We discuss possible economic and market implications.

A hung Parliament with President Macron's coalition losing more seats would make governing even more dificult than it has been since he lost his majority in 2022, with little prospect of the government passing any meaningful legislation to improve the public finance outlook, promote growth or conduct industrial policy. This scenario also increases the risk of gridlock and repeated no confidence votes. Absent major shocks, we would expect that the government would be able to present to the European Commission (EC) a seven-year adjustment plan and pass a budget in line with the 2024 Stability Programme using article 49.3 of the Constitution. Although this would leave public debt on an increasing path in 2025, we expect that the EC would approve the plan on the prospect of a small yearly fiscal adjustment. On foreign policy, we would expect a continuation of the status quo.

A 'cohabitation' between President Macron and a far-right government would, in our view, be more negative for markets, European Union institutions and the prospect of further fiscal and political integration of European Union member states, but the key questions are how much so and over what horizon. We expect that the government would not try to put in place a quantum of policies that could trigger a situation resembling that of the post-2018 election in Italy or the 2022 Liz Truss government in the UK. However, we would expect that over time, at a slow pace, a far-right government would attempt to implement its policy agenda. We judge that this would worsen the long-term fiscal sustainability of the French sovereign and would also undermine the power of European institutions, in line with the National Rally's political manifesto presented in 2022.

The risk that President Macron resigns after a large defeat, particularly if his party loses more seats than currently suggested by polls, has been ruled out by the President himself (see here). However, if this were to happen, new presidential elections could result in the National Rally party controlling both the Parliament and the Elysée. This would allow RN to implement its policy agenda to a greater extent.

French politics introduces risk premium in the EUR, tilting the risk profile further to the downside and beyond economic divergence with the US. It adds to positive UK political risks, reinforcing our constructive view on the pound, but also impedes further weakening in the Swiss franc via the generation of safe haven flows. We also see currencies sensitive to Russia tensions, such as PLN or HUF, as net losers, via increased geopolitical risk premium and uncertainty around EU transfers.

The rise in political uncertainty and the ECB’s reluctance to guide towards more policy easing have compounded OAT net issuance challenges. We see OATs underperforming the rest of the

EGB complex in most scenarios with a clear asymmetry in favour of wider OAT-Bund spreads.
We continue to hold our 10y SPGB-OAT tightener.

Credit has started behaving as a proxy hedge for European political concerns once again, this time led by French banks. The resulting volatility is a headwind for credit in the near term, but it is not a sign of fundamental deterioration or rising credit risk, in our view.

# Global Inflation-Linked Products

## A User's Guide

Michael Pond, CFA, BCI, US | Jonathan Hill, CFA, BCI, US | Iaroslav Shelepko, Barclays, UK

Excerpted from Global Inflation-Linked Products: A User's Guide, published on June 12, 2024

A user's guide focused on educating investors on mechanics, drivers, and key themes of global inflation-linked markets.

In a change from the previous edition of this guide, when inflation was relatively low and stable, inflation is a front-burner macro issue for most investors, regardless of their asset class. Many developed economies realized 40y highs on inflation during the pandemic, hitting 9.1%, 10.8% and 14.2% y/y on US CPI, eurozone HICPx and UK RPI, respectively, and many EM countries, such as Turkey at 85% y/y, experienced much higher peaks. While inflation has generally retreated, it has remained sticky to the upside in many areas and continues to be a key driver of monetary policy. Inflation uncertainty and the resulting volatility in the path of central bank policy globally, and hence, real rates, has also led to larger swings, and opportunities, in inflation-linked market returns. This user's guide educates investors on how these markets, and their underlying inflation indices, work, how they might use them to hedge, or to express a view, and to better understand the market signal they provide.

The Bloomberg World Government Inflation-Linked Bond index is largely unchanged at about \$3.1trn, since we last published the Global Inflation-Linked Products: A User’s Guide, in April 2019, and has more than doubled since the financial crisis. At more than \$1.6trn, the US remains the largest on a market value basis, but the UK index, which has a duration of over 22 years, is larger in risk-adjusted terms. The Emerging Market Government Inflation-Linked Bond index has grown to about \$700bn, up from the \$400-550bn range it had been in from 2011-2020, with most of the volatility due to yield changes.

Demand for inflation-linked bonds grew during the pandemic as inflation surged, and investors looked to hedge inflation-sensitive "risk-on" positions and express views on the inflation outlook. There was also heightened interest in what markets were pricing in, especially from policymakers wanting to ensure inflation-expectations were well anchored. While interest in inflation prints and the outlook remains broad based, demand has largely retreated to prepandemic levels, leading, for example, Germany and Canada to cancel further issuance. This is in part because policymakers have largely maintained credibility by their responses to elevated inflation, and because restrictive policy led to higher real yield and negative returns. But elevated real yields ofer competitive returns and can be a draw for many back to the asset class, and target-consistent breakevens, which imply very little inflation risk premiums, may be seen as structurally attractive if longer-term upside inflation risks, such as those from deglobalization, climate change, energy-transition or debt monetization, become more of a concern. Many are also revisiting how linkers fit into a broad-based asset-allocation framework; in our detailed analysis included here we find compelling arguments for inclusion, especially for front-end linkers.

As with past versions, the main goal of the Global Inflation-Linked Products: A User’s Guide is education. The first section explains the products within the inflation-linked universe and how they work, including a detailed section on linear and non-linear inflation-linked derivatives. The second outlines the features of each country’s individual market. Last, we address key issues for the asset class, deep dive into asset allocation with linkers, modelling and understanding inflation dynamics in the US, UK and Europe, and our frameworks for finding relative value. We also discuss the particular inflation risks associated with the energy transition and update our overview of the many inflation-linked resources we ofer on Barclays Live.

This is the ninth guide with which I have had the privilege of being involved, and, given the publication frequency, it will likely be my last. With each new version, we have tried to make inflation-market fundamentals and frameworks more accessible and provide new ways to find opportunities. We are proud of its history and hope you find this latest one useful for navigating global inflation markets in the coming years.

## Executive Summary

Stress Test 2024

We expect June 26's stress results to confirm that the banking system is strong and resilient as all Large-Cap Banks under coverage remain above their minimum capital requirements during a hypothetical recession. As is typical, the banks will be tested against a severe global recession with heightened stress in both commercial and residential real estate markets, as well as in corporate debt markets. For the largest banks, the global market shock is characterized by a sudden dislocation to financial markets stemming from expectations of reduced global economic activity and heightened inflation.

This year's stress test will also include the Fed's first exploratory analysis. However, only aggregate results will be released, and these will not afect bank capital requirements. The exploratory analysis includes four separate hypothetical elements that will assess the resilience of the banking system against a wider range of risks. This includes two funding stresses applied to all banks tested and two market shocks applied to only the largest and most complex banks.

While there are diferences, this year's stress-test recession scenario seems similar to last year in its stringency. It could result in lower stress test loan losses than last year, but PPNR could be reduced by a greater amount. In addition, lower rates aid capital ratios. Still, JPM recently cautioned that its current SCB seems cyclically low, and it recently showed an illustration with a 50bp increase penciled in for 2024. This could result in some upward bias this year, especially for those bank names that saw outsized reductions last year (JPM, BAC, GS of note).

The Fed uses the results of the stress test to set large bank capital requirements through its stressed capital bufer (SCB). This year will drive the minimum capital requirement from 4Q24 through 3Q25. We expect our entire coverage to have 150bps of excess capital. TFC (inclusive of its recent insurance sale), NTRS, COF, FITB, RF and KEY could have the most 'excess capital, while C, BAC, GS, WFC and MS have the least. Still, this is before Basel III Endgame (B3E), which could have a meaningful impact.

On June 28 after the close, banks will be allowed to publicly confirm their SCB and announce their expected capital return plans, if they desire. We expect several banks under coverage to announce dividend increases that evening and into the following week. The median bank could see a modest 4% increase, approximating last year, reflecting elevated unrealized losses, caution ahead of the Basel III endgame (especially by those facing AOCI opt-out elimination), and economic uncertainty. We expect these factors to also result in very little share repurchase commentary (though we anticipate several banks will continue repurchasing their shares).

Looking out, we believe the finalization of the B3E could prove to be a catalyst for share repurchase activity to accelerate. In the SCB 2024 cycle, we expect the median bank to repurchase \~3% of its shares, up from closer to 2% in 2022 and 2023. Keep in mind, we do forecast loan growth picking up later this year, and more so into next year. If loan growth does not materialize, more capital could be allocated to share buybacks. Given the less capitalintense business models, we expect the Trust Banks (particularly BK, STT) to be the most active repurchasing shares over the 2024 SCB cycle (4Q24-3Q25). Aided by potential B3E relief, several Money Centers (C, GS, BAC, WFC) could be relatively more active. And we expect MTB and TFC to lead the Super Regionals. All these banks are likely to repurchase more than 3% of their shares, we estimate. In addition, while not part of this year's public stress test, we also expect FCNCA to be particularly active in repurchasing shares.

## Basel III Endgame

In late July 2023, the Fed, FDIC and OCC published the Basel III endgame NPR (B3E). The regulators introduced a new 'expanded risk-based approach' (ERBA) for banks with over \$100bn in assets. The biggest banks are most impacted by RWA inflation, and the Regionals are hurt by the elimination of AOCI opt-out. The changes are currently expected to start in mid-2025 and be phased in through mid-2028. We estimate this proposal would increase our composite's RWAs by 20% or \$2.0trn, with the median bank up 6%. In addition, the median Category III/IV bank would see a 200bp reduction to CET1 from the elimination of AOCI opt-out (fully phased in, using 1Q24 figures). Looking at our estimated 1Q24 pro forma fully phased-in expanded riskbased approach CET1 ratios shows more than three-quarters of our coverage is currently compliant despite capital requirements increasing by 17%, or \$171bn.

This said, we expect the bank regulators to release a final Basel III endgame, which includes "broad and material" changes. At a mid-May House Financial Services Committee hearing, Fed Vice Chair for Supervision Michael Barr stated “I expect we will have a set of broad, material changes to the proposal”, while FDIC Chair Martin Gruenberg noted “concerns have been raised related to the proposed treatment for residential mortgage exposure, certain tax credit equity investments, trading activities, and banking activities that generate large amounts of fee-based revenues.” The Fed intends to publish an analysis of a Quantitative Impact Study (QIS) and accept comments as part of the proposed rule-making process that will feed into how it develops the final proposal. As such, the final B3E rule might not be a near-term event.

Making adjustments for the aforementioned areas of concern should reduce the expected RWA inflation. We do not, however, anticipate any changes to the expected removal of the AOCI opt-out provision. As a result, expected revision would favor the larger Category I/II banks more than the Category III/IV banks. Still, we expect the drag from the elimination of the AOCI opt-out provision for Category III and IV banks to significantly diminish prior to implementation with the passage of time (25% by the end of 2025). Against this potential backdrop, we would expect RWA inflation for our composite to be closer to 10% or \$1.0trn (down from \$2.0trn) and an increase in capital requirements by 8% or \$81bn (down from \$171bn).

Based on this hypothetical analysis, we expect GS, MS, JPM and C to be the biggest beneficiaries of the B3E rollback, while we would expect several of the regional banks to see very little relief relative to the original proposal. The Trust Banks (BK, NTRS, STT); JPM and WFC among the biggest banks; and MTB, TFC (inclusive of 1Q insurance/security sale) and COF (w/ & w/o DFS) among the Category III/IV banks, look to have the most excess capital (as a % of RWA). KEY, C, GS, CFG and ALLY stand out on the other end. Of note, these figures include AOCI headwinds at 1Q24. We note that we expect these headwinds to dissipate over time, aiding Category III/IV banks. In addition, we look for the banks to take additional mitigating actions (price, structure, mix) once the final rules are known.

## European election becomes a French drama

Following recent unexpected election results in India, Mexico, and South Africa, last weekend's European election outcomes seemed to be largely in line with polls: the European Parliament drifted to the right, as far-right, eurosceptic, and populist parties gained seats, while the centrist Renew, the Greens, and the Left scoring worse than in 2019. This makes the Parliament even more fragmented, implying that building majorities issue by issue will be harder. That said, since current European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen's party, EPP, gained seats and remains the largest party, we expect the new president to come from this group (and this may well be von der Leyen herself), implying that a major shift in policy is not in the ofing.

Instead, the dramatic twist came when, in response to the poor performance of his political group at the expense of the far-right National Rally (RN), French President Emmanuel Macron called for early parliamentary elections, to be held on 30 June and 7 July. Initial polls suggest that a RN ‘cohabitation’ government or a hung parliament are the two most likely outcomes (see Shades of Grey), but the end result is still far from certain. European markets' performance broadly reflected this sudden uncertainty, with the OAT-Bund spread widening by more than 20bp.

Since the announcement, political parties on the left have already agreed to form a coalition (New Popular Front<sup>1</sup> ) and present single candidacies, with Melenchon’s far-left La France insoumise (LFI) obtaining the highest number of constituencies. On the opposite side of the spectrum, the picture is still blurred, with the Reconquete and centre-right les Républicains (LR) parties divided<sup>2</sup> over support for the RN. Before candidate registration closes on 16 June, the

ability of President Macron's coalition to attract new allies, particularly on the centre-left/right, will be crucial.

## Mexico and South Africa diverge

Meanwhile, in Mexico, markets are less concerned about the fiscal implications of the 2 June election than about the possible deterioration of institutions. Namely, the ruling Morena party has now secured a two-thirds constitutional supermajority in both chambers, alongside allies, implying that it has the political power to implement President Andrés Manuel López Obrador's plan to make judges democratically elected, which would make it likelier to favour the incumbent government. This has kept USDMXN, which has depreciated by more than 10% since late May, volatile this week, and as a result, we no longer expect Banxico to cut rates further this year. In contrast, the South African rand has rallied this week on news that the market-friendly DA party will join a coalition with ANC and the Inkatha Freedom Party<sup>3</sup> .

This is likely to eat into Chinese producers' profit margins in Europe, but our analysis suggests they have room to absorb the tarifs. In this context, it is worth remembering that the tarif increases were a European Commission initiative, as European automakers explicitly demanded that this stop not be taken<sup>4</sup> .

## The data are softening

## Soft US CPI brings some good news

In contrast to global politics, the US inflation data this week brought some encouraging news. May core CPI surprised to the downside, easing to 0.16% m/m SA from 0.29% m/m SA in April. This was led by core services, particularly supercore, while deflation in core goods diminished. Combined with the also softer-than-expected PPI and import prices, this translates into core PCE running at 0.08% m/m in May, about 17bp slower than the previous month. We also mark down our December 2024 core CPI forecast by 0.1pp to 3.2% y/y.

That said, though some of this reflects easing in underlying inflation, we think the May print alone overstates the degree of deceleration. In particular, a substantial part of the decline in inflation and the surprise to forecasts came from volatile components, such as transportation services, including airfares, and "other services", including legal. Also, auto insurance accounted for 6bp of the slowing in core CPI, and given this item's long-running strength and the insurance companies' reports of losses, it remains to be seen whether the weakness can be sustained (and the soft auto insurance CPI does not enter PCE). All this means that in June, core CPI is likely to bounce back somewhat (we expect 0.25% m/m) and core PCE will probably round to 0.2% m/m.

We also expect a solid bounce back in retail sales (0.4% m/m) next week, following April's flat reading, including in the control group (0.6% m/m). Similarly, industrial production might accelerate 0.7% m/m.

## UK inflation should slow amid an easing labour market

Next week's UK inflation could also bring some positive news, as we expect further easing in both headline (2.1%, -0.2pp from April) and core CPI (3.5% y/y, -0.4pp from April), with the latter reflecting softer core goods, as well as services. This should be aided by base efects and a moderation in sequential inflation. Specifically, we think April's large upside surprise in services inflation was due primarily to one-of price resets and volatile components, implying that momentum should normalise.

In part, the expected moderation in the UK's underlying inflation should be supported by the continually weakening labour market. Early this week, the REC/KPMG labour market survey showed salary growth decelerating amid a further increase in slack as vacancies fall and labour availability rises. A day later, this was mirrored in the oficial labour market report, with regular private average weekly earnings growing 0.65% m/m, reflecting the increase in the minimum wage, but the hike was much smaller than we had expected (roughly 1% m/m). Similarly, the quantities side of the report showed continued signs of loosening, with the unemployment rate rising 0.1pp to 4.4% and the vacancies/unemployed ratio falling below the pre-pandemic level.

Next week, we forecast the UK composite PMI to ease slightly, driven in equal measure by a slowing in services and a normalisation in manufacturing output. In contrast, we think EA composite PMI will improve a touch further, to 52.4 from 52.2.

## China's low inflation and soft credit growth reflect demand weakness

While softer inflation data are welcome in most developed economies, where inflation is still above target, China continues to face the opposite challenge. This week's May CPI release printed at just 0.3% y/y, unchanged from April and a touch below market expectations, and core inflation eased 0.1pp to 0.6% y/y. In our view, these persistently low inflation prints reflect continuing softness in domestic demand, particularly private consumption, in turn caused by the ongoing deleveraging in the property sector. Taking these dynamics and the price data on board, we revise down our 2025 inflation forecast by 0.8 pp to 1.2%, only somewhat higher than this year's 0.5% y/y.

China's total social financing and new loans data for May both missed market expectations, painting a similar picture of subdued domestic demand. Weak private sector credit demand was reflected in loans to households and corporates both declining on a y/y basis, while only government bond issuance accelerated notably following behind-schedule issuance year-todate.

Next week's additional data for May is unlikely to tell a diferent story. We expect FAI growth to ease (to 4.1% y/y YTD), retail sales growth to edge higher (to 3.5% y/y) but remain below the Q1 average (of 4.7%), and IP growth to moderated (to 6.5%).

## Central banks remain non-committal

## Marginally hawkish Fed leaves the door open for September

Despite the softer CPI data on the morning of the meeting, the FOMC was slightly hawkish this week. As we expected, the Summary of Economic Projections (SEP) showed a median of only one 25bp cut this year, on the back of a 0.2pp upward revision to the Q4/Q4 24 inflation forecast to 2.8% y/y. Importantly, this dot plot result already incorporates the latest CPI release to some extent, since the participants had the ability to change their projections, but seemingly choose not to. In addition, the longer-run dot moved up to 2.75% from 2.55%, formalising the upside in views about the neutral rate already indicated by the 2026 dot. Also, in the press conference, Fed Chair Jerome Powell was cautious about taking too much signal from one inflation data point.

As a consequence, rates gave back about half of the gains made on the earlier CPI data, which neatly encapsulates this week's developments. The inflation release we received this week certainly could be a stepping stone to the first cut in September. Powell left the door open for this when he characterised the SEP inflation projections as somewhat conservative. However, more sustained progress on inflation is required to actually deliver this cut, and the Fed remains in no rush and non-committal, going where the data takes it.

As the median SEP dots now align with our own baseline rate projection, we maintain our call that the FOMC will cut rates only once this year, at the earliest in September, but December is almost as likely. For 2025, like the SEP, we expect the FOMC to cut rates four times.

## BoJ only has a 'plan for a plan', but July hike remains the baseline

The BoJ held rates steady this week, as widely expected, while tweaking its language on bond purchases. Indeed, the latter was the primary focus for market participants, and the MPC disappointed many of them: it only presented a 'plan for a plan' to reduce JGB purchases, but deferring the decision on the details to the next meeting on 30-31 July. This deferral to July in turn raises the question of whether the BoJ would still hike rates in July, as a rate hike combined with a QE tapering announcement could create excessive volatility.

In our view, the July hike remains the baseline. The MPC has made it clear that it would revise the current excessive accommodation if conditions evolve in line with the growth and inflation outlook indicated in the April Outlook Report. Thus far, the data have been evolving largely in line with those expectations. In addition, the BoJ has announced that it would conduct meetings about JGB purchases with market participants, which should make any move on QE less of a surprise.

We now look to the May nationwide CPI, to be published next week, and the summary of opinions from today's meeting (to be released 24 June) to confirm or challenge our baseline.

## No change from the BoE next week, but August cut in sight

Next week's Bank of England meeting could be something of a non-event, as the upcoming election has imposed a blackout on the Monetary Policy Committee's (MPC) communications. It thus prevented the MPC from guiding the market towards a cut ahead of time, and any change in policy or communication about a change in direction could be seen as politically motivated and/or interfering in the political process. Moreover, much of the data since May have come broadly in line with the MPC's forecast, and the important services inflation was a large upside surprise in the April release, implying little urgency to overcome the high bar imposed by the election.

We therefore expect no change in Bank Rate, the vote split, or guidance next week. Beyond then, however, we think the case for cuts is building, and we forecast the easing cycle to begin in August.

Global Outlook: Steady, sticky, slow

Global growth remains steady...

Stepping back from the weekly volatility, the global macro backdrop is calm and largely favourable. Global growth in particular remains steady, and since our last Global Outlook (GO) in March, we have even nudged up our 2024 forecast to 3.1% (from 2.9% in March). This revision was driven by a cyclical recovery in the euro area after two years of stagnation, as well as stronger growth in emerging markets, due to China's bumper Q1 print and other developing economies. We now expect annual growth of 0.7% (from 0.4% in March) for the euro area and 5% (from 4.4% in March) for China in 2024. Our US headline GDP projection for 2024 is slightly lower than it was, now at 2.5% (from2.6%), but this mostly reflects the drag of volatile components on Q1, while we think underlying growth in the US remains robust. For 2025, we now expect global growth at 3.0%, a touch above the 2.9% we forecast in March.

...contributing to stickiness in inflation...

On the flip side, inflation is proving somewhat sticky. The 'easy' gains from the reversal of earlier pandemic and energy supply shocks were largely made in 2023. Now that the baton has been passed to demand and structural factors, further progress is becoming more dificult. Our aggregations suggest that global inflation was 2.6% y/y in Q1, and that is also where we expect it in Q4 and 2024 as a whole (compared with the 2.5% we forecast for the 2024 average in the March GO), before decelerating modestly to 2.3% in 2025 (2.4% in March). That said, the concern about inflation should not be overstated either. The level of inflation between 2% and 3%, even if somewhat elevated, is in the grand scheme of things fairly benign, and there is little indication of an upward trajectory. Combined with robust growth, this is far from a bad environment for markets.

...making monetary policy easing slow

However, it does imply that central banks are likely to be slow to ease monetary policy. For the Fed, we now expect only one 25bp cut in 2024, compared with the three that we projected back in March. Similarly, we have reduced the number of cuts for the ECB and the BoE from five to three. In addition, since our March GO, currency weakness due to rate diferentials vs. the US and the change in structural inflation have allowed the BoJ to abandon its negative interest rate policy and will likely lead it to hike again this July. While this call is unchanged from three

months ago, we now expect one additional BoJ hike, in April 2025. In emerging markets, central banks are broadly much further along the easing cycle than in the advanced economies, but policymakers there have also turned more cautious, in part as a spillover from US monetary policy.
