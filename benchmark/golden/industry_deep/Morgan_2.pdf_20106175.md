## Greater China

• China: The PBOC eases earlier than expected; 50bp RRR cut and 25bp re-lending/rediscount rate cut

• Hong Kong: December CPI inflation fell to 2.4%oya

December merchandise trade activities beat market expectations; FX reserves rose to US\$425.5bn

• Taiwan: December export orders weakened, dragged by slowing tech orders

• December IP weaker-than-expected; tech production softened after earlier bounce

PBOC Governor Pan announced a RRR cut of 50bps (Figure 1), effective from Feb 5 (excluding banks that already have the lowest RRR at 5%). With that, the weighted RRR of the banking system will be lowered from 7.4% to 7%. Meanwhile, the re-lending and rediscount rates to rural and SME sectors will also be lowered, by 25bps from 2% to 1.75%, effective from Jan 25. The RRR cut announcement comes earlier and is of a larger magnitude than expected.

Figure 1: Policy rates and RRR in China
![](images/43060f0f2650a7b2f1225e2d53bda51c09a0b3f8bbb6d0f9778fe76161edc020.jpg)

The RRR cut is estimated to inject long-term liquidity of about 1 trillion yuan into the banking system. We have been flagging credit quality concerns and NIM compression pressure faced by commercial banks, especially for regional banks given their exposure to the property sector and LGFVs, etc. The RRR cut will help lower commercial banks<sup>’</sup> liability cost, mitigating their NIM pressure. It will also facilitate an accommodative liquidity environment, paving the way to support sizable government bonds issuance going forward (over 10 trillion yuan for 2024 based on our estimate).

The 25bp reduction in re-lending and rediscount rates is not a major surprise to us. We have penciled in an important change in this year<sup>’</sup>s monetary policy operation in our forecast, namely expanded use of structural monetary policy instruments. This is echoed by the PBOC<sup>’</sup>s announcement of setting up a new Credit Market Department to support five sectors: 1) technology finance, 2) green finance, 3) inclusive finance, 4) elderly-care finance, and 5) digital finance. This

implies a more essential role of structural monetary policy instruments in the PBOC<sup>’</sup>s policy operation, compared to aggregate quantitative (e.g. universal RRR) or price-based (e.g. policy rate) policy instruments.

In our baseline forecast, we have assumed a 1-trillion yuan package funded by PSL to support public housing and urban village developments, which has been echoed by the 350bn yuan net PSL injection in December. We also look for an expansion of re-lending facility to support debt restructuring as part of local governments<sup>’</sup> hidden debt resolution. These quasi-fiscal measures are targeted at addressing imminent vulnerabilities in the economy and financial system, and play an important role in our relatively constructive view on the Chinese economy in 2024. The announcement of further lowering the re-lending and rediscount rates will further lower the funding cost for relevant sectors, including SMEs and rural development.

Following the announcement, we remove our 2Q RRR cut forecast, but retain the 25bp RRR cut forecast for 4Q. This is not necessarily a high conviction call as the PBOC could expand medium-term liquidity injection through MLF operation. On the policy rate front, we maintain our forecast for two rate cuts this year, 10bp each in 1Q and 3Q. Instead of more aggressive rate cuts anticipated by the market, we hold the view that the PBOC will continue to face multiple tradeoffs in its policy rate decisions. Supportive factors include 1) deflation pressure, 2) reducing debt repayment burden, 3) mitigated pressure from currency depreciation and capital outflows, and 4) structural decline in natural interest rates, while constraints come from 1) limited room for monetary easing, 2) constraints in the banking sector (NIM compression and capital adequacy pressure) and 3) debate on policy effectiveness. At the press conference, Governor Pan mentioned the need to maintain a reasonable real interest rate level in a forward-looking manner, to match the demand of supporting potential economic growth.

We think the most macro and growth relevant indicator is TSF growth. Governor Pan reiterated that TSF and money supply growth should be in line with economic growth and inflation targets. We maintain our forecast for stable TSF growth at 9.5% this year, leaving credit impulse averaging at 5.0%pts (flat as 2023).

## Hong Kong: CPI inflation fell

Hong Kong<sup>’</sup>s headline CPI inflation fell to 2.4%oya in December, compared to 2.6%oya in November (Figure 2). Seasonally adjusted, headline CPI rose 0.1%m/m sa in December, adding to the 0.1%m/m sa gain in November. Excluding food and energy, core CPI grew 2.8%oya (or +0.1%m/m sa). For 2023 as a whole, headline CPI inflation

was 2.1%yoy, while core CPI inflation was 1.5%yoy.
Figure 2: Hong Kong contribution to headline CPI (%oya)
![](images/613e4d70b3d066bf886febe6bc1ee08041ce15b28bce66e8e59767fc79fbe7a5.jpg)

Looking ahead, we maintain our view that overall inflation in HK should stay moderate in the near term. More than 45% of Hong Kong<sup>’</sup>s merchandise imports are from mainland China. Mainland China<sup>’</sup>s CPI deflation continued in December, though narrowed to -0.3%oya from November<sup>’</sup>s -0.5%oya. Domestically, housing prices<sup>’</sup> contribution to CPi inflation rose significantly in the past three months. Though property prices, as indicated by the Centa City leading Index, have been declining in the past few month, rental prices are on the rise. On the economic outlook, total economy PMI rose 1.2pt to 50.2 in December, adding to the 1.1pt gain in November. On cross-border mobility, the imbalance between inbound and outbound travellers<sup>’</sup> numbers may further weigh on consumption and employment in tourism-related sectors in the near term, which alleviates HK domestic inflation pressure. We expect 2024 full-year CPI inflation to average at 2.1%yoy.

## Exports beat expectations

Hong Kong<sup>’</sup>s December merchandise trade activities came in above market expectations (Figure 3). Both exports and imports rose double-digit in oya terms on a low base. Exports rose 11.0%oya to HK\$386.4bn, following a 7.4%oya rise in November. Seasonally adjusted, we estimate the city<sup>’</sup>s exports rose 0.1%m/m sa in December, after falling 1.6%m/m sa in November. Meanwhile, imports rose 11.6%oya to HK\$446.3bn. Seasonally adjusted, we estimate the city<sup>’</sup>s imports rose 1.7%m/m sa in December, reversing the 0.6%m/ m sa loss in November. The monthly trade deficit widened to HK\$59.9bn in December (vs. HK\$27.9bn in November). For full-year 2023, merchandise exports fell 7.8%yoy, while imports fell 5.7%yoy.

FX reserves have fallen from the peak level of US\$449bn in November 2021. The official FX reserves of HK were recorded at US\$425.5bn in December, compared with US\$424.6bn in November.

## Taiwan: export orders weakened

Taiwan<sup>’</sup>s December export orders came in significantly weaker than expected. Seasonally-adjusted, we estimate that export orders plunged 13.5% m/m sa in December, adding to the decline of 7.8% m/m sa in November. The weakness in December export orders was led by tech orders (Figure 4), which fell 17.6% m/m sa, with broad-based weakness across orders for electronics products, as well as orders for information and communication products. Non-tech orders stayed on the soft side, falling 2.9% m/m sa in December (vs. -1.2% m/ m sa in November). By source of demand, the weakness in December orders seems to be rather broad-based, including a decline in orders from the US (-14.2% m/m sa), Europe (-9.4% m/m sa), China/HK SAR (-10.8% mm sa), and the ASEAN region (-23.4% m/m sa).

Figure 4: Taiwan export orders (level)
![](images/dc0a50a3612b03f0b59baafec39399c3c6f2ffcdd7f3bca362bc31865e55925d.jpg)

Taiwan<sup>’</sup>s tech exports have outperformed tech orders notably since early 2023. That likely reflects the boost in demand for AI-related products, mostly produced in manufacturing plants in Taiwan. The overseas production ratio of tech orders has trended down in recent quarters. Going forward, we look for moderate growth in tech exports, including a structural boost of AI-related products, HPC-related demand, and improving consumer demand going into 2024, while the tech sector<sup>’</sup>s destocking pressure may begin to ease. The significant decline in December export orders, with notable, broad-based softening in tech orders, suggests the growth outlook for overall tech demand may still be rather bumpy in the near term.

## IP weaker-than-expected

Taiwan<sup>’</sup>s December IP fell 4.0%oya. Seasonally adjusted, we estimate that IP fell by 4.2% m/m sa in December, while the underlying trend still shows positive growth at 15.7% 3m/3m saar. Further breakdown shows tech-related manufacturing IP fell 4.5% m/m sa in December (Figure 5), following the decline of 3.8% m/m sa in November and the jump of 10.3% m/m sa in October. Non-tech manufacturing IP also fell 3.0% m/m sa in December. Regarding the inventory cycle, following aggressive inventory de-stocking by the Taiwan tech manufacturers since early 2023, manufacturing (tech) inventory has started to turn up lately, as tech inventory rose 4.9% m/m sa in November (or up 37.7% 3m/3m saar).

Figure 5: Taiwan tech and nontech manufacturing IP (level) Index, 2011=100, sa, 3mma
![](images/d89d8a3559dcf832e8cd1eeb86e159eafd04ea12d15643fd4e6ba62192ca9715.jpg)

On the domestic front, while consumer spending has shown solid post-pandemic recovery in recent quarters, retail spending seems to have moderated lately (Figure 6): retail sales fell 1.9% m/m sa in December, with the sequential trend down 3.4% 3m/3m saar. Regarding the labor market, following decent growth in earlier 2023, total employment has eased in recent months, staying flat (in %m/m sa terms) in October-December. Looking ahead, the recent easing in employment growth, and hence household income, will likely restrain the pace of domestic demand recovery going into 2024.
