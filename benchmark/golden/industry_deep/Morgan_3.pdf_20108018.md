## Market commentary

As expected, this week<sup>’</sup>s key economic data proved more pivotal for the markets this week, while policy actions were more of a sideshow. Most notably, Friday<sup>’</sup>s payrolls report showed nonfarm employment growth eased to 175K in April, softer than expectations, and below the 269k average gain over the prior three months. Average workweek ticked down to 34.3 hours, and the unemployment rate moved slightly higher to 3.9% from 3.8% in the prior month. Meanwhile, average hourly earnings increased only 0.2% in April, and with downward revisions, the year-ago figure cooled off to 3.9%. Overall, these figures are a welcome sign that the labor market is continuing to come into better balance and that policy is restrictive enough to cool off labor demand (see A welcome cooling in the April jobs report, M.

Feroli, 5/3/24). Furthermore, it counteracted some of the strength we have seen with respect to 1Q24 data, including the employment cost index (ECI), which came in above expectations, increasing by 1.2%. The year-ago figure was unchanged compared to 4Q23 and lower than the local highs, but still higher than pre-pandemic norms (see US: ECI is above expectations, rising 1.2% in 1Q, D. Silver, 4/30/24). In response, 2y Treasury yields moved in a wide range, from 4.80% to 5.04% peak to trough, and closed lower by 20bp to 4.80% week-overweek. By week<sup>’</sup>s end, OIS forward markets also moved more dovish, and are now pricing the first full cut by November, versus December last week (Figure 1). Our economist are sticking with their call for a first ease in July.

Turning to the Fed, as widely expected, the FOMC left the fed funds rate unchanged at 5.25-5.50% on Wednesday. The post-meeting statement alluded to stalling progress, adjusting its language regarding the balance of risks between employment and inflation and stating that “in recent months, there has been a lack of further progress toward the Committee<sup>’</sup>s 2 percent inflation objective.<sup>”</sup> At the press conference, Chair Powell reiterated that recent inflation prints have been higher than expected and that it will likely take longer than previously expected to gain the confidence necessary to begin easing policy.

Nevertheless, the press conference wasn<sup>’</sup>t overly hawkish relative to what markets expected, with Chair Powell reiterating the belief that policy is “well-positioned to address different paths that the economy might take,<sup>”</sup> laying out three scenarios that were suggestive of rates either being on hold or being cut (i.e., no hikes).

With regard to the Fed<sup>’</sup>s balance sheet, the Committee reduced the cap on the monthly decline in Treasury holdings from \$60bn/mo to \$25bn/mo, effective June 1. This decision was not a big surprise as it had been flagged at the last meeting, though the more-than-half reduction was slightly larger than expected (see Hikes aren<sup>’</sup>t in the gameplan, M. Feroli, 5/1/24).

Away from Fed policy, Treasury also announced some minor changes to net T-bill issuance as part of its May quarterly refunding. Notably, it plans on adjusting short-dated bill auction sizes (e.g., 4-, 6-, and 8-week bills) over the coming months to reflect its cash needs. In particular, short-dated bill auction sizes will increase in the coming days to meet their oneweek cash needs around the end of May, followed by modest reductions in early to mid-June in anticipation of the corporate tax date, and then rise again in July to auction levels at or near the highs from February and March. Additionally, Treasury will transition the 6-week

CMB to a benchmark status over the coming quarters and expects to still maintain the Thursday settlement and maturity cycle after it becomes a benchmark bill. Details of timing and first benchmark auction will be announced at an upcoming refunding.

Given the higher-than-expected financing estimates Treasury released on Monday, our Treasury strategists also revised up their net issuance forecast for the full year, with the increased funding needs to be met by T-bills. Our strategists now estimate a \$1,898bn increase in privately-held coupons and a \$332bn increase in privately-held bills this year. Importantly, these forecasts do not account for the buyback program, which, based on the details of its buyback announcement with the first liquidity support operation scheduled for May 29, does not suggest the program to be large in size to start. There was no mention of cash management buybacks this quarter (see A lot to digest, but little has changed, J. Barry, 5/1/24).

Overall, with this week<sup>’</sup>s data in hand and the April/May FOMC meeting behind us, the state of play in the money markets remains largely the same. MMF balances will likely remain elevated, which should keep any downward pressure on RRP resulting from near-term bill supply at bay. Said another way, we do not anticipate material drainage to the ON RRP facility in the near term even as T-bill supply fluctuates in the coming months. Furthermore, with monthly Treasury caps now lowered as the Fed begins QT taper, this should be supportive of reduced pressures in the funding markets, especially surrounding month-ends/quarterends, on the margin. To that end, we anticipate ON RRP balances to remain above \$300bn and reserves above \$3tn for the remainder of this year (Figure 2We anticp ONRPbls orm v\$30 d fh  y , also see Interest Rate Derivatives).

## MMF reform: not the same as 2016

The below section is excerpted from our latest special note, MMF reform spotlight.

With the deadline to implement mandatory liquidity fees for institutional prime MMFs rapidly approaching (in October), the potential impact this change could have on the CP/CD market has come into greater focus. Recall that the last time institutional prime funds went through a structural change was in 2016 (on the back of the 2014 MMF reforms). In the months leading up to the effective date, numerous funds announced they were converting their prime funds to government funds. Furthermore, among those that didn<sup>’</sup>t convert, prime funds dramatically shortened the duration of their portfolios in order to build liquidity to meet outflows. Ultimately, \~\$1tn left the institutional prime fund market, and 3m bank CP/ CD spreads to OIS widened by as much as 32bp. Certainly, we can draw parallels in terms of how this upcoming round of MMF reforms could unfold. However, a closer look at the market structure today reveals there are more differences than similarities that we believe will ultimately translate to a much more benign impact on the CP/CD market, all else equal.

For one, the institutional prime market has shrunk significantly in size, while the retail prime market has grown in prominence. Institutional prime funds make up only 45% of the prime MMF market today, versus over 70% in 2016 (Figure 3). The CP buyer base is also much more diversified now, with MMFs comprising less than 25% of the market. Even if prime fund exposure to the CP market were to decrease slightly, the remaining buyer base could step in to absorb lost capacity. Morever, institutional prime funds already devote a significant percentage of their portfolios to rates as opposed to credit. As of March-end, institutional prime funds devoted 59% and 29% of their portfolios to rates and CP/CDs, respectively. During 2016, their allocations to rates and CP/CDs were 25% and 60%, respectively. And among the prime funds that have announced their liquidation or conversion to government funds, exposure to CP/CDs is small, totaling just \$33bn. Not to mention, weekly liquid assets of institutional prime MMFs are already running above 70% of portfolios, not too far from their peak in 2016.

Taken together, we see a much more benign impact on CP/CD spreads as a result of MMF reform, all else equal. That<sup>’</sup>s not to say the risk of wider spreads could not transpire in the coming months, but we believe they will be driven by other factors. To be sure, 6m SOFR FRN spreads have widened 5-10bp from their local tights in early March as liquidity investors turned more defensive heading into quarter-end and tax season. There might be room to widen further as Fed policy uncertainty remains elevated and as the deadline for MMF reform approaches. But the scale of the impact as a result of reforms will not be anywhere close to what we saw in 2016, for reasons noted above (and expanded upon here). Moreover, the technical backdrop continues to be very supportive of spreads, with liquidity remaining ample in the front end.
