## All eyes on Washington

All eyes will undeniably be on Washington next week, as we head towards next week's FOMC meeting as well as the Treasury refunding announcement. But both events are likely to result in outcomes that are by now well anticipated by markets. On the FOMC front, we expect the Fed to hold the funds rate unchanged and shift from a previously held tightening bias to a neutral bias, while also expressing a willingness to be patient in not rushing to cut rates (see FOMC Preview, M. Feroli, 1/26/2024). With respect to the refunding announcement, our Treasury strategists look for Treasury to announce a final round of increases to coupon auction sizes, similar to November and consistent with Treasury's guidance at that time. For Treasury's financing estimates, we estimate \$855bn in private borrowing needs for the Jan-March quarter and \$263bn for the next quarter, although it is likely that Treasury's estimate for the latter will be larger given that they are unlikely to assume a taper-down of

QT as we do (see February refunding preview: Do no harm, J. Barry et al., 1/24/2024). Also important, a formal buyback announcement is possible next week although the timing of the announcement as well as program commencement remains unclear.

Separately, the Fed made an adjustment to its BTFP program this week, likely motivated by the rising use of the facility as merely a source of attractive funding. Since the BTFP offers funding at term OIS plus 10bp, a bank that draws on this facility can obtain 1Y term funding (prepayable without penalty) at about 50bp below IOR (Figure 1). Thus, a bank drawing from this facility and holding the cash as Reserve funds can earn a riskless \~50bp spread, since the trade can be terminated if the Fed were to cut rates by over 50bp. Of course, none of this is new, and most banks have generally refrained from using this emergency funding facility opportunistically. Nevertheless, usage has risen by enough to prompt the Fed to modify the terms of the facility to floor the rate at IOR on the day the funds are drawn. This erases the arbitrage that was previously permitted.

This change has implications for the Fed's balance sheet. BTFP usage has at least partly been contributing to the elevated level of Reserves, and at a minimum this should slow the rate of growth of BTFP (and thus Reserves) over the remaining life of the program. But the overall impact of these changes could be somewhat larger. This week's tweaks to the program is a reminder of the facility's original purpose, and could lead opportunistic borrowers to voluntarily and proactively scale back usage, thereby prompting a larger drop in usage. The actual scale of reduction in usage remains to be seen, but on the margin we believe it is prudent to project a faster ramp-down of BTFP balances. This is reflected in our revised forecast for the Fed's balance sheet size and liabilities mix, which additionally incorporates a true-up to spot balances (Figure 2). This revised projection assumes that 70% of BTFP balances will be paid down by April, and also assumes that QT will end when domestic O/N RRP balances fall to below \$300bn. This latter assumption reflects the idea, hinted at by Dallas Fed President Logan, that a minimum amount of O/N RRP balances may be necessary in the current context for smooth functioning of repo markets. Under these assumptions, and thanks to already lower spot RRP balances, QT could come to an end as early as September.

In the front end, however, swap spreads are looking too wide, and will likely be biased narrower going forward. As seen in Figure 4, maturity matched swap spreads in the 2-year sector remain wide to fair value. Moreover, three important drivers in our fair value model (which we have discussed in detail in our Interest Rate Derivatives 2024 Outlook) for swap spreads in this sector point to lower fair value going forward - the 2s/5s UST curve (which our Treasury strategists expect to flatten), RRP balances (which we expect to decline), and 1Yx1Y implied volatility (which we expect to rise from here) all point to narrower front end spreads. And last but not least, to the extent that BTFP's shrinkage results in replacement funding demands, this could be a source of upward pressure on financing rates and narrowing pressure on swap spreads. The main offsetting risk is 5Y swap spreads, which we expect to continue to drift wider - given a partial beta of about 0.6, a widening in swap spreads in the 5Y sector would pressure front end spreads wider. Therefore, to position for narrower front end spreads in a way that hedges this risk, we recommend initiating exposure to 1:0.6 weighted 2s/5s maturity matched swap spread curve steepeners (see Trade recommendations).

## Swap yield curve

As we noted above, next week's FOMC meeting is likely to reaffirm the Fed's on-hold stance as well as its patience in determining the timing of any rate cuts, especially in the face of economic data that continues to be robust. As a result, we continue to expect forward rates (which are pricing in more accelerated fed easing) to roll up towards spot rates - in other words, we continue to favor constructing positive carry trades on the yield curve.

Ideally, such trades will have three desirable features. First, they should of course offer attractive carry. Second, this carry should be attractive in comparison to the volatility of the overall trade - i.e., the carry should be attractive relative to the risk. And third, when possible, relative value considerations should also line up in favor of the trade. One such trade that has all these three elements is a package comprised of a 3M forward (near-spot) 2s/5s swap curve flattener, paired with a weighted amount of a 2Y forward 2s/5s steepener. Carry on this trade is attractive - the spot 2s/5s flattener offers \~16bp of slide over 3 months, while the 2Y forward 2s/5s steepener has flat carry. Thus, using a 40% risk weight on the spot flattener (reflecting an empirical beta between weekly changes in each curve over the past six months), the package still offers nearly 6.5bp of carry over 3 months. This is attractive in comparison to risk - as seen in Figure 5, the weighted yield spread corresponding to such a trade has traded in a fairly tight range over the past six months. From a relative value standpoint, valuations are attractive - relative to projected fair values from our swap yield curve models (see Interest Rate Derivatives 2024 Outlook for details), we see the forward curve as too flat, while the spot curve is considerably steep (Figure 6) . Last but not least, this trade also has right-way macro risk exposure, in our view. Our Treasury strategists look for a flatter 2s/5s curve, while typical curve evolution patterns around Fed easing would point to a steepening bias in forward curves. Therefore, given these supportive factors, we recommend initiating 2Y forward 2s/5s swap curve steepeners paired with 40% risk in 3M forward 2s/5s flatteners (see Trade recommendations).

In longer tails, one currently attractive way to position for a long gamma view is to take advantage of the cheapness of short expiries versus longer expiries in 10-year tails. For instance, the differential between 6Mx10Y and 1Yx10Y swaption implied volatility (with a 1.1 weight on the latter to account for the slightly directional nature of the vol differential with respect to overall levels of volatility) appears to have sharply cheapened recently to multi-year lows (Figure 11). Thus, we see this as attractive and recommend overweighting 6Mx10Y swaption straddles versus 110% of the vega risk in 1Yx10Y swaption straddles (see Trade recommendations).
