# China Economic Perspectives China by the Numbers (March 2024)

## Our guide to Chinese monthly data

What the numbers are, what they mean, and the outlook going forward.

## Jan-Feb economic data better than expected, but growth headwinds persist

Jan-Feb IP, FAI and export growth came in better than expected and also improved a bit from last December. Retail sales growth was largely in line with expectations. NBS continued to revise down 2023 level base for FAI and property activities, which may also contribute a bit to the positive surprises. On seasonally adjusted basis, we estimate that sequential growth of real retail sales and IP picked up from December pace, while volume of property sales and new starts recovered from December and Q4’s low reading. However, high frequency data showed deeper y/y decline of property sales and weaker-than-normal construction work resumption post-CNY holidays, suggesting weak growth momentum in March (see Jan-Feb data comment).

## Policy support announced at and following the NPC meeting remains modest

At the NPC meeting, the government set an ambitious growth target of “around 5%” for this year, but announced policy support is modest, including around 1% of GDP expansion of augmented fiscal deficit and accommodative monetary & credit policy but no aggressive easing. The government called for continued differentiated property policies easing, equal credit access for non-SOE developers, more support for social housing and urban village renovation, and vowed to support the private sector and push for more structural policies to facilitate structural transition. After the NPC meeting, China released an action plan to boost equipment upgrades and old-for-new “trade-in” of consumer goods, but with limited direct fiscal stimulus.

## More policy stimulus is needed

Our baseline forecast expects PBC to inject another RMB 300-500bn PSL in the rest of 2024 to support social housing and urban village renovation, mainly in construction and investment, and more modest property policy easing. We see further progress of addressing LGFV debt issues, while LGFV financing will likely be restricted, especially for new investment projects in 12 high-risk provinces. Given the lingering growth headwinds, we think more policy stimulus is needed to stabilize growth, especially for the property sector (echoing the State Council's meeting last week), including stronger support for developer financing, more coverage of “white-list” scheme to ensure delivery of stalled projects, more direct purchase of existing home inventory by local governments, and greater fiscal support for consumption.

## Q2 is key for the expected recovery in H2

Our baseline forecast expects Q1 real GDP to slow to 4.1%y/y on a high base from 5.2%y/y in Q4 2023. The stronger-than-expected Jan-Feb data brings upside risk to our baseline projection. Looking forward, exports may improve further on the upswing of the global tech cycle and resilient US economy, while infrastructure investment growth is not expected to accelerate. We expect property sales and new starts to bottom in Q2, which is key to underpin household confidence and consumption. Overall, given the modest policy support so far and continued property downturn, we maintain our baseline GDP growth forecast of 4.6% in 2024. Following the large swing of RMB exchange rate in the past week, we think that RMB faces continued challenges in H1, but the expected narrowing US-China yield spread and growth stabilization in China could lead to a slightly stronger RMB in H2.

## UBS Activity Indicators

 UBS Physical Activity Index (3mma) softened modestly to 6.7%y/y in February, vs 9.4%y/y in December last year, with industrial activities, transportation activities, construction activities and power generation all decelerated from December. This was partly due to last year's high base, especially for transportation and construction.

 UBS Expenditure index edged up in February, primarily driven by stronger net exports, while consumption and investment both softened on 3mma basis compared with December last year.

Slower y/y growth in March than Jan-Feb, partly on higher base. Our high frequency data tracker shows that property sales remained in sharp contraction, weakening further to -54%y/y in the first 24 days of March on a high base, vs - 40%y/y in Jan-Feb and -10%y/y in Dec. Steel production growth also weakened to -5.2%y/y in the first 10 days, partly due to a high base. Auto retail sales softened a bit to 13%y/y in the first 17 days from 18.5%y/y in Jan-Feb. The FTL traffic index slid to -12.7%y/y in the first 24 days from 5.6%y/y in Jan-Feb. Port cargo throughput and container throughput both softened to 1.1%y/y and 5.1%y/y in the first 24 days on a high base, vs 3.8%y/y and 9.8%y/y in Jan-Feb, respectively. Subway passenger turnover remained elevated, with growth moderating to 7.7%y/y in the first 23 days from 20%y/y in Jan-Feb, due to a fading low base effect. Nonetheless, in the 2nd 10 days of March, most high frequency indicators saw less bad y/y growth than in the 1st 10 days (see UBS China Daily Activity Tracker).

## Business Indicators

February NBS manufacturing PMI edged down by 0.1ppt to 49.1, partly due to CNY distortion. New orders stayed at 49 while production index dropped by 1.5ppt to 49.8. New export orders dropped by 0.9ppt to 46.3, while import index edged down by 0.3ppt to 46.4. Raw material inventory edged down, finished goods weakened, while quantity of purchase slid. Input price edged down by 0.3ppt to 50.1, while output price improved by 1.1ppt to 48.1, echoing lingering deflation headwinds. Employment index edged down by 0.1ppt to 47.5, reflecting continued pressures on the labor market. Meanwhile, Caixin manufacturing PMI edged up by 0.1ppt to 50.9 in February, with slightly better output index and new order, and robust new export order index. The underlying manufacturing momentum may have been weaker than the headline readings in both NBS and Caixin PMIs, as the subindex of "supplier delivery time" declined, which is an inverse indicator suggesting longer-time of delivery partly due to CNY holiday distortion and contributes positively to the headline PMIs.

NBS non-manufacturing PMI edged up further by 0.7ppt to 51.4 in February. Services PMI improved by 0.9ppt to 51. Construction PMI edged down by 0.4ppt to 53.5, reflecting decelerating pace of construction activities, partly due to cold winter season and CNY holiday distortion. By sector, PMIs of outgoing and consumption related sectors, i.e., retail sales, railway and road transport, catering, environment and utilities all recovered to >53. In particular, PMIs of airline transport, monetary financial services, and cultural/sports/entertainment all improved to >60. On the other hand, PMIs of property and household services both stayed below 50, consistent with subdued property activities in the past month.

## Property

Property sales declined more y/y, but with a slight sequential rebound. Jan-Feb property sales declined by -20.5%y/y from -13%y/y in December, while new starts contracted by -29.7%y/y (vs -10%y/y previously), both weighed down by a high base. After seasonal adjustment, property sales and new starts volume were both slightly better than Dec/Q4 readings. Real estate investment declined by - 9%y/y, vs -12.5% previously. Developers’ source of funds remained in deep contraction of -24%y/y in Jan-Feb (vs -20%y/y in Jan-Feb). Compared to the official monthly data, high frequency data painted a much weaker picture, with property sales in major cities declining by -40%y/y in Jan-Feb and -54%y/y in the first 24 days of March. NBS continued to revise down 2023 level base of property activities in 2024, which may explain some of the discrepancy between official and high-frequency property data.

Expect property sales volume to stabilize at low levels in the coming months; downside risks remain. The current sharp property downturn was driven by a combination of weakening fundamentals, previous policy tightening, and sliding home purchase intention. The government started to ease policies in Q4 2021, and more notably after July 2023 Politburo meeting. We expect property activities to stop falling on a seasonally adjusted sequential basis within the next few months, with the help of additional property policy easing and improvement of developer financing. On top of the RMB 500bn PSL already injected in Dec-Jan, we expect another RMB 300-500bn PSL in the rest of 2024 to support social housing and urban village renovation, mainly in construction and investment. We believe the government needs to ease property related policy further, including boosting developer financing and purchasing existing home inventory directly, though these may need to be triggered by more bad data and credit events. Overall, we expect housing starts and sales to improve modestly in H2 2024, leading to a smaller fall in property sales/starts/investment, by 5%/10%/5%, respectively, vs -8.5%/-20.4%/-9.6% in 2023.

## Fixed Asset Investment

Overall FAI growth edged up and beat expectation in Jan-Feb. Infrastructure FAI growth stayed robust at 9%y/y in Jan-Feb from 10.7%y/y in Dec. That said, LGFV financing has been restricted and local government land sales revenue was flattish Jan-Feb, partly weighing on infrastructure FAI and related construction materials prices. Meanwhile, manufacturing investment growth strengthened surprisingly to 9.4%y/y from 8.2% previously. Further considering slightly smaller decline in property investment, overall FAI growth edged up to 4.2%y/y from 4% in December, better than expected. The NBS revised down the absolute FAI level in Jan-Feb 2023 significantly by around 9%, which should have explained a bit the upside surprise in FAI growth.

Expect both infrastructure and manufacturing growth to slow in 2024. The central government plans to provide more explicit support to infrastructure FAI, with RMB 1trn of special CGB and RMB 3.9trn of special LG bonds in 2024. However, LGFV financing will likely be restricted, especially for new investment projects in 12 high-risk provinces, which means infrastructure investment growth is unlikely to accelerate. We expect full year growth slowing to 5-6% in 2024 (vs 8.2% in 2023). We see more growth potential in new energy investment, hydraulic & water projects, city-cluster transport networks and public utility and facilities. In the manufacturing sector, FAI in the sectors along the property supply chain will likely be held back by weak demand and profits, although FAI related to the green transition should stay robust while FAI along the tech supply chain could improve. Overall, we see manufacturing investment growing by 5-6% in 2024E (6.5% in 2023). See more in our 2024-25 outlook.

## Industrial Production

Industrial production growth held up at 7%y/y in Jan-Feb (vs 6.8% in December), stronger than expected (BBG consensus 5.2%). The official seasonally adjusted IP level increased by about 1%m/m in Jan-Feb, following 0.5%m/m in December. Higher base last year weighed on y/y value added growth of auto (9.8%y/y, vs 20% in December), special purpose equipment (2%, vs 3.6% previously), and electric machinery (4.6%, vs 10.1%y/y in Dec). On the other hand, upward tech cycle and a low base boosted VA growth of communication equipment (14.6%, vs 9.6% previously). Meanwhile, VA of ferrous and non-metal minerals improved, steel production growth normalized from the sharp decline in December, while cement production remained weak. Industry data indicates that post-CNY construction work resumption was weaker than the seasonal pattern, which likely depressed demand for related construction materials.

 China's inventory growth edged up to 2.4%y/y in nominal terms (vs 2.1%y/ y previously) in February, while softened to 4%y/y in real terms (vs 4.4% previously). Industrial inventory to sales ratio (seasonally adjusted, 6mma) has risen notably since 2022, now still well above the historical average, which means it takes a few more months to destock.

## Consumption

Retail sales growth cooled to 5.5%y/y in Jan-Feb on a high base from 7.4% in December, largely in line with expectation. 2024 CNY holidays saw strong release of pent-up demand, evident by CNY traffic and tourism activities. We estimate that sequential growth of seasonally adjusted real retail sales improved a bit from December pace. Nonetheless, due to fading base effect, y/y sales growth of going-out related products (cosmetics, clothing, sports) softened, and catering sales growth decelerated to 12.5%y/y (vs 30%y/y in Dec). Similarly, service retail sales growth cooled to 12.3%y/y (vs 20% in 2023). On the other hand, sales of property related products (construction materials, furniture, household appliances) improved from December, despite a higher base. Auto sales picked up to 8.7%y/y on a low base and price discount promotion (from 4%y/y previously).

Consumption recovery should continue at a slightly slower pace in 2024. Strong consumption rebound in Q1 2023 has been slowing down since Q2, due to low consumer confidence, cautious hiring and slow labor market improvement, and continued property downturn. In real terms, we estimate that retail sale levels bottomed in July 23 and edged up since August, albeit only slowly. In 2024, the expected stabilization in property activities should prevent household confidence from sliding down further. That will help to support continued post-Covid consumption recovery along with the economy and labor market, though at a slower pace. Q4 excess saving rate increased again following a short-lived drop in Q3, suggesting cautious sentiment continued. Given lingering labor market weakness, soft income growth and subdued consumer confidence, we expect any release of excess savings in 2024 to be small and gradual. We expect annual real consumption (GDP basis) to slow to 5.5% in 2024 from 6.4% in 2023.

## Inflation

CPI rebounded notably to 0.7%y/y in February from -0.8%y/y previously, thanks to a low base due to later CNY timing in 2024 than 2023. Food price saw a much smaller decline of -0.9%y/y (vs -5.9% previously) on seasonal demand and extreme weather. Non-food price also increased by 0.5%m/m, in particular with stronger touring and outgoing price and fuel prices. Core CPI jumped to 1.2%y/y from 0.4% before.

 PPI edged down to -2.7%y/y in February as we expected (vs -2.5%y/y in Jan), and declined by another -0.2%m/m sequentially. Investment goods price within PPI declined further, while consumer goods price saw narrowed decline. The weaker PPI does not bode well for core CPI ahead. We continue to expect PPI to turn y/y positive growth only in Q3 and record a full year decline of around 1% in 2024.

CPI inflation likely at the lower level of the 0.5-0.8% range in 2024. Our baseline forecast assumes pork prices to bottom in the coming months and core CPI to stay stable thanks to continued modest consumption recovery. Nonetheless, we see 2024 full year CPI forecast likely coming in at the lower level of the 0.5- 0.8% range, given the year-to-date weakness, still soft consumption recovery, and excess capacity issues in some sectors. PPI inflation may only turn positive y/y in Q3, averaging a full year decline of around 1%. However, if the property downturn is deeper and longer than currently expected, deflationary risk will rise as a result of falling in raw materials prices, weaker wage growth and consumption, and increase in excess capacity.

## Money & Credit

Credit growth slid by 0.5ppt to 9.0-9.1%y/y in February. Headline new RMB loans softened to RMB 1.45trn from the record high reading of RMB 4.92trn in January (RMB -360bn y/y). However, loans to non-bank financial institutions increased by RMB 405bn (+RMB 387bn y/y), while new bank loans to the real economy (under TSF) weakened sharply to only RMB 980bn (-RMB 839bn). Household loans contracted by -RMB 591bn, while corporate loans softened to RMB 1.57trn, the latter mainly dragged down by bill financing. New TSF recorded RMB 1.56trn in February, about RMB 1.6trn lower than a year ago. New TSF missed sharply due to weak bank loans and shadow credit. Official TSF credit growth and our adjusted TSF growth (excluding equity financing) both slid notably by 0.5ppt to 9.0-9.1%y/y, partly on base effect. Credit impulse turned to a negative reading of -0.8% of GDP following a short-lived expansion in Dec-Jan. See more in our credit data comment.

Credit growth to edge up in the rest of 2024, with some downside risk. In the coming months, we expect property sales and mortgage loans to stabilize gradually, and corporate credit demand to improve later in the year as growth momentum stabilizes, leading to a small y/y recovery of bank loans in the rest of the year. Government bond issuance may stay strong in 2024 on the back of new issuance of the special CGB (RMB 1trn) and slightly larger quota of special LG bonds than last year (RMB 3.9trn), and new batch of special refinancing LG bonds. The latter may swap some outstanding bank loans and/or shadow credit, however. As such, we expect credit growth to stay soft in March, edge up in Q2, hold up in Q3 and decelerate lightly in Q4, ending full year 2024 at 9.5%y/y. That said, given the significant weakness in February and still subdued property activities, our credit growth forecast faces some downside risks.

## Base Money & Liquidity Operations

 Base money growth slid by 1ppt to 6.9%y/y in February (3mma) from December last year, after adjusting for RRR cuts (e.g. Jan 2020, July/Dec 2021, Apr/ Dec 2022, Mar/Sep 2023, Feb 2024 and various targeted RRR cuts) and changes from other liquidity facilities.

The PBC cut RRR by 50bps in Feb, keeping the interbank liquidity ample. The PBC injected liquidity through RMB 216bn of MLF and RMB 150bn of PSL in January. In February, while the PBC withdrew over RMB 1trn liquidity through OMO and kept outstanding MLF largely stable (RMB +1bn injected), the Feb RRR cut kept general liquidity conditions stable. This 50bps RRR cut (on Feb 5) brought down the the overall weighted average RRR for financial institutions to 7.0% from 7.4% and released base money liquidity of around RMB 1trn, helping to keep overall liquidity ample in light of seasonal liquidity demand ahead of CNY holiday and large government bond issuance in the pipeline (see RRR cut comment).

 Expect continued accommodative monetary and credit policy, but no aggressive easing. We expect another small 25bps RRR cut in the rest of 2024, together with other liquidity facilities, to accommodate the expected large bond issuance. In addition, our baseline assumes PBC to inject another RMB 300-500bn PSL this year to support social housing and urban village renovation.

## Interest Rates & Bond Market

 PBC last cut REPO rate in August. Interbank rates typically move up near month-ends on seasonal liquidity demand and edge down at the start of the following month. PBC cut REPO rate by 20bps in 2023 and kept it unchanged in 2024. DR007 averaged 1.94% in 2023 and hovered around 1.8-1.9% lately.

Yields edged down as property downturn deepened. 10-year CGB yield trended down since Q2 2023 due to subdued growth momentum, except for a period of modest rebound in Aug-Oct 2023 on signs of growth bottoming. Since late 2023, 10yr CGB yield has trended down again on expected policy easing and investors’ desire for safety assets, hovering around 2.3% lately. Corporate yields dropped as well, and corporate credit spreads were largely stable recently. February issuance of corporate bonds and government bonds was both weaker than a year ago, largely as expected.

Expect additional modest policy rate cut. In 2023, the PBC cut MLF rate by 25bps, 1yr LPR by 20bps and 5yr LPR by 10bps. In addition, commercial banks were asked to lower rates of outstanding mortgages and deposit rates. In Feb 2024, PBC cut 5yr LPR by 25bps without cutting MLF rate. We see modest monetary easing in 2024, with 10-20bps MLF rate cuts, while aggressive rate cuts remain unlikely. The depreciation pressure on the RMB and the need to protect bank NIM may constrain PBC’s policy rate cuts.

## Trade

Export growth surprised to the upside at 7.1%y/y in Jan-Feb. Imports growth also improved to 3.5%y/y. Monthly trade balance narrowed to \$62.6bn from \$75.3bn. Taking 2019 as the base, exports and imports grew by 8.4% and 5.2% in Jan-Feb period, respectively. Full year exports contracted by 4.6%y/y and imports contracted by 5.5%y/y in 2023, after growing 5.6% and 0.7% in 2022. In real basis, exports and imports expanded by 4.6% and 2.9% in 2023 (see trade data comment).

Shipment growth to the US bounced, smart phone contraction deepened. Exports to the US swung to 5.0%y/y growth from prior -6.9% y/y. Shipments to the EU and to Japan remained in y/y contraction thus shipments to the G3 registered only flat growth. Exports to the EMs still performed much better. Exports to ASEAN swung to 6% y/y growth (vs. prior -6.1%y/y). Y/y contraction of mobile phone exports deepened to -18.2% vs. prior -0.4%. That said, computer exports and IC exports have strengthened, suggesting continuing improvements in the tech cycle despite faded boost from smartphone. Shipment of consumer goods displayed a significant upswing to 20.2%y/y from prior of 0.8%y/y.

A slight trade recovery in 2024. We believe the current data combination reflects continuing improvements in the tech cycle, supporting exporting economies along the supply chain. External consumer demand also shows signs of recovery. On the other hand, a strong rebound in the overall external demand momentum is still missing for China. In the near term, we expect export shipment recovery to hover around current pace, a high base may place downward pressures on y/y change to contraction for the coming months. Nonetheless, for full year 2024, we do expect exports to recover to a small growth of 1.2%. Our forecast faces upside risk from resilient DM demand (see latest view).

## FX Reserves and Capital Flows

 Headline FX reserves contracted by \$12bn to \$3.23trn over Jan-Feb period, with contraction in Jan followed by expansion in Feb. China’s FX reserves increased by \$110bn in 2023, declined by \$123bn in 2022, increased by \$34bn in 2021, following gains in 2019-20 and losses in 2018.

The contraction over the period was partly driven by reserve currency valuation effect. We estimate the negative valuation effect from major reserve currencies' movement has likely been around \$34bn due to incremental USD strength at end-Feb vs end-Dec. While the global financial market rally helped, narrower trade balance has been a contributing factor to the moderation in headline too. Overall capital outflow pressure likely eased over the period, as reflected by the steady cross asset performance recently. The balance of total foreign holdings of China's onshore bonds turned positive in Sep-Dec (RMB 492bn in total) after falling for most of 2023, printing full year with a RMB 282bn gain, after a RMB 616bn loss in 2022 (including a sharp loss of RMB 740bn in the 10 consecutive months over Feb-Nov 2022).

We expect the new normal to continue, with larger 2-way capital flows. The rise in capital outflows will mainly reflect non-government entities’ accumulation of overseas assets, especially as controls on capital outflows gradually ease over longer term. Recent sharp drop in FDI into China is partly due to decline in retained profits and high USD costs, but also reflects pressures of decoupling. We expect FDI to recover in 2024-25, but not back to its pre-pandemic levels. Meanwhile, we believe China's continued financial market opening and decent economic growth potential should continue to attract more inflows (see our deep dive).

## Exchange Rate

RMB depreciated against the USD in Jan-Feb to around 7.2, vs. 7.1 at the end of December. Against the backdrop of strengthening USD DXY index, the CNY appreciated against the CFETs basket by around 2.4%. Full year RMB weakened by 2% vs USD while strengthened by 1% against the basket in 2023. This follows 9% depreciation vs the USD and 2.4% weakness vs the basket in 2022.

RMB exchange rate has been driven by various factors. Resilient trade balance is a supporting factor, while the negative China-US rate differentials and concerns on China's growth momentum slowdown are main headwinds. The PBC lowered RRR for FX deposits by 200bp/100bp in Sep/Apr 2022. The SAFE raised the macro-prudential adjustment parameter for cross-border financing from 1 to 1.25 in Oct 2022, and further to 1.5 in July 2023.

RMB may strengthen to 7.0 by end 2024E. Similar to the recent depreciations, the dynamics of the interest rate differentials, USD index, as well China's growth data, may still bring episodes of CNY weakness ahead. That said, PBC will likely use various tools (including counter-cyclical factor in daily fixing) to prevent further CNY weakness much beyond 7.3 amid rapid episodes of depreciation and significant increase of volatility. Given our expectation of a stabilization of China’s growth momentum as well as the expected Fed interest rate cuts, we expect RMB to strengthen a bit to 7.0 by end-2024E.

## Financial Markets

Total industrial profit growth softened to 24.9%y/y in December from 29.2%y/y before, mainly due to slower revenue growth. Expect 15% upside to MSCI China for 2024. We expect 15% upside to MSCI China in 2024, driven by 10% EPS growth and some normalization in the PE multiple (see Equity 2024 Outlook).

2024E sector allocations: The A-share market has recently had growing trading volume and rising risk appetite. In the short term, UBS equity strategists are tactically bullish on crowed stocks, especially those with sound fundamentals (mostly found in food beverages and new energy), as well as high-beta "offensive" sectors such as TMT and defence. Meanwhile, before investors capture any clear evidence of an ongoing company earnings recovery, we recommend allocation to stocks tied to the valuation system with Chinese characteristics, which have corrected recently and feature high dividend yield, as part of a medium-term defensive portfolio.

## Fiscal

Jan-Feb fiscal revenue declined y/y, while expenditure stayed robust. Headline fiscal revenue declined by -2.3%y/y in Jan-Feb from -1%y/y in Q4 2023. On a comparable basis (after controlling a high base due to delayed SME tax collection and carry-over of tax cut since mid-2023), the underlying fiscal revenue grew 2.5%y/y. Fiscal expenditure softened to a still robust pace of 6.7%y/y from 9%y/y in Q4. Corporate income tax revenue saw 0%y/y from 1%y/y in Q4. Personal income tax slid notably to -15.9%y/y from -3%y/y in Q4 partly due to a high base, as the deduction standard has been raised since August 2023. Thus, Jan-Feb recorded a smaller-than-usual fiscal surplus of RMB 96bn. LG land sales revenue recorded a flattish reading vs a year ago, less bad than -4%y/y in Q4.

Further progress in addressing LGFV debt issue, but not yet the long-term solution. Local governments issued RMB 1.39 trillion of special refinancing LG bonds to swap for qualified LGFV debt and/or pay arrears to corporate suppliers in H2 2023. In 2024, we expect the government to issue an additional RMB 2 trillion or more special refinancing LG bonds and encourage banks to increase restructuring of LGFV bank loans. That said, we think the long-term solution (e.g. adjustment of central-local fiscal relations and debt structure) may not come out soon (see NPC takeaway).

Modest fiscal support in a more explicit way in 2024. As expected, the government announced a headline general fiscal deficit of 3% of GDP, additional RMB 1 trillion special CGB and RMB 3.9 trillion special LG bonds. The combined budget deficit (general fiscal + govt funds) on a cash basis is estimated to be 8.2% of GDP, or 1.2ppts larger than the outturn of 2023. We expect the Augmented Fiscal Deficit (AFD) to expand by 1-1.2ppt in 2024 after the 0.2ppt increase in 2023. Overall fiscal support will be more explicit in stronger on-budget spending, especially from central government. We think local government land sales revenue may continue to decline by another 10% in 2024, while off-budget LGFV financing will likely be muted amid the ongoing emphasis of controlling implicit debt risk (see NPC takeaway).
