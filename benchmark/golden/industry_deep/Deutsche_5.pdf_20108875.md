# Bangladesh's structural and cyclical macro outlook

## Introduction

Since its independence in 1971, Bangladesh's economic progress and transformation has been impressive. From a predominantly rural economy after independence, Bangladesh has transitioned to become a more urbanised economy, through a targeted industrialization policy since the 1980's. Dhaka, the capital city, is a good reflection of the economic transformation that Bangladesh has undergone over the last several decades. Dhaka is the largest city of Bangladesh and currently the fourth-largest and sixth-most densely populated city in the world. It has expended rapidly and currently has a population of nearly 24 million residents in the Dhaka Metropolitan Area.

Despite various external and domestic shocks, Bangladesh has managed to sustain an average real GDP growth of 6.0% since the early 1990s and an even more impressive 6.6% in the last decade, which is higher than the South-Asia average and points to the resiliency of the economy. The strong growth has been propelled by Bangladesh's transformation from an agrarian economy to a more industrialized economy, through targeted policies and reforms. Indeed, manufacturing as a share of GDP has increased by 10% points to 24% currently, from 14% in 2000.

Within manufacturing, garment manufacturing is Bangladesh's main strength and it is a key global player in this field. Bangladesh's core competency is particularly in apparels, commonly known as Ready-Made Garments (RMG), producing massmarket finished products such as knit t-shirts, non-knit men's suits and shirts, and knit sweaters. RMGs contribute more than 85% to total merchandise exports (Bangladesh ranks among the top-three global RMG exporters, together with China and Vietnam) and account for over 70% of formal manufacturing jobs. As per OECD estimates, RMG sector employs around 4.4 million individuals, primarily women (women comprise a significant 80% of the RMG labour force).

Bangladesh's exports have grown rapidly in the last twenty years, increasing to USD55bn in 2023, from USD6.5bn in 2003, almost a nine fold jump. The increase was led primarily by RMG, which saw exports rising from USD5bn in 2003 to USD47bn in 2023. The heavy reliance on one particular export item for growth, employment and development highlights the concentration risk that Bangladesh faces, particularly in a world fraught with heightened geopolitical uncertainty and potential industry-specific risks.

European Union is Bangladesh's largest trading partner, with the latter's exports share with the EU being in excess of 50% at this juncture. EU + UK together account for nearly 60% of Bangladesh's total exports, with apparel comprising more than 90% of those export earnings. Within the EU, Germany is the biggest export destination of Bangladesh, constituting about 15% share (Spain and France are also important). Apart from the EU, the United States is also a key destination with 16% export share. Bangladesh's exports share with Asia is about 15%. Meanwhile, Bangladesh imports mainly from Asia, with almost 80% of imports coming from China, India, and Singapore. Bangladesh's key imports are fuel & petroleum and raw cotton (almost 4% of total imports), the key raw material for RMG.

## Structural challenges related to LDC transition from 2026

Bangladesh is currently facing economic headwinds, reflected in slowdown in economic momentum, acceleration of inflation closer to 10%, sharp drop in FX reserves, rising non-performing assets in the banking system, slowing private sector credit growth, depreciation of the currency and weak revenue mobilization, constraining the space for growth-critical investments. However, these challenges are more cyclical in nature and can be dealt with by appropriate policy mix and reforms push. But Bangladesh, in our view, faces a bigger structural challenge, related to its transition from LDC status, which will determine the course of the country's medium-and long-term economic prospects.

Bangladesh will be transitioning from the United Nation's Least Developed Country (LDC) classification in 2026, which will lead to a possible loss of preferential market access, that it currently enjoys. Being a LDC, Bangladesh has enjoyed preferential access from most countries and has not been subject to tariffs, which was instrumental behind the country's impressive transformation into a global manufacturing hub for the RMG sector and its consequent increase in market share across EU, USA, Canada, Japan and other parts of the world. The only country with which Bangladesh did not enjoy preferential access was the USA.

Once the LDC graduation takes place, Bangladesh will most likely face tariff hikes in most of its major export destinations. As per Asian Development Bank's (ADB) estimates, Bangladeshi exporters may face an increase in average tariffs on their exports, which could be as high as 16.0% in Canada, 12% in EU, 8.7% in Japan, 8.6% in India, and about 7.0% in China.

It is estimated that 70% of Bangladesh's exports are currently benefiting from LDC-specific trade preferences. As ADB has pointed out, several studies show that a tariff increase on 70% of Bangladesh's exports could potentially result in a significant decrease in exports, ranging from 5.5% to as high as 14%, with a particular impact on export earnings from the EU market (Rahman and Bari 2019, Razzaque and Rahman 2020, WTO 2020, UNCTAD 2016). There are counter arguments, that given the large economies of scale that Bangladesh already enjoys and its dominant position in the RMG sector, it will likely be able to remain competitive and consequently exports may not get significantly impacted, but the jury is out on this.

Clearly, Bangladesh's dependency on only one major export item to propel the growth, employment and development engine in the economy faces a grave risk, particularly given the impending LDC transition. It is clear that exports are likely to be impacted post the LDC transition, and therefore unless the policy priorities and reforms push are oriented to offset the likely impact, Bangladesh may have no choice but to contend with lower economic growth, relative to what it has achieved in the past. Indeed, this is a critical moment for Bangladesh, a sort of an inflection point, where the choice of today's policy priorities will determine how the economy will fare over the next few decades.

## Transitioning to a new economic model - long-term agenda

It is amply clear that Bangladesh needs a new economic model to sustain its previously-achieved high growth trajectory and to ensure that employment and socio-economic development do not falter post LDC graduation. Environmental and sustainability concerns will need to be addressed as part of the new model, with ESG now a key parameter that should have clearly defined time-bound goals. Also time is of essence, as Bangladesh's demographic dividend window is likely to narrow and close between 2033-2040. Already, the dependency ratio has started rising - it increased to 53.7% in 2023, from 52.3% in 2022 and 50.2% in 2021 and 2020. We list ten key reforms and strategies that have been highlighted by the OECD, the World Bank and the Asian Development Bank to help Bangladesh transition into a new economic paradigm.

## 10 key reforms/strategies for Bangladesh's next stage of development

1. Focus on innovation: In Bangladesh, only 1.2% of firms invest in R&D—less than half the rate observed in Indian firms. In the next stage of development, it is imperative for the private sector to assume responsibility for innovation and technological advancement through strategic partnerships and M&A in critical sectors. Empirical studies show that, in case of Bangladesh, there is a direct correlation between advanced technology and exports success.

2. Closer integration with Asia: Bangladesh imports 75% from Asia, while only 15% of its exports are sold within the region. Bangladesh’s untapped export potential across ASEAN, South Asia, and East Asia presents growth opportunities. A strategy integrating LDC graduation and regional integration is crucial.

3. Focus on attracting larger FDI flows: Despite various reform measures, FDI inflow into Bangladesh has been modest, averaging around 1% of GDP from 2000 to 2021. The garments sector has attracted most of this investment that has gone into export-oriented enterprises. If FDI flows accelerate and become more broad based, it can help offset the fiscal constraint of low revenue mobilization, which impedes growth-critical investments. Higher FDI flows can also help facilitate knowledge and technology transfers, strengthen the Balance of Payments position, by boosting FX reserves and bring more stability to the currency. The biggest FDI flows come from Unites States (20% of total) and the UK (13%), both investing mostly in energy and banking.

4. Digitalization for development: The government of Bangladesh has launched two initiatives: i) Digital Bangladesh Vision 2021 and ii) Smart Bangladesh Vision 2041, but clearly more needs to be done. For example Bangladesh currently has an internet usage rate of only 25% of its population, which is significantly lower than Vietnam's 70%. In 2022, Bangladesh was home to 2% of Asia’s start-ups, up from almost zero around a decade ago, while India accounts for 40% of the region’s total.

5. Tariff rationalization: Lowering tariffs can stimulate domestic manufacturing, potentially balancing any revenue loss from reduced import tariffs.

6. Ease of doing business: Tackling the high cost of doing business is critical for boosting investment and trade competitiveness. Bangladesh faces the risk of becoming stuck in a cycle of relatively low-wage and low-productivity growth, which could limit its potential for expanding production and trade opportunities. Labour productivity growth averaged 3% between 1990 and 2019, which is not enough to close the gap with more advanced countries.

7. Prudent exchange rate management strategy: This could significantly enhance export competitiveness, benefiting sectors beyond the RMG industry. Maintaining a competitive and stable exchange rate ensures that exports remain attractively priced in international markets.

8. Supply-side reforms: Addressing the sector-specific supply-side constraints can help with export response from non-RMG sectors.

9. Export incentives: Devising WTO-consistent export-incentive mechanisms is crucial for Bangladesh, given its impending LDC graduation.

10. Seeking trade preferences beyond LDC graduation: This is an important strategy for export competitiveness. Graduating from the LDC group does not imply the cessation of preferential treatment altogether. Seeking unilateral trade preferences in the post-graduation period will be essential for Bangladesh, as many competitor countries have taken advantage of free trade agreements (FTAs) to obtain preferential market access. For Bangladesh, striking FTAs within a short period of time will not be feasible. Thus, a renewed focus on retaining trade preferences will be an utmost policy priority. In preparation for Bangladesh’s upcoming LDC graduation, strengthening trade policy and negotiation capabilities to support the export sector will be critical.

## Growth outlook

## Growth likely to trend lower to 5.5-6% range, led by both cyclical and structural challenges; next generation reforms are need of the hour

Bangladesh economy has exhibited strong resilience over the past several decades, but the twin impact of Covid-19 pandemic since 2020 and Russia-Ukraine war from 2022 has dealt a heavy blow to the growth prospects of the country. Prior to the Covid-19 pandemic, Bangladesh recorded an impressive real GDP growth average of 6.6% in the last decade, which was higher than the South-Asia average. Strong export performance played a big part in delivering a high growth outturn for Bangladesh, which also helped reduce the poverty rate from more than 60% in the nineties to less than 20% currently, as per World Bank estimates. Development indicators have also improved significantly, with life expectancy increasing to 73 years by 2020, up from 66 in the year 2000, and the infant mortality rate declining appreciably to 24 per 1,000 live births, from the previous 60 deaths during the same period, as per OECD estimates. Currently, Bangladesh ranks as the 35th largest economy by GDP size and has the eighth largest population in the world.

However, repeated shocks in the form of Covid-19 pandemic, the ongoing Russia-Ukraine war and escalation of geopolitical tensions in the Middle East since 2023 have impacted the Bangladesh economy severely. After recording 7.1%yoy growth in FY22 and 6.9%yoy in FY21, real GDP growth slowed down to 5.8%yoy in FY23 and is likely to average even lower in FY24 at 5.7%yoy, as per our estimates (Bangladesh's financial year starts from July and ends in June).

Higher commodity prices, resulting in increased inflation and erosion of purchasing power among households, monetary policy tightening, along with severe disruptions/volatility in international trade and import curbs leading to shortages of key raw materials and capital goods have impacted private consumption, investment and exports growth substantially in the last two years, thereby pushing Bangladesh's economic growth below the 6% mark.

The ongoing economic stress has led ratings agency Fitch to downgrade Bangladesh's long-term foreign-currency issuer default rating to "B+" from "BB-" (while maintaining a stable outlook), owing to the continued weakening of the country's external buffers. Given this backdrop, we don't expect the Bangladesh economy to rebound sharply in the next few years. Real GDP growth is likely to remain below the 6%yoy mark, probably around 5.8% and 5.9% in FY25 and FY26 respectively, with risks biased more to the downside rather than upside, in the event of an occurrence of further external shocks.

While inflation pressure is expected to ease over the coming years, the lagged impact of the previous monetary policy tightening and higher real rates are likely to keep private consumption growth subdued compared to pre-Covid-19 average, while investment growth may not rebound materially, due to persistent supply-side shortages, higher interest rates and rising macro-financial vulnerabilities in the economy. Banking sector NPA's have increased sharply, which is impacting credit growth and the strategy of mergers between bad banks and good banks stand the risk of generating a sub-optimal outcome.

Also, Bangladesh faces the structural challenge related to LDC transition in 2026, which will likely lead to increased tariffs in key export markets and pose a risk to medium-term exports and growth prospects. Given both the cyclical and structural challenges, we think Bangladesh will likely have to contend with real GDP growth in the range of 5.5-6.0% in the coming years compared to the 6.6% average it recorded in the decade prior to the Covid-19 pandemic. This would still be a decent growth outturn compared to peers, but new growth-and-reforms oriented strategy will have to be formulated to sustain and raise the growth trajectory once again above the 6% mark.

# Inflation, money and credit

## High inflation and rising NPLs have increased macrofinancial vulnerabilities, which need to be dealt on a priority basis, even if it results in some growth sacrifice

Bangladesh's high dependence on imports of fuel, raw materials and other commodities impacted its inflation trajectory since the beginning of the Russia-Ukraine war in Feb 2022. Disruptions in supply chains also added to inflation pressure and pushed up average CPI inflation to 9.0% in FY23, from 6.1% in FY22. Headline CPI inflation accelerated further in FY24 to average 9.7%, a record high. In May'24, CPI inflation remained elevated at 9.9%yoy, with food inflation even higher at 10.8%yoy. Non-food inflation was also significantly high at 9.2%yoy in May'24. Currency depreciation along with domestic tariff hikes of administered petroleum products (domestic fuel prices were linked with import prices from March 2024, to achieve a greater cost recovery and reduce subsidies), gas and electricity dealt a double whammy to the inflation trajectory, with headline CPI currently running significantly above the central bank's target of 7.5%. Unsurprisingly, inflation expectations have worsened as well in the past few years.

CPI inflation will likely ease somewhat in FY25, to about 8.5%, as per our forecasts, aided by a favorable base effect, but it is still likely to be higher than the pre-war average, by about 300bps. Potential escalation in geopolitical risks resulting in higher global oil prices, currency depreciation, domestic supply shortages, import restrictions and weather disruption-related spikes in food prices remain key risks to Bangladesh's inflation trajectory for FY25 and beyond.

Meanwhile, money supply (M2) growth has moderated sharply to 7.4%yoy in April'24, lower than the 10% target set by Bangladesh Bank for Jan-June'24, led by a fall in Net Foreign Assets (NFA), thanks to FX sales by the central bank in the currency market.

While credit and money supply growth moderated in FY24, gross NPAs of the banking system increased to 9% by Dec'23, from 8.2% in Dec'22, led mainly by public sector banks. The NPAs are likely to be appreciably higher, when adjusted for restructured loans and other regulatory forbearances. The capital adequacy ratio of the banking system remains weak, thereby posing a hurdle for effective monetary policy transmission. To deal with banking system issues, Bangladesh Bank (BB) set up a Prompt Corrective Action (PCA) framework in Dec 2023, which will be effective from 31 March 2025. In March 2024, BB announced plans to merge weak banks with strong banks. The jury is out on this, whether such a strategy will work to make the overall banking system stronger and more resilient. Going by India's own experience of banking sector balance sheet stress in the last decade and the subsequent Asset Quality Review and PCA framework adopted to deal with the problem, it looks like the ongoing efforts on part of the Bangladeshi authorities to reduce the vulnerabilities of its financial system could take a long time, which will entail some inevitable growth sacrifice, but in our view, this needs to be dealt with as the utmost priority, even at the cost of lower growth, if necessary.

## Monetary policy outlook

Bangladesh faces a growth-inflation trade-off; BB likely to focus more on containing inflation in the near term, leading to some inevitable growth sacrifice

Bangladesh Bank (BB) has increased policy rates by 375bps between May 2022 and May 2024 to contain inflation and inflation expectations, but CPI inflation continues to be elevated close to 10% and real rates remain negative close to 140bps. However, monetary transmission has been weak thus far, thereby not having the desired impact on inflation and inflation expectations. BB acknowledged in the latest May MPC minutes that Bangladesh faces two critical challenges: persistently high inflation and depleting foreign exchange reserves.

In order to continue with its fight against inflation, BB increased the policy rate further by 50bps in May to 8.50%, and more importantly decided to move towards a market based interest rate by abolishing the existing reference rate SMART for determining the lending rates of banks/NBFIs to improve the efficacy of monetary transmission. As per the new guidance, banks and NBFIs have been allowed to set the interest rates based on market forces of demand and supply, which is a welcome move, in our view.

As we expect CPI inflation to average 8.5% in FY25, real rates are likely to become neutral during the course of the year, with the current policy rate at 8.50%. But in our view, the policy rate may need to go up by another 50-100bps, so as to ensure at least 100bps positive real rates in the economy. This, along with probable improved and faster monetary transmission, should over a course of time be able to bring inflation closer to the 6% mark, and help lower inflation expectations. BB's main focus is to tackle inflation at this stage, rightly so, which is likely to entail some growth sacrifice, in our view.

## Fiscal outlook

## Tax and overall revenue collection need to increase sustainably, to support economic development, while maintaining fiscal prudence

With economic momentum having slowed down and monetary policy expected to remain tight to tackle inflation, it is likely that the Bangladeshi authorities will tolerate a slightly higher fiscal deficit at this juncture to provide incremental support to growth. But we don't expect the fiscal deficit to breach 5% of GDP in the near term, absent a severe growth shock (which reduces revenue and increases the need for providing some sort of fiscal stimulus). The joint IMF-World Bank Debt Sustainability Analysis (DSA) assesses that Bangladesh remains at low risk of external and overall debt distress.

On 6th June 2024, the FY25 national budget was tabled in parliament, with budget deficit for FY25 projected at 4.6% of the GDP vs. 4.7% of GDP revised estimate for FY24. We forecast fiscal deficit to be slightly higher at 4.7% of GDP in FY25, similar to the FY24 outturn and thereafter rise a tad to 4.8% of GDP in FY26. Tax collection, could face downside risks in FY25 as high inflation and rising interest rates keep private consumption and investment subdued, resulting in a lower growth outturn (the authorities have projected FY25 real GDP growth at 6.8%yoy, which is 100bps higher than our estimate of 5.8%yoy). Bangladesh’s tax revenue is largely dependent on indirect taxes (70% of total), particularly VAT (more than one third revenue collection comes from VAT), which can prove to be inflationary.

Total revenue/GDP at 8.3% of GDP in FY23 was significantly lower than comparable peers at 19.5%, as per Fitch estimates. Lower revenues have constrained growth-critical expenditure and have led to a growth sacrifice.

## Tax reforms are the need of the hour

The current IMF programme necessitates Bangladesh to implement several tax reforms to increase the revenue base and tax collection. The FY24 budget and the newly amended Income Tax Law added a number of revenue measures in line with program commitments (expected to increase revenue by 0.5% of GDP in FY24, as per IMF estimates), which include:

n increased applicable taxes on tobacco, land registration, foreign loans and travel;

an environmental surcharge on multiple car ownership;

n removal of several VAT exemptions; and

n increased import duties on select products.

As per World Bank estimates, "Bangladesh currently collects approximately half of its potential revenue, given its economic structure, level of development, and trade openness." The World Bank suggests the following tax reforms to increase the revenue collection: i) rationalizing tax expenditures, ii) adopting a uniform VAT rate, iii) amending the VAT law to facilitate full automation, and iv) reducing supplementary duty and regulatory duty rates to the minimum necessary for transparency and efficiency.

Direct taxes as a share of total tax collection are significantly lower in case of Bangladesh versus comparable countries. A key strategy for the fiscal authorities would be to reduce over dependence on indirect taxes by increasing direct tax collection. World Bank recommends the following in this regard: i) promoting voluntary compliance; ii) additional wealth and capital gains tax; iii) taxes are currently collected by the local governments based on rates that have not risen with property prices. The tax rates could be updated and combined with the wealth tax collected by the NBR to make the system more efficient. A modern property taxation system could be established with rates based on the true market value of the property; and iv) income tax and VAT revenues will need to rise to compensate for the resulting decline in trade tax revenues, as Bangladesh graduates from LDC status, resulting in the loss of preferential market access.

## BOP and FX outlook

## External buffers need to be strengthened

Despite the Bangladesh Bank's efforts to stabilize the exchange rate, and protect the depletion of foreign exchange reserves, there has been little reversal to the trend. Gross FX reserves have fallen steadily to USD18.6bn in May'24, from USD24.8bn in June'23. In order to address this issue and bring more flexibility in the FX market, Bangladesh Bank (BB) decided to introduce a crawling peg system in May'24 for buying and selling of foreign currencies. Under the new system, a Crawling Peg Mid-Rate (CPMR) is set at Taka 117.00 per USD. BB will keep the new regime in review and will adjust the parameters of the regime as needed. The crawling peg system will be an interim arrangement before moving to a fully flexible market-based system in the near future.

Highlighting the weak external buffers, ratings agency Fitch downgraded Bangladesh's long-term foreign-currency issuer default rating to "B+" from "BB-" (while maintaining a stable outlook) on $2 7 ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ May'24. The drawdown in FX reserves have been led by continued FX intervention and capital outflows, which reached worrying levels. Transitioning from a crawling peg system to a fully flexible marketbased system, is likely to lead to further depreciation in the currency, which in turn could add to inflationary pressure. This is indeed a tricky situation, when CPI inflation is already close to 10% at this juncture.

However, given the FX reserves position, it is a better strategy, in our view, to give more importance to building a FX reserves buffer and allowing the currency to depreciate, if necessary. Also, as and when capital inflows pick up, BB will likely buy USD to increase its FX reserves buffer, which should result in a continued but orderly depreciation of the Bangladesh taka. Based on all these factors, we expect the Bangladesh Taka to depreciate to at least 120 by the end of Dec'24. In order to address the inflation problem, associated with currency depreciation, BB can consider hiking rates more (we are forecasting 50-100bps incremental rate hikes), thereby according higher importance to strengthening external buffers, while accepting some growth sacrifice in the process.

The authorities had to resort to import curbs due to domestic Dollar shortage, which led to a steep fall in imports, resulting in the current account balance likely moving to a surplus of 1.2% of GDP in FY24, from a deficit of 0.6% of GDP. As the authorities allow increased flexibility in the exchange rate and uncertainty related to the FX policy reduces, imports are likely to increase once again from the FY24 lows, but the current account balance is unlikely to turn deficit, at least in FY25. We are forecasting current account balance to remain surplus in FY25 as well, at 0.6% of GDP and return to a small deficit of 0.3% of GDP only in FY26.

In the near term, FX reserves position will improve led by financial assistance from the IMF and other multilateral institutions. The IMF has recently approved disbursal of USD 1.15 bn (as part of the 3rd tranche) of a USD 4.7bn loan package for Bangladesh, signed in Jan 2023, to address Bangladesh's deteriorating FX reserve position. The loan will be distributed in seven installments by 2026. IMF cleared USD 447.8 mn of the first installment in Feb'23 and USD 681 mn of the second installment in Dec'23. The IMF was supposed to disburse USD 681 mn this month, but eventually agreed to increase the disbursement to USD 1.15 bn.

Apart from the IMF loan, agreements for USD 500 mn in budgetary support with the World Bank, USD 250 mn with the Asian Development Bank, and USD 100 mn with South Korea have already been signed. Additionally, a USD 400 mn budget assistance deal with Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank (AIIB) is expected on June 26. The financial assistance from all these multilateral institutions add up to USD 2.36 bn, which should push up the FX reserves (as per BPM6) to about USD 21.9 bn by end-June'24.

Financial assistance from the IMF and other multilateral institutions will help the BOP and reserves position somewhat, on a temporary basis, but Bangladesh cannot and should not depend solely on this as a solution to strengthen its reserve adequacy metrics. Net FDI, which remains low at around 1% of GDP average, needs to increase substantially to boost overall capital inflows, so that the Balance of Payments and reserves buffer strengthen sustainably from current levels, but this will require a significant reforms push, in our view.

## Conclusion

Since its independence in 1971, Bangladesh's economic progress and transformation has been impressive. Despite various external and domestic shocks, Bangladesh has managed to sustain an average real GDP growth of 6.0% since the early 1990s and an even more impressive 6.6% in the last decade, which is higher than the South-Asia average and points to the resiliency of the economy. However, repeated shocks in the form of Covid-19 pandemic, the ongoing Russia-Ukraine war and escalation of geopolitical tensions in the Middle East since 2023 have impacted the Bangladesh economy severely.

Bangladesh is currently facing economic headwinds, reflected in slowdown in economic momentum, acceleration of inflation closer to 10%, sharp drop in FX reserves, rising non-performing assets in the banking system, slowing private sector credit growth, depreciation of the currency and weak revenue mobilization, constraining the space for growth-critical investments. However, these challenges are more cyclical in nature and can be dealt with by appropriate policy mix and reforms push. But Bangladesh, in our view, faces a bigger structural challenge, related to its transition from LDC status, which will determine the course of the country's medium-and long-term economic prospects.

Bangladesh will be transitioning from the United Nation's Least Developed Country (LDC) classification in 2026, which will lead to a possible loss of preferential market access that it currently enjoys. Once the LDC graduation takes place, Bangladesh will most likely face tariff hikes in most of its major export destinations. It is estimated that 70% of Bangladesh's exports are currently benefiting from LDCspecific trade preferences. Bangladesh's dependency on only one major export item (Ready-Made Garments or RMG constitute 85% of total exports), to propel the growth, employment and development engine in the economy faces a grave risk, particularly given the impending LDC transition.

It is clear that exports are likely to be impacted post the LDC transition, and therefore unless the policy priorities and reforms push are oriented to offset the likely impact, Bangladesh may have no choice but to contend with lower economic growth, relative to what it has achieved in the past. Indeed, this is a critical moment for Bangladesh, a sort of an inflection point, where the choice of today's policy priorities will determine how the economy will fare over the next few decades.

Clearly, Bangladesh needs a new economic model to sustain its previouslyachieved high growth trajectory and to ensure that employment and socioeconomic development do not falter post LDC graduation. Environmental and sustainability concerns will need to be addressed as part of the new model, with ESG now a key parameter that should have clearly defined time-bound goals. Also, time is of essence, as Bangladesh's demographic dividend window is likely to narrow and close between 2033-2040.

Macroeconomic fluctuations often account for most of the risks associated with exposures to instruments that promise to pay fixed or variable interest rates. For an investor who is long fixed-rate instruments (thus receiving these cash flows), increases in interest rates naturally lift the discount factors applied to the expected cash flows and thus cause a loss. The longer the maturity of a certain cash flow and the higher the move in the discount factor, the higher will be the loss. Upside surprises in inflation, fiscal funding needs, and FX depreciation rates are among the most common adverse macroeconomic shocks to receivers. But counterparty exposure, issuer creditworthiness, client segmentation, regulation (including changes in assets holding limits for different types of investors), changes in tax policies, currency convertibility (which may constrain currency conversion, repatriation of profits and/or liquidation of positions), and settlement issues related to local clearing houses are also important risk factors. The sensitivity of fixed-income instruments to macroeconomic shocks may be mitigated by indexing the contracted cash flows to inflation, to FX depreciation, or to specified interest rates - these are common in emerging markets. The index fixings may - by construction - lag or mis-measure the actual move in the underlying variables they are intended to track. The choice of the proper fixing (or metric) is particularly important in swaps markets, where floating coupon rates (i.e., coupons indexed to a typically short-dated interest rate reference index) are exchanged for fixed coupons. Funding in a currency that differs from the currency in which coupons are denominated carries FX risk.
