# US Economic Perspectives A new US outlook

## An updated US economic outlook: (a little) more room to run

In our new outlook, we are upgrading our expectations for real GDP growth, though we still expect a slowdown in 2024. We now project a labor market expansion that slows, but continues, with only a small rise in the unemployment rate. With less unemployment than we previously projected, we have marked up our 2024 core PCE inflation projections by roughly 0.4 pp, to 2.3% (Q4/Q4), having lost the disinflation from that degree of labor market slackening. We still expect inflation is on a bumpy path lower, but a touch slower than before, and core PCE inflation ends 2025 at 2.1%. (A spreadsheet with additional details is available on our website.) We expect the FOMC to begin “dialing back” the restrictive stance of monetary policy at the June FOMC meeting, with a 25 bp reduction in the target range for the Federal funds rate. We expect that to be followed by two additional 25 bp rate cuts at the September and December FOMC meetings, leaving the target range for the federal funds rate at 4.50% to 4.75% at the end of 2024.

## A two tier economy, but consumer resilience persists

Consumers and fiscal support carried the expansion through 2023. Even though incoming spending data looks weak, we felt we needed to mark up our expectations for consumer spending. Signs of households under stress are spreading, but not all households appear to be under pressure, with those at the upper end of the income distribution wealthier and with abundant liquid assets. In contrast, the business sector of the economy looks unusually weak after coming off 3% GDP growth with high interest rates appearing to depress investment activity. Forward-looking indicators remain soft, and we expect the weight from interest rates to persist. Alongside this, we anticipate that the fiscal tailwind of 2023 will dissipate in 2024. This leaves the sustainability of the expansion heavily reliant on consumer spending. We discuss this in more detail inside.

## FOMC - tough risks to weigh

Overall, if consumer spending holds up, that should keep the virtuous circle of job gains and rising real labor income supporting consumption going. However, if consumer spending softens because consumers run out of steam as savings deteriorates, or precautionary saving rises, or the level of interest rates weighs further on consumption growth, the business sector would likely slow too, and with that labor demand, and hiring. The FOMC would like policy to restore price stability and sustain the expansion. If the FOMC remains comfortable with the pace of growth, and inflation continues to move lower, we expect the FOMC to lower the policy rate 25 bps at the June, September, and December FOMC meetings, leaving the target range between 4.50% and 4.75% at the end of this year. With the slowdown in growth we expect, we think the FOMC would pick up the pace in 2025, and take the target range down to 3.00% to 3.25%. However, without more obvious signs the labor market expansion is at risk, or inflation is below target, the risk assessment of the FOMC may prove hesitant to move from calibration to accommodation. In our prior forecast, with inflation at or below target, and an unemployment rate rising noticeably above the FOMC's longer-run rate, the economic weakness justified accommodation, and a funds rate below neutral. Without those conditions, and depending on the risk assessment of the FOMC, the target range would be expected to remain perhaps near neutral, but err on the side of restrictive unless those conditions were met.

We are updating our economic projections. In our new outlook, we are carrying forward more momentum in the labor market and consumption. We now see less acute weakness ahead, but still below-trend GDP growth. We expect the economy to expand roughly 1-1/4% over the four quarters of 2024, with weak business fixed investment, less support from fiscal policy in 2024 than 2023, and a soft global backdrop. The expansion likely remains highly reliant on consumer spending. We have also marked up our 2024 core PCE inflation projections by roughly 0.4 pp, to 2.3% (Q4/Q4), due to less labor market weakness. We still expect inflation is on a bumpy path lower.

We've marked up projected 2024 real GDP growth and inflation, but still see inflation on a bumpy path lower.

We expect the FOMC to begin “dialing back” the restrictive stance of monetary policy at the June FOMC meeting, with a 25 bp reduction in the target range for the federal funds rate. We expect that to be followed by two additional 25 bp rate cuts at the September and December FOMC meetings, leaving the target range for the federal funds rate at 4.50% to 4.75% at the end of 2024, higher than our prior projections.

We remain cautious. We published a note last weekend arguing net international migration was improving population growth, in turn improving the potential growth rate of the economy. That is only one reason for the forecast revisions. In our November outlook update, we projected the data would hold up through January, and that for seasonal reasons January would look strong. After that the consumption data would weaken, weighing on final demand, and in turn business hiring. Some of that has unfolded. Nominal retail and food services sales have declined over the most recent three months. Sales at the control group of stores used to calculate goods spending in GDP are down too. US manufacturing output is down over the last three months too. Household survey employment has fallen over the last six months. Those series forming peaks could be consistent with a broader business cycle peak. In addition to that hard data, surveys remain tepid. Our leading indicator models suggest slowdown and potential contraction ahead. However, not everything is lining up.

In 2024, we expect 25 bp rate cuts at the June, September and December FOMC meetings.

We are simply not seeing the degree of deterioration to indicate that the acute weakness in our prior projection is imminent. It may be, but it's not here yet. Upper income households remain flush with cash despite stress emerging elsewhere. Saving in the economy remains low too. The data that most forced our hand in revising our economic projection was the weekly filings of initial claims for unemployment insurance and the change in continuing claims. Initial claims filings remain low and the rise in continuing claims has levelled off in recent weeks. The 4-week moving average of initial claims was below 210K in last week's data. While that may be consistent with sub-200K per month gains in NFP, it seems inconsistent with sub-100K gains being imminent. That said, we believe there is substantial potential for mismeasurement in a range of economic data, including the claims data. We also think that nonfarm payroll employment is overstating the strength of the labor market.

Incoming data has been weaker than the prevailing narrative. The expansion may be more fragile than many appreciate.

The highest frequency data, however, is not displaying the kind of weakening that suggest acute contraction is imminent.

## Household sector: a two-tiered expansion

Consumers and fiscal support carried the expansion through 2023, we wrote in "Where's that US recession?" last October. Fiscal support appears fading in 2024. Delinquency rates and eroded savings should weigh on households. With tepid investment in the US economy outside of structures, what's left? Not much. Signs of households under stress are spreading, but not all households appear to be under pressure, as Figure 2 shows. Even though incoming retail sales data looks weak, we felt we needed to mark up our expectations for consumer spending.

The expansion remains reliant on consumer spending. If Y=C+I+G-NX, the I, G and NX are going to be a lot less help in 2024 it looks like.

The main change to our economic projections in this note is removing much of the material consumption weakness. We expect spending growth to slow, but we no longer expect the kind of pull back that would be consistent with economic contraction. Wealth remains high, and the saving rate remains low. As the chart of the saving rate below shows, the share of disposable income households are not spending is quite low in the aggregate. The only previous time the saving rate was this low was during the credit bubble of the 2000s, when households could borrow abundantly to spend outside of income, and housing wealth could be easily monetized. While our analysis suggests the saving rate should be higher, that has not come to fruition. Essentially we are carrying forward in our projection more impact from equity market wealth in our economic projections. That keeps the saving rate low, and supports consumption.

Can the top of the income distribution carry the economic expansion? It looks to be unfolding that way. Using experimental data from the BLS we estimate that from 2017- 2019, the top 20% of the income distribution accounted for 35% of annual growth in Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) and the next 20% of the distribution — the 60th to 80th percentile — accounted for 19%. In other words, over half of consumption growth through that period was driven by the upper end of the income distribution. In level terms, the top 20% of the income distribution accounted for 41% of spending growth through 2017-2019, adding the next 20% of the income distribution, the upper end accounted for 62% of total PCE growth. Using BLS data from the Consumer Expenditure Survey, we estimate from 2005-2019, the top 20% of the income distribution on average accounted for 38% of consumption growth and the next 20% of the distribution — the 60th to 80th percentile — accounted for a further 20%, implying that nearly 60% of consumption growth was driven by the upper end of the income distribution.

Can the top of the income distribution carry an expansion? Seems to be the case.

So, could those at the upper end of the income distribution carry the cycle further? Yes, these data would suggest that they could. Unfortunately for everyone else, headwinds building for the rest of the income distribution suggest that consumption should slow from here. Overall, we project Q4/Q4 growth in real PCE of 1.6% in 2024, 1.7% in 2025 and 1.5% in 2026 — a slowing from the solid 2.6% rate of growth seen through 2023.

We wrote a note that elevated net international migration would support more economic growth in 2024. We expect the increase in population growth to support consumption growth in 2024, but households also face headwinds and pressures. Rising net interest payments, resumption of student loan repayments, and the end of pandemic benefits are pressuring households as the labor market slows, somewhat offset by rising real wages. The signs of stress are spreading.

Delinquency rates are rising, as shown in the charts below, and beyond just younger age groups. Transition rates into serious delinquency in credit cards and auto loans have surpassed the rates seen ahead of the pandemic for younger cohorts, and are rising for higher cohorts. Among 18-39 year olds, 60% hold student loan debt. The resumption of student loan repayments may not be insurmountable for the economic expansion (repayments appear to be settling near pre-pandemic rates of \$68 billion in annualized terms or 0.3% of disposable income) but for those facing higher interest rates on other forms of debt the repayments represent an additional financial burden to build into their balance sheets.

Data from the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis show rising financial distress with the share of individuals who have an account 30 days or more past due increasing. For credit card debt this rate is already in line with the rates seen during the global financial crisis (GFC) — for auto loans it is close, just 0.1 pp below, while for mortgages it is 1.8 pp below the rate seen in the GFC. This is hardly surprising given the nature of the housing bubble in the GFC and the fact that many Americans refinanced into long duration, lower rate mortgages through the pandemic — blunting the pass through of higher rates via this channel. But, with 36.4% of household debt outside of mortgages — this is in no way negligible.

## Business sector, why so sluggish?

Real GDP growth expanded over 3% in 2023. Equipment investment was flat? Intellectual property investment was weak? Manufacturing output fell? The ISM manufacturing composite was below 50? The ISM services composite was below 55? Capex intentions were low, and hiring intentions were low? The answer to all those questions is yes, despite the bullish optimism, and all that data is very inconsistent with 3% GDP growth.

The business sector of the economy looks unusually weak after coming off 3% GDP growth.

Why? We expect that represents the impact of higher interest rates. But it also highlights the reliance of the expansion on consumer spending. The one investment component that did show strength was construction of manufacturing plants. That has now slowed sharply. That was also supported by fiscal policy. Fiscal policy is relatively interest rate insensitive. Outside of that, business investment has been unusually weak for 3% growth.

Looking ahead, with higher interest rates than we previously projected, investment should continue to feel the pressure. Our industrial equities analysts expect structures investment to slow in 2024 and into early 2025. They track on-shoring/re-shoring project by project. We also expect the policy uncertainty surrounding the election to lead to some postponement of investment. Regulatory and tax policy uncertainty has been shown to weigh on economic growth and business investment.

According to the the NFIB, their small businesses optimism index remained below its long-term average for the 26th consecutive month in February. The share of firms that think now is a good time to expand fell 5 pp to 5%. Core capital goods orders have slowed to flat. Hence the risk to the economic expansion: if consumer spending pulls back, there's not much left to carry the expansion forward.

## Government: flipping to drag

In the next chunk of the economy (the G in Y=C+I+G) the federal government has put in place spending caps and once flush state governments are coming under pressure too, after revenue shortfalls in 2023. The fiscal policy support to growth, which we think topped 1 pp in 2023, we expect to fade in 2024, an important part of the economic contour underpinning our expected slowdown in growth.

Spending caps limiting federal spending growth have been put in place. State government revenues were down over 10% in 2023.

We updated our fiscal projections last month with the release of the CBO's revised budget projections, with earlier explanation in our November annual outlook. In the recent update, we "nudged" down our expectations for the deficit this year and next by roughly \$100 billion in each year, from -\$1.825 trillion and -\$1.894 trillion in FY2024 and FY2025 respectively, to -\$1.659 trillion (-\$1.734 trillion adjusted for the timing shift) and -\$1.780 trillion in FY 2024 and FY2025, respectively. Even with the narrower budget deficit, we expect the deficit as a share of nominal GDP to top 6% in each of the next two years. In the years between World War II and the pandemic, the deficit only widened beyond 5% in the years during or immediately following a recession. Outside the GFC, recession widening never pushed the deficit past 5.9%. Having deficits run 6% or more during a period of low unemployment is unusual and we would argue, somewhat worrisome.

## Potential growth boosted by population growth

We recently published a deep dive on US population growth, in the wake of a recent report from the Congressional Budget Office estimating a surge in net international migration has boosted US population growth. While we agree with the broad direction of the CBO's conclusions, for several reasons detailed in the publication we did not take on board the full magnitude of their estimates. We also said that while more population growth should boost potential GDP growth, there are important limitations to the narrative that the surge in net international migration fully explains the expansion's resilience. Plus, an input into potential GDP growth may not feed into actual GDP growth one for one, for example. We did estimate that the added population growth helped ease supply tensions in the labor market, and mitigated nominal wage pressures where the labor market was tightest.

## Labor market: slowing close to stall speed

We expect the labor market to slow in the coming quarters in line with our slowdown in growth, and we have removed the expectations for a negative payroll print from our projections. We think the risks are downward, but the timing has eluded us, and the resilience of the expansion surprised us. Looking ahead, we expect the added labor supply of immigration to help keep the labor market from tightening further in the coming quarters alongside a slowdown in hiring consistent with our slowdown in final demand.

We expect the labor market to slow, but if final demand holds up alright, the labor market should too.

As we noted above, the indicator that most forced our hand in revising our economic projection was the weekly filings of initial claims for unemployment insurance, and the change in continuing claims. We believe there is substantial potential for mismeasurement in a range of economic data, including the claims data. However, even considering the trends in eligibility and claiming, we see little reason for the comparisons to the 2018 and 2019 period to have broken down to such an extent that initial claims filings should be structurally 20K or more lower now than then. As the chart below shows, even some handicapping would leave claims in that range. Note that we would think initial claims at 250K pretty worrisome, but the 4-week moving average was below 210K in last week's data. Also, we do not think one needs to see layoffs rise for employment to contract. Assume just hiring slows. The stock of people collecting UI benefits should rise from a slowdown in people becoming employed. The usual flow of layoffs would no longer flow into new employment as easily, leading more people to file initial claims for benefits. But either way, some move in the claims data should corroborate weakness elsewhere in the labor market if it is really weakening.

We think NFP is overstating job market gains. However, if true NFP was under 100K per month, transitions from layoffs into employment should slow enough to see an impact on the claims data.

In the rear view mirror, the strength is well explained. The strength of the labor market in 2023 was roughly consistent with the strength in real GDP growth. The productivity chart above shows output per hour well aligned with trends. If output in the private sector and hours worked are so well aligned, what's the conundrum? We do not think there is one. After a period of unprecedented catch-up, followed by patterns consistent with labor-hoarding, the labor market is now realigned with demand.

The anecdotes of worker shortages have diminished substantially and, along a wide variety of metrics, the labor market has normalized. For example, initial and continuing claims are back to pre-Covid ranges. Layoff announcements have moved up and have been above the pre-Covid pace. The quit rate has come back down into a more normal range. Although still high, it has followed nominal wage inflation lower and a somewhat elevated quits rate would be consistent with nominal wage growth remaining brisk. In the charts below, continuing claims, initial claims, WARN notices that represent firm layoff plans, layoff announcements, the workweek, all have moved back to a prepandemic range, or somewhat softer.

The question is what lies ahead. At this point in the expansion, the labor market seems likely to follow output. If final demand growth stays strong, the labor market will too. If final demand growth weakens, the labor market will follow. The leading indicators we track look weak. The figure below shows our "leading series" in blue against "other series" in red. We strip out the 16 subcategories of nonfarm payroll employment that most reliably lead others ahead of turning points (contraction in private payroll employment). More than half of the leading sectors are in contraction, which usually signals slowdown ahead.

Another complication we face in forecasting is the gap between what is actually happening and what is being measured. The first row of charts shows what has been happening and what we expect to happen. The left shows a wide gap opening up between the household survey measure of employment, which is used in computing the unemployment rate, and NFP. Based on the QCEW, it looks to us like NFP is overstating the gains in employment. We would not be surprised if the gap was 50K per month. We get the Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages (QCEW) on May 22, for the next checkup. We also show below that NFP overstated employment gains for the 7 months we show in 2008 by nearly 800K. That may be an extreme example, but something to keep in mind. To the right of that chart we show the imputations being made to NFP. By our calculations, a model based imputation, the residual net birth death adjustment is adding roughly 115K per month. As we wrote a few weeks ago, NFP could be declining and we may not know it in real time.

Overall, our expectation for the labor market is that if consumer spending holds up, that should keep the virtuous circle of job gains and rising real labor incoming supporting consumption alive. However, if consumer spending softens because consumers run out of steam as savings deteriorates, or pre-cautionary saving rises, or the level of interest rates further weighs on consumption growth, the business sector would likely slow too, and with that labor demand, and hiring.

People often say that the labor market is a backward looking indicator. It can be. Also, hiring slowing tends to precede or lead the unemployment rate in rising. However, the one lone metric that remains elevated is also the most backward looking of the backward looking labor market metrics: job openings. As we show in the chart below, as we often do, job openings remain elevated and tend to hold up all the way into recessions.

There is also a question about forecasting what we think will happen or what will be measured. We may be forecasting what we think will happen. We are not confident it will be well measured.

Some labor market indicators are backward looking. Among backward looking indicators, job openings are backward looking.

## Inflation on a downward, bumpy path

US inflation continues to slow gradually, though it remains above the FOMC’s 2.0% inflation target. The 12-month change for headline PCE prices currently sits at 2.4% — just 0.4 pp above the FOMC target and roughly 90% of the way back to target from its 7.1% peak in June 2022. Core PCE inflation has also tumbled from a peak of 5.6% in February 2022 to its current rate of 2.8%.

We anticipate further slowing in the 12-month inflation rates as faster monthly increases from early 2023 drop out of the 12-month window, though for shorter windows there has been a pick up. The six-month core PCE inflation rate dropped below the FOMC's 2.0% objective in November and December, but re-accelerated to 2.5% though January and will likely to rise to around 2.9% with the release of the February data next week. That said, issues with seasonal adjustment may be leading to excess volatility at 6-month and shorter horizons.

Despite a strong start to the year, we think base effects will keep the annual rate of PCE inflation trending lower through the summer.

In spite of the strong inflation data to start 2024, there is little sign that inflation is becoming stuck at a high level. In each of the past 11 months, and 15 of the past 16 months, the not-seasonally-adjusted core CPI monthly increase has been below the monthly increase seen 12 months prior — a pattern we expect to continue over the next handful of months.

2% core PCE doesn't mean all components running at 2%.

Something that can get lost in the debate on inflation is that for core inflation to reach 2%, not all components have to be running at a 2% rate. In fact, for the 1995 to mid-2008 period core PCE inflation averaged just below 2%, but core non-rent services inflation ran above 2%, averaging 2.8%. This above target core non-rent services inflation was needed to offset core goods price disinflation for much of the period, which averaged an annual rate of -0.64% through the same period. In January, the 12- month change in core goods PCE inflation was back in deflationary territory at -0.57%, and should this return to its downward trend continue then we expect that core nonrent services inflation above 2% would still be target consistent.

## FOMC to dial back restrictiveness slowly

At the moment, the FOMC appears to have the wind in their sails as they wait for inflation to fall. The expansion has proved more resilient than we or they expected. They appear to have earned the luxury of waiting for inflation to make more progress. They also seem comfortable with a quarterly pace of rate cuts as a base case, a point we made here.

We expect core PCE inflation to fall to 2.5% in the April data release in May. Most policy rules would suggest the FOMC should be lowering the target range already, but they seem to be feeling out a short run neutral rate. If nonfarm payroll employment or GDP growth slows, that could reinforce to them that neutral has not moved up all that much, if any during the pandemic. Even though the median FOMC assumption of the longer run rate was revised up from 2.5% to 2.6% at this week's FOMC meeting, our guess is that revisions to come probably won't move above 2.75%, if our economic projections prove roughly right.

Looking at the FOMC March SEP, we still expect inflation to fall faster than the FOMC expects this year.

If the FOMC remains comfortable with the pace of growth, and inflation continues to move lower, we expect the FOMC to lower the policy rate 25 bps at the June, September, and December FOMC meeting. This sequence has the appeal of skipping acting at the meeting the week of the Presidential election. The November FOMC meeting is on the 6th and 7th, immediately following the Tuesday November 5, Presidential election. The FOMC shifted to Wednesday and Thursday as a result. The politics of acting immediately after a Presidential election could possibly be more complicated than acting immediately before. If the economy stays strong, the FOMC may wait longer than June, and lower rates in September. We view September and December a more likely two cuts this year strategy than any other combination, in part due to the timing of the Presidential election.

The FOMC began discussing plans to slow the pace of balance sheet run off at the March FOMC meeting, setting up another discussion in May, or even a decision perhaps, but on track to announce the decision by or at the June meeting, with July implementation. A quarter start might help with the tables and charts the staff makes for briefings, so a July start makes some practical sense. We expect the Fed to lower the reinvestment cap by \$10 billion per month, which would place the end of tapering in December.

Federal Reserve communications have also suggested, fairly broadly across participants, that the Fed eventually wants to return to an all-Treasury portfolio. While that might seem difficult given the sheer scale of the MBS holdings, even incrementally, participants would like to move that direction. Thus, we expect no tapering of the MBS runoff or prepayments. At some point, we anticipate that the Fed will buy Treasuries to offset the continuing MBS runoff. We also retain a number of long-held views we discussed in detail last October. Namely, shrinking the balance sheet too far raises the risk of repo instability, similar to what unfolded when reserves became scarce in September 2019. The minimum level of needed reserves is difficult to estimate, and so is the timing of reserves reaching their floor, although we anticipate that happening in Q3, creating some risks as runoff progresses.

Watching the JOLTS is a recipe for acting too late.

Without labor market weakness, or inflation below target, policy will be about calibration, not
accommodation.

Tapering balance sheet runoff to begin in July and conclude in December. We will get more clarity on the March meeting discussions when the minutes are released on April 10.

Further out, we expect the pace of rate cuts to pick up a bit in 2025, given the pace of job gains we expect to slow to roughly 100K per month. At that pace or less, the labor market would look weak, perhaps worryingly so. While easy to say that's close to trend, health care seems likely to keep adding 40K jobs per month and local government 10K. The rest of the economy would be moving up 50K per month, that's not going to be lots of little positive gains scattered everywhere. That's going to some little gains and a fai number of sectors losing jobs too. That's going to feel a lot different from where we've been. If that unfolds we would expect the FOMC to feel more confident policy is restrictive and move toward neutral. However, without inflation getting to 2.0%, and the institution being able to declare victory after the tumult of the inflation scare, doing more than simply getting closer to neutral may not be the risk assessment the FOMC makes, until more serious labor market deterioration unfolds.

## Conclusion: how does this end?

We left a number of considerations out of this note because we discuss them elsewhere, including the upcoming Presidential election. We previously reviewed the implications in an essay in our last outlook note. Our recent notes on fiscal policy, as noted above, describe the implication for tax and fiscal policy. We also did not discuss the FOMC's upcoming framework review, which we recently discussed in an economic comment. In this note we simply wanted to review our upgraded outlook for consumer spending, the follow-on upward revision to employment growth, and take on board our revised assessment of population growth.

The added population growth should be a modest tailwind for actual GDP growth, in our view, as well as boost to the nation's potential capacity. Upper income households remain flush with cash and high levels of wealth. Americans continue to spend, with a low saving rate showing no signs of moving higher, sustaining the economic expansion. Inflation should continue to fall, allowing the Federal Reserve to ease off the brakes a bit this year, and more in 2025. But the outlook is not without risks, to the upside if the good times keep rolling, or the much-discussed positive productivity shock emerges.

On the other hand, there are downside risks too. Our assessment is that Americans are under-saving, that the level of economic activity has been importantly financed with a large rise in the federal deficit, and those both are supporting an elevated level of economic activity. That may not end well. Leading indicators remain weak, the expansion looks more fragile than appreciated, and likelihood of recession remains elevated. However, in the meantime, equity market valuations and healthy balance sheets are keeping the upper 20% of the income distribution flush with wealth and cash, home prices show little sign of being dented by higher mortgage rates, and credit card balances are marching higher. After we have spent 16 months forecasting an economic contraction, it’s clear, timing is, actually, everything.
