## Governor Vol-ler moves the market

Policy uncertainty remains at the forefront, spanning the rate outlook as well as balance sheet policy. Implied distributions from the options markets are blending the modal scenario (5 cuts by year end) with a 1-more-hike scenario, with roughly comparable weights. At the same time, comments by several Fed governors on Friday suggest that the timing of QT taper, the composition of reinvestments once QT ends, and the terminal size of the RRP facility are all as yet unsettled topics. This is noteworthy, since balance sheet policy should remain in focus in coming months

## Governor Vol-ler moves the market

This was a mostly quiet week, with yields and spreads almost unchanged as of Thursday's close, but Friday proved to be different. Catalyzed by comments by several Fed speakers, swap spreads widened, yields declined and the yield curve steepened on Friday (Figure 1). Governor Waller commented that the Fed's balance sheet should eventually gravitate back to being mostly short duration in nature, as it was prior to 2008. This would allow the Fed greater balance sheet flexibility in addition to better aligning its portfolio income with the policy rate that it uses to conduct monetary policy (Figure 2). He also reiterated a preference for a UST-only balance sheet. Also noteworthy was Dallas Fed President Logan's comments regarding RRP, where she noted that it would be appropriate to slow the pace of QT as RRP drains, while also noting that the appropriate long run size of the RRP is close to zero.

Of course, these guiding principles for the Fed's balance sheet policy are not new. Indeed, the Dec 2018 FOMC meeting transcripts indicate that similar ideas (i.e., a UST-centric balance sheet as well as a concentration in short maturities) were discussed even back then. Moreover, the O/N RRP facility was only scaled up out of necessity during a period of rapid Fed balance sheet expansion and leverage constraints in the banking system, and its ideal size likely remains something close to zero from the Fed<sup>’</sup>s perspective. This view is not in conflict with an acknowledgement of the short run reality that Reserves have become inelastic at higher levels and some nonzero minimum level of RRP balances might prove necessary for well-functioning money markets in the near term. Indeed, our Fed balance sheet forecast reflects the notion that QT will cease when O/N RRP drops to below \$300bn simply as an acknowledgement of this short term reality (see What<sup>’</sup>s the rush, 2/23/2024).

To us, this suggests that we are in a time of significantly elevated policy uncertainty, spanning the rate outlook as well as balance sheet policy. This week's Fed-speak serves as a reminder that balance sheet policy is up for discussion in coming months. But this dimension of policy uncertainty comes on top of already elevated uncertainty around the policy rate outlook. As we have been highlighting in recent weeks, forward OIS rates merely tell us the average of future policy paths, but do not tell us much about the range of possibilities being averaged over. To glean some insight into the latter, we must look at the probability distribution that is implied by the options markets. In Figure 3, we repeat this exercise and solve for the weights we must attach to scenario-specific Normal distributions such that the combined distribution is consistent with the prices of calls and puts on Dec 2024 SOFR futures. These weights give us information about the range of policy scenarios that are being priced into markets. As seen in the Figure, policy uncertainty remains considerably even as the modal scenario (defined here as the scenario with the largest weight) remains the same (in which the Fed cuts five times by year end). But the difference between the largest and second largest weights is quite small, and the second scenario actually involves one hike by year end. In other words, we are in a state of heightened sensitivity to economic data, because small reassessments of weights assigned to widely separated scenarios can cause forwards to move by a lot.

On the swap yield curve, this causes us to seek three characteristics in our trading themes. First, given the heightened policy uncertainty at the short as well as long end of the curve, we do not favor outright curve exposure and instead recommend well hedged trades that seek to isolate carry. This is because yield curves are likely to be significantly volatile even if ultimately rangebound. Second, given that forward rates at the very front end of the curve are now rather elevated relative to modal expectations of future outcomes, we recommend favoring trades that derive their carry from assumed roll-up that is further forward. In other words, being underweight the Fronts (which still offers positive carry because of still-higher spot yields but this could fail to materialize if forecasted modal outcomes for the funds rate are realized) is less desirable than being underweight Reds, as an example. Third, volatility tends to create mispricings, and we recommend trades that are designed to take advantage of such relative value opportunities.

One example of a trade that combines all these elements is a SFRM5 / Blues flattener paired with a 110% risk weighted 3M forward 2s/10s steepener. As seen in Figure 4, the 15Mx3M / 3Yx1Y curve is very well correlated to the 3M forward 2s/10s swap yield curve, and is currently slightly mispriced (\~3.5bp too steep) relative to this relationship. Moreover, the 15Mx3M / Blues curve offers nearly 25bp of slide over 3 months, while the 3M forward 2s/10s curve steepener has about 17bp of negative slide over the same horizon. This means that the package, even net of the 110% risk weighting, offers nearly 6bp of positive slide over a quarter. Last but not least, carry on this trade derives from assumed roll up in the Reds sector, which is preferable as discussed above. Therefore, given this combination of attractive features, we recommend this trade (see Trade recommendations).

More broadly, this trade is one among a broader set of trades that attempt to take advantage of curves that appear mispriced relative to each other. As seen in Figure 5, there are several pairs of curves involving SOFR futures that are well correlated to each other but with one curve being too flat with respect to the other. For instance, the 8th/11th SOFR futures curve is very well correlated to the 7th/10th SOFR futures curve, but currently looks about bp too flat. This naturally motivates the weighted combination (the 8th/11th contract steepener paired with a beta-weighted 7th/10th contract flattener) as a well-hedged way to isolate relative value. But in addition, the package also offers attractive carry (\~5bp of slide over 3 months) and has a high carry-to-risk ratio. Lastly, all these trades derive their carry from roll-up characteristics further out the curve, which again is a desirable feature as we noted above. Therefore, we recommend trades such as these - in particular, we recommend 85% risk-weighted SFRZ5/SFRU6 flatteners paired with SFRH6/SFRZ6 steepeners. A very similar trade is buying the belly of a 37%/63% weighted SFRU5 / SFRM6 / SFRH7 butterfly (see Trade recommendations).

With Dallas Fed President Logan reiterating her view that moderating QT's pace would be appropriate as RRP balances fall, and with Gov. Waller's enjoinder to move towards a UST-only balance sheet and away from MBS, swap spreads widened across much of the curve on Friday. But perhaps the most material comment of all was Gov. Waller's mention of a desire to shift towards a shorter maturity. Should this ultimately become reality, the Fed could reinvest maturing USTs and MBS paydowns (once QT ends) mostly in shorter maturity USTs, rather than across the curve as it has historically done in recent years.

Friday's Fed-speak developments serve as a reminder that balance sheet policy is far from settled right now. To be sure, these ideas are not new - the topic of steering towards a Treasuries-only balance sheet as well as a desire to shrink portfolio maturity was discussed in late 2018, as we now know and as we noted earlier. Also, President Logan's acknowledgement of the O/N RRP program's short term necessity as well as its long run ideal size of zero are not surprising, although this is an area where the Fed has yet to clearly articulate its policy goals. But given that these comments come at a time where balance sheet policy is being actively contemplated ahead of upcoming FOMC meetings, it appears likely that these decisions are very much in play.

What does all this mean for swap spreads? Should President Logan's comments regarding the short term need for RRP carry the day, this would affirm the notion that QT would likely need to proceed at a slower pace and also halt (at least temporarily) when a low threshold is reached (which is exactly what we assume in our forecast for the Fed's balance sheet). All else equal, this would bring back reinvestment demand for USTs, which is a positive for swap spreads across the curve. But perhaps more meaningfully, should the Fed also decide to actively target a shorter portfolio maturity (i.e., faster than achieving through simple portfolio aging), this would suggest that the Fed's post-QT reinvestment of MBS paydowns and maturing USTs could skew towards the short end, versus the baseline expectation of proportional reinvestment across all new issue tenors. If ultimately realized, this is supportive of wider spreads at the front end and a flatter spread curve.

But it is important to recognize that such decisions are far from final, and are not even imminent. We expect QT to continue all year, meaning reinvestments only come into play next year, after nearly half of the life of a current 2-year note. Moreover, over such a longer term horizon, we must also contemplate the outlook for Treasury's issuance sizes as this can easily offset the impact of any tweaks to the Fed's reinvestment strategy. Thus, we do not see today's developments as underpinning a particularly strong case for wider spreads at the front end.

Rather, the stronger case for a widening bias at the front end rests on T-bill issuance, which is poised to turn negative. In Figure 6, we present a slightly revised model for 2Y maturity matched swap spreads. The two main changes we make to the model we have hitherto used are (i) the inclusion of a factor capturing T-bill net issuance to properly capture impacts from this fairly volatile driver, and (ii) a modification that allows linear as well as quadratic dependence on RRP balances, to properly account for the nonlinear nature of this factor's impact over wide ranging RRP balance values. As seen in Figure 7, this model is much better at explaining 2Y swap spreads in recent months, but suggests that spreads are still slightly wide.

Looking forward, we believe the fast-approaching negative Tbill issuance in 2Q24 is a strong reason to consider a widening bias in 2Y swap spreads. However, given valuations that appear wide and the risk of some near term retracement of Friday's spread widening (which we see as premature), we stay neutral on 2Y swap spreads as we await better entry levels to initiate widening exposure in coming weeks. Further out the curve, spreads remain close to fair value and we also stay neutral for now on spreads in those sectors.

## Options

During a week that was relatively light on data but heavy on Fed-speak, implieds declined modestly across the surface, with 6Mx2Y declining by about 0.1 bp/day and 2Yx2Y remaining relatively flat. Of course, February itself was not light on data, and expectations around Fed policy repriced firmly this month, with markets now pricing in \~70bp of less easing over the course of the year compared to the beginning of the month. As Fed expectations have shifted, delta hedged long straddles have mostly been profitable in February, aided by a lift in implieds and positive returns from gamma net of theta (Figure 8).

Neither the lift in implieds, nor the outperformance of gamma returns over the past month is surprising to us. We have maintained a long gamma bias for a while now, which we continue to maintain going forward, due to a few reasons. First, policy uncertainty has been on the rise in the past month and continues to be elevated (Figure 3). As Figure 3 shows, there is considerable dispersion across the range of Fed policy expectations, which helps to preserve elevated jump risk and delivered volatility at the front end of the curve. In the long end, balance sheet policy uncertainty looms large as we head towards FOMC meetings where this topic will be discussed. Just today, Gov. Waller's comments about the composition of the Fed's holdings portfolio and Dallas Fed President Logan's comment about long-term level of ON RRP serve as a reminder that balance sheet policy is far from settled. Such policy uncertainty is the likely driver of the outperformance of gamma returns last month. Looking ahead, Chair Powell's testimony scheduled for Wed / Thursday and Payrolls data next week have the potential to be high volatility events given markets data and Fed-speak dependence. In addition, long volatility positions have been generally profitable heading into Payrolls in the past year (Figure 9). Also, as we noted last week, while implieds have converged towards fair value in most sectors, they still appear modestly cheap.

Last, but not least, our empirical model for projecting delta hedged long straddles continues to point to positive returns that are statistically significant (Figure 10). As a quick recap, our empirical model for delta hedged straddle returns uses the ex-ante implied volatility, ex-ante ATMF, monthly change in market depth, ex-ante Fed expectations (defined as 12Mx1M minus 1M OIS) and ex-ante short rates (defined as 3Mx3M OIS) as drivers. As the Figure shows, all the drivers are significant and coefficients have the expected signs. Our model uses currently observable factors to project future returns except in the case of market depth, for which we have to take a view. Normally, during periods of high policy uncertainty (in this instance both with respect to front and long end of the curve), we would expect market depth to decline but the opposite has happened last month - market depth has risen across all tenors and has retraced only slightly from its peak (Figure 11). This is unusual based on both the seasonal patterns (see What I tell you three times is true, 12/8/2023, Figure 10) and the elevated policy uncertainty. Given this, we do not wish to assume a decline in market depth even though we believe it is likely. Instead, we conservatively assume a modest increase in market depth when we project returns (which hurts long straddles) and find that long straddles continue to look attractive despite such an improvement in market depth. Therefore, we continue to maintain our long gamma bias and recommend buying delta hedged 6Mx10Y straddles (see Trade recommendations).
