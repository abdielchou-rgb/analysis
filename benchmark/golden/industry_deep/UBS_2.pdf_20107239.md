## Nothing to fear but the lack of fear itself?

Subdued volatility, spreads, skew and intra-market stock correlations, all speak to very strong investor belief in the continuity of Goldilocks, where implicitly strong earnings growth is propelled by a surge in productivity. However, we struggle to see that in the data. Yes, global growth has picked up, but from very depressed levels to something that looks pedestrian. Tactically, we are at extremes that historically see small negative equity returns over the next 1-3 months. We see an increased likelihood of a slowdown in the largest Tech stocks driving the market,

While we would be cautious on equities, we do see value in fixed income (US2y, UK, Korea, Mexico, Brazil): the left tail (weaker growth and more cuts) is significantly fatter than the right tail. We expect only modest downward pressure on the dollar (in H2), but strength vs EMFX (risk premia are atypically low). Synchronized central bank easing cycles may leave us in a pro-carry environment for a bit longer (long AUD, and CHF likely becoming the preferred funder), and we see credit spreads ending the year close to current levels.

## Economics Summary

## Key Messages

• We have removed the US recession from our forecast and revised inflation and the Fed funds path higher accordingly.

• Global core inflation remains highly correlated and continues to decelerate. We expect Fed, ECB and BoE cuts in June, June and August, respectively, and QT tapering in the US from May. We forecast significantly more BoJ tightening than the market.

We have removed the US recession from our forecast. We are just not seeing the degree of deterioration to indicate that the acute weakness we were forecasting for the labour market is imminent.

•The data that most forced our hand is the weekly filings of initial claims for unemployment insurance. Although that data may be consistent with sub 200k payrolls (we estimate roughly 50k overstatement in the monthly payroll data) it is not consistent with negative job growth.

•The labour market resilience, in turn, mirrors consumption where the top 20% of the income distribution appears to be propping up spending. This cohort holds 80% of financial assets, and equity valuations and healthy balance sheets are keeping this group flush with wealth and cash. Pre-pandemic the top 20% drove 40% of consumption and currently it may be driving even more.

•Removing the mid-year contraction from our forecast lifts Q4/Q4 growth in ‘24 from 0.4% to 1.2%, and our core PCE forecast from 1.9% to 2.3% (vs the Fed’s 2.6% forecast). Our new Fed funds forecast for this year is the same as the Fed/ market pricing (3 cuts) but next year we have 6 cuts (vs 3 in the dot plot) as we’re retained a bit more growth and labour market slowing than they assume.

•The risks to our forecasts are to the downside. Signs of households under stress are spreading: goods consumption has flatlined in Q1; a record number of early withdrawals were made from 401k accounts in ’23; financial distress on credit cards and auto loans is back at GFC levels (Fed survey data); and the bottom 80% of the income distribution are running liquid assets (relative to spending) about 1 stdev below historical average. Also household survey employment has been contracting the last 6 months; and capex intentions remain very weak (equipment investment is contracting), consistent with the still restrictive interest rate environment.

The US revision pushes our global growth forecast for 2024 from 2.8% to 3%. That is just a touch above where our global nowcast model is currently tracking median global growth. The nowcast is showing a clear growth acceleration YTD (from 1.6% in Nov to 2.7%; roughly the long run average), but it is entirely concentrated in the ‘consumer’ portion of the (300 variable) dataset. Every other data bucket is running below its long run average. That is consistent with declining inflation improving spending power and consumer confidence, and restrictive monetary policy still weighing on the other parts of the data.

Global inflation continues to decline despite the world still being close to full employment. The monthly annualized run-rate for global core inflation is just 50bp away from landing at pre-pandemic levels (i.e. less than 5bp on a MoM print), and on a trimmed mean basis every single component of core inflation in the 47-country dataset has improved over the last 3 months. The high degree of co-movement in the data remains consistent with dissipating common shocks (food, energy, pandemic), but also the impact of synchronized tightening.

The SNB was the first G10 central bank to cut, helped by a very significant downward revision to their inflation forecast. Other DM central banks are moving closer to easing as well. The Fed we think follows in June, though Powell declined the opportunity to fully rule out May in his press conference; we think the main policy action in May is the start of QT tapering (a \$10bn per month reduction in caps until year end). The ECB, likewise, seems to want to start cutting in June; profits and incoming inflation are in line with their forecast, but they seem to want a slightly longer sequence of declining core inflation before they start. The BoE we have penciled in for August, mainly because the wage data is still running too hot. But the vote shift at the last MPC (hawks no longer voting for hikes) and Bailey’s interview with the FT suggest June is in play. We still believe RBA and RBNZ follow later this year, despite recent inflation data being a bit better.

The BoJ has exited negative interest rate policy and YCC. We believe the next hike (to 0.25%) is in October, followed by further hikes to 1% in ‘25. We expect JGB purchases to continue at a JPY6trn monthly pace for now.

Eurozone: Following 0.5% in 2023, we forecast GDP growth of 0.6% for 2024 and 1.2% in 2025. ## Derivative Strategy

## Key Messages

 Macro DE-correlations may be ending. RE-collection risk is rising.

 Services/manufacturing and yield-driven divergences are abating. These macro events suppressed correlations as well as volatility and vol-of-vol.

Historically, when growth and inflation slowed and rate cuts were delivered, it was the outcome for jobs (soft/hard landing) that determined whether volatility remained low (10-12%) or more regularly in the 20-30% range.

## Economics and equity volatility

Our economics team have updated their outlook for the US economy. They see a soft landing in inflation and growth through the rest of 2024E before a modest recovery in growth through 2025E. This should enable rate cuts (three this year but more next year) to alleviate economic pressures without any material rise in unemployment. They forecast the unemployment rate to rise to 4.1% later this year (currently 3.7%).

We have developed a series of Cookbooks that seek to quantify the impact of each. Our Macro Cookbook looks at four economic variables back to the 1960s. It seeks to understand the impact of the level and the change in the variables on the equity market return distribution. That's 256 potential regimes and return distributions.

## A precarious soft landing

The mean estimate of S&P 500 volatility using our Macro Cookbook is for volatility to be around 20%. However, the distribution of possibilities around this appears unusually high − at just one standard deviation the vol estimate ranges from low teens up to 30%. That doesn't sound super helpful but it is clear that the trajectory of unemployment is critical to the volatility outcome in markets.

The forecast of a very modest rise is unemployment is historically rare − usually rising unemployment has a more convex reaction to initial job losses. The current state and forecast changes in growth/inflation/rates would imply volatility should be 20-30% if unemployment rose materially. But if unemployment doesn't rise, the growth/inflation/ rates regime we forecast would be more consistent with volatility of just 11-12%. In other words, the 'landing' scenario matters quite a lot for volatility.

## Expect higher volatility-of-volatility

Covid lockdowns severely disrupted services/manufacturing cycles. In addition, rapid shifts in bond yields have had divergent impacts across sectors. If both those drivers dissipate (as we think they will), correlations should return to normal with macro factors normally a driver of higher correlations.

Single stock volatility is justifiably low − corporate quality has dramatically improved. That should limit the risk of sharp/persistent spikes but a series of macro-led volatility gyrations could be a tradable opportunity (VIX vol) from current levels.
