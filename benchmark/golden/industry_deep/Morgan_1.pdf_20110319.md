Spreadbites: This Summer’s Vol Spike
and What It Says About Market

Over the past week, the Treasury curve steepened further and mortgages outperformed corporates as the start of the easing cycle draws nearer. However, markets continue to debate whether the Fed’s first move will be a 25bp or 50bp ease. The odds of a 50bp cut fluctuated considerably this week, reaching a low of 10% in the wake of the firmer-than-expected August CPI report on Wednesday before closing Friday pricing in a more than 45% chance following the release of Wall Street Journal and Financial Times articles which cited support for a 50bp ease among many former Fed officials and advisors (Figure 1). What the FOMC ultimately decides is not clear, but we are sticking with our call that they will do the ‘right thing and cut 50bp.

Importantly, our 50bp call is predicated on three key details. First, the broad and continued disinflationary process has likely bolstered Fed’s confidence that inflation will return to their 2% target overtime. While CPI inflation came in slightly firmer than consensus in August, with the core index rising 0.28% last month (consensus: 0.2%), we expect core PCE

Turning to the SEP, if our 50bp cut is realized we expect the Fed to guide markets towards expecting another 50bp of easing this year, 150bp cuts in 2025, and that the 2026 and 2027 median dots will remain near the long-run dot at 2.7-3.0%. In the event they take the timid approach and trim 25bp next week, this year’s median dot would likely only show 75bp of easing. Our economists anticipate minimal changes in the growth or inflation forecasts for both this year and the coming years. GDP growth is expected to remain consistent with the June SEP, maintaining a trend growth estimate of 1.8%. Core PCE inflation is projected to be 2.8% this year, tapering to 2.0% by 2026. This year’s forecast might decrease by a tenth of a percentage point, but the overall profile is likely to remain the same. The unemployment rate projection for the fourth quarter of 2024 was previously 4.0%, and it is expected to be revised up by two-tenths of a percentage point to reflect recent developments (see FOMC preview, Michael Feroli, 9/13/24).

Taking these factors into account, we think the risks skew towards lower yields, even after the recent moves at the very front end of the Treasury curve. Certainly yields could reverse higher is the FOMC delivers a 25bp cut, but we think a smaller-sized cut would likely be accompanied by dovish comments during the press conference which would limit the ability for yields to rise. These dynamics favor a bullish stance and we recommend maintaining 3s/30s steepeners. Previously, we have shown that front-end anchored steepeners tend to outperform in aggressive easing cycles. Though our forecast continues to look for a soft landing into 2025, even 250bp of easing back to neutral over a 9-month period is likely to support more broad-based steepening than observed in the shallow cycles when the Fed cut by 75bp in 1995 and 2019. Moreover, position technicals are likely not an impediment to lower yields as long duration exposure is not pervasive or stretched right now (see Treasuries).

In inflation markets, TIPS outperformed sharply following a firmer August CPI, a partial rebound in energy prices, and rising market expectations of a larger 50bp rate cut next week. Nonetheless, we think the recent outperformance has limited room to run, given that the data this week did little to shift the balance of risks, in our view, especially as the strong 0.50% m/m pace of OER in August is unlikely to be sustained. Five-year breakevens now appear in line with our fair value estimate, while we think further softening in labor market data ahead should drive breakevens to trade with a discount. We add 5s/10s breakeven curve steepeners as a lower-beta expression of our tactical bearish view, while taking advantage of relative value along the curve. One risk to this bearish view on front-end breakevens is the potential for energy prices to continue higher over the near term. Hence, we recommend pairing curve steepeners with an energy hedge (see TIPS).

Unsurprisingly our measure of policy uncertainty as derived from a deconstruction of option-implied probability distributions has risen over the past week, as evident in the diminishing weight placed on the 125bp cuts within the Z4 SOFR contract (Figure 3). But with near-term uncertainty likely to collapse into either 25bp or 50bp states of the world at next week’s FOMC meeting, we think it is prudent to position in conditional trades for both higher and lower yields. In the event of a 25bp cut and higher yields, we recommend initiating 1s/3s conditional flatteners using 3M expiry payer swaptions, while in the event of a 50bp cut and a steeper curve, we like 1s/10s conditional steepeners using 3M expiry receiver swaptions, financed through selling 24% of the risk in 3Mx7Y receiver swaptions to make the package premium neutral. Similarly, elevated near-term policy uncertainty is bullish for volatility for now, but the potential for next week’s FOMC meeting to produce greater clarity could result in reduced risk premia and we like to hedge through long equity futures. We recommend buying 1Yx10Y swaption straddles on a delta hedged basis, coupled with carefully weighted long position in S&P500 futures. Along the same vein, we can also turn to the futures basis to find opportunities. We recommend buying the USZ4 factor-weighted CTD basis which resembles a call option on forward repo rates and thus provides asymmetric exposure to a larger rate cut. At the same time, to gain asymmetric bearish exposure to a smaller cut, we recommend buying the WNZ4 CTD factor-weighted basis which would benefit from increased wildcard optionality in the event of a selloff (see Interest rate derivatives).

Turning to money markets, we observe that short-duration bonds funds have experienced strong inflows, with \$12.4bn in July marking the largest monthly net inflow in three years. This upward momentum is not surprising given strong performance over the past few months, as short-term bond funds have outperformed prime MMFs by as much as 2.2%-pts on a 1yr total return basis. With markets squarely anticipating an easing cycle to begin shortly, we think investors will extend to benefit from lower yields, but this should not come at the expense of MMF AUMs over the near-term. Meanwhile, we note that credit spreads have remained relatively stable in recent weeks despite the compliance deadline for mandatory liquidity fees less than a month away. We anticipate that bank CP/CD spreads will remain stable as we approach the October 2 MMF reform deadline. Should spreads widen due to any event driven reasons, we expect the extent of the widening to be limited, given the substantial amount of cash in the front end and a diversified buyer base ready to step in (see Short term fixed income).

Mortgages performed well this week, leaving spreads at relatively tight levels, particularly for coupons just below par. At the index level, excess returns have been on a fairly sustained upward trend and have again edged out corporates YTD (Figure 4). Despite this outperformance, recent roll cycles have been weak and might reflect a dearth of balance sheet. Last month’s prepayment report showed signs of life in higher coupon conventionals and Ginnie premiums. Looking ahead, lagged application volumes have picked up, boosted partially by UWM’s pricing promotions. The 15-20bp rally since early August has likely brought in a new set of lower WAC borrowers to the refi window and this could mean activity on the stack shifts down in coupon. We continue to prefer 2.5s-3s and 5.5s-6.5s to the belly (see Agency MBS).

Within mortgage credit, we expect spreads at the top of the capital structure to remain rangebound, using any gaps higher as buying opportunities. Further down the credit spectrum, spreads have remained tight amid very limited supply. Against this background, some clients frustrated with the lack of opportunities to traditional CRT have ventured into CLN/ CDS to get exposure to wider spreads, but at the cost of limited supply and liquidity. Consequently, even if we begin to see softer home price prints in 2H24, credit is not likely to materially price wider (see RMBS).

On the regulatory front, Fed Vice Chair Barr gave a speech on Tuesday detailing how the Fed, OCC, and FDIC have adjusted the Basel III Endgame (B3e) proposal. While we await the full document to be released next week (which will be followed by a 60-day comment period), we review here four major takeaways from the speech. First, total capital build for GSIBs should be much smaller at 9% versus the 19% originally proposed. Second, Category IV banks will lose the AOCI filter but otherwise will be exempt from B3e changes. This filter has been able to shield banks from mark-to-market losses on AFS holdings and thus disincentived them from selling underwater securities. By forcing banks to recognize those losses over a four-year period, this change could remove a major hurdle to portfolio rebalancing and ‘unlock’ hundreds of billions of low coupon mortgage collateral held by Cat III&IV banks. Third, a recalibration of GSIB scores could decrease total capital for some institutions and counteract some of the increase in risk capital from B3e. That said, risk capital remains a binding constraint over the near term, and we expect the banking sector will continue to prefer Ginnies over conventionals in the near term. Fourth, risk weights for mortgage loans will be recalibrated lower to converge more closely with Basel adoption in Europe, particularly for sub-80 LTV loans (Figure 5). This suggests banks are more likel to retain loans on balance sheets, reducing securitization and thus RMBS issuance. More granularly, this proposal could make banks more competitive on pricing jumbo collateral and reduce risk transfer transactions, indicating muted CLN supply.

High grade spreads have remained rangebound over the past week as markets debate the size of the upcoming easing cycle while the economic data continue to point to solid growth. Fund inflows have been strong over the prior few months and, with HG bonds returning 1.6% MTD (5.4% YTD), and this should draw further interest, especially with yields still close to 5%. That said, rising dealer balances and the fact that new issues are only 1bp tighter than at issuance over the past fortnight suggests some of this demand has already been filled. Issuance has slowed over the past week, but \$100bn of supply MTD suggests we could exceed the historical \$127bn September average. Our strategists review 2Q24 credit fundamentals this week, which on net send a positive message with revenue and EBIDTA rising while leverage is stabilizing (Figure 6, see HG Credit Fundamentals: 2Q24 Review, Eric Beinstein, 9/10/24). With cooling labor markets, attention in credit markets will soon shift to 3Q earning reports. Despite the potential for elevated volatility with key Fed, election, and earnings event risk on the near-term horizon, we think the technical and fundamental backdrop should be supportive for HG spreads, so long as the economic data hold up. Our economists expect GDP growth will slow but not break over the next year, reaching a 1% trough in 4Q24 before rebounding to 1.8% by 2Q25, and we think this environment should be good enough for credit to do well. The combination of aggressive Fed easing and stable growth should benefit HG over equities. That said, we do not think spreads can tighten much further and trade near our 110bp YE target (see Corporates).

Down the capital structure, high yield bond spreads rose 8bp over the past week to 378bp. This comes despite the S&P500 posting a 3.5% gain this week, resurfacing HY fund inflows, and active capital market activity. As we head into the upcoming easing cycle, we note that HY bonds and loans historically perform well in the 3 months following the first cut, but are mixed on a 12-month horizon, when looking over the last five easing cycles (Figure 7). Also not surprisingly, dispersion among ratings increases further into the Fed’s easing cycle with higher quality credits outperforming weaker credits as economic conditions soften. For example, BB, B, and CCC bonds provided gains of +5.9%, +3.9%, and +0.7% in the 12-months stretch following a Fed cut. What could be different this time around is a Fed which could successfully engineer a soft landing. That said, spreads are \~150bp inside where they typically reside leading into an easing cycle (see High Yield).

The record pace of new CLO origination alongside heavy CLO reset activity YTD has pushed out the CLO reinvestment expiry schedule. This time last year, we had estimated that the percentage of US BSL CLOs exiting reinvestment period (RP) would rise from 32% in August 2023 to 50% by YE24, but currently only 26% of US BSL CLOs have exited RP, and we expect this to marginally rise to 29% by YE24 with 33% by YE25. There is now slower liquidity run-off of demand from existing CLO vehicles as robust capital market activity has mitigated future liquidity needs, even as there are looming signs of US economic slowdown. Indeed US BSL CLOs currently hold \$15bn of loans maturing by YE25 and \$43bn by YE26, compared to \$82bn by YE25 and \$122bn by YE26 at this time last year (Figure 8). Those managers with a higher percentage of their deals still in reinvestment period tend to have lower B3/B- or lower exposure as loans. While default exposure is currently low at 0.32% (range: 0%-1.9% across managers) in US BSL CLO portfolios, we note that this could rise to the extent heavy distressed exchange activity transforms into defaults if the economic situation sours (see CLOs).

Meanwhile the delinquency rates for CRE CLO loans rose 0.8%-pts to 5% in the August remit period, the largest month-over-month change since December, driven by loans backed with multifamily collateral (which account for 72% of the entire CRE CLO market) (Figure 9). Total cash volumes and reinvestment levels continue to decline in part due to the lack of issuance and as most managed CRE CLOs are beyond their reinvestment windows. With delinquencies rising, managers have engaged in loan modification and delinquency buyouts to protect their vehicles and avoid failing note protection tests which would result in forced deleverging. Lower rates on the horizon should improve refinancing opportunities and potentially lead to a pickup in prepayment speeds. Turning to relative value, we think 5yr conduit CMBS LCF AAAs offer greater value than 10yr LCF AAAs in a soft-landing Fed cutting cycle given lower prepay penalty burdens. Meanwhile 10yr paper can outperform in a front-loaded recessionary scenario which causes NOIs to shrink and increase extension risks to 5yr deals. However such performance is asymmetric as an equal-weighted average of these scenarios leaves 5yr paper slightly cheap to 10yr, but not compelling so. We would view any broad market volatility or supply induced backup in 5yr LCF AAA spreads as a buying opportunity (see CMBS).

This week, ABS spreads remained stable as the market absorbed heavy quarter-end supply. The primary market saw substantial activity, particularly in auto ABS, with record volumes driven by broad sponsor participation across asset classes. While new issue prints are off the tights of the year, subscription levels are solid and almost all transactions continue to get done inside of guidance. In the auto ABS sector, the spread tiering for sponsor names has compressed, but the relative ranking of issuers remains largely unchanged. AAA prime auto ABS from Japanese and European automaker captives continue to post the tightest spreads, followed by domestic automakers and top banks. We believe that AAA spread tiering offers excellent relative value from a credit perspective, though liquidity varies notably across issuer programs. We favor PNFED across the capital stack as one of the country’s top credit unions with a more geographically diverse portfolio compared to smaller auto loan ABS pools from regional credit unions with significant state concentrations. In subordinated subprime auto ABS, there are notable outliers in pricing spreads but we do not recommend chasing these. Instead we prefer to stay up in IG-rated paper for better protection from weaker-than-expected consumer, labor market, and macroeconomic conditions (see ABS).
