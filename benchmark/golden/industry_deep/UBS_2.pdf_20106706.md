## Long front end Euro Area real rates

## EURi 1y1y down, nominals up?

The key question this week is what to do with front-end Euro Area linkers. 1y1y HICPx is back to lows, to just above 2% after a bounce on Friday afternoon, while US 1y1y CPI has been going up. But nominal euro rates have risen, broadly tracking US rates with a beta of close to 1 - a beta that has held for a year. The two markets that aren't talking. What could justify this divergence? ## Lower energy should feed back to higher inflation through better growth...

… But lower energy prices don't justify higher real rates. This is a second-order story, better growth story driven by a better inflation story. It's good news and the ECB should be happy to accommodate it.

## US natgas is down even more

Finally, it is also worth noticing that natural gas prices aren't only falling because another winter is behind us and the gas stores are plump. European gas is down because US natgas has fallen by more than half over Q1. The US CPI sensitivity via propane and piped gas is less than in Europe: only about 1%. But the historical implied sensitivity for CPI appears to be similar to Europe's because passthrough is more direct. But US breakevens don't appear to have noticed.

## This all motivates two expressions:

## 1. Long front end euro rates

When inflation expectations are low and rates are high, it doesn't sit well for them to diverge further still. The path of front end real rates is shown for major currencies in Figure 16. Europe's 1y real rate is 1.85% (based on 3m €STR and forward looking y/y HICP). That falls to 0.5% in 1y1y which is a lot more reasonable. But even on the assumption of a rebound to trend growth, which seems consensus, 0.5% is at the high end of 'neutral'.

Trade idea: Receive 2y real rate swaps at 1.15% (IRS vs 6m Euribor against HICPx zero coupon). Target 0.9%. Stop 1.25%. Note that carry about as negative as being long 2y outright. The inflation component doesn't make it worse, but 2y swaps carry -50bp over 6 months. This is a tactical trade and needs validation.

The obvious cash expression for a long real rate view is in 2026 BTPeis. DBRei26s also look attractive. Figure 5 above and Figure 29 below illustrate. The BTPei May 26 is the version we will track in the trade table.

## 2. Long EURi breakevens against USDi

Tactically US into EUR breakevens look attractive again too.

Trade idea: Tactically we like 1y1y EURi against USDi. Now 33.5bp, look for convergence to 22bp. Stop at 40bp.

Trade idea: Alternatively we like 1y1y/2y3y flatteners in EURi against USDi. The 1y1y/2y3y box is now +1.5bp (USDi steeper than EURi). Look for that to return to +15bp. Stop -5bp. Figure 6 shows the two legs of this trade.

Interest in US-EUR divergence trades are popular and understandable. We are more cautious about the theme but aren't sure that it is consensus enough to recommend the opposite, particularly in longer-term breakevens. Our momentum model for 5y5y still sends a strong buy signal. On the theme of the US CPI steepening vs EUR HICPx flattening, we also like 1y1y/5y5y. But in optimising the expression, we note that 2y3y (or 5y) USDi looks cheap on the curve. Figure 7 shows 1y1y/2y3y/5y5y which will tend to go up when the 5y point performs well on the curve.

## Digesting January's diminished deflation

## January posed questions

In case you didn't have your fill last week and want to go back over the fundamentals, there is a lot of material from UBS to digest: Alan Detmeister's monthly must-read and videos with Alan (US), Anna (EUR), and Manik (EM) that address the concern that disinflation is slowing. For the developed markets, the bottom line is summarised in the market-vs-forecast charts (Figures 17, 18, 19) below, as always.

## Disinflation remains, but inflation markets see more, especially in the US

From a strategy perspective, the bottom up estimates continue to suggest that there is value in US linkers. To that we'd add that short-end TIPS look cheap: the Oct-24s pick up around 35bp compared to bills (although that pick up has to be set against the risk of six months of fixings.) In Europe we don't have the easy long-short signal we'd like from a divergence between this bottom-up work and market expectations, but we discuss above why we see value in Euro front end.

## Wage and growth sensitivity tell us what to watch and upside risks

Our economists' discussions also highlight some important sensitivities. In particular, because our economics team is on the bearish side for growth, it's important to understand what more consensus outcomes might mean for inflation according to their models.

## 0.5pp of sustained disinflation relies on wages in Europe

Anna sees a wage-CPI sensitivity for Europe of around 0.3. That implies that a fall from roughly 4.5% to 3%, as projected, helps bring underlying inflation down by around 0.5pp. We need to watch the speed of that decline in wages now that we seem to be at the peak. Negotiated wages for Europe, published last week for Q4 in Europe dipped very slightly to 4.46%y/y. The Indeed postings series are shown in Figures 8 and 9.

## No recession in the US might mean 3% CPI at year end

If you assume a more consensus growth path than the mid-year recession our economists assume, Alan, gives an orientative figure of 0.3pp to add back to US inflation by the end compared to his baseline. That would put core PCE around 2.2% and perhaps 3.0% in CPI. (Note that the UBS growth path also sees a strong growth bounceback in Q4, helped by swift cuts from the Fed.)

## China exporting deflation is not a key risk

Both coincide that there is no sign at this point of China-led disinflation in the US or the Euro Area. We highlighted that Chinese PPI for consumer durables was uncorrelated with non-energy industrial goods two weeks ago.

## UBS is in-line for Europe's February Flash CPI

European flash CPIs for February next Friday are the key event in the diary for this week. Anna's preview was published earlier this morning. Her bottom-up forecast is in-line with the market (2.5%y/y headline, 2.4%y/y HICP ex tobacco.)

From the January detailed release last week not much was learned. Dispersion continues to improve (fewer high inflation series). But low and negative inflation series aren't clearly gaining quickly either. Services 3m/3m momentum fell to 2.25% according to the ECB seasonally adjusted series. But the ECB appears to miss some negative residual seasonality in January - that is the print should be seasonally adjusted higher more aggressively. We see this momentum falling to 2.6% - an improvement from 2.71% in January, but a slight one.

## On watch for pricing power, but February is probably too soon

For those who believe, as I do, that firms learned that pricing power felt good in the pandemic and might be tempted to try again, these first months of the year are important. In the US most inflation comes in Q1, starting in January. In Europe, it usually comes a little later in March and April once sales are done and spring is coming. But it's not too early to be watchful in February. (The UK is on a fiscal year cycle, so April is the key month. We'll discuss the upcoming budget in more detail next week, but indications are that we'll be more interested in supply than measures that will impact inflation.)

## January's clockwork inflation surprise. TIPS like February too.

As a final reflection on inflation indicators we have learned this week, notice that January data (released in February) seems able to surprise economists every year almost without fail. February is also a good seasonal month for TIPS. It's not clear that inflation 'surprises' cause this apparent TIPS outperformance - economists dealing badly with seasonality could be credited for outperformance in January and April. That's more likely index extensions, early-year investment patterns in key industries, and in April, the accrual of the strong February seasonal and the start of the 'driving season' that pushes up oil prices historically. Nonetheless, it's one to note in the calendar for next year. What about next month? March accrues January inflation. Historically it's not a strong month for that, but this year was reasonably good, so that might be supportive. Oil prices and risk appetite will be far more significant though, of course, and there the indications remain bullish.
