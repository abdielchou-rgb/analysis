## Election 2024: Impact on US Equities

Venu Krishna, CFA, BCI, US | Japinder Chawla, CFA, BCI, US | Rex Feng, BCI, US | Madhuresh Rai, CFA, BCI, US | Young Chen, BCI, US | Michael McLean, BCI, US

Excerpted from U.S. Equity Strategy: Election 2024: Impact on US Equities, published on June 18, 2024

Divided government is probable, with legislative gridlock limiting the fiscal policy impact to risk assets. Corp taxes at the lower bound could be a LSD tailwind to SPX EPS & a bigger headwind if raised, but both unlikely. Expanded tarifs would be a modest hit to SPX EPS, mainly via cyclical & growth sectors.

Our base case remains that control of Congress likely will be split between Democrats and Republicans, regardless of whether President Biden or former President Trump is elected. Democrats are slightly favored to win the House and Republicans favored to win the Senate. Divided government would likely result in legislative gridlock as far as enacting fiscal policies, including spending and taxes. The 2025 White House may resort to executive and regulatory actions, where possible, to advance certain policies that do not require legislation (e.g. tarifs).

The corporate tax rate is likely to remain at or around 21%. A "red wave" expansion of corporate tax cuts would be challenged by budget scores and concerns around the growing deficit. A unified Democratic government would likely seek to raise corporate taxes, though we view a "blue wave" as the least likely outcome.

Trade and regulatory implications are murkier. Former President Trump's election could present the risk of higher tarifs, though it's dificult to determine actual policies that would be enacted at this stage. A second Trump term could see marginal easing of antitrust merger enforcement compared with Biden's first term, and any broader deregulation would likely not happen immediately.

Tax implications for equities: 21 or bust? While unlikely, should the corporate tax rate fall to 15% under a unified GOP government, we estimate a 3.2% tailwind to S&P 500 EPS in such a scenario. Comm Svcs, Financials, and Industrials should benefit the most. While even more unlikely, should the corporate tax rate rise to 28% in a "blue wave", we estimate a 5.5% hit to SPX EPS, with an additional 1.9% hit to SPX EPS from the 4% tax on corporate buybacks. Tech and Healthcare are likely to feel the impact most.

Trade: mutually assured reduction. If Trump were to enact tarifs equivalent to those discussed on the campaign trail, we would expect to see retaliatory tarifs from other countries. We estimate that SPX earnings will be negatively impacted by 2.6% by the proposed Trump tarifs and another 1.2% by retaliatory tarifs. Discretionary, Energy, Materials, and Tech sectors appear to be most at risk given their strong dependency on global supply chains.

IRA: no clean getaway. Clean energy beneficiaries of IRA tax incentives could face headwinds under a second Trump term. Impacted industries include HVAC OEMs, EV auto OEMs, renewable energy, and industrial suppliers of grid equipment and services. Conversely, companies that are dependent on fossil fuels including ICE auto OEMs, coal-based utilities, oil & gas producers/ servicers, and industrial suppliers of legacy thermal power businesses could see upside in a second Trump term.

## European Equity Strategy

## Green shoots for the European consumer

Emmanuel Cau, CFA, Barclays, UK | Matthew Joyce, CFA, BBI, Paris | Magesh Kumar Chandrasekaran, CFA, Barclays, UK | Emmanuel Makonga, Barclays, UK | Arihanth Bohra Jain, Barclays, UK | Silvia Ardagna, Barclays UK | Mariano Cena, Barclays UK | Jack Meaning, Barclays UK

Excerpted from European Equity Strategy: Green shoots for the European consumer, published on June 19, 2024

We see green shoots in EU/UK consumer. More retailers are talking about trading up due to improving real income, and rates may have peaked. But the increase in real consumption will be slow and French political limbo is unhelpful short term, but the low-end consumer may win either way. We like Retail (both food/non-food).

economists expect more robust growth in real household consumption, both in the euro area and in the UK, driven by an increase in real disposable income. However, the increase both in real income and in real consumption will be gradual. At the end of our forecast horizon there will still be a notable gap versus the pre COVID-19 trend implied level, as the negative COVID-19 and energy shocks are having very persistent, if not permanent, efects on households' real income.

Macro and political developments could benefit the low-end consumer. High energy costs have dampened consumer spending over the past couple of years in Europe. However, real wages are improving as inflation keeps falling and the labour market remains resilient. Moreover, the rate-cutting cycle in the EU and UK, albeit shallow, should still provide relief to the consumer. Political uncertainty in France may hit sentiment near term, but we note that all the main parties have low-end consumer support measures at the top of their agenda.

Favour low-end consumer exposure with Retail and Food Retail, UW Autos, + new basket. Our view that low-end consumers are set to benefit more leads us to favour Retail and Food Retail, as a positive mix of rising real wages and falling interest costs should benefit disposable income. We stay UW Autos, where Q1 results and forward indicators are both weak, and have seen sentiment towards the sector dive again, while Chinese competition remains a threat. We introduce our new EU Consumer basket BCEUECON, made up of stocks from both Discretionary and Staples, with at least 50% sales exposure to Europe, screened for liquidity.

Investment Sciences: NLP analysis of Stoxx 600 transcripts by the Investment Sciences team suggests European corporate executives have adopted a more positive tone on the consumer, albeit not overly so. Looking at mentions of the consumer/consumption, and weakness/ softness, and capturing the evolving context through time, language developments appear broadly optimistic, although at the sector level, there is some negative consumption sentiment for communication services, IT, and utilities, which contrasts with more positive sentiment on consumer discretionary. Interestingly, ‘weakness’ references are more related to (macro)economic or general weakness, rather than to consumer weakness specifically.

Sectors: Our sector analysts are broadly positive towards the consumer space picking up. In Staples our team look for improving organic sales growth and volume/mix, and a tempering of private label in favour of branded goods. They feel the best way to play an improved European consumer with volume/mix drivers over the next few quarters would be Heineken and Diageo in Beverages, Unilever in Food, Beiersdorf in HPC, and Britvic. In the UK our UK SMID team favour JD Wetherspoon, Greggs, Dunelm, Victorian Plumbing, and Frasers Group (all OW rated). In UK and European Food Retail they favour Tesco, Sainsbury's and Jeronimo Martins, while in General Retail they favour H&M. In Airlines, worries about the end of post-Covid revenge spending remain, with Ryanair recently guiding summer unit revenues lower to 0-5% growth from 5-10%, although even this lowered growth guidance still shows strong consumer demand.

## FX & EM Macro Strategy Quarterly Outlook

## Of underpriced divergence and rising political risks

Themistoklis Fiotakis, Barclays, UK | Lefteris Farmakis, Barclays, UK | Andrea Kiguel, BCI, US | Mitul Kotecha, Barclays Bank, Singapore | Marek Raczko, Barclays, UK | Shinichiro Kadota, BSJL, Japan

Excerpted from FX & EM Macro Strategy Quarterly Outlook: Of underpriced divergence and rising political risks, published on June 18, 2024

The direction of our views is intact. The market is underpricing divergent levels of capacity utilisation as political risks mount. Equally, however, we think this is the time for the dollar to shine – failure to do so would require us to review our frameworks anew.

Divergence is stickier than you think: Despite better European and Chinese activity, relative output gaps with the US have not narrowed, implying diferential easing paces in favour of the USD.

A market in denial: Our r\* and Taylor Rule-based frameworks suggest that the market continues to anticipate a relatively dovish Fed compared to G10 central banks.

Limits to divergence at play? We explore mechanisms that could limit policy divergence, including FX pass-throughs, a global inflation factor, sticky services inflation and spillovers from strong US data. We find they are not binding in the current context.

From Br-eturn to Fr-exit: Domestic politics in Europe are back in the limelight. We argue that they squarely favour the USD but also CHF and GBP relative to the EUR.

EM central banks face FX tradeofs...: The EM economic pain from rate hikes and the space in EM forward rates vs the US are both limited; policy easing may weigh on FX going forward. We think fundamentals compel easing in Czech, Colombia, Korea and Philippines, while fiscal dynamics require lower yields in Mexico, Brazil, South Africa and Indonesia.

...and constraints: Yet, we show, the stock of external debt may not permit excess FX weakness in Mexico, Colombia, South Africa and Korea. Political noise may further constrain easing in Mexico and Brazil. Czech, Philippines and potentially Indonesia may have a bit more room to manoeuvre rates to the cost of the currency, while politics are currently supporting ZAR.

Food for Thought: Mind the (Earnings Growth) Gap

Venu Krishna, CFA, BCI, US | Japinder Chawla, CFA, BCI, US | Rex Feng, BCI, US | Madhuresh Rai, CFA, BCI, US | Young Chen, BCI, US

Excerpted from U.S. Equity Strategy: Food for Thought: Mind the (Earnings Growth) Gap, published on June 21, 2024

The massive Y/Y EPS growth gap between Big Tech and the rest of the SPX is expected to close by YE24/1Q25 as base efects kick in, acting as a headwind for Big Tech but a tailwind for the rest of the index. Should this materialize, it could cause market concentration to finally ease, clearing the path for 'broadening'.

FIGURE 1. Massive EPS growth delta between Big Tech and the rest of SPX is expected to close by early 2025

![](images/b011cb58ca3097f418bcc6ca5cda748621c39d078398cdf1f447da2f6ab9ec7a.jpg)
Data as of 6/18/2024
Source: LSEG Data & Analytics, Bloomberg, Barclays Research

A question we've often heard recently is "what will it take for the rally to finally broaden"? Some measures of market concentration eased earlier in 2Q, but it proved fleeting; Big Tech reconsolidated their grip on the market after earnings season proved their exceptionalism once again. As such, we continue to think earnings growth is the key, and therein see some light at the end of the tunnel for those hopeful that the rally will establish a broader base. Consensus expects Y/Y EPS growth for Big Tech to shift into a lower gear over the next few quarters; we think this is likely a result of base efect headwinds after the group's breakneck earnings growth over the last 18 months. Simultaneously, Y/Y EPS growth for the rest of the S&P 500 is expected to pick up dramatically as the opposite happens: base efects begin to act as a tailwind. Recall that the ex-Tech portion of the index has contended with earnings contraction for over a year at this point, with NTM revisions only recently turning positive. All in, Big Tech and the rest of the S&P 500 are expected to deliver similar YoY EPS growth beginning around YE24/1Q25. Provided the macro backdrop remains supportive, we think this could go a long way toward justifying a broader rally.

## Europe

Goldilocks not bothered by France

Emmanuel Cau, CFA, Barclays, UK | Matthew Joyce, CFA, BBI, Paris | Magesh Kumar Chandrasekaran, CFA, Barclays, UK | Emmanuel Makonga, Barclays, UK | Arihanth Bohra Jain, Barclays, UK

Excerpted from Equity Market Review - Goldilocks not bothered by France, published on June 21, 2024

Calm has returned to EU cross-asset markets. But a still hard-to-call outcome of the French election argues against a quick decompression in risk premia. Meanwhile, as more central banks shift to dovish mode, the goldilocks narrative remains very much intact, which continues to support the grind higher in equities.

France on hold. Cross-asset markets have stabilized since the sharp initial re-pricing last week, as investors continue to figure out the potential outcomes of the French elections, and their implications. Based on our conversations, they don't seem too worried overall, with several of our HF clients looking to buy the dip sooner than later at the single stock level, while also seeking downside protection at the index level. As we discussed in our report earlier this week French election: Assessing risk/reward, the pull-back in French markets, and the 'contagion' to countries like Italy, looked quite harsh to us, and potentially overdone given the possibility of market friendly outcomes (or not too unfriendly at least). Yesterday's uneventful French government debt sale was a relief, suggesting that markets may be indeed pricing in the current uncertainty fairly (albeit at the highest OAT-Bund spread in 7 years). However, with the latest polls keeping the far right party comfortably in the lead, followed by the left wing coalition, a hung and fiscally lax parliament remains a real possibility. Of course, under this scenario, nominating a caretaker government is an option for President Macron, which could reassure markets. And the spike in procurations (allowing a family member or friend to cast your vote for you), running at 6x the rate vs. the 2022 election, should benefit the more moderate parties. Still, we believe that France's two round election system and tactical voting at the constituency level make the final outcome hard to call, which argues against a quick decompression in risk premia.

Meanwhile, Goldilocks macro keeps pushing stocks higher. Although French politics dominate the headlines, the direction of monetary policy remains the key driver of risk appetite, in our view. Following the ECB's rate cut two weeks ago, SNB's unexpected cut yesterday and the BOE's 'dovish' hold that leaves the door open to a cut in August, have further reinforced the goldilocks narrative. Mid-cycle rate cuts tend to provide a mild stimulatory efect to the economy that can help equity earnings, while lower rates help on the valuation side. Even with some more mixed data in the last few months, and higher geopolitical tail risks, we do not see the 'Goldilocks-ish' environment being de-railed in the short run. Our macro strategists agree (see Global Outlook Calm on the surface, 20 Jun) and argue that \~trend GDP growth of around 3% is enough to maintain a preference for equities over bonds into H2. At a more granular level, we see the environment of rising disposable real income, and rate cuts, as broadly positive for the EU/UK consumer, and addressed it in our note this week (see Green shoots for the European consumer, 19 Jun).

## Orderly credit market softness

Bradley Rogof, BCI, US | Dominique Toublan, BCI, US

Excerpted from US Credit Alpha, published on June 21, 2024

Credit hit a soft patch in the last month. Market moves imply that this is not driven by credit risk fears but by lower Treasury yields and heavy issuance. We expect these two factors to become more favorable for spreads in the near term. However, spreads are still tight and there are tail risks such as the French election.

Credit spreads are trading in the middle of their year-to-date range after widening across the board over the last month. Spreads reached their cycle tights in early May across IG, HY and leveraged loans. Since then, IG is 8bp wider at 93bp, HY is 27bp wider at 316bp, and LL sold of by 31c at \$96.68. This sell-of was driven by lower Treasury yields, thanks to a low CPI print and some flight to quality on the back of the French snap poll surprise, and by heavier-thanexpected issuance.

However, the sell-of within credit has been orderly. There has been no flight to quality within credit. To the contrary, Bs outperformed BBs which outperformed BBBs which outperformed As over the last week. The BBB/A industrial spread ratio is close to 10-year lows and the lowest since March 2022, implying that investors continue to feel comfortable about credit risk, the overall macro background, and credit fundamentals.

Atlantic. The reaction has been most pronounced in the CDS indices. iTraxx Main underperformed CDX IG by over 7bp since the snap election announcement. Investors are now close to their lightest risk position in Main across the series in the last three years, but also the longest risk they have been in CDX IG during the same time period. We believe that the gap is stretched and see opportunities; we propose trade ideas in the Derivatives section. In this Alpha, we also analyze the Europe versus US situation in the IG and in HY sections. For a broader discussion of the French elections and the risks that are likely to persist until the second round of polls on July 7 and potentially even beyond, see Between a rock and a hard place.

As discussed above, lower Treasury yields and high issuance have likely been the two main factors of the recent credit sell-of. However, we believe that Treasury yields should increase and issuance should slow down in the near term. First, our rates team expects the 10y Treasury yield to increase by about 25bp to 4.5% by the end of 3Q24, as the economy is more likely to rebound than slow and with the market already pricing in material easing (see here). Second, issuance is likely to follow its usual seasonal pattern with earning blackouts and a summer slowdown. Historically, July is one of the best months for both IG and HY in terms of excess returns.

Overall, we are constructive on the global economy, but risk assets are pricing in little risk. As we discuss in our latest quarterly Global Outlook, Calm on the surface, we expect steady global growth, uneven but continued progress on inflation, and a slow easing cycle. However, credit spreads are tight and the equity markets are at an all-time high. They are pricing little risk and investors should keep a wary eye on tail scenarios.

## Europe

Ratcheting supply up

Søren Willemann, Barclays, UK

Excerpted from European Credit Alpha, published on June 21, 2024

We revise our supply forecasts up across most parts of European credit. Tellingly, in spite of supply having surprised to the upside, before the French election news, markets were tighter YTD, reflecting strong demand technicals, and the improving macroeconomic environment.

With little in the way of macroeconomic news, European credit markets are treading water going into the first round of the French election on Sunday 30 June. This can be seen in both the lacklustre primary market (Figure 8) - €IG saw only €5bn of supply in the week to Wednesday 19 June - and the fact that spreads have remained close to the levels reached on 14 June. Notably, 10yr OAT-Bunds have shown no sign at all of retracing the sell-of, while Main has tightened a little from the wides (Figure 9).

Absent polls showing the French left-wing coalition splintering, or polling badly, we expect the market to remain similarly "grind free" this coming week, with everyone looking for the results from the first round on Monday 1 July. As we discuss in detail in France election – implications for European credit (European Credit Research: France election – implications for European credit, 21 June 2024), we see three scenarios for the election outcome: (1) a hung parliament; (2) a 'cohabitation' scenario; with the tail risk being (3) a left-wing majority, albeit this looks remote given current polling.

From an economic perspective, none of these scenarios are favourable versus the status quo with potential long-run implications. However, from a markets perspective and for credit in particular, in scenarios (1) and (2) we would expect spreads to benefit from a relief rally and retrace a good portion of the recent widening. Scenario (2), in our view, would be seen positively in the sense that a RN-led government is likely to be pragmatic in the near term. In scenario (3), we think there is room for considerable further widening given a relatively radical and potentially fiscally expansionist manifesto from the far left that is likely to create friction with the EU and put the sovereign rating of France under immediate pressure.

Looking past the French election event, across this Alpha we take a fresh look at supply for the rest of 2024, with a general theme of upward revisions to earlier supply estimates.

In IG, issuance is far ahead of our initial forecast for 2024, with non-financials the main contributor and financial issuance only a touch ahead of our forecasts. Accordingly, we have revised higher our forecasts for both financials (to €330bn from €310bn) and for non-financials (to €350bn from €280bn).

Issuance of subordinated debt (AT1/T2) by banks has also surprised to the upside, particularly in Tier 2, also driven by banks having front-loaded issuance in H1 24. Our bank analysts dial up their AT1 and T2 forecasts to €33bn (€30bn previously) and €40bn (€34bn previously), respectively.

On the Covered Bond side, however, we stick to our FY24 supply forecast of €170bn, with less than €60bn (35%) of supply remaining for the rest of the year.

## Waiting isn't easy

Christian Keller, Barclays, UK | Andrea Kiguel, BCI, US | Andreas Kolbe, Barclays UK

Excerpted from The Emerging Markets Quarterly, published on June 19,  2024

Amid steady global growth but sticky inflation, markets await more clarity on the path of US rates. In the meantime, idiosyncratic developments are fuelling volatility in EM assets. We expect limited beta conviction and continued issuer diferentiation to prevail in EM credit, while EM rates should remain rangebound.

Local: The Fed remains a constraint for EM local markets, where margins of safety are still running thin and the 'easy' part of the disinflation process is behind us. Fundamentally, high rates are not causing much pain. EMs (overall) are still pricing some degree of normalization in the policy stance, but terminal rates are likely to stay priced above neutral amid a shallow cycle expected in the US. We thus expect rates to remain rangebound. Limited room for error leaves us tactically looking for policy diferentiation, but the window to capture the move is short: rates trades require getting ahead of the move in spite of negative carry costs, while weaker currencies are trades that can be chased. LatAm's moment is mostly behind us, and we favor divergence in CEEMEA and parts of Asia. Politics has become a more relevant driver for diferentiation, both in FX and rates.

Our high conviction OWs include Ghana and Sri Lanka based on our expectation that the respective restructuring processes can be successfully concluded soon, and we see further upside in the post-restructuring bonds of Zambia and Suriname (both OW). ## Economic Outlook

## Undercurrents

Christian Keller, Barclays, UK | Akash Utsav, Barclays, UK

Excerpted from Global Economics Weekly, published on June 21, 2024

In our Barclays’ Global Outlook: Calm on the surface, published this week, we emphasise how the generally favourable global macro backdrop could be challenged by political risks. With data soft this week – and euro area flash PMIs already reflecting political uncertainty in France – the focus remains on politics more broadly.

## Much political uncertainty...

In principle, the global macro backdrop is not unfavourable: our baseline is for steady global growth, as an expected sequential slowing in China data should be ofset by still resilient growth in the US and India, and some cyclical pickup in Europe. Uneven but continued progress on inflation should allow for a slow easing cycle.

However, powerful undercurrents of political uncertainty pose a risk to this seemingly calm backdrop. France is preparing for the first round of its snap elections next weekend (30 June), where the ultimate outcome is likely to an uneasy 'cohabitation' or a hung parliament. The US election season is heating up, with the first live TV debate scheduled for next Thursday, while current polls for the UK elections (4 July) suggest potentially very disproportionate results with a large mismatches between votes and seats. Germany's coalition government continues to struggle to bridge its diferences over the 2025 budget, as it announced this week that it would no longer be able to meet its self-set 3 July deadline for an agreement. The incentives of all three coalition partners make a breakup of the German government unlikely, but then again: who had predicted early elections in France?

## ...soft data...

In the US, May retail sales this week registered a disappointing 0.1% increase following April's dip. However, this mainly reflected the drag from lower gas prices, and the details were more solid than the headline suggests. The control group posted a healthy bounce-back, positioning PCE for a Q2 increase similar to Q1's 2.0% q/q saar gain. Meanwhile, industrial production exceeded consensus expectations, growing 0.9% m/m, led by broad-based gains in manufacturing, as well as solid prints for utilities and mining. This defies the downbeat signals from recent ISMs, once again placing manufacturing on a sideways trajectory over the past 12 months. Overall, we read this week's data as suggesting the economy retains solid momentum as we proceed through the second quarter.

Next week, the most important data release will be the May Personal Income and Outlays report, where we expect core PCE inflation to decelerate to 0.08% m/m while real PCE could grow 0.2% m/m. We will also watch the third estimate of Q1 GDP, house price releases, and the University of Michigan survey.

In the euro area, June PMIs disappointed this week as the composite eased by 1.4pt to 50.8, compared to the consensus expectation for a slight increase, driven by both services and manufacturing. Geographically, the decline in the PMIs was broad-based. However, the flip side of this weaker growth picture is softening price indicators despite continued resilience in employment PMIs. Next week will bring the June flash HICP for France, Italy, and Spain, the ECB's money and credit release, and the European Commission survey.

In the UK, May inflation data sent a complicated signal for policy as headline (2.0% y/y, -0.3pp) and core (3.5% y/y, -0.4pp) printed broadly in line with expectations, but services again

surprised to the upside. That said, over half of the surprise in service came from the volatile airfares component. Core goods were softer than expected and moved into outright deflation.

In Japan, CPI inflation strengthened to 2.8% y/y in May from 2.5% y/y in April, alongside a similar pick-up in core CPI (2.5% y/y, +0.3pp), driven by the hike to the renewable energy levy imposed on electricity rate. On the other hand, the BoJ core measure, which excludes perishables and energy, eased to 2.1% y/y (-0.3pp), and all three measures were slightly softer than consensus. Even so, going forward, we expect CPI inflation to prove more sustainable as services inflation remains firm due to the pass-through of higher labour costs. In addition, the rollback of energy subsidies, as well as higher oil prices and JPY depreciation, will be tailwinds for goods inflation. Accounting for the latest results, we maintain our forecasts for core CPI inflation at 2.7% y/y for FY24 and 2.0% for FY25. Next week, we will watch Tokyo CPI for June.

In China, this week's data presented a mixed picture as retail sales recovered, industrial production and fixed asset investment growth slowed, and housing market activity continued to slump. However, we think the pick-up in retail sales may not be sustainable given the one-of boost to online sales from eCommerce giants' changing promotional strategies and still-poor fundamentals<sup>2</sup> . In addition, the slowdown in industrial production suggests exports are not growing fast enough to ofset weaker domestic demand. Overall, we continue expect a sharp slowdown in growth momentum to only 0.2% q/q saar in Q2 and an average of 3.5% in H2. We maintain our base case that the 5% growth target will be achieved, but the risks are tilted to the downside.

## ...and a more dovish than expected Bank of England

As expected, the Bank of England this week held Bank Rate steady at 5.25% in an unchanged vote split (seven in favour of no change and two in favor of a cut). However, the MPC made adjustments to the guidance which, in our view, lay the groundwork for a first cut in August as the monetary policy summary and minutes de-emphasised the role of services inflation and qualified data surprises. The minutes also revealed that the decision to vote for a hold was "finely balanced" for some members. We continue to expect the Bank to begin its easing cycle in August.
