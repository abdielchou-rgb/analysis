# US election & its implications for Asia

The US Presidential election is shaping up to be among the most important geopolitical events of the decade. In a report back in February, we had explored what the outcome of this election could mean for emerging markets. The opinion polls since then continue to point to what remains a tightly contested race between President Biden and the presumptive Republican nominee and former President Trump. In this report, we zoom in more specifically on some of the key topics that the two candidates are likely to focus on in their respective campaigns as they relate to China and, more broadly, Asia.

n Policy towards China will likely remain hawkish. A strategic shift was already underway in the relationship between the US and China by the time President Trump first came to the White House; and has since spanned both the Republican and Democrat administrations. Regardless of who is elected to be the next President, we think US policy towards China is likely to be heavily influenced by an increasingly skeptical public perception of China, a country which is viewed within US policy circles as both a global strategic competitor and a challenger.

1 Three key policy topics will be in focus. The three areas of most economic consequence for US policy on China are trade, technology and investment.

More tariffs to come. Tariffs will likely remain a key trade tool deployed by the next administration. The difference between Trump and Biden would be whether the same is targeted or broadbased and whether more unilateral or possibly multilateral in nature.

n Broadening the 'small yard, high fence' strategy. Restrictions might no longer be limited to high-tech fields but could potentially be broadened to include all emerging technologies and non-cutting edge products considered relevant to national security.

n Access to US capital could become more restrictive. Further monitoring and control of cross-border investments between the US and China will likely persist and possibly tighten.

## China policy to stay hawkish

A strategic shift was already underway in the relationship between the US and China by the time President Trump first came to the White House. But it was his administration’s trade/investment policies that materially redefined the engagement between the two countries. This shift has largely carried over into the Biden administration. Regardless of who is elected to be the next President of the United States, US policy towards China is likely to be heavily influenced by an increasingly skeptical public perception of China (Figure 1), a country which is often viewed within US policy circles as both a global strategic competitor and a challenger (Figure 2). Depending on how the elections turn out, this most critical of geopolitical relationships could end up being driven by a preference towards either: a) unilateralism and possible (likely verbal) confrontation – a possible scenario under a Trump reelection, as identified by many political commentators; or b) multilateralism and competition – which most commentators associate with the Biden administration, including a systematic approach to using the legislative framework to deal with strategic competition, and to form stronger global alliances such as Quad, AUKUS and IPEF.

We believe the markets are likely to focus on trade, technology and investment as the three areas of this relationship with the most potential economic consequence.

## Trade policy – tariffs & more

How we got here. The Trump administration took a zero-sum approach to trade early on in its term, whereby it viewed the US as losing out to countries it imported from, particularly China. In June 2017, President Trump initiated a Section 232 investigation – citing national security reasons – on imports of steel and aluminium, and which eventually resulted in additional tariffs being placed on Chinese steel and aluminium destined for the US. This started a series of tit-for-tat use of tariffs, which subsequently – mostly imposed under Section 301 (Figure 3) – resulted in close to 66% of Chinese exports to the US being subject to additional tariffs, and at a rate of more than 6x before the 'trade war' began. Compared with US tariffs versus the rest of the world, average US tariffs on imports from China remain elevated, at 19.3% vs 3% for the rest of the world. What might be next? The use of tariffs has come back into focus in the election campaign, with Trump suggesting the possibility of, among other things:

n imposing 10% additional import tariffs on all US imports;

n incentivizing multinational companies to exit China and return to the US.

To this end, a Republican White House could consider reinstating a Section 301 investigation similar to that used in 2018, or potentially introduce broader tariff hikes by invoking the International Emergency Economic Power Act of 1977 President Biden has opposed the proposal of across-the-board 10% tariffs, arguing that it would be a tax on the middle class.

But a second Biden term might still consider tariffs on select Chinese goods, especially raising concerns over: (a) ongoing Chinese government subsidies to key industrial sectors; and (b) overcapacity (China accounts for a third of global production but only a sixth of global consumption). As a result, the WSJ recently reported that the Biden administration is discussing raising tariffs on some Chinese goods, including electric vehicles, in an attempt to bolster the US clean-energy industry against cheaper Chinese exports. The President has recently also asked for steel tariffs to be tripled. This can be done via the Section 301 or Section 232 route. On top of that, in February, the administration initiated an investigation into Chinese-made vehicles including EV cars, which opens the door for the administration to take more active measures including the potential of banning or restricting 'smart' Chinese vehicles in the US.

Of the two approaches, the US public seems to be more supportive of President Biden's approach. In a recent polling, a clear majority favored a “smart, firm, strong, and diplomatic” approach, with 73% of respondents saying the US should hold high-level diplomatic talks with China.

What might China do in response to a possible second round of trade wars? Among the options potentially available to China are: a) imposing more tariffs on imports from the US, including possibly on a rising services surplus that the US enjoys with China, mostly due to education and tourism (Figure 9); b) encouraging Chinese firms to substitute US products, such as aircraft or phones; c) imposing export restrictions on key materials over which it has significant influence and control, such as rare earths; d) export more of its goods to emerging markets or manufacturing and exports its goods via a third country; and e) using currency as a tool to offset the price impact of tariffs on competitiveness (Figure 10). During the trade wars in 2018-19, the RMB depreciated broadly in line with the increase in US tariffs, and by a total of almost 15%. By that logic, an additional 10% tariff imposed on US imports from China (all else equal) could see the RMB weakening to near 8.0, if permitted and/or encouraged by Chinese authorities to offset the impact of the tariffs. The policy reaction function in China, however, would be constrained by the cost that such a rapid depreciation would have on the confidence in the currency and the resulting impact on capital outflows.

## Technology policy - broadening the 'small yard, high fence strategy

How we got here. Concerns around China's technological advancement predate the Trump presidency, and were already apparent in the Obama administration, with attempts to limit China’s access to American technology through commercial channels. In December 2016, for example, President Obama blocked a Chinese deal to buy a German semiconductor company, Aixtron, on national security grounds.

After President Trump took office, he instructed USTR to initiate a Section 301 investigation into China’s forced technology transfers and the infringement of US intellectual property rights, which subsequently led to a range of actions, including the barring of Chinese companies from investing in US technology firms, and export controls to block additional US technology exports to China.

These attempts were, if anything, ratcheted up under the Biden administration, with the advancement of technology seen as the most important field in strategic competition. To ensure an effective and sustainable strategy, the administration worked with the private sector to create what National Security Advisor Jake Sullivan has called a ‘small yard, high fence’ strategy – imposing heavy restrictions, but only on a small number of important technologies. This strategy includes:

n Imposing export restrictions on US products to Chinese firms. According to the US Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS), there are \~600 Chinese entities on its export ban list, with more than half of them having been added during the past two years.

n Foreign direct product rule. In October 2022, the BIS further tightened its rules on export controls in order to limit Chinese technology firms' access to: (1) high-end AI chips, (2) US chip design software, (3) US-built semiconductor manufacturing equipment, and (4) US-built components via a provision called the "foreign direct product rule", or FDPR, which was first introduced in 1959 to control the trading of US technologies. It essentially prohibits the sale of a product made using US technology, even in a foreign country.

n Restricting firms' expansion in China through the CHIPS and Science Act of 2022. The aim of the Act is to: (a) reduce US reliance on foreign-made semiconductors, while (b) bringing high-tech innovation in manufacturing and R&D back to the United States (re-shoring). However, those awarded the funding would be restricted for a period of ten years from expanding chip manufacturing capacity in China, with firms being barred from expanding output by more than 5% for advanced chips and 10% for older technology in China.

n Forming alliances to limit China's access to other countries' advanced technology. Apart from the measures listed above, the US government has been striving to strike deals with its allies to limit Chinese access to advanced technology. For example, in 2023, the Netherlands and Japan, homes to critical makers of semiconductor manufacturing tools, reached an agreement with the US to mirror recent US export control restrictions on semiconductor technology exports to China.

n Formation of the Chip 4 coalition. The US has also formed an alliance with Japan, Taiwan, and Korea. These four economies are not only the largest chip producers globally, but they also cover all the major areas of the semiconductor value chain.

n Investment restrictions on China. In 2023, President Biden signed an executive order that restricts US outbound investments to China, Hong Kong, and Macau in sensitive technology sectors, including advanced computing chips and microelectronics, quantum technology, and artificial intelligence (AI) due to US national security.

n De-risking. The administration has also promoted a “de-risking” and “friend-shoring” policy, designed to reduce its dependency on China and to “marginalize” China on technology standards and industrial supply chains.

What might be next? Irrespective of the outcome of the elections, the next US administration seems likely to continue with efforts to restrict China from acquiring the technology needed to advance on various fronts, including AI and advanced chip manufacturing, as well as dual-purpose-use technology.

A second-term Biden White House we think would not just build on its ‘small yard, high fence’ strategy but potentially look to 'widen' the size of the 'yard'. In other words, they might look to expand restrictions to beyond high-tech fields, and include all emerging technologies and non-cutting edge products considered relevant to national security. Indeed, this has been the trend in 2024. For example, in Oct 2023, the US government banned exports of the Nvidia H800 chip (a less advanced chip) designed for Chinese markets, cloud services, and semiconductor manufacturing equipment services for Chinese customers. Congress is also asking Commerce Secretary Gina Raimondo and U.S. Trade Representative Katherine Tai to investigate foundational semiconductors, also known as legacy chips, imported from China. In our view, this is likely an attempt to at least decouple on the technology front with China.

We believe the approach by the Biden administration to restrict China's advancement on the technology front could also evolve – from just strict export controls to now including investment restrictions, financial sanctions, high tariffs, operating license bans, and market access limitations. For example: export controls on semiconductors and related products; anti-subsidy investigations and tariffs on key minerals and metal products (such as aluminum products); Section 337 investigations on certain Chinese electronic computing devices and their components and modules; and a Proposed Rulemaking Notice , which has been used to investigate Chinese-made electric vehicles.

On top of that, the President would also likely continue to make the case to Congress and the American people that there is a need to make generational investments in American innovation to outcompete China. Hence, on top of the over \$2tn in investments in clean energy technology, infrastructure, semiconductors, and advanced manufacturing through the Inflation Reduction Act, the CHIPS and Science Act, and the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act, more acts will be introduced to advance US innovation and encourage more manufacturing to be done in the US.

In keeping with history, a Trump administration might opt for a more aggressive approach. So far, little has been said directly in the campaign on this issue, but comments by Robert Lighthizer, among others, who served as the US Trade Representative for President Trump, argue in favour of expanding restrictions placed on China to a wider set of key technologies. He has also proposed more farranging controls on US investments in China, including requiring US firms to disclose any technological investments they make, which could open them up to restrictions based on ‘national or economic security’ considerations.

Meanwhile, the US Congress is also looking to broaden the government’s toolkit in this space, including by: a) prohibiting investments in Chinese firms, including those on US government sanction and red flag lists; b) expanding the definition of ‘critical technology’ in the Foreign Investment Risk Review Modernization Act; and c) expanding the list of sensitive sectors over which CFIUS has jurisdiction.

China's reaction. China increasingly views these actions as having the aim to harm China’s ability to advance its manufacturing capabilities and its economic growth, and alleges that the US administration is pursuing 'technological hegemony'. A further tightening in restrictions could prompt China to respond more proportionately, for example by:

n introducing an anti-trust investigation targeting American companies operating on Chinese soil, for example Micron;

n placing US companies on China's own unreliable-entity list;

n limiting US access to China's rare earth materials via export controls;

n slowing down reviews of mergers involving US firms, as with Intel’s proposed acquisition of Tower Semiconductor in 2022;

n banning government and quasi-government entities from buying US electronic goods like iPhones; and

increasing subsidies to domestic technology firms.

## Investment policy – limiting access to US capital

How we got here. During the Trump administration in August 2018, the US Congress passed the Foreign Investment Risk Review Modernization Act (FIRRMA), which expanded the jurisdiction of the Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States (CFIUS) to address growing ‘national security’ concerns. CFIUS subsequently rejected a series of attempts by Chinese corporates to acquire US firms. In November 2020, former president Trump signed an executive order barring Americans from investing in a collection of 31 Chinese companies that the White House argued supported China’s military. A few stocks among these names were thereafter delisted from US stock exchanges, including China’s three major telecom providers.

The Biden administration not only expanded this list (named the NS-CMIC list) in June 2021 to 60 companies by issuing a new executive order, but it also expanded efforts to scrutinize outbound investments, even in the face of opposition from the US Chamber of Commerce and other business interests. In a speech in 2022, National Security Adviser Jake Sullivan highlighted that the administration was keen to address outbound investments in sensitive technologies, particularly investments that would not be captured by export controls and that could enhance the technological capabilities of US competitors in the most sensitive areas. In late 2023, President Biden signed an executive order to restrict investments by US companies in parts of the Chinese economy, such as advanced technologies that could enhance China's military and intelligence capabilities.

The Biden administration has also tightened the rules on Chinese firms' access to US capital markets. In December 2020, the Holding Foreign Companies Accountable Act was passed, which enables the US SEC to prohibit the trading of securities of foreign companies listed on US stock exchanges if the company is unable to submit to an audit by the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board (PBAOC) due to legislation in its country of origin. The HFCAA also requires companies to declare whether they are owned by a foreign government. A number of Chinese companies subsequently de-listed from the US exchange, and there has been a slowdown in new Chinese firms being listed in the US, despite China finally permitting PBAOC to audit Chinese firms.

What might be next? Again, the direction of travel on investment restrictions seems unlikely to change, irrespective of the outcome of the elections, with likely continued or further monitoring and control pursued on cross-border investments between the US and China. This could include measures to discourage corporates from other countries to invest in China, limit Chinese banks' access to the Fedwire and/or widening the jurisdiction of CFIUS. There will likely be particular focus on any specific measures that might restrict US investments in Chinese financial markets. Indeed, Congress has been working on various legislations to prohibit funds from investing in Chinese companies, in an effort to address the strategic, commercial and national security risks it believes Beijing poses to the US economy and financial markets. For example, earlier in April, bipartisan representatives of the US Congress’s certified public accountant caucus have unveiled a bill entitled the No China in Index Funds Act, which includes civil penalties for violations of the proposed legislation.

Although these bills need to pass both of the two Houses and will take time, the implications could still be immediate. In fact, with the worsening in US-China relations and concerns over potential regulation prohibiting investment into the Chinese markets, the US Federal pension fund (FRTIB) has decided to exclude HK and China investments from its portfolio in November. We have also seen foreign ownership in Chinese assets declining.

China's reaction. Despite the tightening of investment flows into China, China is unlikely to take strong retaliatory actions. Rather, the Chinese authorities have suggested they will continue to: (a) remove investment barriers to encourage more investments into China to help upgrade its manufacturing base, including shrinking its negative investment list, and (b) continue to offer a level playing field to incentivize more private investments into China.

However, China can still consider targeted measures, including (as mentioned above):

n introducing an anti-trust investigation targeting American companies operating in China;

slowing reviews of mergers involving US companies;

prohibiting US companies from gaining more market share in China; and

n discouraging Chinese firms from listing in US exchanges and/or financing themselves using US financial institutions.

Apart from the above and the recent willingness of the US authorities to use their financial tools, including sanctions, to limit certain countries' access to their capital markets and the USD, China will likely push ahead with diversifying its FX reserve holdings into other foreign currencies including gold. Indeed, over the past 17 months, China has bought 10mn oz of gold and slowed its US asset purchasing (\~\$50bn in total over the past 12 moths vs \~\$250bn in the prior 12 months, Figure 16). Furthermore, China has been actively looking to internationalise the RMB, which has been bearing fruit recently. Over the past year, the use of the RMB has risen notably, according to SWIFT (Figure 17).

China could also encourage Chinese firms to re-list in more 'China-friendly' markets, such as Europe and/or Asia. At the current juncture, there are \~256 Chinese firms (Figure 18) listed in the various US exchanges, with a total market capitalisation of \~ \$850bn. \~64% of these Chinese firms are IT, Healthcare and consumer discretionary firms(Figure 19).

All in all, regardless of which administration will be in the White House next, US-China relations are likely to remain poor and ongoing US restrictions on China will continue to discourage investment inflows, leading to the RMB being vulnerable to external forces, particularly in an environment in which the US-China interest rate differential is likely to remain wide and China’s economic headwinds remain strong.

## US elections & rest of Asia

US-Asia relations were widely perceived as having taken a back seat during the Trump presidency. While the Pentagon described the Indo-Pacific region as “the single-most consequential region for America’s future” in a strategy report in 2019, the President scaled back his engagements with Asian leaders (Al Jazeera). He did not attend any ASEAN+3 summits during his four years in office, a stark contrast to his predecessor; and he did not appoint a resident ambassador to ASEAN despite the request from the House Foreign Affairs Committee. Among the first trade deals that the Trump administration renegotiated was the Korea-US Free Trade Agreement (KORUS), even though the revised version essentially ended up as just a tweak on the original.

The Biden administration has, by contrast, shown interest in renewing its commitment to South East Asia. Yohannes Abraham was appointed as the new US Ambassador to ASEAN in 2021. President Biden has attended two of the three ASEAN+3 summits, with the latest one attended by VP Kamala Harris. Biden announced the elevation of US-ASEAN relations to a Comprehensive Strategic Partnership (CSP) during the 2022 summit in Phnom Penh. VP Harris followed it by announcing during the 2023 summit in Jakarta the establishment of the first US-ASEAN Center in Washington DC through a public-private partnership, with a mission of strengthening US-ASEAN ties. Furthermore, an ASEAN-US Special Summit was held in Washington DC in May 2022 to celebrate 45 years of ASEAN-US Dialogue Relations, only the second such summit to be held since 2016. President Biden announced the Indo-Pacific Economic Framework for Prosperity (IPEF) shortly after the special summit. Members include the US, seven ASEAN member states, Japan, South Korea, India, and Australia, among others.

Among the issues at the forefront of relations between the US and Asia are:

## Supply chains

A key difference between the policy platforms of the two candidates is Trump’s emphasis on re-shoring, which runs contrary to IPEF’s focus on developing multilateral trade and supply chain coordination, versus Biden’s approach on friend-shoring. US investments in ASEAN have increased during the Biden administration as companies pushed to diversify their supply chains and ‘de-risk’ their exposure to China. Trump’s plans to impose across-the-board 10% tariffs relate to his emphasis on bringing jobs and manufacturing back to the US, and could significantly impact plans to diversify supply chains to alternative production centres. He has highlighted his desire to end the IPEF and pledged to push for a “Trump Reciprocal Trade Act” that would give him authority to impose a reciprocal tariff on any country that imposes one on the US. However, the IPEF supply chain deal has a provision preventing a member nation from withdrawing for three years after the agreement takes effect (2023). But it is "quite plausible" that a return of a Trump administration could engage in a de facto exit from the IPEF by being absent from the meetings.

Another concern on Asia's supply chain is Mr Trump's well-documented opposition towards ESG and renewable energy, which could impact the EV sector and its supply chains based in Asia, including raw mineral mining, battery production, and EV assembling. Thailand, Indonesia, and Malaysia have seen a rise in EV investments, which could be at risk. A second-term Biden administration would likely continue the push for a clean economy and boost EV sales growth.

## Security umbrella

The Biden administration has gone to considerable lengths to reiterate its security commitment to its Indo-Pacific allies. The US Navy has been sailing warships through the Taiwan Strait on a near-monthly basis to demonstrate its freedom of navigation. Relations between the US and the Philippines have also improved significantly, particularly since President Marcos has taken office – with the US having committed to supplying coastal and air defense systems, radars, military transport aircraft and drones in the next five to 10 years. Several senior US officials, including President Biden himself, have reiterated that the US-Philippines mutual defense treaty could be triggered if the Philippines were attacked. The Philippines has reciprocated by increasing the number of bases and strategic sites that the US military can access in the country. The US Department of Defense in April said it has allocated more than \$100mn by the end of the fiscal year for infrastructure investments for the nine locations. On top of that, US Congress is pushing forward a bill to authorizes \$500mn a year in Foreign Military Financing (FMF) grant assistance to the Philippines over the five fiscal years to 2029 boost its defenses. While a second-term Biden administration would be watched for whether it continues with its engagement with the security issues in the Indo-Pacific region; many in the region worry that a Trump White House might choose to pivot towards a more transactional approach in managing US relations in the region, including possibly a trade or security deal with China. On cross-strait issues, while there remains bi-partisan support in the US Congress for Taiwan, it’s not clear if a Trump administration would prioritise dealing with the same. He has expressed discontent, for example, with Taiwan’s success in becoming the world's leading semiconductor manufacturer. The Chinese have recently suggested that a Trump White House might lead to the US abandoning Taiwan, adding to concerns around the future of the US security umbrella for the region.

## North Korea

The US elections come against the backdrop of rising concerns about North Korea, including ongoing missile tests by the North Korean authorities. In October last year, Korea's Joint Chiefs of Staff warned of the possibility that Pyongyang could carry out a Hamas-style sneak attack, occupying territory along the inter-Korean border or taking South Korean hostages. The Trump administration had significantly more engagement with Pyongyang during its term than its successor. There are reports – which Trump has denied – suggesting that he is considering a plan to let North Korea keep its nuclear weapons, while offering the leadership financial incentives to stop making new bombs. Given that he has in the past referred to joint military exercises with South Korea as pricey ‘war games’, there are concerns that a Trump administration could end these. Any doubts about the US commitment to the Korean Peninsula could further the debate in Seoul around developing its own nuclear arsenal, leading to a greater regional risk premium.
