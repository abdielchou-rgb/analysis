Measuring AI’s rate of change will be critical in understanding where incremental alpha opportunities exist.
Our global mapping effort allows us to attempt this exercise, even at this early stage of diffusion.

Measuring and Mapping AI's Rate of Change: Our first AI Mapping report surveyed all our analysts in 4Q23 to determine their stocks' exposure and materiality to the AI debate. This report and its survey findings are the first update since. The latest survey shows 337 stocks have changed exposure (e.g. from Adopter to Enabler) and 446 stocks have changed materiality (e.g. from Moderate to Significant). \$26 trillion of market cap and c.\$10 trillion of revenue has changed either AI exposure or materiality since 4Q23, according to our analysts. AI is now more material to 97% of Utilities coverage and we add 307 new stocks - mainly Japanese - to the analysis.

## Generating AI Alpha From Here:

• (1) Enablers with rising AI materiality. The 15 stocks re-categorised as having Core to Thesis exposure to AI have rallied >25% YTD. We believe this strategy of picking stocks where AI materiality is increasing will continue to work for investors for the remainder of 2024. Enablers with "Significant" exposure to AI now have 20% upside to MS Base Case target prices compared to only 14% upside for Core-to-Thesis Enablers. On a sector basis, we see further upside for Utilities - which have already delivered 15% alpha YTD. The question for Enablers is whether Software, which has lagged semis by 60%, can close the gap as history suggests it should.

• (2) Adopters with strong pricing power. With the majority of corporate AI Adopters targeting cost-out ROI, we favour those Adopters with strong pricing power. There are 135 stocks in our global coverage that meet this criteria and that have outperformed those with lower pricing power by 24% since ChatGPT was released, a trend we see persisting.

## Tech Diffusion

A Morgan Stanley Research Key Theme of 2024

We asked Morgan Stanley Research analysts across our global stock coverage three questions, the last of which is new in this work relative to our initial AI mapping work conducted at the end of 2023:

• How do you currently classify the company's AI exposure? 7 categories from Enabler down to Disrupted (see Exhibit 38 )

• How material do you believe AI will be to the investment thesis in the next 12 - 24 months? 5 categories from Core to Thesis down to Insignificant ( Exhibit 45 )

• How do you perceive this company's pricing power (i.e. ability to retain cost savings vs passing through to customers)? 3 categories of High, Neutral, Low ( Exhibit 58 )

## What's New in this analysis - 5 key changes and findings

1. 337 stocks (\$11trn of value) have changed exposure and 446 stocks (\$15trn of value) have changed materiality. 147 stocks (\$3.7trn of value) have changed both exposure and materiality. 79 have moved up in both, 24 have moved down in both. Stocks that have changed categories represent \$26tr in market cap, \$10tr in revenues and \$1.4tr in capex spend.

2. 58 stocks (net) moved from Adopters to Enabler/Adopter; 44 stocks (net) from Protected to Adopter; 4 stocks to Disrupted; 21 stocks to Wildcard.

3. AI is now more material to 86% of Utilities coverage; AI is now less material to 15% of Financials and Telecoms coverage.

4. 135 Adopters have been designated as having “High Pricing Power” and have outperformed "Low Pricing Power" Adopters by 24% since January 2023. We did not previously survey for this pricing power component.

5. 307 stocks have been mapped for first time; 209 Japan stocks have been mapped for first time.

## Why AI's Rate of Change Matters

This report maps the evolution of companies' exposure to AI, how that evolution is perceived by analysts and how it is priced by the market. The reason we think this exercise is valuable to investors is because of: (1) the acceleration in the capabilities of foundational AI infrastructure, and (2) the fact that this technology is diffusing faster than any before it and into more industries than any before it (see here, here and here).

Over the past 18 months, the speed of change in underlying model capabilities has ratcheted up from reliable output that can displace or supplement 5 second or 5 minute tasks, to models that are providing reliable output (i.e. minimal hallucination) for 15 minute to 1 hour tasks. In the context of this rate of change of foundational models,

corporate adoption and its materiality to global stock markets is likely underappreciated and showing no signs of decelerating.

Our regular CIO survey shows robust spending and AI adoption plans by companies in 2H24 and beyond. Open source model downloads are tracking at a 352% YoY growth rate and consumer-facing bot interfaces are showing +58% and +68% YTD and YoY growth respectively, per Google Trends data. For the time being, we see no air-pocket in demand.

## Generating AI Alpha From Here

When using this survey as a gauge for future potential alpha generation, we would highlight the following three key observations:

1. Enablers - Materiality matters: Re-ratings are the norm rather than the exception for any stock where AI is becoming more material for the investment case. Since January 2023, Core to Thesis Enablers have returned >100% versus c.25% for moderately exposed Enablers ( Exhibit 20 ).

## (2) Adopter Alpha: Pricing Power is Key

While it may look as though Adopters have been left behind in Exhibit 20 , when we look at just the valuation contribution to returns in isolation, the market is beginning to price future earnings expansion by the Adopters. The Enabler/Adopter and Adopter categories are now largely indistinguishable from one another based on re-rating since January 2023 ( Exhibit 28 ). This was not the case at the start of the year. On the one hand this is driven by a re-rating and then a de-rating cycle for the Enabler/Adopters as they have upgraded into their higher valuations. On the other hand we think it could signify an early sign in the passing of the baton in where the market is ascribing value through the AI stack.

While it has taken longer than we'd previously assumed, the market is beginning to take the Adopter opportunity more seriously. AI Adopters, particularly since January 2024, have outperformed Disrupted companies by 12% and the combination of Wildcard and Disrupted companies by 10% ( Exhibit 29 ). The Adopters have also outperformed all non-Enabler categories by 6%, almost all of which has been delivered since the start of the year. As ROI on Adopters' spending ( Exhibit 23 ) comes to fruition, we see no reason that this outperformance would not continue.

## Global Economics (Seth Carpenter)

## • Economics: How GenAI May Reshape Labor and the Economy

The clearest link from AI to the macroeconomy is through the labor market and higher productivity. The effects are impossible to know with precision ex ante, however some key implications can be analyzed. Normally, the question of whether AI boosts the level of productivity or the growth rate of productivity would be the first question. But because the benefits will likely take years to be fully incorporated, the distinction becomes blurred. Faster productivity growth should boost the equilibrium interest rate (often called r\*) by boosting the underlying potential growth rate of the economy. Economies grow based on labor supply growth, capital investment, and productivity growth. In theory, r\* is boosted 1:1 with faster productivity growth, and this effect theoretically should be built into the entire yield curve. Indeed, if these higher rates take place because economic growth has picked up, investors would observe a correlation between rising earnings and rising rates, both driven by productivity.

Of course, theory and practice are often a bit different. We expect a "soft landing" for the US economy in coming years, and if that outcome coincides with a meaningful pickup in productivity, the economy could actually develop excess capacity and have inflation fall more than it would have otherwise. In cyclical terms, then more productivity could lead the Fed to lower interest rates more to allow the economy to fully absorb the extra productive capacity and to avoid having inflation fall below target. But over time, as noted above, the policy rate will have to rise to a higher level than in the past. The effect on interest rates and the yield curve, then depend critically on both the extent of the faster productivity growth, which will boost rates over the long term, and the short-term disinflationary effects, which will lead to easier policy (compared to a base case) in the short run.

Of course, productivity means different things to macroeconomists and the details will matter. If a journalist uses AI tools to do more and more-extensive research, yielding a better output, the measured result will still only be one daily newspaper per day, so productivity may not change. Similarly, in the short run, the adoption and diffusion of new technology requires spending that will take time to give a return, and hiring to develop processes that will only bear fruit over time. In the very near term, productivity may in fact suffer and aggregate demand rather than supply may be boosted until the investments are made, and the additional output is realized.

## US Equity Strategy (Andrew Pauker)

• US Equity Strategy: Leveraging AI to Drive Efficiency

Corporates have been increasingly focused on driving operational efficiency. This dynamic is centered on both expense discipline and on select investments that have the potential to foster future productivity, including AI. On this front, there is a notable amount of overlap among the industries discussing operational efficiency most prevalently and those that have the potential to realize more significant AIdriven efficiency gains based on the framework we introduce in Leveraging AI to Drive Efficiency.

Companies have been rewarded for focusing on efficiency gains. Data from our factor database shows that companies that demonstrate high operational efficiency are outperforming on a relative basis. This has been a durable trend which we expect to continue given the combination of sticky cost structures and slowing pricing power for the average company in an environment where corporates are working to preserve margins. In our view, these factors should add to the incentive to invest in AI as a means of driving future productivity.

These dynamics underscore the likely productivity improvement which we see playing out for early adopters this year. In terms of early adopters, our analysts see efficiency/productivity gains occurring within Software/Internet first before the broader market/economy. Our proprietary industry group framework focused on AI-driven efficiency gains corroborates this view as Software/Internetrelated groups screen as top beneficiaries.

We believe the broader market should benefit from AI-driven efficiency gains starting next year. We see AI-driven productivity adding an additional 30 bps to 2025 net margin for the S&P 500 (see our Mid-Year Outlook for more). In terms of industry drivers of productivity gains, our framework highlights that servicesoriented pockets of the market possess a more significant opportunity with respect to AI-driven efficiency gains; these groups include Software Services, Consumer Services, Health Care Equipment & Services, Financial Services, and Media & Entertainment. Just these groups alone represent over 30% of expected 2025 net income for the S&P 500, which speaks to the potential margin opportunity.

## Measuring Rate of Change - Materiality

AI Materiality is about the importance of AI to the 12-24 month investment case. Since our last survey - Mapping AI's Diffusion - AI's Materiality to 446 stocks under coverage has changed, according to our analysts, representing a market value of >\$14 trillion. Of this market cap value, 67% is in the US, 19% in Europe with APAC, Japan and Latam representing more modest shares of 9%, 3% and 2% respectively (albeit the majority of new stocks added to our coverage but not in the previous survey are Japanese listed). The companies that changed materiality have collective revenue of >\$5 trillion in FY24e, \$1.1bn of EBIT and \$614bn of capex, based on our analysts' estimates. The stocks deemed by our analysts to have changed in their AI materiality were generally larger than the average of our wider global coverage at \$33bn average market cap, or \$7bn median; but similar in market cap size to the stocks recategorised into new exposures.

Below we present a simple scoring system to summarise the recategorisations. The range of materiality stretches from 'Insignificant' at one extreme to 'Core to Thesis' at the other. To track the rate of change, we convert moves towards the right as +1 and moves to the left as a -1. Hence a move from 'Moderate' to 'Core to Thesis' would be categorised as +2 in the following charts. This quantification on the sliding scale of Materiality helps us to see a number of higher-level changes in the data and structure of the market, such as which geography or sector's market cap is becoming more geared into the AI narrative and how quickly.

## Investors Should Focus on Pricing Power

While valuation and earnings momentum in the whole Adopter category is helpful, we believe investors need to be choosier on their AI Adopter stocks. Companies that best adopt new technology to scale their businesses while maintaining robust barriers to entry, tend to accrue most value through history (see here). This final point, we believe, is going to become increasingly important for investors trying to select between the 1328 stocks that our analysts consider to be Adopters.

As the capabilities of models broadens from language to vision to robotics to healthcare and the cost of inference declines rapidly, pricing power will be of paramount importance. The reason we are so confident in this assertion is best shown with a cross-section of the research, design and manufacturing costs associated with bringing products to market in a pre-generative-AI world. According to our broad spectrum of analysts covering the respective industries involved in bringing these products to market, some examples of the pre-AI cost base susceptible to change are as follows:

• \$2-10m to make an hour of hollywood-quality video content

• \$10,000-\$1m to produce a music track

• \$60-150m to make a AAA game

• \$500k-5m to make an Indie game

• \$50,000-500,000 to make an enterprise website

• \$200k-1m to build high-end and complex app

• \$5-100m to create a blue chip marketing campaign for a new product

• \$65m direct costs of a phase 2 clinical trial

• \$285m direct costs of a phase 3 clinical trial

• \$1b of Biotech R&D needed to filter down candidate drug compounds

• £30-100m for a new MedTech device to come to market

• \$2.6bn to bring a new drug to market over 10 years

• \$125k average on-shore US software developer salary

• \$40k average call centre agent salary in India

• \$1-2bn to develop an all new car design and production

• \$3-5bn to develop an all new car platform

With these figures in mind, we don't doubt that Adopter companies can materially change their cost structure whilst maintaining or even growing their revenue base over time. At this stage, however, most applications are cost-out rather than revenue-up related proofs of concept, according to ScaleAI's survey of adoption ( Exhibit 56 ).

Exhibit 56: Companies' application of AI projects or proof of concepts within business
![](images/ea67e4df119ec7c2e0d713441e138462cac49b2e43a45fa351024a643f727a61.jpg)

And, in the absence of AI Adopters having robust pricing power, we'd argue that any margin gains from cost-out ROI improvements risk being eroded. In other words, deflationary pressures will tend to accrue to end-customers. Adopter companies using AI to operate with structurally lower cost bases than incumbents will be able to pursue market share over pricing.

History gives us plenty of precedents for this phenomenon. For example, when Ford released the Model-T (using electricity and the moving assembly line) it cost less than one-third of the price of an average car at the time. From that point, Ford was able to reduce the cost another \~70% in the coming fifteen years. By 1929, 80% of US auto sales were accounted for by three companies, General Motors, Ford and Chrysler ( Exhibit 57 ). The moving assembly line is an example of the extreme change tech diffusion and tech deflation can bring to emerging industries. Vast TAMs, but ultimately winner-takes-most TAMs. Returns fell for incumbents as electrification retrofitting was required.

AI Adoption has the potential to create similar challenges for incumbents if those corporates cannot demonstrate their pricing power strength concurrent with their adoption of the electricity of the modern age, AI.

## Michelle Weaver

AI has been the hottest market theme in the US over the past two years as investors piled into semiconductor stocks before broadening out to the AI power trade. Using the proprietary database created in our AI analyst survey we analyzed the composition of total return for stocks that are AI Enablers, AI Adopters, or both AI Enablers & Adopters (note: the 'Both' group was a separate category in the survey and does not include stocks that are only Adopters or Enablers). Overall, stocks that are AI Adopters have underperformed the broader market while stocks that are Enablers or both Enablers & Adopters have outperformed the market ( Exhibit 67 ).

Companies that are AI Enablers have seen substantially higher returns than the market, and returns have largely been powered by multiples with modest contributions coming from revenue and margin ( Exhibit 68 ). We saw higher returns for Enablers where AI is core to the thesis and a bigger contribution to return from revenue.

Companies that are both AI Adopters & Enablers have also significantly outperformed the broader market. For companies in this group, the more material AI is to the thesis, the stronger the returns are ( Exhibit 69 ). The contribution to returns for this group are more balanced than the pure adopters or enablers. While returns are still being driven by multiples, a substantial proportion of return has come from net margin and revenue.

## Methodology & Synopsis

Between 27th March 2024 to 15th April 2024, our global Morgan Stanley Research analysts completed a simple survey of just two questions for each of their stocks under coverage. This was a broad survey, based on the analyst's view of AI's impact on their stocks over the next 12 to 24 months. Our analysts tagged their coverage stocks on three distinct criteria:

First: The company's role in, or impact from, AI (categories shown below in Exhibit 103 ); and

Second: The speed of expected diffusion of disruption from AI to the investment debate on the stock ( Exhibit 104 ).

## Stock Mapping Considerations & Learnings

Our latest AI stock mapping survey of our analysts will have some of the same inherent caveats for investors to consider as with other similar surveys, as well as some different ones:

• Qualitative not quantitative assessment: In the first instance we have not asked our analysts for revenue, profit nor capex exposure. This is because we considered the AI theme too early or insignificant for most analysts to estimate these figures credibly in the absence of disclosure from companies.

• Broad ML rather than narrow Generative AI: Many analysts – particularly those with more knowledge of the subject – asked the question: "Is this just about Generative AI, or is this about Machine Learning more broadly, which many companies have been doing for years?". The answer is that we encouraged analysts to answer with the latter in mind.

• Company disclosure: Similar to the difficulty of analysts being asked for revenue exposure estimates to the climate change theme, being asked for exposure to AI presents challenges. Companies – even those closely exposed to a theme - tend to avoid quantification, which leave analysts to best guess exposure levels.

• Analyst knowledge: Many analysts – particularly in adjacent sectors to the theme (i.e. Airlines for AI) – are estimating their coverage's exposure relative to their own perceptions of AI. Most analysts however may not be up to date on the latest

developments and therefore may underestimate the scope or speed of diffusion into their sectors, both to the upside and downside. Consequently, and not entirely unexpectedly, the use of Don't know and Insignificant makes up nearly three quarters of all stock responses.

• Wider use of Beneficiary Enabler than expected: Beneficiary Enabler we had expected to be used primarily for semis, semicap, hyperscalers as well as builders of foundational Large Language Models (such as GPT-4 from OpenAI/Microsoft, Gemini from Alphabet or Llama from Meta). What we found was a broader tagging of data centre construction companies, heat exchangers, medium-voltage cabling and mining companies required for critical metals in the supply chain, for example.

• Narrower use of Disrupted than expected: Disrupted will be an important category as we focus on the 'Rate of Change', which we will monitor through periodic updates to this analysis over the coming years. Currently the number of companies tagged as Disrupted by our analysts is lower than we would have expected when we embarked on this project. However, where they are tagged as Disrupted, the analysts' conviction in AI's materiality to the investment case (i.e. core to thesis rather than don't know) tends to skew towards the more material end of the spectrum.
