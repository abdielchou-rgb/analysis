## Market views

Treasury yields have declined decisively and the curve has steepened in the two weeks since we published our Midyear Outlook, thanks in large part to supportive labor market and inflation data, and incremental dovishness from Chair Powell. On labor markets, nonfarm payrolls increased 206k in June, but this was accompanied by 111k in downward revisions to the prior two months, and the 3-month run rate has slowed to 117k. Moreover, the unemployment rate rose to 4.1%,rising for the third consecutive month, consistent with a gradual loosening of the labor markets (see Jobless rate creeps up again, Michael Feroli, 7/5/24).

Turning to inflation, core CPI rose 0.06% over the month (consensus: 0.2%), with the longawaited moderation in both tenant’s and owners’ equivalent rent finally appearing, with each of these components rising at their most benign rate since 2021. Given the last two months of inflation data alongside the ongoing loosening in labor markets, we now think the Fed will ease in September and a quarterly pace thereafter (vs. November previously) until the Fed funds rate returns to neutral (see Sticky inflation is coming unglued, Michael Feroli, 7/11/24). Finally, even before the June CPI data, Chair Powell had turned incrementally more dovish, saying that the labor market “appears to be fully back in balance.” Given these dynamics, 2-, 5-, 10-, and 30-year Treasury yields have declined 26bp, 22bp, 16bp, and 10bp, respectively, and are now trading in the lower quarter to third of the ranges they have held in 2024 (Figure 1).

Given this move, the money markets are fully pricing a 25bp ease at the September FOMC meeting, 62bp of easing over 2H24 and more than 170bp by YE25 (Figure 2). With the first expected cut merely 10 weeks away in our forecast, we are in the timeframe prior to other easing cycles when yields have consistently declined. Indeed, Figure 3 shows intermediate yields declined decisively in the quarter prior to the first cut prior to the shallow 1995 and 2019 easing cycles, and that the move in the current episode has lagged both of these examples. However, yields are already considerably lower than their peaks last fall after the last hike. Moreover, as we have written recently, the inversion of the yield curve and the negative carry associated with long duration positions makes location and timing important when adding duration. Accordingly, we are hesitant to add duration at these levels, particularly as OIS forwards are pricing a more dovish path for the Fed than our own forecast: while we see easing inflation and rebalancing labor markets as a support for easing, a sharper rise in the unemployment rate and a more drastic slowing in employment growth is required to justify a faster pace of easing. Given that the next employment report is still three weeks away, we think it will be challenging for the front end to price in an even more dovish path of easing. Thus, we remain neutral on duration at current levels.

Meanwhile, the long end of the curve is quickly approaching the steep end of the range it has held since the Fed downshifted its pace of hikes early in 2023 (Figure 4). As we’ve written in the past, the long end tends to anticipate shifts in monetary policy and has consistently steepened ahead of the first Fed ease, with this steepening beginning 2-4 months prior to the first cut (Figure 5.and iformhls, utep  w). Accordingly, we think this cyclical presents a strong support for maintaining steepeners as a lower beta way to position for the coming Fed easing cycle, with a better carry profile than outright longs. Locally though, 5s/30s has steepened approximately 20bp over the last two weeks, and the curve may turn more rangebound here if the front end stabilizes around current yield levels. Moreover, it appears that the curve has steepened more than would be implied by the shift in Fed expectations: Figure 6 shows that 2-year yields have explained about 65% of the variation in the 5s/30s curve over the last three months, the curve tends to steepen 3.5bp for each 10bp decline in front-end yields, and currently appears about 4rbp too steep on this basis. Accordingly, we think the curve may struggle to steepen absent further weakening in data over the near term, and beta-weighted shorts in front-end Treasuries offer a good hedge to steepeners. Given this backdrop, we recommend maintaining 5s/30s steepeners, but recommend selling a beta-weighted amount of 2-year Treasuries to protect the gains in this position over the near term (see Trade recommendations).

## 20-year sector update

At the end of May, we cautioned the 20-year sector had benefitted from a carry-seeking environment, and that further outperformance was unlikely, absent a further step-down in implied volatility (see Treasuries, U.S. Fixed Income Markets Weekly, 5/31/24). Accordingly, amid the unwind of carry trades over the last month, it’s not surprising the 20-year sector has cheapened along the curve: as Figure 11 shows, the 10s/20s/30s par butterfly is currently in the 45th percentile of its 3-year range, versus the 6<sup>th</sup> percentile on May 31. Interestingly, the magnitude of the cheapening over the last 6 weeks appears to be somewhat in excess of fundamental factors: Figure 12…and thiscepg rob xf lvy,  u  m shows 20s appear slightly cheap relative to the level of rates, and the shape of the curve, as well as the level of implied volatility, which we use as a proxy for risk appetite in this less-liquid sector of the Treasury curve.
