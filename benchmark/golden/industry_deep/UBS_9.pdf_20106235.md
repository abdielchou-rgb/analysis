## A monthly synthesis of all our views in a single document

We summarise our views on the main economies and investment strategy on the following pages. The aim is not to be comprehensive, but rather to provide a succinct summary of the main changes in our views and recommendations over the past month. The contributing analysts are listed in the relevant sections of the report—if you have any questions on any particular area, please reach out to them directly.

## Becoming more selective

Our highest conviction remains to be long rates though we likely need weaker data to validate current front-end pricing in the US; real rates remain too high and we still like steepeners (despite the looming Fed QT taper, the supply of duration goes up materially in 24/25). In Europe, the UK is our preferred market, but we also like 2y bunds (we are neutral duration). Within EM the rally has eroded value for most markets but we still like longs in Brazil, Korea and Mexico. For equity markets the challenge is that most good news is priced and risk premia are very tight. From these levels returns are normally limited but we prefer N Asian equities, including Japan, to the US, and, in turn, prefer US equities to Europe. Our strong preference for defensives and Quality keeps us cautious on most operationally levered European equities. We remain long Tech but other than being long software in the US, we are expressing that view largely in N Asia (hardware, semis, China internet). Despite a cautious view on global growth and cyclicality, given the relative valuations we prefer Japan and Small Caps over other developed markets and Large caps, respectively. In FX, we remain moderately bearish on the USD, but expect progress in that direction to be slow for most of Q1. We are long JPY, long AUD and own long-dated EURUSD volatility. We remain bullish on gold and advocate adding to exposure on dips. In credit, we expect largely rangebound spreads; in IG we like 3-7yr, BBB non-financials, and specifically utilities and telecoms; in HY higher rated tech and noncyclicals.

# Economics Summary

## Key Messages

•US consumption remains resilient but the Sahm rule has now triggered in 38% of states.

•90% of the global core inflation run-up has been reversed and the impact of rising shipping costs should be insignificant.

The three most salient features of the global economy right now are: (i) US consumer strength relative to everywhere else; (ii) the breadth and speed of the global inflation decline (widespread rate cuts are now just a few months away); and (iii) China’s inability, so far, to stabilize its property market. Taking these in turn:

US real GDP in Q4 came in at a much higher than expected 3.3% annualized, mostly because of stronger inventory buildup, and better net exports (those two factors explain 82% of the forecast miss vs our estimate; investment came in weaker than we expected). However, consumption was also a touch stronger (2.8% vs our 2.5% estimate), in part because the savings rate once again ticked lower (3.7% in Dec). For context, over the last 4 quarters, US consumption has increased at a rate of 2.6% while that in the Eurozone is running at -0.4%, Japan at 0% and the UK at 0.7%. Our baseline is that depleted excess savings stocks, fewer fiscal transfers and lower (and more expensive) credit availability should mechanically lower US spending in the next few quarters, but the spending resilience is striking. Consumer confidence also ticked up in the Michigan survey.

Inflation continues to recede rapidly. In the US, core PCE prices annualized 1.86% over the last 6 months, and the YoY rate (2.9%) came in a full percentage point below where the FOMC was forecasting it to be just 6 months ago. In the Eurozone, core inflation is down to 3.3%YoY—the speed of disinflation is as impressive as in the US except that it started a few months later. Globally, the monthly run-rate of core inflation has now retraced 90% of its pandemic run-up, and the cross-country data remains highly correlated, suggesting that most of the inflation the last 2 years was driven by supply side factors (energy, supply bottlenecks) that have dissipated. We estimate the impact of the global rise in shipping costs (due to ships avoiding the Red Sea due to the Houthi attacks) to be very small (less than 5bp for the US). This is because freight and insurance costs are a very small part of overall import costs, and there are no container shortages or goods shortages as in the pandemic.

China property sales and starts declined further in December vs November, despite a continuous ratcheting up of policy support. The fact that Q4 GDP came in a touch stronger than expected was because the rest of the economy (retail sales, exports, infrastructure and manufacturing investment, and production) compensated. Mechanically the Q4 outcome lifts our 2024 growth forecast from 4.4% to 4.6%, but we are leaving our quarterly path unchanged until see a more decisive trough in the property market. The most recent measures (reserve requirement cuts and increased loan support for property developers to meet their debt service and other costs) on paper significantly enhances credit support but the potential size of the loans is still uncertain.

Central bankers in recent weeks have pushed back somewhat against the aggressive easing being priced by markets. But unless Powell categorically rules out a March cut at this week’s FOMC we stick with March for the first Fed cut, mainly because we are starting to significantly exceed the real Fed Funds rate the FOMC had pencilled into their own forecast. Also, notwithstanding the strength of consumption, hiring is clearly stalling and the ‘Sahm rule’ (a 50bp increase in unemployment vs the trough the last 12m) has already triggered in 38% of US states (weighted by population). That’s never happened unless immediately preceding, during or following a recession. The ECB, we believe, remains on track for an April cut, following the more dovish than expected ECB press conference where Lagarde denied having provided calendar based guidance in Davos. The SNB has turned dovish, having substantially lowered its inflation forecasts and halting its FX sales - we think the 1st cut is in June. The BOE is probably the most hawkish among the major central banks; although wage/inflation data have come in below its expectation, the level of wage growth remains too high and parts of the MPC are still voting for hikes. We have pencilled in the 1st cut for May, but the risks are skewed to later.

## United States

## Key Messages

Real GDP slowed less than expected in Q4 but the labor market is showing signs of slowing.

 We expect real GDP to expand \~2.7% (Q4/Q4) in ’23. GDP then expands just 0.3% in '24E before reaccelerating to 2.4% in ‘25E.

 A mild correction within the 2024 slowdown should raise the u-rate to 4.9% in ‘24E, helping to push core PCE inflation down to 1.8%, before rising to 2.1% in 2025E.

The January FOMC meeting is not in play. But the faster than expected disinflation is increasing policy restrictiveness to levels at odds with the FOMC’s stated preferences. We expect the intermeeting dataflow will prompt them to begin to re-calibrate nominal rates in March.

Inflation is falling much faster than the FOMC expected. We estimate core PCE prices rose 1.86% at an annualized rate in the second half of the year, compared to the FOMC’s 2.0% objective, pulling the 12-month change down to 2.9% in December. The Fed had expected core inflation to end 2023 at 3.9% as recently as June. With the mid-point of the target range for the federal funds rate at 5.4% since July, the real policy rate is rising much faster and sooner than anticipated, supporting our expectation for recalibration of the nominal policy rate beginning at the March FOMC meeting.

Our baseline still expects an economic slowdown coming quarters. Real GDP came in stronger than expected in Q4 (3.3%), largely because of a continued build in inventories, but also positive net export growth and somewhat stronger than expected consumption (separate data for December showed the saving rate declining to 3.7%, its lowest level since 2022). Substantially less fiscal stimulus, the level of interest rates, and credit tightening present headwinds for growth in 2024. The labor market has slowed considerably, though we expect the January data released in February to continue to look resilient. We expect the ongoing pressure of higher interest rates, coupled with the gradual erosion in household balance sheets, and uncertainty surrounding the Presidential election to weigh on business decisions, leading to weak 2024 growth and a mild economic contraction in the middle of the year.

Our expected labor market slackening should be disinflationary. The details of incoming price data support our outlook that inflation falls faster than the FOMC expects, with core PCE inflation dropping from \~5% in 2022, to 2.9% in 2023E and, with unemployment rising in the second half of 2024, to 1.8% in 2024E, before moving back up to roughly \~2% in 2025E.

We expect a first ‘calibration’ cut in March, with ‘accommodation’ cuts to follow. The January meeting is not in play, but we expect the dataflow ahead of the March meeting to provide enough evidence to the FOMC that they need to mark to market the policy rate to the faster-than-expected disinflation to avoid an excessively restrictive policy stance. However, with inflation at roughly 2% mid-year the FOMC gains substantial room to address rising growth concerns and move to more neutral policy to support expansion. Given our projections, we expect the funds rate to end 2024E in a range of 2.75% to 2.50%, move down to 1.25% in 2025 before downturn risks subside, and end 2025 at 1.75% to 2.00%.

## Derivative Strategy

## Key Messages

## Macroeconomic volatility

Volatility in macroeconomic data has been elevated for some time. This would normally be a driver of higher stock market volatility, but it hasn't been for three reasons:

There has been a directional trend in bond yields. This regime shift in yields has driven significant divergence within the equity market between stocks positively and negatively exposed to yield changes (cyclicals vs. Value/Growth factors).

 Covid introduced sharp divergences between services and manufacturing that lowered stock correlations too.

 The outperformance of the largest Tech companies in recent months on the "AI theme" has driven stock correlations to their lows in the US.

Dispersion in expectations for near-term macro data remain unusually high even though recently, the market reaction to important data releases has diminished. If volatility in bond yields has been driving equity dispersion, then the recent peak in bond yields may change that. Our machine learning model has already picked up a decline in the marginal contribution of bond yields to equity volatility. We expect that macroeconomic volatility will more directly translate into equity volatility now that these correlation-suppressing factors are beginning to weaken.

## The Macro Cookbook

This framework connects S&P 500 volatility with US macroeconomic variables across 256 regimes back to the 1960s. It takes in the full market return distribution rather than just averages. We therefore get richer insights on the level and trajectory of equity volatility based on economic forecasts for the next couple of years. Based on an expectation of softening US growth, falling inflation and interest rates, and modestly rising unemployment, we think S&P 500 volatility will average 15-25% in the coming quarters – a higher level than we experienced in 2023 and modestly higher than the market currently implies.

## The Market Cookbook

Using 3-month forecasts for real yields, breakeven inflation, credit spreads, and our growth index, we use a complex lattice model (multi-nominal tree) to calculate expected index and sector returns and volatility. In the US, we forecast for a modest slowdown in our growth index which is the main contributor to the model suggesting modest market weakness, led by more cyclical sectors. Volatility should rise toward the 15-20% range which intersects well with the conclusions from the Macro Cookbook.
