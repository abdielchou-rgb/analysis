We examine three key steps in the ASEAN journey from 50GW of renewable energy now to 500GW by 2050: 1) the coal plant bottleneck, but potential early retirements; 2) grid infrastructure upgrades; and 3) growing regional linkages as the ASEAN Power Grid (APG) again rises up investor agendas. While we are early in a long-term story, policy developments are encouraging. Country mix, and favourable policy exposure is key to stock selection. Our top picks are SCI, ACEN and TEN, while we introduce coverage of Indonesia renewable stocks.

## ASEAN's RE capacity to reach 500GW by 2050, contributing 50% to power mix

Energy security urgency is rising in ASEAN. Rising power consumption has shrunk the gap between available supply (200GW) and demand (150GW). Assuming a 4% CAGR in power demand over 2024-50, we estimate ASEAN's demand will rise to roughly 400GW by 2050, requiring installed capacity to expand from 300GW to 1,000GW. With new coal plants not being considered, gas, hydro and other RE capacities would logically need to be expanded substantially to achieve this. Our analysis indicates that, in 2024- 50, ASEAN's coal-fired capacity could fall 50%, while its gas capacity rises by 180%, hydro capacity by 250% and RE capacity by 900%.

## ASEAN's three key RE challenges are surmountable

1) Early coal plant retirement: While 69% of ASEAN's 103GW coal capacity is set to be retired only after 2050 (of which 80% is in Indonesia and Vietnam), we expect financing programmes for 2050 net zero targets, eg Indonesia's Just Energy Transition Partnership, to accelerate the process. 2) Grid transformation: ASEAN is making progress with about 40% of its transmission and distribution grids being newly added in the last 10 years. The International Energy Agency (IEA) estimates ASEAN grid infrastructure could expand from 4.9m km now to 12.7m km by 2050. 3) Regional interconnection: Linkages are needed to allow countries with surplus RE to supply those with a deficit. To this end, the ASEAN Power Grid (APG) initiative resurfaced in 2023.

## Country exposure holds key to stock picks, as policies and IRR could vary

Vietnam started ASEAN's clean energy journey in 2020, the Philippines picked up the baton in 2022, and passed it to Thailand and Malaysia in 2023. Singapore is also on board, picking up the pace of its clean energy imports. But while the RE transition has begun in most of ASEAN, power market structures and RE policies differ (Singapore and Philippines have merchant markets), and hence country IRRs vary significantly. We like stocks that are nimble (expanding into foreign markets) and have exposure to high IRR countries (India, Vietnam, Philippines). Our top three picks are SCI, ACEN and TEN.

## Why we expect 500GW of RE capacity by 2050

## We see ASEAN demand growing by 4% CAGR in 2024-50

ASEAN's current electricity demand is close to 150GW. While demand in higher GDP growth countries, such as Vietnam and the Philippines, is growing by 6-9% annually, demand in Malaysia and Thailand is growing at a slower pace in the range of 3-5%. Assuming overall power demand in ASEAN grows by 4% annually over 2024-50 would imply power demand in ASEAN would rise to 400GW by 2050.

## To meet 400GW power demand, ASEAN would need available supply to be slightly higher

In the power market, supply meets demand. Hence, should demand grow to 400GW, the region's available supply should be at least as much. Adding a 25% reserve margin 'buffer' to that number, ASEAN would at any given point need around 500GW of cumulative available supply.

## To maintain 500GW of available supply, installed capacity would have to be at least double that

Capacity factor, a measure of power plant utilisation, differs among the various types of power generation. Different types of power generation have different capacity factors. While traditional coal- and gas-fired power plants have capacity factors between 70- 90%, hydro plants have a capacity factor of around 45%, and other types of RE have a capacity factor of only around 20%

Given that coal plants have fallen out of favour and their capacity is set to fall gradually over 2024-50 (we are assuming a 50% reduction in coal-fired power plants during the period), gas-fired plants would have to be the 'transition' fuel, to ensure base-load capacity in every country remains stable.

In view of these factors, we believe hydro and other RE capacities have to go up substantially to eventually be able to feed into the '500GW available supply' requirement by 2050. Note that for every 1GW of additional RE capacity, available supply rises by only 200MW.

## 2. Grid transformation

Grids form the backbone of power systems. With the advancement of clean energy in ASEAN, the role of grids is set to become even more important, as they could become the bottleneck to the transition to clean energy. Delayed action by countries on grid transformation could prolong reliance on fossil fuels.

Grid development means digitalisation, modernisation, expansion and strengthening, as well as more effective utilisation. To decarbonise electricity supply and effectively integrate RE, IEA estimates that 80m km of grids in ASEAN would need to be added or refurbished by 2040. Wind and solar photovoltaic (PV) plants are planned to account for around 80% of the total increase in power capacity in the next 20 years, which makes it imperative to modernise distribution grids and establish new transmission corridors to connect RE plants, such as offshore wind projects at sea or solar PV projects in the desert, to demand centres located far away in central areas. Grid enhancing technologies are required to unlock the potential of demand response and energy storage through digitalisation.

## The ABC of grid infrastructure

Lines, cables and transformer capacity are the mainstays of electricity grids. Grids are differentiated according to their voltage levels. Medium-voltage lines supply electricity to villages and SME industries, while low-voltage lines serve residential and commercial users. Together, these two lines form the 'distribution grid'. The high-voltage grid connects utility size generation, distribution grids and large industrial consumers, while the extra high voltage and ultra high voltage lines transport electricity over long distance. These three lines form the 'transmission grid'.

Grids carry alternating current (AC) via rotating generators in thermal and hydroelectric plants. However, RE plants, such as solar PV and wind projects, connect to the grid through power electronic converters. AC is used in grid systems to ease the changing voltage levels using power transformers. However, direct current (DC) is used in projects involving subsea cables connecting wind projects, cross-border interconnectors, and transmission from hydropower plants.

High-voltage direct current (HVDC) point-to-point transmission, which involves fewer power losses is now being used increasingly for all kinds of distances – short, medium and long. Most HVDC links today operate at voltages between 300kV to 800kV. HVDC systems are efficient for onshore power transmission and connecting offshore wind farms from remote locations. HVDC transmission losses are roughly 3% versus 7% for AC lines. The global length of HVDC lines have tripled since 2010, passing 100,00km at the end of 2021, but still representing only 2% of the total transmission length.

## Constructing a transmission line is a complex task

Transmission lines require a variety of components and technologies, such as control systems, substations, transformers, lines, and cables. Materials, such as copper and aluminium, need to be procured to manufacture cables and lines. Aluminium is now considered superior given it is three times lighter and less expensive than copper, and is preferred for overhead power lines and for underground and subsea transmission cables, while copper is being used for underground and subsea cables.

Solar PV and wind projects, for example, typically have a 25-30 year lifecycle, whereas grid elements, such as overhead lines, underground cables, power transformers and circuit breakers, all have a lifecycle of more than 40 years. However, if these grid assets operate near or above capacity consistently, they have a shorter lifespan, presenting safety and reliability risks. ## D) Digitalisation

Power flows are increasingly unpredictable with the rise of RE, which requires new digital technologies. In global grid investments, digitalisation is becoming increasingly important, growing from 12% to 20% of total grid investment between 2016 and 2022. Digitalisation involves the implementation of new digital solutions to enhance the observation of grids for real-time monitoring and control of energy flows, especially in 'distribution grids', where around 75% of global digital investment in 2022 was directed. The investments are necessitated by the requirements of managing new energy resources, such as RE plants and electric vehicles (EVs).

Smart grids are hence required to maximise system reliability,flexibility and resilience. Within smart grids, smart meters provide a grid health measure for grid operators, in additions to enhancing the visibility of load flows in the 'distribution grid'. Advanced monitoring and control devices improve real-time system information monitoring. Advanced automation tools help identify quickly the faulty element in the grid. Dynamic line rating allows real-time monitoring of weather conditions and line temperatures for transmission lines.

## 3. Interconnection of ASEAN countries via ASEAN Power Grid

## ASEAN Power Grid taking shape to facilitate RE transfer

The ASEAN Power Grid (APG), a 20-year-old plan, has started taking shape, with all ASEAN nations beginning to put in effort to facilitate the transfer of energy from countries where there is surplus RE capacity to countries with a deficit. Up till now, ASEAN countries have only engaged in bilateral power trading to meet some countries intermittent requirements for energy security, but the latest 100MW Laos-Thailand-Malaysia-Singapore (LTMS) multilateral trade has opened up the possibility for ASEAN countries to collaborate more closely on energy transition, and establish a marketplace to pair buyers and sellers.

An interconnected ASEAN power grid is a critical step in the region’s energy transition. However, challenges remain, in particular navigating the complexities of designing and building interconnected grids, alignment of policy, technical challenges, commercial feasibilities and large capital investment.

Interconnections between countries through transmission lines involve the construction of transmission lines, substations and flexible AC transmission system (FACTS) equipment. In synchronous grids, all interconnected systems maintain the same precise electrical frequency, requiring a high level of technical compatibility and operational coordination. Where synchronous grids are not possible, HVDC technology could be used to handle any rated voltage and frequency.

## All 'lines' lead to Singapore for now

Singapore is the only country in ASEAN that does not have the land space to build out RE capacity in its domicile. Hence, it is imperative for the country, to fulfil its net zero ambitions, to import RE electricity from neighbouring countries. The Singapore government has a target of 4GW of RE imports by 2035. The plan has already been put into action, as 100MW of APG experiment was initiated from Laos-Singapore via Thailand and Malaysia in July 2023. In addition, the Singapore government gave an import license to Sembcorp Industries (SCI) to import 1.2GW of 'Offshore Wind electricity' from Vietnam via a 1,000km subsea cable, which is to be built over 5-7 years.

While we think that Singapore's RE import lines would expand – going into countries, such as Malaysia and Indonesia. We also believe that once other countries, such as Thailand, Philippines and Vietnam, have built up sufficient RE capacities to feed their domiciles, there could be RE power exchange between them and other ASEAN countries with RE deficit through the APG. For now, the progress of APG interconnection has been focused on lines into Singapore:

 Laos-Thailand-Malaysia-Singapore (LTMS) transfer of 100MW RE electricity in July 2023

 1-2GW of RE electricity being planned to be imported from Sarawak region within next 2-5 years

 Preliminary stages of new RE electricity that could be imported into Singapore from Indonesia over next 3-10 years

 1.2GW of offshore wind electricity to enter Singapore from Vietnam by 2033

## APG to also facilitate ASEAN countries' energy security

The APG currently has a total power generation capacity of 8.3GW, which is being used by some ASEAN countries to maintain energy security through bilateral trade, as and when the are short of power and depending on connectivity. Almost 3x the size of the current capacity is under construction. Existing routes include Malaysia-Singapore, Thailand-Malaysia, Vietnam-Cambodia, Lao-Cambodia and Thailand-Cambodia. Other routes, such as Malaysia-Indonesia, Indonesia-Singapore, Philippines-Malaysia, Thailand-Myanmar, are under construction. ## ASEAN could learn from Europe on connectivity

Europe established the first transnational interconnection in 1906 when Switzerland constructed transmission lines to connect with France and Italy. As of today, Europe has the largest interconnected grid, which operates as a unified system, supplying electricity to over 500m customers in 27 countries under a Synchronous Area Framework Agreement (SAFA), via a frequency of 50Hz supported by flexible HVDC links. Market integration is high, given cross-border energy trade and collaboration. In addition, more than 40 new interconnections are under development.

The interconnector between Germany and Norway, known as the North Sea Link, supports the integration of RE by facilitating the transfer of surplus hydropower from Norway and wind power from Germany. A new European-driven concept is using 'meshed HVDC offshore grids' for regional inter connection, that allows offshore assets to link to different jurisdictions, allowing the grid connection of the generator to also act as an interconnector. 'Meshed offshore grids' could be adopted to support projects located remotely, such as offshore wind plants, in our view.

The second pivotal question we address is: if all 400GW of projected clean energy materialises, will it result in higher IRRs on a country level? ## Sensitivity of equity IRRs is skewed to two key factors

The sensitivity of equity IRRs to the six inputs varies significantly. Equity IRRs are most sensitive to changes in installation costs and power tariff. Equity IRRs are also highly sensitive to capacity factor, but solar and wind projects' capacity tends to remain stable at 18-25%.

 Installation costs and power tariffs are the two key factors in IRR: Given that 65% of the projected 400GW of clean energy capacity would be geared towards solar, the two key factors determining the eventual IRRs for ASEAN power producers are solar project cost and feed-in-tariff (FIT).

Other factors typically have a lower impact on IRR: The sensitivity of equity IRR to these three inputs is usually not as high as the first three inputs, but it is still relatively significant. For example, if the cost of debt goes up from 8% to 9%, equity IRR would come down by 0.8%.

## Solar supply chain expansion in China will keep ASEAN costs down

The solar PV industry has a long supply chain. Upstream companies purifies polysilicon from industrial silicon smelted from silicon ore. Polysilicon is the primary raw material used to make solar cells. Midstream companies turn polysilicon rods and ingots into wafers, which are then fabricated into PV cells. At the end stage, the PV cells are assembled into modules, namely solar panels.

There has been a 20-fold expansion in polysilicon, wafer, cell and modules in China in the last 12 years. Global solar PV demand (ex-China) today stands at around 400GW, while China's PV industry total capacity is inching towards 1,000GW. China shipped 310-325GW of solar PV panels in 2023, by our estimate. IRENA forecasts that China's solar panel production capacity will rise 1,262GW by 2030E, while global solar panel demand would reach around 800GW. This implies that the cost of solar panels would not see upward pressure. Currently, the cost of the installation of solar projects in Asia is around USD0.9m/MW, of which around 50% is the cost of solar panels.

## Thailand: a gas-fired market moving towards RE

Thailand's power market is practically the inverse of the Philippines'.  Majority of coal-fired plants (5GW) set to retire before 2050: Thailand has 21 gas-fired power plants with 7G of capacity, which are scheduled to be phased out from 2024. 72% of these plants are set to be retired before 2050.  Around 11GW of new gas-fired capacity to come online over 2024-30: There are plans to commission 19 new gas-fired plants over 2024-30 in Thailand. This would increase effective gas-fired capacity from the current 23GW to 34GW.

 RE capacity should go up by roughly 10GW over 2023-30: Thailand started RE auctions in 2023, auctioning 5GW of new RE projects. We expect another 5GW round of auctions to be held in 2024. ## Q: Which companies are undergoing wellbalanced transformation?

By balanced transformation, we mean 1) not being dependent on domestic expansion; 2) careful selection of geographical market exposure; and 3) adequate funding arrangements for expansion. We have studied the plans of all eight major ASEAN power producers. Based on these three parameters, our three top picks SCI, ACEN and TEN - stand out.

## All eight major ASEAN producers are transitioning

We have studied the capex plans of all eight major ASEAN power producers. ## Appendix-2

## Equity IRRs are determined by six variable inputs

There are six key inputs that determine the volatility of a project's IRRs, with a qualitative aspect to each of the six inputs. Power tariff, for example, is typically one of the biggest drivers of equity IRRs. The six variable inputs are:

1. Project installation cost/MW: In our view, this along with power tariff and capacity factor are the three largest determinants of eventual equity or project IRR for a RE project. For solar projects, this input is determined by variables, such as solar power modules, inverter costs, engineering, procurement and construction (EPC) costs, land costs and transmission line costs. We are currently witnessing global installation cost/MW for solar projects in the range of US\$0.3-1.0m/MW.

2. Power tariff: Assuming most RE projects have a fixed tariff that the producer receives from the buyer (typically the state utility of the government), power tariffs are fixed in advance for an agreed sales period (typically 20-25 years). However, power tariffs for solar projects can vary significantly across markets globally. We are currently global tariffs for solar projects in the range of US\$3-9cents/kWh.

3. Capacity factor: The amount of electricity as a percentage of total capacity that a solar panel and a solar farm generates is the capacity factor. Capacity factor in the case of solar projects is determined by a variety of factors, including resource quality (sunnier locations have more hours of direct, high-angle sunlight), tracking capabilities (additional equipment to track the sun's angle helps to maximise energy input into the solar PV system), and inverter-sizing (larger inverters convert the direct current produced by solar PV panels to grid-ready AC power). We are currently witnessing global solar capacity factors in the range of 10-25%.

4. Debt ratio: Higher project debt ratios typically lead to higher equity IRRs, and vice versa, as the amount of equity invested fluctuates accordingly, thus determining the absolute returns to the equity shareholders. Typically, in Asia, the debt ratio ranges between 65% and 75%.

5. Cost of debt: This has an inverse relationship with equity IRR. The higher the cost of debt, the lower the equity returns. Cost of debt can vary significantly between markets.

6. Operating expense ratio: Operating expenses for RE projects – solar and wind in particular – typically account for around 10-20% of revenue, depending on economies of scale. There is usually no fuel cost for RE projects, unlike for coal- and gas-fired projects.
