# Inflation trends: US, UK and euro area on diverging paths back to target

We develop a comparative macro framework to evaluate inflation dynamics in the US, euro area (EA), and UK. We start with a top-down view looking at aggregate demand/supply dynamics to understand price dynamics at the granular component level. We then try to assess the strength of medium-term price pressures by focusing on labour markets.

1 Aggregate demand vs supply. Comparing real domestic demand relative to Q4-2019 with real GDP as a proxy for aggregate supply, we find the UK has likely seen the largest demand/supply imbalance, with negative supply shocks resulting in excess demand. The US also saw elevated demand relative to supply, though comparatively less than the UK. The EA stands out with domestic demand growing at a significantly slower pace than aggregate supply.

n Underlying price dynamics. Despite the global inflation surge, the spread of price pressures has filtered through very differently across geographies since 2019. The biggest single drivers of inflation were owner-equivalent rents in the US, food & energy commodities in EA, while services and core goods in the UK far outstripped the rise in commodities. Looking ahead, underlying inflation metrics have now convincingly turned in all three areas, but remain elevated relative to the central banks' targets on traditional metrics.

n Inflation expectations remain broadly anchored in our own dbDIG surveys – even if volatile.

n Mixed labour market dynamics. In the UK, labour supply has recovered to its pre-pandemic level, but unlike the EA or US remains far below the prepandemic trend. Labour demand remains robust in all three regions but has started to decline. In recent years wage and unit labour cost pressures, correspondingly, have been most pronounced in the UK. The US productivity outperformance has led to seemingly more sustainable real wage gains for workers. In the euro area, real wages have only just recovered amid weaker bargaining power than in the UK and weaker productivity growth than in the US.

The speed, timing, and pace of monetary policy normalization will therefore differ across geographies given the heterogeneous inflation situation. Our analysis suggests a deeper easing cycle in the euro area than the US, while the Bank of England should deliver the slowest and most gradual normalization in 2024.

## Introduction

In this article we take a deeper look at the global drivers of inflation over the last few years. We take stock of the current situation and study how the inflation outlook may evolve across the US, euro area (EA), and UK.

First, we set-up a broad comparative macro framework. We start by looking more generally at aggregate supply and aggregate demand including what the near-term outlook means for short-term inflation dynamics and underlying inflation.

Second, we shift to a medium-term outlook by focusing on inflation expectations – looking more closely at labour market dynamics. The focus on labour markets, including productivity, gives us a better understanding of how wages and domestic price pressures could evolve in the medium-term.

Our conclusions are four-fold.

First, comparing real domestic demand relative to Q4-2019 with real GDP as a proxy for aggregate supply, we find the UK has likely seen the largest demand/supply imbalance, with negative supply shocks resulting in excess demand. The US also saw elevated demand relative to supply, though comparatively less than the UK. The EA stands out with domestic demand growing by a significantly slower pace than aggregate supply.

Second, despite the global inflation surge, the spread of price pressures have filtered through very differently across geographies since 2019. The biggest single drivers of inflation were owner-equivalent rents in the US and food & energy commodities in EA, while services and core goods in the UK far outstripped the rise in commodities. Looking ahead, underlying inflation metrics have now convincingly turned in all three areas, but remain elevated relative to the central banks' targets on traditional metrics.

Third, in the UK, labour supply has recovered to its pre-pandemic level, but unlike the EA or US remains far below the pre-pandemic trend. Labour demand remains robust in all three regions but has started to decline. In recent years wage and unit labour costs pressures, correspondingly, have been most pronounced in the UK. The US productivity outperformance has led to seemingly more sustainable real wage gains for workers. In the euro area, real wages have only just recovered amid weaker bargaining power than in the UK and weaker productivity growth than in the US.

And lastly, the speed, timing, and pace of monetary policy normalization will differ across geographies given the heterogeneous inflation situation. Our analysis suggests a deeper easing cycle in the euro area than the US, while the Bank of England should deliver the slowest and most gradual normalization in 2024.

Relative to end-2019, the US economy experienced a strong aggregate domestic demand shock with supply capacity struggling to match it. The euro area, on the other hand, did not experience such a boost to domestic demand. Looking at current conditions through the prism of PMIs presents a similar conclusion (Figure 2 and Figure 3). Demand has picked up in recent months but continues to look softest (and close to recessionary levels) in the euro area. This is driven by an improved outlook for goods consumption and residential investment as the economy and labour market remain resilient despite headwinds.

n We expect euro area GDP to remain broadly stagnant around its summer 2022 level through to summer 2024. We expect growth to only sustainably return to positive territory from mid-year as transmission peaks and disinflation eventually lifts consumption.

n The UK ended 2023 in a mild technical recession. Looking ahead, we expect GDP growth to pick up steam from spring onwards, lifted by positive sentiment, a recovery in the housing market, easier financial conditions and slightly looser fiscal policy.

## Decomposing the CPI/HICP dynamics

## Building a broad comparable framework for comparing inflation

In order to assess the overall post-pandemic inflationary impact across the three countries, we build fully comparable sub-components of the monthly inflation indices followed closely by market participants. To be more specific, we compare the euro area and UK (based on Eurostat/ONS methodologies) with US CPI subcomponents. We define the components as follows:

n Energy is the combination of fuel prices and also utility bills. In the euro area and UK this is defined as such by Eurostat and the ONS. In the US CPI, utility

bills are termed energy services <sup>1</sup>

n Food, alcohol and tobacco (FAT): in the euro area and UK this is defined as such by Eurostat and the ONS. In the US, we build this sub-component as the sum of food at home, alcohol at home, and tobacco products.

1 Non-energy industrial goods (NEIG): in the euro area and UK this is defined as such by Eurostat and the ONS. In the US we build this sub-component by subtracting alcohol at home and tobacco products from commodities less food and energy.

n Services ex. housing: Housing is a big difference in trans-Atlantic inflation measures. We exclude rents from the EA and UK services basket. In the US, we exclude from services: energy services, rents of primary residents, and owners' equivalent rent, and we include food away from home.

n Housing: this is defined as rents in EA and UK. In the US, this includes rents of primary residents and owners' equivalent rent.

Figure 5 presents the weights of these four baskets. US housing accounts for about a third of the US CPI basket while housing (rents) in Europe account for a little more than 5% of the respective baskets. This divergence is entirely explained by the inclusion of owner-occupied rent in the US CPI, which accounts for about quarter of that country's basket.

When looking at the structure of consumption baskets outside of housing, the relative composition of the inflation baskets differs by far less across the Atlantic. This is illustrated in Figure 6 where we look at the relative weights of each component in the basket excluding housing. For instance, households spend about 10% of their non-housing consumption on energy in all three geographies.

## Components contribution to the inflation shock

The interaction of aggregate demand and supply across geographies naturally feeds into the evolution of prices in the economy. In this section, we decompose the inflation evolution through its various components. Figure 7 shows the relative contribution to the inflation shock between Dec-19 and Dec-23 of the various subcomponents. The price level shock in the US was driven by the strong contribution from owners' equivalent rent (OER), which is not surprising given its relatively large weight. Outside of that, food and energy had far less of an impact than in Europe. Excluding housing, the core inflation shock looks surprisingly similar between the euro area and the US, while the UK stands out with a much sharper price level increase to services and core goods.

## Labour market and wages

The speed with which labour market participation normalized post-Covid differed across geographies. In the euro area, labour supply at the end of 2023 is back on its pre-pandemic trend while the US is within reach of achieving this. In the UK, labour supply has recovered its pre-pandemic levels but remains significantly below trend, despite historically strong net migration flows (we would note that sampling issues within the LFS call for some caution in interpreting the latest labour market data in the UK).

Putting labour supply and demand dynamics together results in the price of labour clearing in the market: wages. Wage growth increased in 2022-23 to historical highs as a result of the strong search for labour and skills by firms. Post pandemic, euro area and US wages peaked around similar levels of 5.5% yoy while the UK wage growth peaked at much higher levels (above 7.0% yoy). We expect wage growth to converge back across geographies towards 3.0-3.5% yoy in 2025/26. In 2024, we expect more persistence in the euro area wage measures, while the UK should experience an abrupt deceleration (owing to rapidly declining inflation expectations – including a softening in public sector pay settlements). In the US, after jumping to 5.0% yoy in Q4-23, we expect wage momentum to slow to 4.0% yoy in H1-24 and 3.5% yoy by end-2024.

As labour demand softens, expectations normalize and labour supply continues catching up to trend (at least in US/UK), wage pressures are set to diminish. Still, the sharp fall in headline inflation rates in our forecasts should imply an improvement in workers' real wage growth. To illustrate this point, in Figure 27 and Figure 28 we use our wage and inflation forecasts to track the implied real wage levels for private sector workers relative to Q4-19<sup>3</sup> :

n In the US, private sector real wages were 4.2% higher when deflated by the GDP deflator and 2.75% higher than at end-2019 when deflated by the CPI. Our wage and inflation forecasts imply that US workers should continue to experience meaningful real wage gains in the coming years.

n In the UK, the negative labour supply shock and high labour demand resulted in workers still experiencing real wage gains of 2.8% at end-23 vs end-19 when deflated by the GDP deflator and 1.4% when deflated by the CPI. Elevated wage growth amid falling headline inflation rates should keep those real wage gains increasing for UK workers up to 5.0% at end-25 when deflated by the deflator and 4.5% if deflated by the CPI.

n In the euro area, workers' real wages remained lower than prepandemic when deflated by the HICP at the end of 2023. The gap between HICP and GDP deflator reveals the importance from a macro and firms' perspective to consider the split between workers and firms of the permanent external price shock experienced in recent years. As workers in the euro area try to recoup their lagging purchasing power, nominal wage growth may remain elevated as reflected in our forecasts (see here). While the negotiated set-up of wage dynamics in EA may facilitate a smooth, albeit slow, process for this real wage catch-up to occur over the years as the Figures below highlight, it may make it harder for policymakers to gain confidence in wage growth rates being consistent with mediumterm inflation at target.

## Productivity, unit labour costs and labour share

Workers in the US have experienced material real wage growth and should continue to do so compared to euro area workers despite an overall consumer price index shock of similar magnitude. To better understand this we now look at how productivity has evolved and the split between workers and capital.

Figure 29 highlights that labour productivity in the US picked up amid the reopening as well as throughout 2023. Our latest forecasts expect the US to continue experiencing a productivity boom which translates into a unit labour costs outlook that allows for a soft-landing and inflation to converge towards target (Figure 30). In the euro area, labour productivity has stagnated in the last four years, as firms relied exclusively on labour input to boost output. The combination of a tight labour market with an externally-driven inflation shock resulted in nominal wage and unit labour costs inflation. In the UK, the euro area dynamics were amplified even

further.<sup>4</sup>

Finally, in the US corporate profits increased as firms were able to pass on higher price increases to consumers. Figure 31 highlights the counterpart: how the labour share has fallen since the end of 2019, despite higher real wages. Once again, the productivity boom in the US is key to understanding this development. In the euro area, the labour share has remained broadly stable and is expected to remain as such. In the UK, relatively strong real wages alongside stagnating productivity have resulted in a large pick-up in unit labour costs and a rise in the labour share.

## Policy rates

The inflation and labour market outlooks are important drivers of our expectations for Central Bank policy rates decisions in 2024 and are also geographically consistent. The euro area inflation shock was the least domestically-driven and is the one correcting fastest with the lowest upside risks in the medium-term. The US has made a lot progress on inflation but still needs to prove that wages and domestic services are on a sustainable path towards target. The UK has experienced the most severe negative supply-driven shock with still relatively elevated wage and services inflation.

n We continue to anticipate that the Fed's first rate cut will come in June but that the Fed will only cut rates by 100bps this year. Next year, we expect the Fed to gradually move rates closer to a neutral level of 3.5%. Risks are balanced around our Fed expectations this year, but we see longer-term risks skewed towards a higher nominal neutral rate (e.g., 4.0%).

n The path to monetary easing is getting murkier. Data needs to surprise to the downside if the ECB is not to delay the first cut beyond April (see here). We continue to forecast 150bp of cuts in 2024 and a further 50bp of cuts in 2025, implying a terminal rate of 2.0%. We now see this easing cycle as more likely consisting of quarter point cuts than anything larger. Uncertainty is high, however, and the parameters of the easing cycle remain subject to change.

n More evidence of spare capacity will likely be needed before the BoE can adjust Bank Rate – something we think it will have by spring. For now, we continue to think that recent data are consistent with some gradual easing, though whether the MPC opts to wait until June before adjusting rates is becoming a more pertinent question (our basecase is for a May-24 rate cut). We expect only three quarter point rate cuts in 2024, with a more backloaded easing cycle. We expect Bank Rate to eventually settle at 3.0% by mid-2025.

Macroeconomic fluctuations often account for most of the risks associated with exposures to instruments that promise to pay fixed or variable interest rates. For an investor who is long fixed-rate instruments (thus receiving these cash flows), increases in interest rates naturally lift the discount factors applied to the expected cash flows and thus cause a loss. The longer the maturity of a certain cash flow and the higher the move in the discount factor, the higher will be the loss. Upside surprises in inflation, fiscal funding needs, and FX depreciation rates are among the most common adverse macroeconomic shocks to receivers. But counterparty exposure, issuer creditworthiness, client segmentation, regulation (including changes in assets holding limits for different types of investors), changes in tax policies, currency convertibility (which may constrain currency conversion, repatriation of profits and/or liquidation of positions), and settlement issues related to local clearing houses are also important risk factors. The sensitivity of fixed-income instruments to macroeconomic shocks may be mitigated by indexing the contracted cash flows to inflation, to FX depreciation, or to specified interest rates - these are common in emerging markets. The index fixings may - by construction - lag or mis-measure the actual move in the underlying variables they are intended to track. The choice of the proper fixing (or metric) is particularly important in swaps markets, where floating coupon rates (i.e., coupons indexed to a typically short-dated interest rate reference index) are exchanged for fixed coupons. Funding in a currency that differs from the currency in which coupons are denominated carries FX risk.
