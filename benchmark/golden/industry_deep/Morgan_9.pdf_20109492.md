Last week, investor debate centred on the unwind of various of carry trades in FX markets. While each of these drivers has had its own dynamics and idiosyncratic drivers, the weakness in activity data coming from the eurozone and China raised some concerns about global growth, following the softer data seen from the US in the first half of July. Our economists see downside risks to their forecasts for both the eurozone (see here) and China (see here), with further risks posed to the latter's economic momentum by this week's official PMI (see here)

These data misses were relatively small and don’t on their own explain the extent of the decline in risk assets, even if they did support USD.

It's likely in our view that JPY strength added to investor anxiety over the performance of risk assets and global economic activity, given JPY's reputation as a safe haven, even though JPY strength has largely been driven by domestic factors or factors unrelated to the global business cycle.

Our generally upbeat view on USD has been informed to a large extent by the USDsupportive developments we've been expecting to occur outside the US – namely, weaker growth, lower inflation and market pricing of rate cuts outside the US, helping to keep USD supported by default.

## CNH versus JPY divergence still in play

Admittedly, there is an inherent tension between our generally upbeat view on USD and the risk that carry trades are unwound on the back of global growth concerns, since USD is generally a high-carry currency versus much of global GDP.

Long USD versus Asia is after all a carry trade. Yet the cross should also gain some support as growth in China starts to slow.

Since concerns about China growth are a key factor behind FX behaviour, we think that it is contradictory to see USD/CNH actually fall as growth weakens and onshore equities fall. Staying long USD/CNH is the appropriate position, in our view, reflecting continued expectations of US outperformance and having the benefit of also being high carry, though the latter is incidental rather than motivating.

This week we saw USD/CNY fall in sympathy with USD/JPY, alongside an unwinding of carry trades. In the end though, we expect to see some divergence between USD/CNH and USD/JPY, and see JPY continuing to outperform CNH.

In the short term, we expect to see a rate hike from the BoJ at the upcoming policy meeting. This should boost JPY, by helping to compress yield differentials relative to the US. The FOMC also meets on July 31, and should signal that it is close to cutting rates. The team expects to see lower 2-year UST yields, helping the yield compression with JPY rates.

In China, the PBOC has been easing policy and onshore CNY FX-implied yields have dropped sharply to around 1% in the past week and are at now at the bottom end of the range of the last 10 years ( Exhibit 4 ). Hedging onshore FX risk in China has thus rarely been cheaper, particularly given the high yields on offer in USD. The yield differential of a long JPY versus short CNY position has also moved substantially in JPY’s favour over the past four years.

How the authorities in Tokyo and Beijing engage with the risk of tariffs is also likely to influence the performance of their currencies relative to USD and each other. We expect this dynamic to result in JPY outperformance versus CNY/H.

## BB on the up

In our mid-year outlook, we highlighted the rich pricing of most BB rated credits but that it was justified by the fundamental improvements that most have seen in recent years: We also noted that the fundamental improvements qualified some as IG upgrade candidates. This included Paraguay and Azerbaijan, which both received their first upgrades to IG by Moody's and Fitch, respectively, on Friday. We find that Paraguay's upgrade was more anticipated than Azerbaijan's according to market pricing.

Notably, these were the first credits to see upgrades to IG since Croatia (or Greece if using a broader set of countries). Before their index ratings reach full IG and they are added to IG indices, they both still need one additional upgrade.

Paraguay needs one upgrade by either S&P or Fitch, both of which are at BB+ ratings with stable outlooks. S&P last upgraded the credit in February, meaning that the road to an upgrade to IG may be longer. However, Fitch last moved in December 2018. Fitch last published a ratings report on Paraguay in November 2023 and published a report in June highlighting fiscal consolidation remaining on track. Still, agencies tend to be hesitant to upgrade a credit without a positive outlook beforehand, particularly upgrading a credit from HY to IG.

Azerbaijan would need one upgrade by S&P or Moody's, which are at BB+ stable and Ba1 positive, respectively. S&P last upgraded the credit in January 2021 and has since tended to publish research updates in June, most recently publishing last month. Moody's also recently published a ratings update turning to a positive outlook earlier this month, meaning that further upgrades from either are unlikely to be imminent.

## Are investors too bearish on CNY?

## We have been clear about our view on CNY:

1. The weakening balance of payments points to a weaker CNY.

2. The PBOC has the ability and willingness to keep CNY stable if it wants.

3. The market could see a strong USD and increasing concerns about tariffs before the US election.

4. We are concerned about the PBOC reaction to the potential tariffs, the chances of which could increase with a Republican White House.

5. We expect the PBOC to let USD/CNY go higher and preemptively alleviate the depreciation pressure on CNY before the US election.

6. Such a move in USD/CNY could raise concerns about China exporting deflation via FX depreciation, which could weigh on the rest of Asia FX (see Asia Macro Strategy Mid-Year Outlook: Stress Testing Our Three Themes, June 3, 2024).

For our views on the election, potential tariffs and their impact, see US Public Policy: Navigating a Noisy Election, June 14, 2024, and Asia Economics: The Viewpoint: How US elections could affect Asia, July 2, 2024.

We started to see signs of the PBOC's tolerance for a weaker CNY as it moved the fixing from 7.11 to 7.13 in June and July. This increases our confidence that USD/CNY could move higher before the election. We express the trade via short CNH/INR and long 6m USD/CNH 7.40/7.60 call spread.

What is the consensus: Our conversations with clients suggest that almost all investors agree with the view that CNY could be weaker in the short and medium term but there is different view about when the PBOC will let CNY weaken – whether it would be before or after the election should tariff risks rise.

Can Trump surprise? Given the bearish sentiment/positioning on CNY, we have received increasing questions about whether President Trump would actually raise tariffs should he be elected in November. Could he sound hawkish on China but reach some deal just with the threat of tariffs? If that happened, it could have a significantly positive impact on CNY given the bearish positioning in the market.

What if tariffs are not raised when everyone is so bearish on CNY? Our initial reaction to such a question is that it is unlikely, based on what we saw in 2018/19 and what President Trump said during a Bloomberg interview two weeks ago, i.e., that tariffs seem more likely than not.

However, after deliberating over the past few days, we find ourselves thinking about the alternative: what if the US and China enter lengthy trade negotiations, before tariffs are announced, at a time when the market consensus is bearish on CNY?

We went back to review what happened in late 2019 when the US and China were intensively negotiating the trade deal. For context, we were more optimistic about the trade deal and we recommended short USD/CNH in September 2019 (see EM Strategy Update: Short USD/CNH: Has the Market Underestimated the Importance of the Soybean Tariff Exemption? September 20, 2019).

At that time, the market was pretty pessimistic given the back and forth on the tariffs the two countries imposed on each other and hawkish comments from trade negotiators. The consensus was that there wouldn't be a deal or the deal would be shaky and without too much rollback of previous tariffs even if there was a deal.

## The logic of our analysis is to consider what China and the US wanted.

From China's perspective, growth slowed down from 6.9%Y in 1Q18 to 6%Y in 3Q19. China needed a trade deal in order to revive growth. But it had to sign a trade deal that would look fair from the Chinese perspective, i.e., roll back some tariffs.

From the US side, President Trump had some incentive to sign a trade deal given that the US was heading into an election cycle in 2020.

We spent a lot of time looking into China's agriculture imports from the US. Soybean is the most important agriculture export for the US. The US soybean season runs from September to March and China normally starts to import from November. Had the US pushed China to walk away from the trade deal, China would not have imported in late 2019/early 2020. This would have left the next imports to happen in late 2020, which would have been too late for the election. This was one incentive to sign a trade deal in late 2019 rather than delaying it into 2020.

The other incentive was to keep the conversation going rather than breaking the deal completely. This is particularly important when two countries are negotiating. It is plausible that the US may have been concerned that if it took an overly tough line in the trade negotiations, China could have walked away because Beijing could wait for the US election cycle to see the potential change of trade policy.

Indeed, the US may have had an interest in avoiding the breakdown of negotiations given its stated goal of a comprehensive trade agreement. After trade negotiations stalled in May 2019, negotiations resumed quickly after President Xi and President Trump's call on June 18 and their follow-up meeting at the G20 Summit in Japan on June 28, with the US election due in November 2019.

Fast forward to 2025, and the market could face a similar situation. The concern has been that President Trump could announce tariffs immediately should he be elected in January 2025. Could it be possible? Many investors we talk to believe so. What is the alternative?

We think it would depend on when two leaders are going to talk over the phone. For context, in November 2016 when President Trump was elected. President Xi called and congratulated him on November 14. Both sides had another call on February 10, 2017 after President Trump took over as president.

In 2020 when President Biden won the election, instead of a call, President Xi sent a congratulation message to President Biden on November 25. The presidents had the call on February 11, 2021 after President Biden took office.

If history repeats itself, the very first call between the two presidents would be in February 2025. Now the question is whether tariffs would rise before that should Trump be elected. If we use the same logic above to argue that he doesn't want China to walk away from negotiations, there might be a good chance that he will wait for the call to happen to make the decision.

Hence, the alternative scenario could be that after the February call both sides agree to engage in trade talks in order to avoid tariffs on each other.

Our point here is that while we ultimately believe that President Trump would raise tariffs on imports from China, we cannot rule out a period of negotiations first.

This would be a risk to the consensus bearish view on CNY.

What does it mean for the CNY market? We are comfortable with our current view on CNY and believe that the market still underestimates the risk that the PBOC lets CNY weaken in a strong USD environment into the US election. But we also believe that investors might be too bearish on CNY when considering the actual tariff hikes should they happen in early 2025.

In other words, we should be cautious on CNY from now until November or January (depending on the election result) and we should fade bearishness on CNY in January/ February 2025.

What to trade: We would like to positive for downside in USD/CNH following January's inauguration day. Instead of buying outright puts, we are selling a 3-month knockout to cheapen the premium – a 9-month USD/CNH 7.0 put with 3-month downside American knockout at 7.10 would cost 39bp, saving 65% of the premium from a vanilla option.

## Definition of terms

Buy/Long: The analyst expects the total or excess return (depending on the nature of the recommendation) of the instrument or issuer that is the subject of the investment recommendation to be positive over the relevant time period.

Sell/Short: The analyst expects the total or excess return (depending on the nature of the recommendation) of the instrument or issuer that is the subject of the investment recommendation to be negative over the relevant time period.

Selling Protection or Buying Risk: The analyst expects that the price of protection against the event occurring will decrease over the relevant time period.

Buying Protection or Selling Risk: The analyst expects the price of protection against the event occurring will increase over the relevant time period.

Pay: The analyst expects that over the specified time period the variable rate underlying the swap agreement that is the subject of the investment recommendation will increase.

Receive: The analyst expects that over the specified time period the variable rate underlying the swap agreement that is the subject of the investment recommendation will decrease.

Like: Based on current market conditions as of the date of this report the analyst expects that the relevant securities of the issuer that is subject of the recommendation will perform favorably over the relevant time period as compared to the overall market of comparable securities by other issuers. This is not intended to be, nor should it be interpreted as a formal fundamental rating of the issuer or its creditworthiness.

Dislike: Based on current market conditions as of the date of this report the analyst expects that the relevant securities of the issuer that is subject of the recommendation will perform unfavorably over the relevant time period as compared to the overall market of comparable securities by other issuers. This is not intended to be, nor should it be interpreted as a formal fundamental rating of the issuer or its creditworthiness.
