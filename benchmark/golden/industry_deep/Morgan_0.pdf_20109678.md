## Market commentary

Just like the Olympics, there was no shortage of price action in the fixed income markets this week. The agenda was packed with economic data and policy decisions that sent rates tumbling across the curve. In the front-end, 2-year yields took a dramatic plunge, dropping by 52bp to 3.87%, its lowest level in 15 months. Meanwhile, 10-year yields fell by 40bp to 3.79%, dipping below 4% for the first time since February. In the money markets, the 1m1y OIS curve and the 1m1y T-bills curve further inverted, closing the week at -112bp and -92bp, respectively.

Yields moved significantly lower following the release of the July jobs report, which was weak across nearly all measures. Non-farm payrolls increased by just 114k, and there were downward revisions of 29k for the prior month. More importantly, the unemployment rate ticked up by 0.2%-points to 4.3% in July, the fourth consecutive increase and now up 0.9%-points from the cycle low. The average workweek fell a tick to 34.2 hours, and average hourly earnings increased only 0.2%, bringing the year-ago gain down 3.6%. Moreover, the BLS reported that Hurricane Beryl had “no discernible effect” on the employment and unemployment data (see Weak jobs to prompt strong Fed action M. Feroli, 8/2/24). This reading raises further concerns about the fragility of the labor market, which is increasingly becoming a key area of focus for policy makers. To be sure, at the post-FOMC meeting, Chair Powell acknowledged that some committee members were in favor of a rate cut at the current meeting. However, he emphasized that future decisions would be data-dependent, with a particular focus on labor market conditions (see Powell has a labor market bee in his bonnet, M. Feroli, 8/1/24).

To that end, additional data releases this week indicated further cooling in the labor market. The Conference Board consumer confidence survey was revised down significantly in June, from 24.0% to 19.8%, and further to 18.1% in July. Meanwhile, the JOLTS report showed declines in openings, hires, quits, and layoffs in June. The vacancy/unemployment ratio dropped to 1.20 from 1.24 in May, reaching a new cycle low and now similar to pre-COVID levels (see US: Confidence and JOLTS consistent with easing labor market, A. Reinhart, 8/1/24). The Employment Cost Index showed a 0.9% increase for the three months ending in June, causing the year-ago rate to edge down to 4.1% from 4.2% the prior quarter. This pullback, reflected in both wages and benefits, aligns with the slow disinflation in price inflation and the gradual loosening of the labor market (see US: Comp disinflation resumes; ADP jobs near BLS estimate, A. Reinhart, 7/31/24). Elsewhere, the headline ISM and PMI manufacturing surveys for July both showed a loss in momentum, with the ISM report indicating a much larger deterioration to a notably lower level of 46.8, just above its June 2023 cycle low (see US: Weaker July manufacturing surveys show divergent trends, M. Hanson, 8/1/24).

All told, our economist now expects a 50bp cut in September AND November, followed by 25bp cuts at every FOMC meeting thereafter to 3% next year. Admittedly, from a risk management perspective, there’s a strong case to act before September 18<sup>th</sup>, but we think Chair Powell will unlikely want to add more noise to what has already been an event-filled summer. Considering this, our Treasury strategists have revised their forecasts accordingly. They now forecast the 2-year yield and 10-year yield to reach 3.20% and 3.50%, respectively, by YE24. Similarly, we have adjusted our forecasts downward and now foresee SOFR and EFFR ending the year at 4.10% ( Figure 1Given thawoxpc mrgs , dju f ly). Markets have also priced in a faster pace of easing, with OIS forwards expecting 121bp of cumulative rate cuts by YE24 and 232bp of cumulative rate cuts by YE25 (Figure 2Markets hvpicd nf og, wOIS x 12buml y YE4 3 5).

With Fed cuts coming more into reality and a more aggressive Fed easing agenda on the horizon, liquidity investors will likely continue to extend further out the money markets curve. In fact, we have already witnessed increased demand for T-bills, particularly in the 17-week and 26-week maturities, with bid-to-cover ratios steadily rising over the past few weeks (Figure 3). On the credit side though, as we discuss further below, it’s been a bit more challenging to extend into fixed given the drag on MMF yields heading into MMF reforms in October. Regardless, ON RRP balances should gradually decline, with levels expected to range between the low to mid \$300bn levels in the near term. Modest increases in T-bill auction sizes next week should further support a lower ON RRP balance.

It is perhaps worth noting that SOFR registered 5.35% as of August 1, down from 5.38% at month-end but up from 5.33% the day before month-end. As expected, a combination of Treasury settlements and dealers’ balance sheet constraints pressured SOFR higher on month-end. In fact, primary dealers’ inventories of Treasuries have continued to hover near their highest levels in terms of total positions (Figure 4). Additionally, the end of July coincided with the Canadian quarter-end, which may have contributed to the upward pressure on the benchmark rate as some Canadian dealers reduced their balance sheet exposure. Similar to the past couple of months, there has been a bit of a month-end hangover, but we anticipate SOFR will continue to decline lower, albeit at a slower pace. As previously noted, we currently do not foresee any funding concerns and expect QT to continue for the remainder of this year.

## August refunding review

On refunding, Treasury announced unchanged auction sizes for 3-year, 10-year, and 30-year notes and bonds, as well as other nominal coupon and FRN auctions, indicating no need for increases until the second half of 2025. Following this week’s announcement, our Treasuries strategists continue to expect \$2310bn in privately-held borrowing this year, coupons increase by \$1909bn and bills by \$400bn. In the near term, Treasury expects short-dated T-bill auction sizes to modestly increase next week, followed by reductions in early to mid-September, ahead of the corporate tax date.

Notably, TBAC discussed the role of T-bills, emphasizing its importance to serve as an optimal shock absorber to allow Treasury to issue coupons in a manner consistent with its “regular and predictable” tenet, supporting lower funding costs, lower term premia, and distribution to a broad and diverse set of investors. On this note, the TBAC members argued that “Evidence of a structural and persistent increase in the term premia could increase the appropriate range for T-bill usage (and vice-versa). Treasury should prioritize being Regular & Predictable rather than timing shorter-term moves in term premia.” While the T-bill share is currently very close to the 20% average observed over the past three decades, our strategists believe this effectively widens the optimal range from 15-20% to closer to 15-25%, assuming 20% remains the long-term average, which supports our view that Treasury can maintain the current coupon auction size through mid- to late-2025.

Lastly, on buybacks, Treasury plans on increasing liquidity support buyback operation sizes and engage in cash management buyback operations over the coming quarter. Treasury plans on increasing the maximum purchase amount per operation from \$2bn to \$4bn across the five nominal coupon buckets in the front-end and intermediate maturities, while leaving caps unchanged at \$2bn for the two long-end buckets and at \$500mn for the two TIPS bucket. Cash management buybacks will conduct four operations of up to \$5bn each in September to manage T-bill supply around the corporate tax date, though the impact is expected to be modest (see Treasuries).

So why might liquidity investors not be adding more duration via fixed rate exposure? First, we think it’s fair to say there remains a lack of conviction in terms of how the easing cycle will unfold. While incoming data has shown continued softening in inflation and a labor market that is coming into better balance, there is a degree of uncertainty as to just how many Fed cuts that translates into in 2H24.
