## Debt: It's What's For Breakfast, Lunch, and Dinner

Elections in France, the UK, and the US put a spotlight on debt and deficits – subjects in which investor interest will remain long after the elections pass by. Our economists offered their thoughts on the matter in Elections and fiscal (un-)sustainability. Beyond the elections, the IMF projects higher gross debt-to-GDP ratios for each country over the next five years ( Exhibit 1 ).

Investors usually ask questions about debt sustainability whenever government debt-to-GDP ratios rise to new highs, or, as in the most recent episode, when think-tanks project higher highs than before. Absent from discussions is the fact that previous concerns usually amounted to needless hand-wringing, at least in countries with modern monetary systems such as Japan and the US.

Most recently, the US Congressional Budget Office (CBO) projected that, by 2054, gross federal government debt as a percentage of GDP would rise from 122% to 179% ( Exhibit 2 ). This, alongside the CBO's recent update to its 10-year budget projections, generated another round of investor questions.

Consider the fact that the previous two-decade rise from 55% to 122% occurred without the bond market throwing a tantrum – something we would have seen via higher term premiums. Of course, the 2009-2019 explosion in debt-to-GDP occurred at much lower interest rates than exist today.

As a result, the CBO projects much higher net interest payments over the next 30 years – increases that exceed noninterest spending ( Exhibit 4 ). The CBO's projections for interest rates come with large error bands, of course.

But at least the CBO itself isn't projecting a more dramatic rise in Treasury yields as a result of debt sustainability concerns – a projection, as unintentional as it might have been, we agree with wholeheartedly.

The similarity between Taylor (1993) rule-implied policy in the CBO projections and the 1990s strikes our interest. Our economists have drawn comparisons between the current environment for Fed policy and that of the mid-1990s (see The 1990s … a precedent for the unprecedented?).

At the same time, the CBO projects 10y Treasury yields to remain between 4% and 4.5% – roughly 200bp lower than in the mid-1990s.

• We think the implied flattening of the yield curve in the CBO projections makes sense. The larger Fed balance sheet and those of other central banks like the ECB and BoJ have placed downward pressure on term premiums since 2008.

Investors, though, worry about a re-steepening of the yield curve driven by term premium expansion. And if rates in the future rise more than the CBO projects, then that could drive net interest expense even higher than projected already.

For now, we aren't concerned about such a large term premium expansion causing longend rates to return to 1990s levels.

• Non-federal government debt grew at a much lower rate over the past year than in the mid-1990s – so much so that, when added together with federal government debt, total debt also grew slower over the past year than in the mid-1990s.

Between 1995 and 1998, total debt grew between 17% (1995) and 25% (1998) of GDP compared to 13.6% over the past year ( Exhibit 5 ). Importantly, federal government debt did not drive that growth ( Exhibit 6 ).

Rather, other sources drove the rise in debt, such as financial businesses, nonfinancial businesses, and households. The mid-1990s, growth in non-federal government debt could lead a reasonable person to conclude that the Fed hadn't set monetary policy in restrictive territory during that period.

• If true, it offers an interesting perspective on why the Fed didn't lower rates much or quickly in 1995, and why it had to reverse course with a rate hike in 1997.

In addition, lackluster growth in non-federal government debt over the past year hints at a different stance of Fed monetary policy than in 1995-1998. That could mean growth in federal government debt – 10.1% of GDP over the past year versus 0-2% from 1995-1998 – is playing an important role in holding up economic growth.

Indeed, instead of worrying about higher term premiums with more federal government debt growth, investors may want to consider what could happen to term premiums, and rate levels more generally, if the federal government reduced its borrowing – leaving the restrictive stance of monetary policy to operate unchecked.

In the meantime, we think investors will continue to worry about larger deficits and higher term premiums, despite the more recent decline in the cash flow deficit we cited two weeks ago. As worries grow, so too might term premiums, for now.

## Two Elections, Two Different Meanings for Markets

## French elections: Heading into the first round

On Sunday, June 30, the first round of the presidential elections will take place, with the second and decisive round taking place on July 7. The snap elections were called by President Macron following a very strong result obtained by the right-wing party Rassemblement National (RN hereafter) in the EU elections.

The decision has been seen as a sort of gamble by President Macron given that his party was already lagging both the RN and the left-wing in the polling. This event has, in recent weeks, been the main driver in euro-rates price action.

Uncertainties around the outcomes remain elevated given intricacies in the electoral system. An important condition, granting second-round qualification (in a given district), is to pull in more than 12.5% of registered voters, making participation rate a crucial variable in driving the outcome of the results and adding to the overall complexity of the situation. Different scenarios are possible:

1. A "no majority" outcome, which is seen in the short term as market-positive as it would imply an unchanged fiscal stance in France, even though it could result in political instability over the medium term.

2. An RN majority, which markets seem to see as a benign outcome given that the right-wing party has recently shown a more fiscally conservative approach despite lingering uncertainties around its full manifesto. Based on the measures already disclosed (i.e., pension reform and VAT cut on energy products), our economists estimate an increase in France’s deficit of \~0.7pp of GDP.

3. The left coalition NFP wins, which is likely the most adverse outcome from a market perspective given the left's aggressive fiscal stance, which could add as much as \~1.5pp of GDP in spending.

4. President Macron’s party wins – a very low-probability outcome as current polls imply.

RN also leads in terms of seats projections. Only a few pollsters see RN having an absolute majority, but polls have historically provided a poor indication of the realized outcome (see here).

In the meantime, in the lead-up to the event, the OAT/Bund spread widened by \~35bp, peaking last Thursday around \~82bp and currently stabilizing around the 80bp mark, i.e., the upper of the new "trading regime" that we envisioned on the 10y OAT/Bund between 65bp and 80bp, depending on the political premium priced in by the market (see From The Grind To The Unwind).

• We see the expected value on the 10y OAT/Bund spread at \~72bp, and perhaps a benign outcome on the elections could tighten the spread around those levels.

The implicit fair value on the OAT/Bund, consistent with the current average rating of France, is seen at \~67bp ( Exhibit 8 ), while still an indicative fair-value range gauged by combining valuations on both the OAT and Bund ASW is around 65bp.

So far, indications from the positioning side seem to point to an unwinding in shorts from investors, following the first widening push observed in the aftermath of the EU elections.

## UK elections: Unlikely to be a market mover

The UK elections, to be held on July 4, will be a major focus in the coming week, and we reported our base case expectations here. Broadly speaking, we expect the outcome to have a limited impact on the gilt market – predictions vary, but given current polling, anything but a Labour majority would be a big surprise next Thursday.

Any outcome far from consensus might impact valuations, and we see two main tail risks ahead of the elections: 1) a slim majority/hung parliament; and 2) a "Labour supermajority" – anything perhaps above 480 seats given current polls. Should those tail risks materialize, we expect the market reaction to lean toward a repricing of some gilt premium, as expectations for more fiscal easing might increase in both cases.

Indeed, a slim majority/hung parliament might require more concessions to minority parties, and a Labour supermajority could imply total control of the legislative program; both of them might fuel investor speculation about higher fiscal spending ahead of the next fiscal event, which is still some months away, and this would probably limit in any case the impact of the election outcome on gilt valuations (at least in the short term).

In addition, these appear to be tail risk events, and a result broadly in line with current polls should have a fairly muted impact on the gilt market, with even domestic investors' focus probably more on the French election implications.

## United States

A game changing debate? While we expected the presidential debate to highlight rising fiscal deficits, the debate also delivered one key change – based on betting markets and polls, there has been a clear relative shift in the probabilities of President Trump winning over President Biden. This brings the possibility of a divided government with a Republican president, and a Republican sweep, much more into focus – and makes us to re-evaluate our assumptions going into the elections.

Steepeners suddenly more attractive: While we have been vocal proponents of holding duration longs over steepeners (see the Problem with Steepeners), we think the sharp shift of probabilities a second Trump presidency may be a unique catalyst that makes curve steepeners attractive. Both bull steepening and twist steepening have become more likely.

What has really changed vs. our prior assumptions? The key issue is the market now has to contend with rising probabilities of changes in immigration and tariff policies in an economy where growth has already been cooling, making the market more likely to price more rate cuts. On the other hand, higher prospects of a Republican sweep, amid growing focus on deficits, could put upward pressure on long-end term premiums.

Trades we suggest: We suggest adding 2s20s curve steepeners – expecting both – a pathway to more cuts as well as markets adding more term premiums in the long-end. We maintain our receive position on the July FOMC OIS swap, an attractive risk-reward trade with very limited downside. We also maintain our long 2y breakevens that we suggested last week – which looks even more attractive as markets contemplate higher probabilities for a second Trump presidency.

The difference between the Republican sweep and divided government if Trump is reelected president is that prospects of fiscal expansion are higher in a Republican sweep scenario, which could increase term premiums in Treasuries. Similarly, the prospects of fiscal expansion could be seen mitigating negative growth effects of more stringent policies on immigration and tariffs in a Republican sweep scenario, while a divided government under a second Trump presidency may be seen as just detrimental to growth.

So what has really changed in our thinking? The key issue is the market now has to consider rising probabilities of deportations and tariffs in an economy where growth has already been cooling, making the market-implied Fed path likely to add more rate cuts. In addition, because this divided government scenario comes with higher inflation risks, markets will likely keep elevated term premiums in the back-end, furthering the scope of twist steepening.

On the other hand, higher prospects of a Republican sweep, along with a backdrop of recent CBO projections of higher deficits, could put upward pressure on long-end term premiums (see Exhibit 13 ).

Overall, an election scenario of a Trump presidency with a divided government would likely allow the front-end to rally, and a Republican sweep scenario would likely open the door to significant term premiums expansion in the long-end. We think both bull steepening and twist steepening have become more likely.

## Cash EGBs: Finding value in other EGBs spreads

Apart from the ASW box suggested above as a risk-off hedge, we think that for the time being fading the recent widening in either the OAT/Bund or BTP/Bund spread is like catching a falling knife, considering the uncertainty and the volatility associated with those spreads. Additionally, over the medium term, we cannot exclude that the market will continue to pose a discount on issuers with less strong fundamentals.

As such, we prefer fading the French risk in countries that: 1) exhibit less risk of a credit downgrade and are fiscally more sound; 2) have supportive supply dynamics in 2H; and 3) have been adversely affected by the widening in the OAT/Bund spread, perhaps predominantly due to liquidity considerations as opposed to the market repricing the outlook on those issuers.

For the first point, we think countries like Spain, Austria, Finland, the Netherlands, Portugal, and Ireland all look attractive from a fiscal standpoint, with projected deficits below 3% through 2025 ( Exhibit 26 ).

## UK Elections Outcome Unlikely To Be A Market Mover

The UK elections, to be held on July 4, 2024, will be the focus of the upcoming week, and we reported our base case expectations here. Broadly speaking, we expect the outcome to have a limited impact on the gilt market - predictions vary, but given current polling, anything but a Labour majority would be a big surprise next Thursday.

Clearly, any outcome far from consensus might impact valuations and we see two main tail risks ahead of the elections: 1) a slim majority/hung parliament; and 2) a "Labour supermajority" (anything perhaps above 480 seats given current polls). Should those tail risks materialise, we expect the market reaction to lean towards a repricing of some gilt premium, as expectations for more fiscal easing might increase in both cases.

Indeed, a slim majority/hung parliament might require more concessions to minority parties and a "Labour supermajority" could imply a total control of the legislative programme; both of them might fuel investor speculation about higher fiscal spending ahead of the next fiscal event, which is still some months away, and this would probably limit in any case the impact of the election outcome on gilt valuations (at least in the short term).

In addition, these appear tail risks events, and a result broadly in line with current polls should have a fairly muted impact on the gilt market, with investor focus probably more on the French elections implications.

Aside from the UK elections, the past week has been relatively quiet on the data front, especially with respect to domestic data releases. The final GDP 1Q 2024 print was stronger than expected but did not affect price action materially, while US core PCE came in broadly in line with consensus.

All in all, yields have traded range-bound despite ending slightly up, while the gilt curve has flattened out gently at the back-end, at least at the time of writing (see Exhibit 35 ) which makes valuations still compelling to hold back-end flatteners in our view, a position we initiated on June 21, 2024 (see the GMS: The Debates Rage On.)

While we reckon valuations are still attractive for initiating flattener trades, macro data might affect materially the shape of the gilt curve, and although the next round of labour and inflation data is still a couple of weeks away, in the near term, US NFP poses a risk to gilt curve valuations. That said, front-end rates sold off marginally last week, while remaining firmly below recent highs, with 1y1y SONIA at \~4.15%, which should limit the scope of any rally in the coming days - barring of course any material surprise on US NFP.

Near-term BoE expectations have also remained fairly stable over the past week, as the market prices \~60% probability for a first cut at the August meeting (see Exhibit 36 ) which sits marginally on the cheap side when compared to our modal view of three cuts to be delivered starting from August. However, after the repricing post the June MPC (see our considerations here) we do not find any near-term MPC valuations particularly attractive. A BoE data-dependency approach means an August cut should largely depend on the next inflation print and Deputy Governor Broadbent's departure from the MPC increases further the risk around the timing of the first cut, as it is unclear which side of the committee his replacement Lombardelli will align with.

But the BoJ will be taking a forward-looking approach to monetary policy and might thus be prepared to adjust its settings if it senses a further improvement in the “likelihood” of underlying inflation reaching +2%. It is thus understandable that some have started to see a greater chance of the BoJ being forced to quicken its rate hikes now that USD/JPY is above the psychologically significant 160 level and threatening to generate additional inflationary pressure.

Quashing speculation about a more hawkish BoJ is liable to prove difficult if USD/JPY remains elevated, so we suggest continuing to position for underperformance of the belly sector by paying in 10s via 2s10s20s fly as we see a higher rates expectation to be priced in (see Exhibit 45 ).

As we discussed in "Japan | Assessing The Potential QT Impact", a June 21 meeting of the Study Group on Government Debt Management included a suggestion from the MoF that issuance of super-long JGBs might need to be cut back over the medium to long term out of supply/demand considerations.

And other action is already imminent, with the MoF having announced that liquidity enhancement auctions for July onwards will see offerings reduced by JPY100 billion for super-long JGBs and meanwhile increased for shorter maturities.

That said, demand for super-long JGBs is liable to remain weak until investors are given a clearer picture as to both the timing and the size of reductions in issuance. Any reductions during the current fiscal year (through March 2025) would likely first be discussed by the MoF with market participants. It is by no means inconceivable that lower issuance levels could kick in from October (2H FY2024).

Gauging the supply/demand impact is, however, liable to be difficult until remaining uncertainty is dispelled, and we are thus maintaining a bearish stance on 30y+ JGBs (given that supply of interest rate risk is currently so high for this part of the curve) in anticipation of domestic investors remaining reluctant to add to their duration exposure.

We meanwhile suggest sticking with 20y-long positions with a view to benefiting from the global duration rally. We believe that any data releases showing a second or even third successive month of weakness could lead to the rally in UST yield. Accordingly, we would expect short covering by fast money players to buoy the higher-yielding 20y JGB sector under a UST rally scenario, and accordingly advise maintaining long 20y JGBs on 5s10s20s fly.

## CHF | To fund or not to fund? That is the question

Once again, investors are debating the benefits of funding in CHF versus going long the safe haven. Pressures and risks are two-sided for now, keeping us neutral in spot (while long a strangle). However, the punitive nature of being long CHF (or receiving Swiss rates) and the ephemeral nature of uncertainty lead us to believe funding ultimately will prevail.

The first round of French parliamentary elections is happening this weekend and the current polls seem to be suggesting that a 'no majority' scenario is most likely. Our economists have discussed in detail what this scenario mean. On the macro strategy side, we've also discussed in the note what an adverse scenario could mean for OAT/Bund spreads and the EUR. Below, we outline our thoughts on the EUR impact from the French elections.

Eurozone break-up concerns are the main downside risk for the EUR. The biggest risk for the EUR from the French elections, in our view, has less to do with fiscal policy but more with what the results could mean for the French presidential election in 2027 (or before, should it be called earlier) and, more broadly, what it means for the future of the EU.

We are more relaxed about France's fiscal deficit risk weighing on the EUR. In fact, looser fiscal spending would be good for growth on the margin. The resultant inflationary impact is likely to lead to a more hawkish ECB, all else equal. Strong growth and higher rates as a result should be EUR-positive. However, there is a fine line between looser fiscal policy being growth positive and looser policy leading to debt sustainability concerns. It really comes down to how the deficit will be funded.

In the bear case of a substantial widening of the deficit, we think this could offset the growth and policy support for the EUR. Given that the European Commission is set to launch the Excessive Deficit Procedure for France, there is limited room for the incoming government to manoeuvre if it chooses to adhere to the EU's fiscal rules. But if it decides to disregard these rules and widen the fiscal deficit anyway, we think this would lead to our bear case scenario of a rise in eurozone break-up concerns.

Put differently, fiscal deficit risks for the EUR will matter insomuch as this leads to wider concerns over the future and stability of the European Union.

Increased political risk premium is warranted in EUR, but not much has been priced in. Some risk premium has been baked into vol markets, but it isn't extreme by historical standards. FX vol markets seem relatively sanguine heading into the election month, with 1-month ATM vol in both EUR/USD and EUR/GBP remaining near their historical lows ( Exhibit 58 ).

On the other hand, vol skew has turned significantly more bearish since the announcement. Again, the move has been less extreme than what we saw around the 2017 French presidential election ( Exhibit 59 ). We think some risk premium has been priced in but all in all, it seems bearish EUR positioning remains relatively light.

## Trading Strategy 1 – "Trade Longs/Fade Shorts"

When the 5-day moving average crosses above the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract (long duration) and hold for a 25-business-day period. When the 5-day moving average crosses below the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract and hold for a 25-business-day period. In short, this strategy buys futures when the Simple Moving Average Crossover (SMAX) generates both a long and a short signal, given the historical outperformance of long signals traded long and underperformance of short signals traded short. Given that the SMAX could generate both a long and a short signal within the predefined holding period, an investor may have a 200% long position since each of the two signals would be traded in separate portfolio sleeves.

## Definition of terms

Buy/Long: The analyst expects the total or excess return (depending on the nature of the recommendation) of the instrument or issuer that is the subject of the investment

recommendation to be positive over the relevant time period.

Sell/Short: The analyst expects the total or excess return (depending on the nature of the recommendation) of the instrument or issuer that is the subject of the investment recommendation to be negative over the relevant time period.

Selling protection or Buying Risk: The analyst expects that the price of protection against the event occurring will decrease over the relevant time period.

Buying protection or Selling Risk: The analyst expects the price of protection against the event occurring will increase over the relevant time period.

Pay: The analyst expects that over the specified time period the variable rate underlying the swap agreement that is the subject of the investment recommendation will increase.

Receive: The analyst expects that over the specified time period the variable rate underlying the swap agreement that is the subject of the investment recommendation will decrease.

Unless otherwise specified, the time frame for recommendations included in the Morgan Stanley Fixed Income Research reports is 1 - 3 months and the price of financial instruments mentioned in the recommendation is as at the date and time of publication of the recommendation.

When more than one issuer or instrument is included in a recommendation, analyst expects one part of the trade to outperform the other trade or combination of other trades included in the recommendation on a relative basis.
