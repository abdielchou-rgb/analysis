# Buy European, not the US consumer. How to implement this.

In general, we remain cautious of cyclicals; they are pricing in very high PMIs, are close to peak price to sales relative to defensives and intra-index correlation remains very low (a warning signal for cyclicals). However, we want to be overweight areas of the European consumer. Retailing has been the most 'defensive' cyclical with a negative correlation to PMIs (in both the UK and US).

Why the European consumer? Real wage growth is accelerating in Europe and the UK to be above that of the US at 2.1% and 3.1% on latest available data, respectively (having been well below); we think employment trends in Europe are picking up relative to the US. Excess savings in Europe and the UK are very high (at 8% and 16% of GDP, respectively) and thus provide insulation against macro shocks versus just 2% of GDP in the US (with US excess savings completely spent outside of the top income quintile); and consumer confidence readings also imply a pick-up in Europe/UK consumption and a slowdown in the US. We believe the UK consumer looks particularly well set (with greater political certainty than Europe, according to the polls). In 2025, UK consumption growth is forecast by UBS to be above that of the US.

Moreover, we view the European and UK consumer sector as the place to be if: i) there is downside risk to the oil price (with OPEC+ market share at historic lows and c6mbd of spare capacity); ii) the Euro or sterling strengthens (as US growth slows sharply while Europe accelerates) and iii) 1% off short rates in Europe adds c60bp to consumption after a year (with much more mortgage debt being floating in Europe than the US). Retailing and consumer services have outperformed 100% of the time in the 12 months after ECB rate cuts.

What to focus on:i) what features well on REVS with 60%+ revenue from Europe: Whitbread, Inditex, Greggs, Next and B&M – of which Greggs and Whitbread are Buy rated. Michelin (Buy rated) also scores well on REVS. ii) When we drill down, we particularly favour UK retailing (sharp acceleration in consumption, cheap valuation, strong earnings momentum, cautious positioning) with a focus on housing (Buy-rated Sainsbury is 25% non-food). iii) We like the fundamentals of budget airlines – 80% is consumer discretionary (Buy-rated easyJet), Jet2 has a FCF yield of 20.6% (2024), consumer-focused hotels (Melia and Whitbread) and iv) autos look cheap but face significant issues (Neutral-rated Renault is the only almost pure European consumer play) and we would choose to play any autos recovery through the admittedly less cyclical tyre companies (Pirelli and Michelin both Buy rated on 10.4% and 9.2% FCF yield for 2024, respectively). v) UBS has a Buy on H&M and Neutral on 3I (of which 65% is Action Group). We continue to be underweight luxury (where only 35% of revenue comes from Europe/UK) given the poor outlook for Chinese housing and still demanding valuations.

In the US, the outlook does not look good for the consumer, especially low end (who have depleted their excess savings and have limited exposure to the positive wealth effects) -– wages are set to slow to 3% and employment proxies suggest a slowdown. ## Why do we think that consumption growth in Europe could rise above the US at some point in 2025?

The UBS core scenario is that US consumption growth (in year-on-year terms) slows from being 4% above that of Europe currently to being the same (in the UK slows from being 2.5% above the UK to almost the same). We think that there is a clear risk that European and even UK consumption growth rises above that of the US.

There are, of course, three major components to consumption: real wage growth, employment and the savings ratio. We deal with each in turn:

## i) Real wage growth accelerating in Europe versus the US

Wages have proven to be much stickier in Europe than the US. In Q1, wage growth actually accelerated in the Eurozone on two of the key indicators. European wage negotiations, especially in Germany, tend to be for longer periods than the US and thus there is a much greater lag when responding to lower headline inflation as pointed out by our European economics team (see here). By contrast, the best lead indicators of US wage growth show a sharp slowdown.

## Why downside risk to oil?

We can see in Figure 35Oil vsPMI below that oil is now being driven by supply-side factors (with the fit between global PMIs and the oil price breaking down, implying that oil is driven by supply-side factors). On the supply side, OPEC+ has nearly 6mbd of spare capacity (hence OPEC+ market share is down to 48.5%, the lowest since OPEC+ started in 2016). OPEC+ recently agreed that that they would end on a pro-rata basis over 12 months the 2.2mbd of voluntary cuts starting in October.

 Saudi Arabia might at some point, as in 1985, be better off increasing output and letting the price fall. Saudi is producing 3mbd below peak capacity – if they increased output by 30%, the price might not fall by 30%. We found it curious as to the timing of the recent sell-down of the stake in Saudi Aramco (surely you sell when you think the price of oil is at a peak).

 Around half of the growth in oil demand has come from China and most of the China data has been disappointing.

 Non OPEC+ supply is rising with our team highlighting in 2024 it will be close to 1.4mbd (above demand growth), though we admit that the US rig count has remained remarkably unresponsive to a higher oil price. Non-OPEC capex (in real terms) is rising – a sign of incremental positive surprises potentially on supply.

## vi) Stronger Euro

A stronger currency helps disposable income for the consumer (by lowering import prices) while it is clearly negative for exporters or those companies who have high transactional earnings.

The risk is that ultimately the Euro strengthens. UBS forecasts Euro/\$ 1.05 for end '24 but Euro/\$ 1.10 for end '25.

## What about the French elections?

Clearly, there is a temporary downturn in confidence as reflected by PMIs and, as highlighted two weeks ago, the OAT/Bund spread, European to US volatility, the sector adjusted P/E discount to the US are all close to crisis levels (at peak BTP/Bund spread, the sector adjusted P/E discount of Europe to the US was 22% versus 20% now).

We wrote about the French election here. We believe that political crises can provide buying opportunities, and one should buy when uncertainty is at its peak (which we believe was Friday 14 June). Our core view is that valuations are close to crisis levels, the RN will likely revise down plans to be pragmatic (knowing that otherwise financial markets will discipline them and in turn they could lose credibility ahead of the 2027 election). Political stalemate could be a mild positive as it brings fiscal drag. We don’t believe Italian spreads should widen from here with Italy wanting to access the €100bn of outstanding EU Recovery money, and thus Italy will we think abide by EU fiscal rules. Moreover, the mutualisation of debt via Target 2 and the EU Recovery Fund lowers the pain threshold of the ECB to act. In aggregate, the fiscal position of the Eurozone is better than the US (with a primary budget deficit of 1.1% of GDP in the Eurozone for 2024 and on IMF data the primary budget deficit in 2023 was 3.8% of GDP in France for 2023 versus 5.8% in the US).

## iv) Budget airlines;

Budget airlines are almost pure plays on European consumption (as only c20% of demand at most is corporate) and there is clearly a good structural story, in our opinion.

The ONS shows that recreational travel has been 2x GDP seen since 2000 (unlike business travel).

We now have much better barriers to entry (slot and airport capacity at peak times with, for example, easyJet having 43% of slots at Gatwick and Ryanair 70% of slots at Stansted), shortages of planes (because of Boeing’s and more recently Airbus' manufacturing issues).

For the industry as a whole, volumes are only back to pre-Covid levels (20% more for Ryanair).

The structural cost and carbon advantage of the budget airlines has increased (as they have a younger fleet and fewer legacy labour costs with Ryanair buying new planes at the trough of the cycle). easyJet unit costs, according to our analysts, are ex fuel, 13% lower than IAG and 62% for Ryanair.

Ryanair now accounts for almost 18% of the total air travel market and over c45% the budget travel market in Europe, and this implies a much more rational market for other players.
