# When the Rubber Meets the Road

We maintain our high-level view that the UK's recent stagflationary dynamic was largely a product of the energy shock, and not permanent structural shifts in the economy. This hypothesis will be tested in the coming months. Core follows headline inflation towards target. Growth picks up, but potential is capped at under 1.5%Y. The BoE cuts three times this year.

Growth | Accelerating: After a sharp drop in 2H23, GDP bounced back in 1Q24. We see a modest payback in 2Q24, before quarterly growth re-accelerates towards 0.35%Q (\~1.4%Y), our estimate of the UK's potential growth. Household spending growth should improve – but the normalisation in government spending should be the main boost to growth in 2024, before private consumption takes over in 2025.

Labour market | Catch-up time: Various surveys suggest that the labour market is now broadly balanced. After a drop in hiring in 1Q24, we see moderate quarterly employment growth and an ongoing improvement in labour supply – helped by another 350-400k of net migrant arrivals this year. This should leave the jobless rate at just under 5% by the end of this year.

Inflation | Suddenly, then gradually: Momentum in inflation has looked very soft since last August. This, combined with sizeable base effects, should push headline inflation to below 2%Y in May and June. While the reversal in base effects should mean some bounceback in headline figures in 2H24, we see inflation at around 2%Y over our forecast horizon. Softer pay growth next year, anchored inflation expectations, waning rental pressures and less of an uplift from indexation in administrative prices should all allow for services inflation to average 3%Y next year – leaving core at target over 2025. We see risks as broadly balanced.

BoE | Down...and into the unknown: We see two rate cuts in the coming months – in June and August – with the BoE then pausing to assess the impact of its actions on the economy. However, services inflation prints in the coming weeks have to come in line with the BoE's forecasts for the cuts to be delivered. We see a final November cut this year. In 2025, as the BoE gains confidence that 2%Y core can be attained sustainably, we see it cutting towards 3.25% at successive meetings, from February next year. Active sales are paused from next September, with passive QT and TFSME repayments driving balance sheet normalisation over 2025.

Risks: The UK's overall fiscal drag this year is 80bp, narrowing, on our estimates, to 40bp next year. A bigger fiscal drag next year is the largest domestic downside risk to our growth forecasts, we think. The global upside scenario sees stronger Chinaled growth, with the BoE stopping its cuts after the first 50bp. The downside scenario sees US stagflation capping the initial BoE cuts at 50bp too. Yet, higher rates over 2024 lead to a deceleration in growth and continuation of cuts next year.

# Growth: Don't Call it a Comeback...

Bottom line: ...we've been here all along, waiting for the supply-side shocks to pass. On the tailwinds of somewhat healthier supply-side dynamics, a dissipating energy shock, a robust global outlook and a more balanced domestic policy mix, the UK economy leaves the recession behind. The speed limit (i.e., potential growth) is still low (just under 1.5%Y, we think), risks still moderately skewed to the downside and some removal of monetary policy restrictiveness a must for the recovery to broaden and gain more sustainable footing. We see growth at 0.7%Y in 2024 and 1.2%Y in 2025.

Assessing the starting point: We think it is worth re-examining the very strong start to the year, considering that the 1Q24 print brought an additional 30bp to our full-year 2024 GDP growth forecast – as we upped it from 0.4%Y to 0.7%Y, on a mark-to-market adjustment alone. The headline was strong, the details...a bit of a mixed bag. First, household consumption growth came in at 0.2%Q, leaving real-term spending at 0.75% below its pre-recession level. Consumers were nowhere near the main drivers of this strong print. Second, the upside surprise was almost entirely concentrated in the sharp drop in imports – which on its own contributed 80bp to the headline growth print. Car and machinery imports fell particularly sharply. On the other hand, exports fell as well, but in large part due to non-monetary gold. This has a neutral impact on GDP as it does imply a boost to inventories. We also note that while goods exports fell, services exports rose on the back of travel and intellectual property services. Travel services – which envelope education – have been a strong boost to UK trade in recent years. The likely drop in the number of international students this and next year might bring an end to that.

Let's wait and see: Hence, on the whole, expenditure details look somewhat sluggish and prone to reversal – especially the uptick in residential investment, which might be challenging to repeat in 2Q24, given the uptick in mortgage rates in 1Q. That said, the output-based breakdown shows an economy firing on all cylinders. Look at the services sector alone – up by 0.8%Q in 1Q24, with a 0.5%Q uptick in retail & hospitality sectors, a 1.4%Q jump in transport & ICT and a 0.6%Q rise in business services & finance. There were some one-offs in 1Q, but this looks a very healthy picture of the UK economy. On the whole, we do think that some of the 1Q strength will unwind, and we find it hard to see how imports don't normalise in 2Q24. The sentiment boost that meant some front-loading of the impact of interest rate cuts is likely to wane, and the Easter effect that boosted consumer-facing services in March is likely to reverse in April. Car manufacturing, which had an incredibly strong start to the year, already corrected meaningfully on the month in March. Overall, we see a 0.2%Q pick-up in 2Q24. Thereafter, for reasons that we will outline below, we see a more sustained (broad-based) pick-up, with quarterly growth averaging 0.3-0.4%Q – matching our assumption of the UK's potential growth.

Expenditure breakdown | Consumers | After the shock: The UK consumer is around 60% of the UK economy. The UK consumer, simply put, is the UK economy. It is no surprise then that the uptick in real wages has generated so much enthusiasm around a potential upswing in UK activity. The underlying picture, we fear, is a bit more complicated. First off, we note that real wage growth – in annual terms – turned positive in 2H23 already, with its peak, we think, materialising in 1Q24. In addition, real earnings have been improving in sequential terms since early 2023, bringing about not a surge in real consumption growth, but rather a rise in the savings rate. In addition, (average) real wage growth does not equal (aggregate) real disposable income growth. The latter rose by 2.2%Y in 2023, helped by a healthy dose of population expansion, as well as robust non-labour income. The latter (real AWE) was flat on the year. Indeed, we see real disposable income growth slowing to 1.0%Y in 2024 and 0.8%Y in 2025. Overall, consumption growth should improve this year – but gradually, from mildly negative figures in 1H24, towards \~1%Y in 4Q24.

Household balance sheets | Thrifty Brits: The rise in the savings rate has been one of the most notable features of this cycle in the UK. We say notable as no other peer country has seen such a steady and robust rise in the (cash-based) savings rate versus the usual historical norms. The US, for example, has seen a decline in the savings rate over the past year. The cash-based savings ratio in the UK averaged 0.2% between 1997 and 2019. As of 4Q23, it stands at 8.1%. This counters a theory that was very popular in UK macro in the immediate aftermath of the pandemic (and which we never subscribed to): that UK consumers would spend their 'excess' pandemic savings. Instead, the 'excess' savings were eaten up by inflation, and the household-held M4/household incomes ratio normalised as (nominal) household resources rose – not as deposit holdings declined. We push back strongly against the idea of 'excess' savings in nominal terms, waiting to be deployed. Following the inflationary shock the UK economy has seen in recent years, comparing any nominal values to their historical trends is deeply misleading. That said, it is undeniable that the nominal stock of debt to stock of savings ratio remains at a very low level – indicating fairly healthy household balance sheets.

So what next for the savings rate? We think that the healthy rise in savings in the past two years can be chalked up to two key factors. First, strong monetary policy passthrough and high deposit betas. The substitution channel, as we have long written, is one of the key ways through which monetary policy operates – incentivising savings over

spending. Second, the prospect of higher mortgage payments has left a third of UK households with mortgages likely in a state of preparing for the upcoming hit to their finances. With interest rates likely to come down this year, both of these factors start to subside. Hence, we see the cash-based savings rate declining very gradually. The favourable impact of net interest payments on disposable incomes should start to reverse, as the pass-through of lower rates to the stock of savings is faster than the pass-through to mortgages. Indeed, monetary tightening has not yet been passed through in full to the outstanding stock of mortgages. That said, we had always been fairly sanguine about the impact of interest payments on consumption growth – it is a fairly small share of overall disposable income.

Inequality | Mind the gap... Finally, we would like to note that the aggregate data mask important inequalities. On our estimates, between 1Q21 and 1Q23, the bottom 20% of income earners saw an average 1.6pp of a bigger real wage hit than the top 80% of income earners. The bottom 20% of income earners traditionally have a very low savings rate in any case (<5%, while the top 80% of earners save \~25% of their incomes), and if anything have likely weathered the cost of living crisis by drawing down their credit balances. We see the deviation between nominal durable goods purchases and consumer credit flows as fairly striking – with the weakened correlation between the two series in recent years suggesting that lower-income households likely relied on consumer credit to cover nondiscretionary spending (i.e., food and utility bills).

...but fortunes turning this year: Earnings-wise, at least, the fortunes are turning this year – the bottom 20% of income earners have in 4Q23 already recorded positive real wage growth of 3%Y, with the top 80% of income earners still recording mildly negative real wage growth in December 2023. Higher-income earners have seen a sharper nominal wage growth drop in recent months, and with the introduction of the near-10%Y minimum wage hike, this divergence between high- and low-paid workers has a bit more to run. Next year, as the minimum wage growth increase will likely only be \~3%Y, we anticipate higherincome earners to see stronger pay growth once again. As a reminder, the top 20% of income earners in the UK account for \~40% of all non-discretionary spending.

Risks around our forecasts: As noted, we expect private consumption growth to strengthen over our forecast period, averaging 0.2%Y in 2024 and 1.2%Y in 2025. Part of the optical weakness of the 2024 average is down to a very low off-ramp into the year – which, as we noted, had not been repaired over 1Q24. Risks are balanced, we think. The sentiment uptick as interest rates are cut could lead to a sharper drop in the cash-based savings rate than we anticipate, boosting consumption. It is also of course always possible that immigration figures surprise to the upside – we are assuming \~400k arrivals this year, boosting aggregate figures. On the other hand, the labour market has slackened quickly, and hiring appetite still looks soft. Should unemployment expectations rise, consumers could retreat meaningfully. A more hawkish BoE than expected is also a risk.

Private investment | Fiscal versus monetary policy – do we have a winner? Slowly but surely, against all odds, business capex is emerging as a relative positive of the UK economy. Compared to 4Q19, business investment is 5.6% higher in real terms, while private consumption, for example, is 1.7% lower. Business investment is a volatile statistical category, prone to large revisions, and the quarterly upticks in 4Q23 and 1Q24 did come after a very weak 3Q23. All this said, private capex has performed better in recent quarters than we had expected.

As we had written in our 2024 Outlook, part of the reason why is fiscal policy – UK businesses are able to deduct their capex fully from their pre-tax profits. Another reason is likely the relatively robust state of large corporate balance sheets, which has allowed for corporates with strong cash positions to invest in ICT, AI technologies and green energy generation. That said, the refinancing hit to the corporate sector is set to be larger this year than in 2023. On the other hand, the SME sector has seen credit demand crater and its 'excess cash' eroded, with the corporate insolvency rate up in the sector last year. As we suspect, the usual rhythm of the fiscal year might have supported some last-minute capital expensing-related capex in 1Q24, and we see a mild payback in 2Q24. Thereafter, we see capex growth firming as borrowing costs fall.

Residential investment | Down, down, down...and up: The housing market is the part of the economy most sensitive to rising interest rates. Indeed, since its recent peak in 3Q22, residential capex contracted by 12% in five consecutive quarters. As mortgage rates declined at the turn of the year, the category saw a large 4.1%Q uptick in 1Q24. We see a mild drop in 2Q24, in response to a mortgage rate repricing over 1Q24, with a gradual bounceback over the remainder of the forecast horizon on firmer signs that mortgage rates are likely on the way down. On the whole, we see residential capex up by 0.8%Y in 2024 and 2.4%Y in 2025, following a 7.3%Y drop in 2023. The bounceback in 2025 could be larger, although residential capex did see an above-trend surge through 2021 and 2022.

Government | Spending up, investment down: Using the OBR's March 2024 government spending projections in nominal terms and our implied GDP deflator, we estimate government consumption rising by 3.7%Y in 2024 – a sizeable uptick helped also by the end of public sector strikes, which were prevalent in 2023. Indeed, government consumption thus becomes the main driver of growth this year, with a 75bp boost to headline GDP growth. The mild drag comes from government capex, where recent Budgets opted for a swap – the capital-expensing policy was set in motion to support private capex, while government capex plans were not topped up for inflation – hence, we see public investment down by 1.3%Y in 2024 and 2.2%Y in 2025. We leave a modest buffer in our figures for a touch stronger public consumption growth next year, as we assume that the next government will top up sectoral spending plans in the upcoming post-election Spending Review.

Net trade and inventories | Normalising: In the post-Covid, post-Brexit era, the most prominent feature of the UK's external sector has been the robustness of services exports and the weakness in goods exports. Goods exports, in volume terms and stripped of erratics, are still \~20% below their 4Q19 levels. Meanwhile, services exports are 11% higher. The UK's key services exports – professional services, finance, travel – are a strong beta to the global cycle, with the US share of UK services exports rising in recent years. Hence, robustness of the US economy should imply continued solid performance of services exports. That said, we do see a drop in education-related exports this and next year, given the evident drop in the number of international students applying to study in the UK (which is likely partly do to with immigration regime changes). On the imports front, we reiterate that we do expect some normalisation in 2Q24, with domestic demand firming spilling over to a pick-up in imports over our forecast horizon. So, in 2025, net trade, on stronger imports, becomes a drag on headline GDP. The current account deficit, normalised after the external commodities shocks, hovers around 3% of GDP in 2024 and 2025. We see inventories as a drag this year, following the large 2021 and 2022 boost from the category, which was only partially reversed in 2023. In 2025, we see them as a neutral contributor.

Where does this leave us? At 0.7%Y, we are above the Bloomberg consensus in 2024, with our 1.2%Y forecast in line with consensus for 2025. Benchmarking ourselves against the key UK official forecasts, we note that we are marginally below the OBR's projections (0.8%Y in 2024 and 1.9%Y in 2025), but above those of the BoE (0.5%Y in 2024 and 1.0%Y in 2025).

Risks | Remixing the mix? The UK's overall fiscal drag this year is 80bp, narrowing, on our estimates, to 40bp next year. Irrespective of the general election winner, we see some reversal of the recent tax cuts, in favour of topping up current spending plans (see more in Fiscal Policy: Wake Me Up When November Ends ). Hence, we anticipate a shift in underlying fiscal plans, but not an additional layer of fiscal restrictiveness. That said, we see a bigger fiscal drag next year as the largest domestic downside risk to growth and our BoE forecasts. Our global upside scenario sees stronger China-led growth, with the BoE stopping its cuts following the first 50bp of easing. The downside scenario sees US stagflation capping the initial BoE cuts at 50bp. Higher-for-longer rates eventually lead to a further deceleration in growth and a continuation of cuts next year.

Mind the speed limit: For all the focus on the UK's sluggish demand growth in recent years, the key to understanding the country's growth/inflation mix is to focus instead on the demand/supply-side balance – and the output gap. We maintain our view of the UK's potential growth at just under 1.5%Y, with potential labour supply growth at \~0.7-0.8%Y and with labour productivity at \~0.5-0.6%Y – the latter is broadly in line with the BoE's and the OBR's near-term assumptions, but the former is more bullish than the BoE's estimates (which, to be fair, the BoE highlighted was based on stale population projections), and less upbeat than the OBR's projections. This broadly results in the economy maintaining a mild negative output gap over the forecast horizon (of -0.25 to - 0.50% of GDP), allowing for the economy to disinflate. Stronger near-term growth – in the absence of a productivity growth uplift in the near term – suggests an uptick in domestically generated pressures, and worse economic outcomes further down the line, as monetary policy would be kept in more restrictive territory than in our base case.

## Labour Market: It's…Complicated

Bottom line: There are many complex questions in UK macro at the moment. There are also many nominally simple ones, which we – or rather the ONS – can't confidently answer; for example, what is the UK's unemployment rate? What is the size of the country's labour force? Data issues remain a problem. That said, demand for workers seems flat at best, hiring is of normal difficulty and the labour market has gone from extremely tight to balanced, we think. There is likely a bit more slackening ahead, with the jobless rate set to rise further towards 5%. Nominal wage growth is likely to fall further – towards 5% by the end of the year, and 3% by the end of 2025.

Your guess is as good as mine: The UK still has no official reliable estimates of the country's unemployment rate. The quality issues that have plagued the Labour Force Survey have remained in place even as the ONS resumed the publication of the data earlier this year. Alongside the currently published LFS, the ONS is conducting (but not yet publishing) the Transformed LFS, with higher response rates and thus more reliable results. We assume that when the data are finally released in September this year, the TLFS will show similar current jobless rates as the LFS, but with the unemployment rate trajectory altered. We think it will highly likely show that the trough in the jobless rate in summer 2022 was lower than 3.6%, and that the surge in joblessness in 2Q23, followed by a sharp correction in 4Q23, was likely a statistical oddity. The main implication of this adjustment is that it will likely make the BoE's estimate of a higher NAIRU from November 2023 seem somewhat ill-judged. A lower jobless rate means less slack even with nearterm NAIRU at 4.25%, as opposed to the BoE's revamped 4.5% estimate.

Demand | Flat, at best: Much like the BoE, we, given data issues, rely on a number of labour market surveys – the REC Report on Jobs, PMIs and the BoE's own DMP. On the back of these, we see demand for labour as flat(ish). Late last year, we had started to worry that the rise in proposed redundancies and the sharp slide in vacancies were signs that the labour market was moving from an orderly rebalancing towards a sharper deterioration. And while most of the signals are still mildly bearish – as we note, as per survey data, hiring appetite is flat at best (with companies maintaining headcount, but not necessarily expanding it), and vacancies remain well below their pre-Covid trend-implied levels – there are some signs of stabilisation. For example, even the very bearish REC Report on Jobs seems to have stopped deteriorating in April, and redundancy intentions seem to be broadly aligned with usual seasonal norms. Unemployment expectations in the GfK consumer confidence survey are flat-lining too.

Since we have to produce a forecast underpinned by the official ONS data, we assume that the sizeable drop in employment in 1Q24 (likely partly a statistical mirage) will largely (albeit not fully) reverse over 2Q24. More broadly, we see a mild contraction in employment this year. We see a moderate pick-up next year. By and large, we assume that the pick-up in activity will take a bit of time to feed through to employment growth, While the evidence of labour hoarding in the UK is not as convincing as in some other DM peers, it is fair to say that the economic malaise of 2H22 and 2023 had less of an impact on

employment levels than expected – likely as companies recovered their headcount post-Covid in 2022 and (largely) held onto it through 2023, in anticipation of a pick-up in activity as the energy shock dissipates. This kind of dynamic depressed productivity growth last year, but will flatter it over 2024.

Supply | A data warning – again: The myriad LFS issues collide in the assessment of recent changes to the UK's labour supply. First, there is the already mentioned problem of creating a representative sample when the survey response rates are so low. But the second issue is the assumption around the size of the UK population, which the LFS consistently lowballs. Even as in February this year, the LFS was reweighted to account for a bigger population than had been assumed before, the ONS projections published in January again showed larger population figures than those now underpinning the LFS.

Considering that such a big part of the UK's post-Covid narrative is the idea that the country has seen a large labour supply shock, the fact that we can't properly account for the scale of this supposed hit is deeply troubling. As of 1Q24, the ONS says that the participation rate in the 16+ cohort is 62.6% – the lowest recorded since 1997. Again, according to the ONS, between 3Q23 and 1Q24, the UK's labour force shrunk by almost 150k, a meaningful near 0.5% drop. At the same time, the DMP survey suggested that recruitment difficulties eased and the REC Report on Jobs is showing fast-paced improvements in candidate availability. It is hard to point to ONS data for any serious analysis, given these basic discrepancies. Instead of relying on the recent ONS figures, hence, we will focus on two key trends we are monitoring (again, on a range of indicators) – immigration flows and youth, health-related inactivity. We had set the basic framework around these issues in our 2024 Outlook already.

Immigration | Time for some cooling: Since very early on in this cycle, our view has been very clear – the UK's labour supply difficulties have more to do with UK-born workers than immigrants to the UK. Data since 2022 reinforce that initial take. Immigration flows have in the past two years kept aggregate UK data supported in face of an energy and interest rate shocks, and have likely softened the contraction in GDP per capita figures too. According to the ONS, long-term immigration to the UK reached its record high in the year ending June 2023, totalling 672k in net terms (\~1% of the total UK population). The ONS states that "the increase in non-EU immigration in the year ending June 2023 was mainly driven by migrants coming for work, largely attributed to those coming on health and care visas". Indeed, as per ONS data, 278k work visas were approved for non-EU migrants in the 12 months to June 2023. However, HMRC data show that between June 2022 and June 2023, the number of non-EU/non-UK-born employees rose by 569k. No matter which route non-EU migrants use to come to the UK, they seem to end up in employment.

Even when health and social care workers are excluded from non-EU employment, it now surpasses the peak 2019 level of EU employment as recorded by the HMRC. When the NHS figures are included, the level of non-EU employment is 33% higher as of December 2023 than the peak levels of EU employees. As per the ONS LFS data (as the HMRC does not provide data on unemployment per nationality), the UK's labour force rose by just over 200k between 4Q19 and 4Q23, of which the number of active UK-born people above the age of 16 dropped by 900k, EU-born activity was down by 130k and non-UK/non-EU migrants provided a labour supply boost of 1.3 million. In other words, all of the UK's labour supply growth was driven by non-EU migrants. Given well-known quality issues with the LFS figures, we note that HMRC data paint a similar picture – of 4.6% employment growth between 4Q19 and 4Q23, all of it is accounted for by non-EU nationals.

However, we think that this scale of net immigration will be challenging to sustain this year, considering several factors:

1) Non-EU migration, much like EU migration was pre-Brexit, is a buffer for the UK labour market. When labour demand is large, immigration surges. When labour demand subsides, immigration flows are likely to slow. While over 1Q24, skilled worker visas are still running at \~6k per month, we think there is some chance of these figures slowing towards 4-5k over the remainder of the year.

2) Recent government changes to the UK's immigration regime were designed to lower immigration flows. The pay threshold for skilled workers was hiked from £26.2k to £38.7k, with the 'going rate' now set from the 25th percentile of earnings in the occupation to the 50th; care workers and students are not allowed to bring their dependents anymore. 3) The number of asylum seekers has likely also peaked, given sharp one-off flows from Ukraine and Hong Kong in recent years.

4) The number of international students applying to study in the UK has fallen sharply thus far this year.

The OBR sees net migration flows of \~340k this year and just over 270k next year, compared to the ONS projections of \~530k this year and \~450k next year. We assume a net inflow of \~400k – somewhat closer to the OBR's higher immigration scenario, as the watchdog has had a tendency in recent years to underestimate inflows. This implies a potential labour supply expansion estimated at 0.7%Y in 2024 and 2025.

Health-related inactivity | Action and reaction? The UK has managed just fine with net immigration flows of 300-400k prior to 2020. That said, in that period, we also didn't experience such an apparent dramatic drop in the participation rate of UK-born workers. Indeed, our key focus for the year ahead are UK-born workers – particularly those between the ages of 16 and 34. As per the DWP, the number of new Personal Independence Payments (a type of disability benefit) is up sharply for the younger population – near-doubling for those 16 and 17 years old, and rising by \~75% for 18-24- year-olds. The prevalent condition seems to be mental health-related (depression/ anxiety).

We have already written about how we think that the disability benefit system interacts with the LFS, and potentially pushes those de facto unemployed into the inactivity bracket. But in any case, we are three years past the pandemic and reaching the point when those inactive/unemployed are less likely to ever come back to work. We think that this is particularly valid for older workers. However, we struggle to see how younger workers can remain in inactivity in such historically large numbers for a prolonged period of time. We think that these are workers who could be absorbed into low-paid jobs by the recent sharp minimum wage increases – and will monitor this thesis.

Wages | Another can of worms: The official wage growth data (AWE) are based on a survey of firms, with response rates that have held up better than the LFS. However, even AWE looks challenging to understand these days – and the BoE has also acknowledged that the volatility of the series in recent years suggests some quality issues. In any case, based on a range of indicators – HMRC PAYE data, DMP survey, REC Report on Jobs – we see underlying pay growth in the UK at \~5.5%Y at the moment. This is a slowdown from last year, but still at least 1.5pp too high to be consistent with the inflation target.

Beyond the minimum wage spike: The impact of another sharp uptick in the National Living Wage (i.e., minimum wage) will be in focus in the coming months. Last year's legally mandated pick-up was 9.7%Y, while this year's increase is 9.8%Y. When looking at the sector most exposed to the rise in the NLW – distribution & hospitality – we note that its regular AWE rose by 4% between June and February last year. This would suggest that 40% of all workers got a pay rise aligned with the NLW hike – compared to \~20% of workers nominally on or around NLW-level pay in the sector.

The indirect effects, it seems, were sizeable – or pay increases went above and beyond those mandated by law. Considering more labour market slack this year, and the existing premium versus the NLW, especially in hospitality, and less so in retail, we model a 3.5% cumulative rise in AWE in this sector. Unlike last year, we don't expect a surge in professional services pay around April – although this is the key upside risk to our numbers.

Overall, we see regular private sector AWE growth at just below 6% 3M/Y in 2Q24. As public sector pay and NLW are unlikely to see anywhere near as high increases next year as was the case in 2022 and 2023, we see ongoing pay disinflation in 2H24 and 2025. In 4Q24, we see regular pay growth at 4.9%Y (4.7% 3M/Y for the private sector alone, versus the BoE at 5.0% 3M/Y), and in 4Q25, at 3%Y, as the dissipating energy shock and labour market rebalancing drive earnings growth towards target-consistent levels. Our forecasts are a touch weaker than the BoE's in 2024, and a touch stronger in 2025.

# Inflation: Suddenly...Then Gradually

Bottom line: On flattering base effects, inflation falls towards the 2%Y target – and below – in the coming months. Headline inflation is almost certain to bounce back from the early summer trough, but there is a wide range of views on the scale of the pick-up. We see a muted increase, as we expect services disinflation to kick in as food and core goods inflation start to pick up (moderately) into 2025. Our high-level framework remains one focused on the impacts of the UK's recent largescale energy shock, with core thus likely to follow headline inflation towards the 2%Y target as the terms of trade shock dissipates, and as we view the inflationary process as not structurally impaired in recent years.

Here comes the spring again: Last year, in our mid-year outlook, we wrote that depending on the near-term growth and inflation dynamic, "in our base case, the BoE hikes once more. But risks are clearly skewed towards (at least two) more". The report came out a few days before the April inflation print, which surprised sharply to the upside, leading to indeed not just the additional June 25bp hike we anticipated, but a June 50bp move instead, and a final August hike. If you are reading this report on its publication day, you are likely once again far more interested in the April print coming out in three days' time than our medium-term views. Similar to last year, the BoE's next steps depend significantly on the next two inflation releases – if services inflation comes in line with the BoE's forecasts, it is likely to start its cutting cycle in June. If we get upside surprises once again, even an August cut seems at risk.

Our April forecast: There are two elements to getting the April forecast right: the first is properly accounting for all the 'administrative' price changes that happen on April 1, and the second is assessing whether we are likely to see a notable uptick in the prices of key non-'administrative' categories at the start of the fiscal year, such as catering or various core goods items. The sizeable upside surprise last year came largely from the second part of the equation – with a surge in core goods inflation a particular surprise.

On the re-indexation front, water bills are due to rise by 5%M; broadband charges are due to increase by \~8%M; postal charges are rising by 8%M as well; VED by \~6%M; Spotify has hiked its prices by \~10%M and the TV licence will rise by 6.6%M; social rents are due to rise too, we think by the same %M as in April 2023, and dental charges will be up \~3.9%M as well. However, prescription charges are due to rise in May. Of course, the key change is the drop in utility bills – which are due to fall by 12.3%M. Given the weakness in their momentum in recent months, we think it is almost impossible that we see an uptick in core goods prices which matches that seen in April last year. Overall, we see services inflation at 5.5%Y, core goods inflation at 0.6%Y and headline inflation at 2.2%Y. While our headline and core goods inflation forecasts are a touch stronger than the BoE's, we do see risks to our numbers as skewed to the downside – and we note that we are aligned with the BoE on the services front. This, we think, is key to delivering the June cut. Note also that we expect a pretty meaningful downside surprise in services inflation in May in the print that will be released one day before the June meeting.

Beyond April | Energy, food and core goods: The collapse in imported price pressures has reverberated through the non-services parts of the basket. Energy deflation is set to take another leg lower as utility bills fall by 12.3%M in April, and then by 6.0%M in July. The scale of food disinflation has been striking – from 19%Y in April 2023 to 4.0%Y in March, and what we expect will be a local trough at \~1%Y on average in 2H24. Core goods disinflation has also taken off – although the process has been far slower than we had anticipated. Indeed, as the BoE zooms in on the pass-through of lower imported cost pressures to core goods and food prices in the May MPR, we take a step back to examine why aren't these categories seeing price deflation already?

Food prices-wise, we note that the recent slower translation of PPI (which is in mild deflation) into CPI disinflation mirrors the food PPI/CPI upswing over 2022. In addition, the market structure of the UK food retail sector – which is competitive, but where price changes are largely benchmarked off discounters – suggests little incentive for an all-out price war. In fact, volumes sold are bouncing back, albeit from staggeringly low levels of 2022 and 2023, thus dampening the likelihood of price cuts. Finally, we also note that as of 1Q24 there is little evidence that UK food prices are deviating meaningfully from those in the euro area. Indeed, the cumulative change in prices since December 2019 is almost identical in the UK and the euro area – just under 30% – despite additional red tape post-Brexit. We see no food price deflation in the UK, and forecast food inflation at \~2%Y over 2025, increasing a touch as wholesale costs pick up into 2025.

Services | A sticky wicket: Services inflation is now the main contributor to headline inflation – 3.2%Y headline inflation in March is a result of a 0.9pp contribution from food, alcohol & tobacco, 0.4pp from core goods and 2.7pp from services, with energy 0.9pp of a drag. The momentum in core inflation – excluding administrative and volatile prices, as well as rents – has slowed, and its 3M/3M SA annualised rate stands at just under 5% on our estimates.

The biggest contributors to services inflation are categories that are second-most labourintensive (where we separate the services part of the basket into five quintiles based on their labour intensity; the fourth quintile includes recreational & sporting services, restaurants & cafes and accommodation services). Still, it is notable that in the past six months it is this fourth quintile which has seen the sharpest disinflation. Note that the most labour-intensive categories are by and large more administrative in nature (hospital services, dental services, child care, education), while the least labour-intensive services are rents and insurance categories.

The fact that this last year has brought 4pp worth of disinflation in this second-most labour-intensive services category – which we see as a relatively clean distillation of underlying domestic price pressures – is encouraging, we think. It tells us that as commodities price pressures subsided, and demand was reined in, pricing power in the sector softened – it is no coincidence that the margins boost to the GDP deflator turned negative at the turn of the year.

Yes, labour costs are still high, but wage disinflation is step 2 in the UK's broader disinflation process. Once the commodities-related tailwind ends (over 2024), subsiding wage pressures (over 2025) should ensure that services inflation falls to levels more consistent with broader core inflation at 2%Y. This energy shock-centric interpretation of the UK's inflation predicament has been the key part of our UK macro framework in recent years – one that is now endorsed by Deputy Governor Ramsden. As headline inflation falls, indexation pressures should subside too, and while rental pressures have more to

run, inflation in new lets has slowed sharply, suggesting that the category will become a disinflationary driver next year. Where headline leads, core (services) will follow, in sum. Overall, we see services inflation averaging 5.0%Y in 2024 and 3.1%Y in 2025 – ending this year at 4.0%Y and next year at \~2.5%Y. We see risks around our forecasts as balanced.

Summary | Testing times: We see headline inflation below target in May and June, picking back up modestly in the summer months, but ending the year at 2%Y. This is where we differ versus the BoE's forecast and the RPI fixings. However, we are only marginally below the Street consensus, as per the latest Bloomberg data – with the delta versus Street consensus a bit bigger over 2025. Taking a step back, we note that the key divergence among the UK macro watchers is the degree to which headline inflation will bounce back as the 'low-hanging disinflationary fruit' is picked. We think not much, as services disinflation will continue, and the core goods and food inflation bounceback will be limited. As already noted, our forecasts are underpinned by our high-level view that, in the aftermath of the energy shock, core inflation will follow headline inflation towards the target – as wage pressures deflate and expectations remain anchored.

In the 'energy-focused' framework, the four key disinflationary steps are:

Step 1: Dissipating external inflationary pressures drag headline inflation lower;

Step 2: Falls in headline inflation lower inflation expectations;

Step 3: Easing in inflation expectations is a key driver of weakening pay growth;

Step 4: Weaker pay growth feeding into weaker services inflation, which further broadens out the disinflationary process.

We are entering steps 3 and 4 in 2H24.

Risks | Alternative views: Another thesis around UK inflation is that the string of supplyside shocks has left the UK economy a tighter one (more inflationary, that is) and that the inflationary process has simply changed for good – companies alter prices more quickly, and predominantly to the upside; inflation expectations are stickier. Hence, as the commodities tailwind dissipates and easy base effects are overlapped, inflation should bounce back meaningfully. Add a dollop of worries around weak sterling should the Fed not cut rates this year at all, and upside risks to commodities prices given ongoing geopolitical tensions, and it is no surprise that this small, open economy is assessed by RPI investors as likely to see headline inflation returning to its current levels in a year's time. The thesis has many proponents on the MPC as well – Haskel and Pill, to name a few. While Mann has also historically been worried about the underlying inflation process, we took her post-March MPC statements around waning corporate pricing power as a sign that she might be rethinking some of her priors.

Absent external shocks, the next few months should show which one of the two basic hypotheses on UK inflation is more accurate. The risks we are monitoring to stress test our views are: 1) The pace of core goods disinflation/deflation, where as we have already noted, we worry that the aftermath of Brexit has left this market less competitive; 2) Wage disinflation excluding the impact of the minimum wage hike, where we expect not only spot wage growth data to fall, but also businesses to adjust their expectations for the year ahead – towards the 3.5-4.0% range by year-end; and 3) We think that UK inflation expectations are anchored – while there have been many theories around how these are now more backward-looking, the data are hard to argue with. As food and commodities inflation fell, inflation expectations returned to their pre-Covid norms. However, firms' pricing behaviour is a hawkish risk. A higher share of services companies adjusting their prices more quickly than pre-Covid implies high sensitivity to the balance of supply and demand. Stronger-than-expected demand growth, combined with this pricing behaviour shift, could lead to stickier services inflation than we anticipate.

RPI | The wedge to normalise: From a peak of 60%Y, MIPS inflation has subsided towards 36.1%Y, and there is more normalisation to come if we are right about the BoE's policy normalisation push this year and next. On the other hand, we are expecting house prices to end the year at 3%Y real-terms growth, which implies a boost to the wedge from housing depreciation. Sharp weight adjustments this year introduced an additional element of volatility into forecasting the wedge. Overall, we are looking for RPI inflation at 3.6%Y in 2024 and 3.2%Y in 2025 – well below market-implied fixings, especially over 2H25.

# Monetary Policy: Down – and into the Unknown

Bottom line: Given divisions on the MPC, the messaging is non-committal and the reaction function backward-looking. Up to two cuts depend purely on two services inflation prints coming in the next few weeks. We see cuts in June, August and November this year, with Bank Rate at 3.25% by the end of 2025 as decelerating core services inflation paves the way for policy normalisation. The very near term depends on the data, but we are more confident about the quantum of cuts ultimately likely to be delivered. We expect all QT from October to be delivered via roll-off, with a pause (not a definitive end) in gilt sales.

Where we are | Past the peak: At 5.25%, Bank Rate sits in restrictive territory. We are likely past the marginal peak of the impact of the BoE's tightening – albeit with the drag on the economy still accumulating. The May meeting brought a 7-2 vote for Bank Rate to be kept on hold. While the guidance remained non-committal – "the MPC would keep under review for how long Bank Rate should be maintained at its current level" – the communication following the meeting was fairly clear – summer cuts are in play, should the data come in line with the BoE's forecasts.

Where we are going: We expect the BoE to cut rates in June. Our reading of the recent MPC speeches is that most of the internal MPC members are ready to start removing restrictiveness. That said, it appears that Chief Economist Pill is not quite there yet. While we think that Ramsden, Bailey and Broadbent all subscribe to the theory of an unwinding terms-of-trade shock, where core inflation simply lags the normalisation in headline inflation, it appears that Pill is closer to some of the external MPC members who likely view the UK economy as structurally tighter in the aftermath of all the recent supply-side shocks. However, we think that should April and May services inflation come in line with the BoE's forecasts, Pill will join the rest of the internals – and Dhingra – in voting for a rate cut in June. The three remaining externals are likely to vote to keep rates on hold.

What speed are we going? Many clients we talk to summarise the BoE's predicament as "why rush?" And certainly, whether the BoE starts cutting rates in June or August is somewhat of a secondary question in the grand scheme of things. That said, the context of the June meeting should be as follows: 1) The SNB and Riksbank have already cut rates, with the ECB and the BoC also likely to cut rates in early June; 2) On our forecasts, May headline inflation, released the day before the June meeting, is set to slip below 2%Y; 3) The economy has already seen off a recession, and the jobless rate has ticked up in 1Q24. While LFS data are unreliable, as we noted in Labour Market: It's…Complicated, even HMRC data show falling payrolls. If NFPs turned negative for several months in a row, and headline inflation was below the 2%Y target, we wonder if the market would be so divided over the Fed's ability/appetite to cut rates?

From dovish hikes to hawkish cuts: In any case, given the divisions on the Committee, it is almost certain that should the rate cut be delivered in June, it is likely to be a hawkish one. What other choice does the BoE have – especially with the Fed most likely waiting until September to cut rates? We don't quite expect the BoE to emulate the Riksbank – which explicitly said at its last meeting, delivering a rate cut, that a June follow-up is not its base case. That said, we expect the MPC to incorporate the language of "gradual and moderate" cuts into the June meeting minutes, remaining focused on the message of "cutting while staying restrictive".

What is the limit? But just how much can the BoE cut and remain restrictive? And how quickly is it willing to move? For the first question, the BoE staff estimates from the February 2024 MPR are a useful guide. While the three approaches the BoE staff outlined result in three different estimates of the average restrictiveness over the BoE's forecast horizon, the median figure seems to be \~75-100bp. However, we do note that the analysis the BoE did back in February was underpinned by market-implied Bank Rate expectations that were \~70bp lower than current market pricing. While the near-term r\* is a challenging concept to pin down, and an evolving threshold too, it is not, we think, controversial to say that the BoE could cut rates by 50bp and remain restrictive. This informs our call for an August move, to follow up the initial June cut.

Autumn pause: Once the initial restrictiveness is removed, we anticipate that the BoE will want to slow down – and so at the August forecast meeting we expect the MPC to push back against the idea of further consecutive cuts, guiding instead towards a November reassessment of the outlook and policy stance. Since we don't anticipate as large of a bounceback in inflation in 2H24, and since our US team expects the Fed to cut three consecutive times from September, we see the BoE delivering a rate cut in November. Hence, we expect Bank Rate to end this year at 4.5%. Thereafter, we see the BoE cutting rates at every meeting from February through to August, lowering Bank Rate to 3.25% – guided by firming evidence of falling services inflation.

What are the (local) risks? First things first, we have the tricky business of April and May inflation prints. We are a bit worried about just how data dependent the BoE remains, although we understand that following the experience of last year, there is a special focus on the pay and price prints around the start of the fiscal year in April. All this said, should services inflation surprise so much that it removes the June cut from consideration, how could the BoE then use just one additional inflation print (June data, released in mid-July) to start cutting rates in August instead? This is all to say that should data in the coming weeks come in hot, we see clear risks of a cutting cycle that is delayed, but faster – with

September through to December action possible should we get a repeat of last year (big price increases at the start of the fiscal year, and limited inflation pressures thereafter). Note that while RPI market fixings see headline inflation higher than we do in 2H24, these estimates – on a conventional RPI-CPI wedge – are not that far from the BoE forecasts. Should these materialise, there is some chance of no further action this year after the summer – although Fed cuts in 2H24 could change the calculus somewhat. However, we want to stress that the RPI market is not signalling anything the BoE is not already projecting.

And global? On the Fed, an obvious hawkish global risk is a less aggressive cutting cycle than our US team projects. According to our FX analysts, should the market price out all rate cuts for the Fed from here, they see \~1.20 levels for cable as plausible. While unhelpful for the near-term inflation dynamic, this is not a dramatic depreciation. In any case, any Fed versus Europe divergence is likely to feed through to weaker cable, with eurosterling flat. Sterling ERI is composed of a basket of currencies, reflecting the UK's trade patterns and the presence of the US in the UK product markets – the US is 21.8% as of 2022 of ERI, and the euro area is 40.8%. This suggests that, all else equal, a 5% drop in cable translates to \~100bp of a drop in ERI, or \~20bp of an inflation boost with a conventional FX pass-through coefficient of 0.2 This is equivalent to a boost of what around one rate cut could result in. On the whole, our rule of thumb is that a deviation of more than two cuts does look challenging. However, FX moves would have to be monitored in tandem with surveys of pricing power to deduce whether weakening sterling is indeed an operational cost UK businesses can pass on to consumers.

Re-mixing the mix? On the dovish side, as we write in Fiscal Policy: Wake Me Up When November Ends , there is some chance that the fiscal drag next year is larger than we expect, with the fiscal policy mix (more current spending, less tax cuts) a bit more disinflationary too. More fiscal policy restrictiveness implies some room for less monetary policy restrictiveness – i.e., more rate cuts.

On the Bernanke Review: We note that the coming months will see the BoE working on the implementation of the Bernanke Review. We worry about what this might mean for the BoE's communication strategy, considering the Review's recommendations around "deemphasising" its central forecast. Should the MPC adopt one key Bernanke advice – "the MPC should...be exceptionally clear in warning about situations in which it judges the

standard conditioning assumptions to be inconsistent with its view of the outlook" – we would see this as a positive step. Indeed, Governor Bailey did comment on the totality of the market pricing at the May meeting, suggesting that it was likely that more cuts would have to be delivered than were priced back then. However, we worry that "de-

emphasising" the central forecast might in practice translate to an overly elevated focus on the near-term risks. This implies a potentially more reactive than proactive BoE in the future. Finally, while it is certainly possible that the BoE explores producing its own rate path, we still think that this is less likely than not. The divisions on the MPC and the size of the MPC would make producing a "best collective optimal judgment" on the future rate path incredibly challenging. Hence, we think that the BoE will – for now – stick with its current practice of producing market rate-based forecasts.

MPC | Comings and goings: The biggest shift on the MPC will be the departure of Deputy Governor Broadbent, who will – as of the August meeting – be replaced by Clare Lombardelli. While Lombardelli has so far said little on the UK outlook, based on her time at the OECD we suspect that the risks are skewed towards her seeing the inflation risks as less benign than Broadbent. On the other hand, we do think that there is a strong chance that with Broadbent retiring, the most influential voice on the Committee will be that of the longest-serving internal member – Ramsden – who, of course, has already voted for a rate cut. August will be Jonathan Haskel's final meeting too. Given his voting patterns, it is near impossible that he would be replaced by someone more hawkish.

QT | Pedal to the floor? The latest BoE guidance on QT came via Deputy Governor Ramsden's speech. Ramsden reiterated the BoE's view on QT so far – minimal impact, as operations were well communicated and pre-announced. However, several nuances in the speech suggested how the BoE might proceed with QT even as rates are cut. For one, QT and balance sheet size reduction is presented as policy normalisation – as the reduction in interest rates is likely to be communicated. Second, Ramsden not only reiterated that QT (and active sales) can continue as interest rates fall, but noted that even as the BoE's reserve holdings reach the so-called 'Preferred Minimum Range of Reserves (PMRR)' (which lies, we think, between £335-570 billion), QT could continue. Even if the BoE was to stop active QT post-September 2024, passive QT rises to just under £90 billion in 2025. This, combined with TFSME repayments – £68 billion due this year, £125 billion next year – implies that the BoE could take \~£215 billion of reserves out of the system even if active sales stop from 4Q24 (or following the start of the cutting cycle). This would bring the BoE very close to the upper bound of its preferred minimum reserves.

We indeed assume that both active and passive QT continue until September, with passive QT in place thereafter: Note that we do not expect the BoE to end active sales for good. Rather, we expect it to message that total QT between October 2024 and September 2025 will be £90 billion, which implies that all of it would be undertaken via gilts roll-off. Active sales would then, in theory, remain in the policy toolkit for a later date, when redemptions would not be as concentrated as should be the case in 2025.

The asset mix: The BoE is then minded to use a variant of the current floor system to control short-term interest rates. It already stands ready to meet demand for additional reserves through regular repo Open Market Operations (OMOs), lending reserves against high-quality collateral at Bank Rate. The appropriate mix of assets to back the preferred level of reserves will, as per Ramsden, be decided in consultation with relevant stakeholders, including the Treasury. In theory, the BoE could sell its entire gilts portfolio, backing the appropriate level of reserves with facilities such as STR. While this is possible in theory, we assume that relevant stakeholders – the DMO and HMT – will prefer the BoE to hold onto some stock of gilts on its balance sheet (perhaps not within the APF, however) – as the ECB recently announced would be the case with its balance sheet. We would expect this consultation and the medium-term balance sheet strategy review to start in the autumn.

# Fiscal Policy: Wake Me Up When November Ends

Bottom line: We are hesitant to treat another (potential) fiscal statement a month before the general election, where the incumbent government is 20pp down in the polls, as a meaningful economic event, considering the prospect policies are reversed swiftly. Indeed, assuming a November election, the newly elected government might have to rush to revise the current spending plans, topping them up by £30-40 billion, if history is any guide – and potentially reversing some recent tax cuts in the process.

Near term | Another one? The biggest near-term question asked by clients on UK fiscal policy seems to be if and when we will get another fiscal event ahead of the general election. Another fiscal event certainly is possible, despite being a somewhat puzzling political choice – given the apparent low payoff from two NICs cuts undertaken in the past six months. We do not see a clear path to a plausible fiscal plan, given the current backdrop. Certainly, just by virtue of extending the forecast horizon, the OBR would be likely to 'generate' \~£5-10 billion of 'fiscal space'. That said, PSNB-ex came in at £6.6 billion more in FY2023/24 than the OBR expected, and the delta in the current BoE pricing versus pre-March expectations can shave around another £5 billion via higher projected interest payments.

Then there is the prospect of solidifying the current government’s fiscal plans which assume that departmental spending (ex NHS, defence, childcare, schools and overseas aid where the government has made specific commitments) would from FY2025/26 fall by 2.3% per year. In this scenario, the government, in the run up to the election, would be announcing a cut in current spending of more than 2% per year. We note that the government 'generated' its fiscal space in autumn 2022 by assuming very austere postelection spending plans, which look very challenging to deliver.

Traditionally, in the run-up to Spending Reviews – three-year planning documents where detailed departmental spending plans are outlined – governments top up the current expenditure envelope by \~£30 billion. In short, when current spending goes from a vague medium-term commitment to detailed day-to-day plans, governments tend to increase funding. Finally, there are other factors to consider. For one, meeting the defence spending pledge recently made by Prime Minister Sunak – that the defence budget will rise to 2.5% of GDP by 2030 – requires another £10 billion. Then there are the infected blood compensation payments, likely totalling north of £10 billion (or even £20 billion as per some estimates), and set to be paid out over a number of years, considering their magnitude – with a potential meaningful impact on the Chancellor's 'fiscal headroom'.

What happens after elections? More broadly, we are hesitant to treat another fiscal statement a month before the general election, where the incumbent government is 20pp down in the polls, as a meaningful economic event, considering the prospect that policies are reversed swiftly. Irrespective of the election winner, the incoming government would likely have to hold an emergency fiscal event shortly after elections. We think any giveaways are then likely to be reversed. Indeed, if the current government fails to top up the near-term spending plans ahead of elections, the next government is likely to find

itself in uncharted waters, in our view. As per the Institute for Government, "budgets reach a 'cliff edge' moment in December – completing a Spending Review any later would lead to high levels of instability – and with government departments, local authorities and devolved administrations not knowing their budgets from April 2025".

Our working assumption is that at least some of the recent NICs cuts will be reversed to top up spending plans. With a total reversal of the recent 4p NICs cut, an extra £10 billion of space generated by lengthening the forecast horizon, and perhaps some help from lower anticipated BoE rates, the newly elected government could just about top up current spending coffers by £30-40 billion. While very uncertain, our fiscal and growth figures imply headline deficits of 3.4% of GDP in FY2024/25 and 3.1% in FY2025/26 (versus the OBR at 2.7% and 2.3%, respectively). While still relatively elevated, these are decent declines from \~5% of GDP deficits in the immediate aftermath of the pandemic.

Beyond 2024 | Mind the assumptions: And so, should the current polls play out, we differentiate between the noise before and the policies after the general election. As noted, we assume that at least some of the recent tax cuts will have to be reversed in order to top up departmental spending plans. We also note that fresh governments are normally inclined to impose any fiscal austerity at the start of their term. The example of Brown's tax policies in 1997, following Clarke's easing in 1995 and 1996, is a good one. While we don't assume a larger fiscal drag next year (our base case remains 40bp), we see it as a sizeable risk. We also assume that any borrowing increases in the very near term, as noted, will be aimed at funding current expenditure – not more inflationary household tax cuts. More broadly, a higher medium-term tax and debt burden to support the size of the state remains our base case for both potential Labour and Conservative governments. However, we do acknowledge emerging challenges with any meaningful expansion of the size of the state – which demographic pressures might require – in the absence of a potential growth pick-up. In particular, with the UK's income tax system already fairly progressive, hiking taxes for those better off might prove a challenge, in the post-Brexit/ post-Covid world of increased mobility of individuals. Without global co-ordination, raising significant tax receipts from wealth is difficult too.

Copy/paste? Where we do see a difference between the current government and a potential Labour-led one is public investment. When the Conservatives won the 2019 election under former PM Johnson, they ran on a manifesto which promised unchanged personal taxes, but where extra funding was committed for public capex – i.e., 'levelling up'. As chancellor, Prime Minister Sunak delivered £60 billion in public capex spending in his first Budget in March 2020, with the aim of pushing net government investment towards 3% of GDP. Hunt and Sunak have recently reversed course – a tax cut for businesses via full capital expensing was funded in large part by real-terms cuts to public capex. Instead of 3% of GDP, net public capex is now projected to come in at under 2% of GDP by FY2028/29. Labour, it seems, might revert to the original 2019 plan of stepping up public investment – perhaps with a set of fiscal rules that take investment spending out of the fiscal framework.

Challenging medium-term picture: The interesting thing about the UK is, of course, the degree of fiscal pessimism, which seems excessive to us upon a cursory glance at debt/ GDP ratios in major European economies ex Germany and Sweden. In addition, UK fiscal constraints are self-imposed – the government decides the framework and it can amend it as it sees fit. There is no Excessive Deficit Procedure as for peripheral economies in the

euro area. That said, there are three key issues. First, the direction of travel is clearly concerning. A sizeable primary surplus is needed for the net debt/GDP ratio to start declining in five years' time. The UK primary balance has not been in a surplus since FY2001/02. Second, inflating debt away is not as easy in the UK as elsewhere, given that a third of UK debt is in linkers. This is before we even touch upon the interplay between Bank Rate (i.e., short-term rates) and public sector interest liabilities. Third and most importantly, the market appetite for gilts is the constraint.

High rates forever...or? All this said, we note that a lot of the medium-term pessimism implicitly assumes that interest rates will be structurally higher for longer. Low potential growth (\~3.5%Y in nominal terms), high interest rates and an elevated starting debt burden – it's no surprise that the maths do not add up, and even before we take into account rising spending needs given demographic pressures and defence spending commitments. That said, the market-implied Bank Rate the OBR incorporated in its March forecasts sees the policy rate at a little above 3% towards FY2028/29. However, deep recession risks that would lower the degree of monetary restrictiveness would open up more space for a serious fiscal response. So, while it might be harder for fiscal policy in the UK to be proactive, it can still certainly be very reactive.
