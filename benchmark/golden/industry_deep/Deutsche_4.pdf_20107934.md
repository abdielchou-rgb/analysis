Small beat on China manufacturing PMI: down 0.4pts in April to 50.4, consensus looked for a 50.3pt outturn. The service PMI missed, down 2.1ppts to 50.3 in April. Yi Xiong notes the drag here likely came from the property sector, where new home sales stayed low, as well as from government activities on the back of slower government bond issuances thus far this year.

China's politburo held its April meeting yesterday and set a positive policy tone by proposing new measures and accelerating existing measures on multiple fronts. Yi Xiong with 5 takeaways, including that fiscal spending will accelerate and PBOC may cut both the policy rate and the RRR.

A big miss on Australia retail spending, down 0.4%mom in March, consensus looked for a 0.2%mom rise. February revised lower as well, leaving year-ended growth in nominal retail spending at 0.8%, weaker than at any point on record outside of covid and the introduction of the GST. The weakness suggests the RBA's powerful household cashflow policy transmission mechanism is gaining traction and reduces pressure for a policy response to the beat on Q1 CPI.

Matt Raskin thinks the sharp post-GFC drop in model estimates of r-star is largely accounted for by factors other than lower trend growth. The GFC might have prompted a historical household sector deleveraging, and the associated balance sheet repair weighed on equilibrium rates over the 2010s. Matt thinks these dynamics have now run their course.

Matt Luzzetti updates previous work showing an extraordinary correlation between the yield curve slope and the spread between the present conditions components of the two popular consumer confidence surveys – University of Michigan and Conference Board. The confidence gap remains a reliable and stable predictor of the 2s10s yield curve slope, and says the curve should still be inverted, but somewhat steeper than it currently is.

Alan Ruskin on trading the FOMC. US 2y note has rallied on all of the last four FOMC days, and eight of the last 10 final FOMC days of meetings since Feb-23. The 10y Treasury note reaction has been even more pronounced, rallying on nine of the last ten final FOMC days. USD has tended to weaken consistently, but only moderately. Alan does not expect the Fed to correct the market's recent more hawkish predilections.

AI newsletter. David Folkerts-Landau explains the significance of "small language models", and looks at how "deepfakes" are spurring debate in different ways in India and Connecticut in a crucial election year.

China Chartbook. Yi Xiong shows how China's beat on Q1 growth means the economy is growing without a recovering property sector. PBOC needs to cut rates, but it faces tighter constraints from preserving bank profit margins and keeping the exchange rate stable. Yi now sees only 20bps of cuts before end-2024.

Halfway through the season, equity earnings have surpassed Binky Chadha's constructive expectations. Beats are running at 9.2% in aggregate, a near threeyear high and almost double the historical average of 4.9%. If this holds, earnings growth is on track to accelerate into the double digits to 11.8%, driven by a combination of steady mid-single digit sales growth and margins rising to the highest in nearly 2 years. Binky has raised his 2024 EPS base case forecast from \$250 to \$258, implying 13% growth over 2023.

European households are now benefiting, on net, from higher interest rates. For non-financial corporations, Michael Kirker estimates a similar NII from policy rates as seen in the 2005-08 tightening cycle – around 1% of NGDP . The peak impact on NII from higher rates is expected to occur in Q2 this year, after which, NII should recover from the second half of this year.

Meanwhile, Sanjay Raja shows that UK household balance sheets are now in a much healthier place, with the stock of household savings now exceeding the stock of household debt. And Heiki Mai shows that German mortgage borrowing could pick up moderately this year, after it had flattened out in 2023. The reallocation from traditional bank deposits into higher-yielding products such as savings bonds is set to continue.

US and European equities remain the most important drivers of high-frequency moves in FX. Rohini Grover with the details.

Macroeconomic fluctuations often account for most of the risks associated with exposures to instruments that promise to pay fixed or variable interest rates. For an investor who is long fixed-rate instruments (thus receiving these cash flows), increases in interest rates naturally lift the discount factors applied to the expected cash flows and thus cause a loss. The longer the maturity of a certain cash flow and the higher the move in the discount factor, the higher will be the loss. Upside surprises in inflation, fiscal funding needs, and FX depreciation rates are among the most common adverse macroeconomic shocks to receivers. But counterparty exposure, issuer creditworthiness, client segmentation, regulation (including changes in assets holding limits for different types of investors), changes in tax policies, currency convertibility (which may constrain currency conversion, repatriation of profits and/or liquidation of positions), and settlement issues related to local clearing houses are also important risk factors. The sensitivity of fixed-income instruments to macroeconomic shocks may be mitigated by indexing the contracted cash flows to inflation, to FX depreciation, or to specified interest rates - these are common in emerging markets. The index fixings may - by construction - lag or mis-measure the actual move in the underlying variables they are intended to track. The choice of the proper fixing (or metric) is particularly important in swaps markets, where floating coupon rates (i.e., coupons indexed to a typically short-dated interest rate reference index) are exchanged for fixed coupons. Funding in a currency that differs from the currency in which coupons are denominated carries FX risk.
