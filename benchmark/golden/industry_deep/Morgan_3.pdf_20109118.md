# Less Rich than You Think

Credit is not as rich as index spreads imply. Relative to history, duration is lower, dollar prices are lower, and ratings quality is either better (HY) or improving (IG). The marginal buyer is driven by high all-in yields, which can support spreads for some time.

## Key Takeaways

Credit spreads are near prior cycle tights. But the indices have changed over time, impacting historical comparisons. Adjustments are necessary.

In IG, accounting for lower duration, dollar prices, and improving ratings, "adjusted" spreads are still 15-30bp off the levels hit in prior tights.

In HY, spreads appear richer than IG, but factoring in composition changes, they are 20-60bp off prior period tights.

■ Marginal demand vs. recent history is coming from "yield buyers" such as insurance/annuities. We expect this flow to remain healthy through a shallow Fed easing cycle. It means weaker growth and/or a much larger drop in yields is the bigger risk for credit.

– Yield buyers should continue to support tight spreads for IG and BBs, but Bs/ CCCs are more geared to fundamentals and earnings, alongside lower funding costs.

Introduction

In our mid-year outlook, we wrote that there is a lot to like about corporate credit. The Morgan Stanley house call is for a moderation in inflation, modest cooling in growth, and the start of central bank easing, with the Fed expected to cut in September. Demand has been strong from multiple pockets, while net supply has been modest. Fundamentals have largely held up, and will likely improve in the coming quarters.

However, one factor that is not supportive of credit is valuations. IG and HY spreads are in their bottom deciles over the past two decades. Investors we speak with largely agree that the macro set-up is healthy, but push back against the current level of spreads. Even compared to other fixed income assets, US credit looks quite rich, a theme that comes up a lot in our client conversations.

Our corporate credit survey – while three months dated at this point – showed that investors remained skeptical of valuations. In IG, over 68% of respondents saw the Bloomberg Index wider than 100bp by year-end, while just 3% saw spreads below 80bp. In HY, few investors saw the index spread staying below 300bp (\~6%), and most saw it wider than 350bp.

We'd concede that US credit is not cheap, and that there are better opportunities in securitized, EM, and EU credit. Our forecasts see HY spreads holding near current levels and IG spreads actually moving marginally tighter. A core element of this thinking is that credit indices may not be quite as rich as they look.

## We make three key points:

1) When growth is healthy, spreads can stay tight for an extended period; the modernera tights on the Bloomberg IG index were \~51bp, hit during the "soft landing" of 1997, when the fed funds rate was at 5.5% for some time (and not during the eras of QE or the structured credit boom).

2) The marginal buyer of credit in the current regime is "yield-driven" and likely to be less spread-sensitive than prior cycles. The outlook for yield-buyer demand remains constructive through a shallow easing cycle from the Fed.

3) The floor on credit spreads could be lower in this cycle given how the market

## Change in quality mix

Broadly speaking, the quality of the current IG universe is weaker than during the 1990s, when BBBs accounted for \~29% of the index. Through the 2000s and 2010s, the index quality deteriorated, with the share of BBBs reaching a peak of 52% in mid-2021.

But since then, IG quality has been improving. BBBs now account for 46% of the index, similar to 2016 ( Exhibit 6 ). Notably, the share of low BBBs has declined, and they now account for just 11% of the total IG index ( Exhibit 8 , similar to 2015). The share of single As has increased, up from 39% during the post-GFC lows in 2017 to 44% currently.

Additionally, we estimated the average rating of the index by applying a numerical value to the broad rating by its market weight, and similarly find that, since 2021, the IG index has been improving marginally in quality ( Exhibit 7 ), although it remains worse than in the 1990s.

To adjust for the difference in the quality composition of the index, we weight the spreads of each rating bucket at prior cycle tights by today's index weight and come up with an "adjusted" measure. For example, since BBBs are \~50% of the market today, we would

look at spread of the BBB index in 1997, but use a 50% weight instead of the actual 25% at the time. This analysis effectively eliminates the impact of shifting ratings over time by factoring in the same composition at prior reference periods.

Generally, a lower-rated market versus today should trade at a wider spread, all else equal, while a higher-rated index would trade tighter, and we apply those adjustments below. After doing so, five of the reference tights between 1997 and 2018 get adjusted wider, i.e., making current spreads look more expensive. On the other hand, the 2020 and 2021 references get to a tighter spread level, since ratings were worse.

We summarize the results in Exhibit 8 . Overall, we note that the ratings-level adjustments are quite small in aggregate, ranging from -2bp to 8bp. It makes current market valuation lsook in line with the pre-GFC tights seen in 2005/07. However, even after this quality adjustment, spreads remain a ways off the tights from 1997.

## Duration

The IG index duration and the weighted average maturity increased sharply in 2020-21 when IG yields were at historical lows and companies termed out debt maturities. However, as rates have risen and long-end supply has lagged, index duration has come down. Currently, US IG index duration is down from a peak of 8.8 in 2021 to 7.2, a level last seen in 2016. The index maturity decreased from 12.4 in 2021 to 10.9 today ( Exhibit 9 ).

We see the shift more easily in the index composition by tenor mix ( Exhibit 10 ). Longer tenors dominated immediately post-COVID as IG companies issued longer-dated debt at cheap funding costs. But the trend has since reversed. Shorter tenors (<5y) have increased, while the long end has decreased – a relationship we see both by market and by par value.

## Dollar price of the index

The biggest spread adjustment supporting current valuations is for the dollar price of the index. The IG index price is near \$90, where it has stayed since mid-2022 after yields rose in line with rate hikes. The only other time the IG index dollar price was this low was in October 2008. In every other reference period we look at when spreads were this tight, dollar prices were well above par, and at least 10pts higher than today's levels.

Exhibit 12: Index price below \$95 for almost two years
![](images/6857f7d8bc531ace51eed2f7cb0a247b7b1bc418f1554e4f89c6b729cedc13f6.jpg)
Source: ICE BAML, Morgan Stanley Research

There are many ways to consider spread adjustments for the dollar price of the index. At a high level, high dollar prices mean higher losses in default as all securities have the same claim in bankruptcy. Skeptics might argue that such a "worst-case" methodology is unnecessary for high grade bonds that are risk-remote. However, we have seen a clear spread premium between high-dollar versus low-dollar bonds over time, suggesting that this factor is important. Also, while IG companies are risk-remote in the very near term, many investors hold their bonds over very long periods of time. Over that long horizon, default probabilities are not trivial.

In Exhibit 13 , we explored three different adjustments for the dollar price: 1) We use a probability of default using a risk-neutral measure (CDX IG 10y); where we don't have data for CDX IG, we use the 10Y OAS as a proxy; 2) We use the historical average single A and BBB default rate from Moody's (3.5% over 12 years, or 0.29% per year); and 3) We use the long-term market-implied premium where \$1 equals roughly 1bp in spread compensation.

The three measures give different magnitudes of spread compensation for the lower dollar price of today's index, but the price-driven compensation is non-trivial in all instances. For the combined number of all these spread adjustments, we use the average of the three spread adjustments at each time period.

## Bringing it all together

After combining these three shifts (ratings quality, duration, and price), we estimate new "adjusted" cycle-tights. The spreads of the three periods before the GFC get marked wider as the higher dollar price of the index at the time does not offset the adjustments made for a lower index quality and higher duration.

Meanwhile, the four periods between 2014 and 2021 are all adjusted tighter, driven primarily by the index price difference, which outweighs the adjustments from ratings mix and duration. June 2021 saw the biggest downward spread adjustment after combining these three factors, given its significantly higher duration and dollar price versus the index today, suggesting that spreads were richer than the \~80bp level implied.

For the sake of clarity, we are not saying that IG spreads will hit these "adjusted" levels any time soon. In fact our base case in IG is 80bp, modestly tighter than current levels. The financials sector – the biggest industry within IG – has changed a lot over the past two decades. Liquidity dynamics are different versus 20 years ago. ## High Yield – Richer than IG, but Not Extreme Yet

Similar to Investment Grade – The Case for Hitting New Tights , our analysis on HY spreads dives into current valuations by comparing current levels to prior historical tights, adjusting for structural changes in the index.

We adjust the ICE HY Index (H0A0) for changes in: 1) Ratings mix; 2) Dollar price of the index; and 3) Secured versus unsecured composition. We skip the adjustment for duration in HY because, compared to IG, the range of maturity buckets is more limited and the term structure for HY companies is not as well defined. So, in our view, changes in duration matter less in HY for valuation adjustments. Index spreads were trading at 293bp on average based on the seven periods when the index reached local tights ( Exhibit 15 ). At 327bp now, the HY index is tracking 35bp wider from these historical levels.

Our key finding is that taking into account an improved ratings mix, a lower index price, and a larger skew to secured bonds, we estimate that the comparable spreads at prior cycle tights should be on average \~35bp tighter.

## Dollar price of the index

Higher rates and yields have consequentially edged the HY dollar price down relative to history, although prices have rallied 5pt in 2024 to date. The average price of the HY index is currently \~95pt, which is roughly 10pt lower than prices at previous cycle tights ( Exhibit 24 ). Today's cash price represents the 18th percentile in the post-GFC period.

Exhibit 24: Current index prices are lower than those during previous historical tights
![](images/4ff4d6e529696b1b81285b20e776ba27147fdd7bb5addfa1c645fe828c40e171.jpg)
Source: ICE, Morgan Stanley Research

Similar to our previous analysis in IG, a higher dollar price means higher loss given default, so investors need more compensation for their expected loss on a high-dollar bond. For each \$1 of price premium, the incremental spread compensation is the probability of default, for which we look at two measures. One is the risk-neutral measure based on historical 5Y CDX HY spreads (assuming 40% recovery rates). The alternative is to use the historical median HY default rate from Moody's (3.4%). The total spread adjustment is the product of the per \$ default compensation and the price difference between prior tights and now.

Exhibit 25 shows our adjustment of prior cycle tights by applying the spread compensation per dollar to the difference in index price on our reference date versus today. We find that prior tights in our seven periods should be adjusted \~40bp tighter (decreasing spread compensation) using a lower dollar price today under the first method, and \~30bp tighter under the second. Even in the more conservative second method, the HY index has room to compress from current levels.

So far, our price adjustment considers risk to the downside (i.e., credit loss). On the upside, low cash price requires less compensation for call risk, given that the call option is further out of the money. This again implies that our price adjustment skews to the conservative side.

The main driver of tighter "historical tights" is today's lower cash price. As discussed above, with the average price of the HY index at 95pt, nearly 10pt lower than previous cycle tights, lower prices imply better convexity given the lower loss severity in the event of a default. Moreover, the quality mix of the HY index has improved notably, with the share of BBs increasing and CCC decreasing. The adjustment in quality improvement accounts for \~10bp of the spread adjustment.

These two factors are partly offset by the rising share of secured bonds in the index. As of now, a third of the index is secured, versus 17% on average historically. Given that issuers of secured bonds skew lower in rating versus the broader market, this consideration biases the spread comparisons wider for the current index.

Layering that with the adjustments from better quality mix and lower cash price, our estimate that current HY spreads are roughly 35bp off historical cycle tights suggests that spreads are not as rich as one might think.

## Demand Drivers of Valuations

In the sections above, our analysis was objective in nature, without consideration for 'qualitative' changes in over time. Ultimately, how tight spreads can go, and whether they can stay there, is a function of where the marginal bid for the asset class clears. For instance, if the marginal investor in credit at these levels is one driven by long-term spread valuations or 'excess' return-focused, one could argue that these levels feel shaky and could unwind quite quickly.

However, at the risk of stating the obvious, as tight as spreads are, all-in yields remain very compelling in absolute terms, and relative to many other metrics. For instance, investors can earn over 5% income on investment grade bonds today. This is much higher than most measures of inflation we can look at. 10Y breakevens are currently at 2.28%, while 5y5y forward measures are at 3.68%. So in effect, long-term investors can reasonably expect IG credit to beat inflation (i.e., positive real returns), even assuming some marginal losses over time on defaults. For long-term asset allocators, risk/reward in high grade bonds looks better than stocks ( Exhibit 27 ). Importantly, this does not change even if we assume rates go somewhat lower as the Fed cuts rates.

Similarly, the credit market has a high proportion of investors that are focused on liability management (as opposed to quarterly or annual returns). Based on data from the Fed, insurance companies and pension funds account for roughly 30% of the ownership base in the credit market. Insurance companies have seen significant inflows to fixed annuities with all-in yields at attractive levels. Much of this investor base is buying corporate bonds to immunize against future liabilities rather than looking to generate 3M or 6M total/excess returns.

Overall, we think all-in yields will remain compelling for some time, with our economists calling for a shallow rate-cutting cycle from the Fed. This should support continued healthy credit demand from the retail (cash on the sidelines in money market funds) and institutional channels (annuity flows, pension de-risking). We think IG is the biggest beneficiary of this yield-based demand, while BB fundamentals are good enough to move in sync with IG.
