## State of the US Consumer

## Smart shopper

Jonathan Millar, BCI, US | Renate Marold, BCI, US | Powell Eddins, CFA, BCI, US | Priya Ohri-Gupta, CFA, BCI, US | Hale Holden, BCI, US | Terry Ma, CFA, BCI, US | Adrienne Yih, BCI, US | Seth Sigman, BCI, US

Excerpted from State of the US Consumer: Smart shopper, published on June 5, 2024

Our cross-sector analysis heading into Q3 2024 shows that US consumers have extended their shift toward value-focused spending, but demand has not vanished. There are early signs of credit concerns, but more data are needed to determine if they indicate material cracks.

• Following the update from March, US consumers are holding steady (still). We acknowledge there are some adjustments in spending, but we are far from sounding any alarms. Various credit metrics show deterioration, but we do not view these as imminent indicators that aggregate spending is set to weaken.

• Our data science research suggests that US consumer health still leans positive, but there are a few signals that we think align with the narrative of a moderation in consumption, such as a continued trend toward lower-end retailers, and a pickup in mentions of low consumption and pressure on consumption in earnings calls.

• Credit card issuers have noted the consumer remains healthy, but is self-regulating spending. Higher earners have fared better than those earning lower incomes. Loan growth is widely expected to decelerate through 2024, but remain above the pre-pandemic average, and credit card delinquency trends show early signs that losses may be headed toward a peak.

• Delinquency (DQ) metrics in consumer ABS do not appear too concerning, but broader DQ metrics from the Federal Reserve / Equifax (which we believe are an earlier indicator) suggest that consumer credit is starting to weaken materially.

• In IG credit, we see consumers sharpening their focus on value. We also have some indication that this prudence is moving up the income spectrum.

• In HY credit, we are tracking an increase in promotional intensity (combined with this year's headline event risk), particularly in staples, which we expect to accelerate into the second half of the year, suggesting margins may be close to peaking.

• Our Q1 24 promo checks in specialty retail equity show that demand has sequentially softened relative to Q4 23. Underlying trends are a challenge to decipher because of stubborn inflationary pressure, colder temperatures, and 53rd week calendar shifts.

• In hardlines/broadlines equity, we also see the efects of the value proposition, along with event-driven sales, increased marketing, and some diverging channel trends (including shifts to online).

## Energy Sigma

## Overreaction

Amarpreet Singh, BCI, US

Excerpted from Energy Sigma: Overreaction, published on June 6, 2024

We think oil markets have overreacted to the mildly negative OPEC+ meeting outcome. Demand indicators have certainly softened somewhat recently, but are not falling of a clif, in our view. In that context, the OPEC+ response seems appropriate to us.

Oil prices are down 3% since the OPEC+ meeting over the weekend, which we described as being mildly negative relative to our baseline balance estimates. We maintain the view that the OPEC+ meeting in itself probably did not warrant this kind of price reaction.

A key pushback to our view has been that the group has run out of ways to deliver a market surprise. A simple rollover of production cuts for a quarter, along with a timeline to phase them out gradually, is not enough to support a market that has been weakening despite the cuts. Kpler data show that global crude inventories have stabilized since a large build in April, but our seasonal diference index for the US that takes into account refined product inventories as well has been declining and last week stood at the weakest level since September last year.

Those are good points, but while there has certainly been some softening in the demand indicators recently, it is not clear to us that demand is falling of a clif. Yes, NBS manufacturing PMI in China and ISM manufacturing in the US were weak, but the global manufacturing PMI bounced back last month. Yes, US demand for March was weaker than expected, but vehicle miles traveled were up 3% y/y in April, according to FHA. More important, Barclays' economists think the recent data likely exaggerate concerns about US consumers. Yes, our apparent demand indicator for China has softened somewhat, from 16.5 mb/d in Q1 24 to 16.2 mb/d in Q2 24 so far, but this likely overstates the slowdown, as key refined product inventories have declined by 100 kb/d over the same period, according to JLC. The implied 200 kb/d sequential slowdown in demand should be viewed in the context of a sharp slowdown in sequential activity growth, which our economists expect to recover somewhat in the second half.

A rollover of existing cuts, combined with the prospect of improved country-level compliance, therefore seems to us a suficient response to somewhat weaker demand, especially given the seasonality in demand that also weighs on OPEC+ exports because of the increase in power burn in the second half of the year. The question then becomes what if demand deteriorates further due to a sharper slowdown in broader activity. That is certainly a tail risk, and while incremental cuts could be dificult to negotiate, we think the group will likely be able to come to an agreement to support fundamentals, as market share concerns continue to recede amid growing signs that non-OPEC+ supplies are slowing largely in line with our forecast. US oil production has been surprising to the downside so far this year, and oil output growth in Brazil has averaged 180 kb/d y/y during the first four months, compared with almost 500 kb/d in Q4 23. We forecast that it will fall below 100 kb/d y/y in Q4 24.

## What's Worked and What Hasn't: 2Q24

Venu Krishna, CFA, BCI, US | Japinder Chawla, CFA, BCI, US | Rex Feng, BCI, US | Madhuresh Rai, CFA, BCI, US | Young Chen, BCI, US

Excerpted from U.S. Equity Insights: What's Worked and What Hasn't: 2Q24, published on June 6, 2024

Concentration rising again after 1Q24 results reinforced Tech exceptionalism; growth vs. value begins to price in rate cuts; Utilities are the latest beneficiary of AI hype as investors look for upside outside of the traditional Tech space; pricing power is weakening amid soft demand and we see more margin risk ahead.

Broadening? Not quite. Interest in upside outside of mega-cap Tech sufered a setback during earnings season as 1Q24 revealed Big Tech's ongoing dominance over earnings revisions and margin upside. The Leadership Gap between the top 6 and the rest of the SPX was at the 94th percentile as of the end of May, up from the 70th percentile in January. The percentage of SPX constituents beating the index slipped to 33%.

Growth vs. value begins to price in rate cuts. Growth is our top performing factor thus far in 2Q despite negative exposure to real rates; earnings revisions are acting as an ofset. Markets appear to be positioning for an impending rate cut in terms of reducing Value exposure: our Cheap Quality basket has sharply underperformed the MSCI USA Quality index over the weeks since April core CPI surprised to the downside.

AI hype engulfs Utilities. Utilities have outpaced every other SPX sector in terms of valuation expansion YTD. While often thought of as a way to position for a rate cut, we'd point out that Utilities have historically delivered middling returns in the 6 months leading up to a Fed cutting cycle. Instead, we think that Utilities are the latest sector to get caught up in AI-related hype via power sales to data centers, which was evident in 1Q24 results. Investors are starting to look for upside outside of the traditional Tech space.

Pricing power is weakening. Our screen of stocks that exhibited strong pricing growth during peak inflation substantially underperformed the S&P 500 last month. With the Fed facing a narrow path to a September rate cut and signs of consumer stress appearing in quarterly results, we think there's more margin risk ahead.

Markets are sanguine, but macro overhangs persist. Vol is subdued but macro sensitivity (equity correlation to Treasury yields) is high. Inflation has been the most important macro data point for equities and is likely to remain so. That said, ISM manufacturing drove a bifurcation between cyclical and defensive equities, showing more dispersion around macro data.

## Europe

## Tech and Rates dependence

Emmanuel Cau, CFA, Barclays, UK | Matthew Joyce, CFA, BBI, Paris | Magesh Kumar Chandrasekaran, CFA, Barclays, UK | Emmanuel Makonga, Barclays, UK | Arihanth Bohra Jain, Barclays, UK

Excerpted from Equity Market Review - Tech and Rates dependence, published on June 7, 2024

Tech and rates keep driving equity market gyrations. Outsized Big Tech stock impact on performance is a headache for investors, although more in the US than Europe, where concentration has fallen. ECB and BOC cuts add to the global easing cycle, and should help growth. But data dependence calls for barbell sector allocation.

Tech hegemony and market concentration. With NVidia becoming only the third company ever to reach a \$3trn market cap, and ASML rallying 8% in one day (providing >50% of the daily return on SX5E), it is hard to argue that Tech hegemony is falling and the long awaited broadening out is actually happening. On days like Wednesday this week, it felt like the equity markets on both sides of the Atlantic were a one stock show, and that being in or out of these can make or break your performance. So Tech performance begets Tech performance in a kind of self reinforcing loop (even if Nvidia and ASML's stellar performance masks increased dispersion within the Tech space). However, this is still far more true in the US, where concentration remains near all time highs (see our US strategists take on this in U.S. Equity Insights What's Worked and What Hasn't: 2Q24, 6 June). In Europe, which is a less Tech-centric and more diversified market anyway, the market concentration has actually been coming down a bit ytd (bar Wednesday this week!). As per figure 2, the ytd performance of S&P500 with or without NVidia (the top stock contributor) looks very diferent. In Europe, the performance gap of SXXP with our without ASML (top contributor) is very narrow. This likely makes it somewhat 'easier' for active managers to diversify and beat their benchmark. While we recognise Tech hegemony remains a large driver of overall market returns, this is still far more true for the US than other ex-US markets. We think diversification has merit, so keep our tactical OW on Europe and broader sector allocation, as per our latest chart pack (Consolidation and rotation – June Chart Pack, 5 June). A simple piece of evidence that this is working is that US and European markets (even including the Nasdaq), are delivering approximately equal returns ytd, despite what is going on in Big Tech.

More rate cuts welcome. The global rate cutting cycle is gaining momentum, with the BOC and ECB joining the party this week. Being data dependent though, means the market mood is hanging on every growth/inflation data print. The surge in bond/equity correlation of the past few weeks was reflective of a'bad is good' market mindset. But with yields having already fallen a lot in just a few days and equities continuing to rally, the bond/equity correlation appears to be reversing now. It is getting increasingly hard to tell whether bad data is good or bad, and vice versa, as the market's reaction function seems to be changing daily. However, the fact is that despite rates moving up and down a lot this year, equities have held up, as growth/earnings came in better than expected. So rates direction matters more for equity market internals than its direction. With two big numbers - NFP today and US CPI next week, we should hopefully have a better understanding of near-term Fed policy moves by this time next week. But generally, we still think we are in a global mid-cycle rate cuts regime, which should provide a mild stimulus to the economy and extend the cycle down the road. Hence our continued mid-term positive outlook for equities, based on the view that bond yields should be more range-bound while earnings stay resilient. As discussed in our chart pack on Wednesday and last week's EMR (Get ready for ECB cut(s), 31 May), we believe that a more barbell sector allocation makes sense in this environment.

The cutting cycle has begun

Bradley Rogof, BCI, US | Dominique Toublan, BCI, US

Excerpted from US Credit Alpha, published on June 7, 2024

Two G7 central banks cut rates for the first time this cycle. We expect the Fed to cut too, but later this year. Economic surprises have been negative, weighing on Treasury yields and volatility. The rates market should remain on edge with the heavy economic calendar. Spreads remained range-bound, but BBBs have compressed.

Two G7 central banks cut rates for the first time this cycle. Both the Bank of Canada and the ECB cut rates by 25bp this week, for the first time since 2020 and 2019, respectively. These cuts were widely expected by the markets, with an 80% chance for the BoC and a 100% chance for the ECB according to WIRP. In both cases, the decision was driven by inflation that has declined from last year's peak and relatively soft economic growth. The market expects more cuts from both central banks in 2024: 2.1 more cuts are priced in for the BoC and 1.7 more for the ECB.

The market is increasing its expectations for Fed rate cuts in 2024. It expects the Fed to stay put next week, but to cut twice in 2024. The expected number of cuts for 2024 has increased from 1.3 a week ago to 1.9 now, driven by the recent softer-than-expected macro data. In this week's High Yield and Leveraged Loans section, we analyze the implications for bond-loan relative value. We find that a switch from loans to high yield makes sense if the Fed cuts four times or more in the next twelve months.

Overall, economic surprises have been negative, but there were some mixed signals this week. The ISM Manufacturing PMI was soft, especially on the new order side, which fell to a one-year low. However, the ISM Services PMI print was at the high end of the last 18-month range. Barclays Economics believes that the soft manufacturing print was driven somewhat by noise (see here) and that the services numbers did show resilience but likely exaggerated underlying growth (see here). Overall, our economists expect one cut in 2024, most likely in September.

The data softness has driven Treasury yields lower and vol higher. The 10y Treasury yield was at 4.30% as of midday Thursday, 31bp lower than the recent peak on May 29. This is the widest one-week trading range for the 10y point since December 2023. Overall, the curve bull flattened, but yields moved about in parallel five years and out. The MOVE index has also jumped, from a local low of 82 two weeks ago to 102 now. However, it is currently in the middle of its year-to-date range and nowhere near the 120+ levels at which credit spreads tend to react quite negatively.

The NFP data on Friday and CPI and the Fed next week will be key for short-term market moves. The main question is whether these two data points will confirm the recent softness in employment data and whether or not inflation is still on a good trajectory. The data are obviously important for the Fed's dual mandate. Note that dots will be published at the FOMC meeting next week.

We believe that US consumers are crucial for the medium term and find that they are still in an okay place. Barclays Research put together a cross-sector analysis heading into 3Q24. It shows that US consumers have extended their shift toward value-focused spending, but demand has not vanished. There are early signs of credit concerns, but more data are needed to determine if they indicate material cracks. See State of the US Consumer: Smart shopper.

In credit, it was another week of heavier-than-expected supply and sideways secondary markets. High yield and investment grade spreads were marginally wider w/w (+2bp to 88bp in IG and + 6bp to 311bp in HY). Still, spreads continue to trade in a very narrow range: 3bp for IG in the last month and 22bp for HY. We believe that this softness was mainly driven by lower and more volatile Treasury yields. At the same time, issuance surprised to the upside again this week across IG, HY and leveraged loans.

However, spreads continue to compress within IG. The BBB versus A industrial basis is the narrowest since 2007. BBB industrials are trading only 39bp wider than A industrials. The basis has narrowed by 10bp year-to-date and is at the tightest level since mid-2007, when the IG Industrials index was around 100bp, versus 85bp now. The spread ratio is also quite low, at 1.60x, versus 1.76x at the beginning of the year. The ratio has rarely been below this level since the GFC, reaching 1.51x in 1Q22, but it still much higher than the 1.3-1.4x levels where it consistently traded in 2006 and 2007 (Figure 2). We find the 5y and 10y points too compressed but believe that there are still opportunities in BBBs in the front end (see Front end looks BBBetter).

## Europe

The first cut is the deepest

Søren Willemann, Barclays, UK

Excerpted from European Credit Alpha, published on June 7, 2024

The ECB delivered the first cut, as expected, albeit with a hawkish tint. Our economists and the market expect the next cut to occur in September. Investors seeing cuts happen could further strengthen demand as they focus on locking in high yields.

Going into the ECB meeting, markets were relatively quiet, with a slight widening bent (IG +1.5bp, HY +6bp from Friday, 31 May), with a fairly active primary market allowing CDS indices in particular, but also Bs, to outperform €IG (Figure 8).

The ECB gave the market the long-expected first rate cut, but with a hawkish tint, leaving Bund yields higher with the announcement and Main slightly wider. The main reason for reading the cut as hawkish is that Eurosystem staf projections for both headline and core inflation were revised up for 2024 and 2025, with headline inflation now expected to average 2.5% in 2024 and 2.2% in 2025, and not decline below 2% until 2026.

Cutting now while forecasting above-target inflation for the foreseeable future puts a lot of faith in the long-term forecasting ability, in our view. We also interpret the language from the ECB statements as implying that decisions on future cuts will only come in months when the Eurosystem staf revise projections, the next being September. Incidentally, the market is already pricing a 95% probability of another cut by then, in line with our economists forecasts. This leaves both little scope for 'new news' in the near term and only downside to a September cut being priced out.

With the first rate cut done, we see a real chance that investor attention, even more than before, turns to locking in high yields while they can. This could strengthen an already-supportive demand technical, particularly from the retail side.

Feeding this demand, issuance from reverse Yankees (US issuers in EUR) has picked up meaningfully after two years of subdued activity, given higher funding costs in Europe at that time. As we discuss in the IG section, on a hedged basis, there is little diference between issuing in EUR or USD, but for issuers who do not need — or want — to hedge EUR liabilities (for example, if they have meaningful earnings in EUR), there is arguably still a meaningful yield advantage to issue in EUR over USD. As such, we expect reverse Yankee supply to remain high at these levels, in line with historical norms.

Staying on the topic of supply, we look at the progression of the maturity wall in HY, which we would characterize as 'relaxed': €110bn of HY debt is due by end-2026 (31% of the market), versus only €41bn for loans (11% of the market). Things could be spiced up by the fact that 16% of bonds maturing in 2025 are rated CCC, double their index weight, and the Other Financials sector (mostly REITs) stands out as having both wide spreads and the second most bonds due in 2025-26 (Autos has the most).

If the markets remain supportive, issuers' patience may be vindicated (our data show that issuers waiting for lower rates has become the norm over the past two years), but should the market experience a new shock — particularly from higher rates and volatility — European HY (less so loans and US HY) would be more vulnerable.

Last week, we surveyed the European hybrid capital landscape, and concluded that AT1s and insurance T2s were the best longs here. While corporate hybrids and bank LT2s may have good carry for their beta, they are unlikely to see more beta compression.

This week, we expand on this and consider relative value to US hybrids, and cross-currency in general. Our key conclusion is that European hybrid capital, while compressed, appears less compressed than the US hybrid capital space: US bank preferreds are very tight, as are US corporate hybrids. We thus favour the European hybrid capital space over the US. We also show that, on a spread basis, \$AT1s and \$LT2s are wide to €AT1s and €LT2s, respectively (Figure 9).

## Economic Outlook

## Non-committal

Christian Keller, Barclays, UK | Gabriel Casillas, BCI, US | Akash Utsav, Barclays, UK

Excerpted from Global Economics Weekly, published on June 7, 2024

Data dependence leaves policy makers non-committal. The ECB delivered an initial cut, but its future rate path remains uncertain. The Fed and BoJ are likely to stand pat next week, but while a BoJ hike in July looks likely, any future Fed easing is complicated by continuing strong US job growth. Next week's US CPI is crucial.

Data dependence implies non-committal policy makers...

## ECB delivers hawkish cut

On Thursday, the ECB delivered its much-telegraphed 25 basis point rate cut. This marks its first policy rate easing since the rapid hiking cycle that started with the deposit rate at -0.50% in July 2022 and ended at 4.00% in September 2023. The ECB thus moved before the Fed and the BoE, but after initial rate cuts by the Swiss National Bank (March) and Sweden's Riksbank (May), as well as a cut by the Bank of Canada earlier this week. It also comes after a number of EM central banks, which started to hike rates far earlier than core central banks and are already ahead in their easing cycles.

However, the oficial communication accompanying the decision was cautious and noncommittal on the future rate path. President Lagarde indicated that she did not know how long it would take until the next policy move, suggesting that another move as early as July might be unlikely. By highlighting that there could be unexpected bumps in the road and that any decision would remain data dependent, the ECB even left potential future cuts in September and beyond uncertain.

All this suggests that the ECB's Governing Council has not taken a view at this stage on the path of rates and will wait until later in the summer to assess data and the evolution of the inflation outlook before deciding on its next steps. This should not come as a surprise: central banks that are longer confident in their models' inflation predictions and seek signals in each new data print are reluctant to provide forward guidance on when, how much, and how long they can cut rates. Put diferently, central banks' high data dependence makes them noncommittal to any policy cycle.

We continue to expect cuts in September and December of this year and an additional three cuts in H1 2025, but recent developments have, on the margin, lowered the chances of such an easing cycle.

## Fed to reiterate "high for longer"

The FOMC appears all but certain to leave policy rates unchanged next week, with communications emphasizing data dependence and reiterating its intention to hold rates "higher for longer." We expect no material changes to the FOMC statement, retaining forward guidance linking rate cut prospects to gaining greater confidence that inflation is moving sustainably toward 2%. We expect the updated Summary of Economic Projections to continue to show GDP growth and unemployment running close to trend through 2026. For core PCE price inflation, we expect an upward revision of 0.2pp in 2024, to 2.8% Q4/Q4, reflecting upside surprises in recent monthly prints, with projections for 2025-26 unchanged. These may well change following Wednesday's CPI report, though Chair Powell and other committee members will not have the details about May PPI on hand to adjust fully to next week's estimates.

With the upgrade to inflation in 2024, we expect the median dot to show only one rate cut in 2024, compared with the three shown in March. This should be a close call, given the positioning of the dots in March, when a shift of just one dot would have shifted the median to two cuts. With little change to the projections for inflation and activity beyond 2024, we expect the median fed funds target range to remain unchanged over the remainder of the projection period, implying five cuts in 2025 (versus three in March), followed by three more in 2026 (unchanged).

In the press conference, Chair Powell is likely to acknowledge modest progress on inflation since the May meeting, but to retain skepticism that inflation has convincingly resumed its move back toward the 2% target. He will likely note that the rate cuts indicated in the dot plot are not written in stone and will evolve with the data, reiterating that the committee is prepared to hold rates steady until it gains confidence that inflation is moving sustainably toward target.

## BoJ to prepare for July hike

The BoJ is likely to stand pat at next week's monetary policy meeting (MPM), but much focus will be on communication, especially at the post-MPM press conference. Our base case is still a rate hike in July, and we will look for hints in that direction. Some market participants expect significant announcements regarding quantitative tightening (QT), including concrete numerical targets, but we believe the BoJ likely wants to prioritize rate hikes. The BoJ will thus seek to avoid any market disruptions that full-fledged QT could trigger ahead of rate hikes. Taken together, we think at the June MPM, the BoJ will likely put more emphasis on clarifying the normalization strategy for rate hikes and QT and reconfirm its intention to adjust purchases flexibly depending on market conditions under the current settings decided at the March MPM. In addition, we believe it could very well add language to its statement saying that "JGB purchases are in a gradual downtrend over the medium to long term.”

## Euro area Q1 GDP details reconfirm rising labour costs

The final release of euro area Q1 GDP this week provided important insights regarding the wages-profits-productivity nexus. In particular, compensation per employee edged up to 5.1% y/y in Q1 from a revised 4.9% in Q4 23 (prev. 4.6%), slightly above the annual growth rate inferred by the June ECB staf projections (5.0% y/y).

Certainly, one-of payments linked to collective bargaining agreements, notably in Germany, drove this acceleration, and the Q1 GDP deflator decomposition confirms that profits continue to bufer rising labour costs. However, at the same time, labour productivity remained broadly flat, and if the expected recovery in productivity does not materialise, concerns will arise about how long profit margins can continue to absorb increasing labour costs.

## Strong jobs growth confirms robust US labour market dynamics

This week's US employment report for May confirmed the resilience of the US labour market, showing a strong rebound in job gains, payroll income, and a re-acceleration of average hourly earnings. Nonfarm payroll employment posted a strong, broad-based 272k gain (Barclays 200k, consensus 185k), following April's relatively soft (and downwardly revised) 165k gain. The

3mma pace, at 249k/m, was only modestly slower than the 261k over the preceding threemonth period ended in February and a fair bit faster than the 198k/m pace in November.

The numbers also imply that the virtuous cycle of strong labour market and solid consumer spending remains intact. Factoring in the re-acceleration in average hourly earnings (+0.4% m/m) and a rebound in aggregate hours worked (+0.2% m/m), payroll income posted a robust 0.6% m/m increase. This brings it to a robust 6.0% on a 3m saar basis. Barring a sudden increase in precaution by households, this should help restore monthly gains in real PCE in the coming months. The tailwind should only be reinforced by the latest gains in household wealth: this week's Financial Accounts estimates through Q1 boosted the cumulative change in net worth from Q4 19 through Q1 24 to \$19.1trn (+\$2.7trn), after adjusting for inflation.

That said, the sustained weakness in the household side of the report indicates that the economy is running weaker than the establishment survey would suggest. May's 408k decline in household employment caused the unemployment rate to round up to 4.0% (3.964%) for the first time in this expansion, despite a 250k decline in the labour force. The household numbers equate to a 456k decline in payroll employment if one adjusts for the diferences in scope between the two surveys, albeit on the heels of strong gains in March (+352k) and April (+563k). This payroll-adjusted household employment measure has fallen by 1mn since November, compared with a 1.5mn increase in payroll employment over the same period, widening the cumulative discrepancy between these two measures to 4.1mn in May.

However, we take the household survey measure with a grain of salt, and we suspect the FOMC will as well. Indeed, the Fed has access to extensive employment microdata from Automated Data Processing (ADP), which it uses to monitor the labor market in conjunction with the BLS estimates. This week's ADP employment estimate, which is measured independently of the payroll survey, showed a cumulative increase in private payroll employment of 1.0mn from November to May. This is very close to the 1.2mn increase in the private sector measure from the payroll survey and completely out of step with the household measure (unless one assumes that the government sector has shed millions of workers since late 2023).

## ... and core inflation remains elevated

All eyes will be on next week's CPI report, coming on the morning of the FOMC decision. We forecast headline inflation at 0.12% m/m (3.4% y/y), considerably slower than April's 0.31% m/m (3.4% y/y). However, this comes entirely from declines in energy prices, with core inflation expected to hold steady at 0.29% m/m (3.5% y/y). Within core, we expect readings for core goods (0.0% m/m) and core services (0.4% m/m) to be similar to April's, with efects of modest firming in rent measures ofset by modest disinflation in ex-housing services that reflects another decline in the volatile airfares component.

We expect the May PPI release, which arrives the day after the FOMC meeting, to show a 0.2% m/m (2.6% y/y) increase in headline final goods and a relatively firm 0.3% m/m (2.5% y/y) increase in core. As always, the implications for core PCE price inflation will be determined by the details. The net foreign travel component of core PCE, which our research has identified as as a key contributor to this year's inflation resurgence, will be informed by Friday's import price release. We expect ex-petroleum import prices to post a firm 0.4% m/m gain, albeit somewhat slower than April's 0.7% m/m.

## ... and the global election cycle continues to surprise

## Unexpected election outcomes in India, Mexico, and South Africa

In India, the NDA's narrower-than-expected victory led to uncertainty as the BJP fell short of a majority. Despite expected policy continuity under Mr. Modi's leadership, there are concerns about potential shifts in revenue expenditure in the upcoming budget. The focus remains on the cabinet formation and possible coalition dynamics post-elections.

Mexico's recent general elections marked a significant victory for the ruling party Morena across multiple fronts. Claudia Sheinbaum's presidential win surpassed expectations with a wide margin, securing a two-thirds constitutional supermajority in both chambers alongside allies. While expectations lean towards fiscal responsibility and green initiatives under President Sheinbaum, there are concerns about the implications of the constitutional supermajorities, including fiscal deficit widening and potential institutional shifts.

In South Africa, the ruling ANC called for a Government of National Unity (GNU). President Ramaphosa revealed on June 6, 2024, that the ANC had preliminary talks with EFF, IFP, DA, NFP, and PA, among others. The National Executive Council (NEC) of the ANC assigned the Negotiating Task Team (NTT) to form a GNU to address the country's challenges. The NTT will detail power-sharing arrangements. The ANC is expected to hold most cabinet positions, ofering legislative positions to the opposition. The DA and IFP have agreed in principle. The EFF is open to engagement if the GNU aims to address apartheid and colonialism. Action SA, a party with a 1% vote share, is hesitant due to concerns about the ANC's governance capability. While markets may not be excited about the inclusion of the EFF in what is in some respects a hybrid government, rather than a pure ANC/DA coalition, we believe they will take some comfort in the fact that the combination of the ANC and the DA accounts for more than 50% of parliament, giving the country a solid chance of being able to implement market-friendly policies, positioning the combination to outvote the EFF on controversial and potentially unproductive policy issues such as land expropriation and nationalisation.

## UK election: Labour party leading the polls, emphasizing "securenomics"

Current polls give Labour a significant lead over the Conservatives, pointing to a potential majority of 150 to 200 seats. Historical trends suggest a swing back towards the incumbent party closer to elections, potentially reducing Labour's lead to a still substantial 50-100 seats. Betting odds and website trafic data also indicate strong support for Labour, making them the front-runners to secure the most seats in the upcoming election.  Manifestos of both parties will be released next week. We expect fiscal prudence from Labour and the Conservatives, with Labour emphasizing "securonomics" and the Conservatives targeting pension protection, reduced National Insurance contributions, and increased defense spending.

## European Parliament elections under way

The European Parliament elections, held every five years in June, are taking place from June 6 to 9 this year. With 373 million eligible voters across the EU, the elections will shape the bloc's direction for the next five years. Crucial issues such as wars, immigration, and climate change are influencing the political landscape, with predictions of notable gains for hard-right parties. The parliament's role in approving the commission's makeup and appointing its president, currently Ursula von der Leyen, holds significant weight. The freeze on the EU-China investment agreement due to MEP sanctions shows the parliament's influence on international objectives. Immigration, climate change, security, and support for Ukraine are key concerns, with migration and climate change persisting as divisive topics within the EU.

## Potential tarifs on Chinese EVs

The European Commission is preparing to impose tarifs on electric vehicles (EVs) imported from China in response to Beijing's subsidies to its car industry. The potential tarifs, expected to exceed the current 10% rate, could afect both Chinese and Western manufacturers that produce cars in China for export to Europe, potentially leading to retaliatory measures from China. The EU aims to address what it views as unfair trade practices and market distortions by Beijing, reflecting a more assertive stance on trade issues and a desire to prevent a repeat of past challenges faced by European industries in competing with state-supported Chinese rivals.
