## Overview

Credit spreads remained relatively stable, despite a sharp pickup in equity volatility. This is not unusual in a historical context, but we see a number of reasons for credit investors to be cautious in the near term, including tight valuations, mixed macro data, earnings guidance, and weak seasonality.

## Credit mostly calm amid the equity storm

Credit markets remained steady despite a pickup in equity volatility. Equities started the week strong after last week's sell-of, but that quickly changed on Wednesday following lackluster earnings guidance and weaker-than-expected macro data. The S&P declined more than 2% that day, its first loss of at least 2% since February 2023, and the VIX closed above 18 for the first time since April. Credit spreads, in contrast, were fairly muted, with derivative and investment grade cash spreads only slightly wider week-to-date as of Wednesday's close (Figure 1), while high yield cash spreads were unchanged.

The lower sensitivity of credit spreads to equities is not unusual. Credit-equity betas tend to decline at higher equity valuations, especially in terms of upside moves, as spread tightening potential is limited, while equity valuations are theoretically uncapped (Figure 2). We observed this dynamic in the first half of July, when the S&P gained 3.8% and SPW (equal-weighted S&P) gained 4.5% through July 16, while investment grade and high yield cash spreads were only 4bp and 6bp tighter, respectively. More recently, as the S&P has sold of, down more than 4% over July 16-24 (and SPW is down 2.6%), investment grade cash spreads are only 3bp wider, while high yield spreads are unchanged. There have been a number of examples when the S&P sells of \~5% and credit is generally okay. However, it has historically been dificult for spreads to stay calm if the S&P falls 10% or more.

Credit tends to be negatively convex at tight valuations, as there is limited upside but potentially significant downside if fundamentals worsen. We view the lack of downside (so far) in the recent move as an indication that investors are not currently worried about credit fundamentals in aggregate, and we see signs of this lack of concern in relative performance within credit as well, one example being high yield outperforming investment grade recently in both cash and CDX.

Despite this lack of concern, we see reasons for caution. Index valuations remain close to multi-year tights, making the upside/downside for credit spreads unattractive, in our view. Recent macro data continue to paint a mixed picture, with this week's home sales data (new and existing) missing expectations and the S&P manufacturing PMI falling back into contractionary territory, while advance 2Q GDP growth beat expectations. Weaker guidance/ outlook this week from companies such as Whirlpool and Dow and recent softness in commodity prices could also change the narrative about the real economy (to keep tabs on earnings, we are once again publishing our weekly Earnings Scorecard).

# 1H24 trading volume recap: Bonds lead the way

Corporate bond volumes and portfolio trading have picked up in 2024, and single-name CDS volumes remain near multi-year highs, as investors continue to focus on credit selection. Macro products are more mixed, with CDS indices lower but ETFs and TRS higher, with the exception of HY, which is lower across all three.

Macro credit volumes were mixed in the first half of 2024. CDS index volumes were lower amid muted volatility while most ETF and TRS volumes were higher. The main exception was US high yield, with volumes lower across CDX, ETFs, and TRS. We think that increased dispersion and a shift away from macro concerns have likely driven activity away from the HY macro products and toward bond trading.

Single-name CDS activity was mostly flat y/y after last year's surge in activity. Corporate single-name CDS volumes in the US and Europe remain near multi-year highs, supported by a continued focus on credit selection, as well as bank hedging flows. Net notional outstanding is now at the highest level since 2016.

Investment grade cash index volumes increased 19% y/y in 2024, while volumes for the full investment grade universe increased 21%. High levels of concentration among the top 50 tickers continued in 2Q24. With the large rise in trading volumes, turnover increased in most sectors, most notably fincos, pharma, retail and apartment REITs, and autos.

High yield cash volumes also increased in 2024, with TRACE volumes up 13% y/y. The size of the index has stayed flat this year following two straight years of contraction, as the torrent of rising stars seen in 2022-23 slowed to a drip. As a result, turnover has reached post-COVID highs, increasing from 1.4x to 1.6x. The increase has been consistent across all ratings buckets.

Portfolio trading gathered significant momentum in 1H24. April 2024 was a record month for portfolio trading, with volumes of \$110bn, which represented 14% of total corporate bond volume for the month.

Trading volumes for leveraged loans picked up steam as well, increasing 7% y/y. Index turnover increased to 0.6x from its post-GFC low of 0.5x. Meanwhile, bid-ask spreads continued to narrow amid strength in loan prices, with the median bid-ask tightening to \$0.50, the tightest since February 2022.

## US and European macro products and single-name CDS

## CDS indices: Lower volumes amid a general lack of volatility, with CDX.HY down the most y/y

Aside from short-lived bouts of volatility in mid-April and in European markets in June, markets remained mostly calm in 1H24, which we think contributed to lower CDS index volumes y/y across IG and HY CDS indices (Figure 3 and Figure 4).

CDX IG and HY volumes showed sharper drops versus 1H23 (-9% and -17%, respectively) than iTraxx Main and Xover volumes (-4% and -10%, respectively), with the latter two benefiting from the bout of French election-induced volatility in Europe. In general, CDX HY and Xover have shown sharper drops than CDX IG and Main, which we attribute to two factors. First, economic growth has surprised positively in both regions, reducing the desire to express negative views on growth-sensitive risk. Second, idiosyncratic risk has picked up meaningfully (particularly in Xover, which also has fewer constituents), and this could also serve to reduce the desire to express macro views using these indices, as single-name news can drive the performance of a macro trade.

Despite the small y/y drop across indices, activity levels and liquidity remained healthy, which, based on anecdotal evidence, was supported by continued strong interest in relative value trading and significant participation in the CDS index market by CTAs.<sup>2</sup> We expect both of these to remain important drivers of activity in 2H24, which, when combined with the more traditional uses of CDS indices (placeholders for cash, expressing directional views, hedging), should support volumes, especially with the US election still ahead.

## Credit ETFs: Sizable y/y increases for US IG and loans and IEAC and IHYG in Europe, while US HY ETF activity continued to decline

To assess activity in the US credit ETF market, we aggregate turnover data for the five largest investment grade and high yield ETFs and the three largest loan ETFs by AUM. For European activity, we use aggregated IEAC volumes for investment grade and aggregated IHYG volumes for high yield.

In the US, high yield ETF volumes declined again, by 11% y/y (Figure 5). Lower HY ETF activity is consistent with the decline in CDX.HY volumes that was previously discussed and in line with lower HY TRS volumes (which we discuss in the next section). US high yield is the only market where we observed a decline in activity across all three portfolio products - CDX, ETFs, and TRS. We think that this is likely due to a continued shift away from macro concerns and toward single-name selection to generate returns, especially with high yield index spreads historically tight but dispersion elevated. The decline in HY ETF activity was not uniform, however. HYG volumes were down a more modest 6%, JNK and SHYG were down more than 40%, and USHY and SPHY volumes were actually up, by 20% and 381%, respectively, with the latter starting from a low base.

In contrast to the decline in overall US HY ETF volumes, investment grade and loan ETF volumes increased, by 23% and 22%, respectively (Figure 5 and Figure 6). For loans, the rebound in activity is an encouraging sign, but volumes remain nearly 40% below 2022 levels. The rebound in IG ETF volumes has taken them to a new high, with activity levels supported by increased portfolio trading activity and what appears to be a shift in investor allocations, with the two short-term ETFs in the top five by AUM, VCSH and IGSB, experiencing nearly \$3bn in outflows year to date and the two intermediate-term ETFs, VCIT and IGIB, receiving \$4.5bn of inflows (LQD, whose holdings tend to skew more intermediate to long term, had \$1.5bn of inflows). Consistent with the fund flow trend, volumes for intermediate and long-term ETFs within the top five by AUM were 11-36% higher, while volumes for the short-term ETFs were 26-29% lower.

For Europe (Figure 6), IEAC has seen significant growth, with volumes up 57% in 1H versus the same period in 2023 - and a near doubling of trading volumes since 2022. We attribute this to the continued adoption of portfolio trades in Europe, which motivates the use of ETFs to manage risks, in turn creating more liquidity in a virtuous circle. We note, however, that IEAC volumes, at an average of \$2.8bn per week, are less than an eighth of the weekly volumes of the large investment grade ETFs in the US: there is still ample space for growth. The IHYG ETF has also posted meaningful growth, up 27% y/y, balancing the reduction in Xover volumes.

## TRS: IBOXHY TRS activity continued to slide, while other TRS are up - especially QW5A (EU IG)

In the US, TRS activity picked up for both investment grade (+7%) and loans (+34%), but declined for high yield (-20%). As previously discussed, we think that HY portfolio product volumes have declined amid a greater focus on single-name selection. Even with the decline, IBOXHY TRS remains the most active TRS contract, averaging more than \$1.1bn/week in volume, while the gains in the other TRS have come from a lower base (Figure 7).

For Europe, the QW5A IG TRS has grown significantly - both compared with the lull of 1H23 and relative to 2022. The increase in trading volumes in both the IG TRS and the IEAC ETF is noteworthy in the context of broadening adaptation of portfolio trading: there has been no sign that this has cannibalized volumes in cash portfolio products; if anything, the opposite has happened. IBOXXMJA volumes are little changed versus 2023, in contrast to the continued decline in IBOXHY trading volumes.

## Single-name CDS: Flattish volumes for corporates after 2023's surge

Single-name corporate CDS volumes were mostly unchanged in 1H24 in both the Americas and Europe, after a meaningful increase in 2023 (Figure 8). European sovereign CDS volumes were also flat, while Americas sovereign volumes declined but remained 11% above 2022 levels.

For the US, anecdotal evidence suggests that bank hedging flows (whether new hedges or unwinds of existing ones) remain an important driver of corporate CDS volumes for both index and non-index names. Index arb has also been active this year, more so recently in CDX.HY than in CDX.IG, and we expect it to continue to support single-name CDS activity, especially in less liquid credits.

As in the US, European corporate CDS volumes<sup>3</sup> are mostly unchanged relative to 1H23. We expect idiosyncratic risk - in terms of both refinancing concerns and the focus on potential LBOs - to continue, allowing single-name volumes to stay at these elevated levels.

## Single-name net notionals point to increased risk-taking

Another way to gauge the activity in single-name CDS is to look at the evolution of single-name CDS net notional, or open interest.<sup>4</sup> Looking across European and US single-name CDS, net notional declined meaningfully from 2015 (and earlier) to 2018, after which net risk was sideways for years (Figure 10). This has changed with year-on-year increases in both 2023 and 2024.

We view this as a healthy sign for the single-name CDS market: in addition to volumes in general having increased since 2022, the amount of risk being taken (long and short) has generally increased as well. It would appear that reports of the single-name CDS market's death are greatly exaggerated.

Where has the increase in single-name net risk come from? Dissecting the change from July 2022 to now by region and type of single-name CDS (Figure 11), the answer is clear: it has been driven by the US single-name CDS market, with increases north of 20% across IG, HY, and financials. In contrast, European IG and financials have grown more modestly - and European HY single-name CDS have even seen a decrease in net risk.

We believe that the stagnant European HY single-name CDS market can be attributed to the near absence of index-intrinsic arbitrage trades being executed in Xover, owing to a long tail of highly illiquid entities. European HY single-name CDS's "underperformance" of the rest of the market in a net risk evolution sense is, to us, another reason that Xover index rules should be changed - either by reducing the number of entities or by changing the rules to allow more "crossover-type" names to become eligible.

In the first two quarters of 2024, ticker-level concentration told diferent stories. In 1Q24, concentration was 1.3pp lower than the historical average for the same period, but in 2Q, trading among the top 50 tickers as a percentage of all volumes grew by 0.8pp q/q (Figure 14), and trading among these top tickers accounted for 47.4% of volumes, 0.5pp higher than the seasonal average. As we have highlighted in prior notes, the Fed's hiking cycle triggered an increase in concentration in trading volumes among the most popular tickers, so 1Q24 was more of an outlier, with 2Q24 a return to the new status quo.

Despite the recent elevated ticker concentration, spread volatility has been low this year (Figure 15). Although the causal relationship is uncertain, the two factors have historically been positively correlated, likely as a result of investors' using the most liquid bonds from large capital structures when they cannot access macro products. The recent bucking of the trend comes against a backdrop of minimal spread volatility, with index-level spreads trading between 85 and 95bp for the entirety of 2Q.

Also contributing to the turnover increase is the fact that the high yield index has stayed relatively constant in size through 2024. After peaking at nearly \$1.6trn in par outstanding in late 2021 following the spate of COVID-era fallen angels, the index has shrunk every year since and ended 2023 at \$1.33trn. ## Portfolio trading

## An update on transparency

As a testament to the importance and continued growth of portfolio trading (PT), on May 15, 2023, FINRA introduced a new TRACE portfolio trade flag, which required dealers to report their portfolio trade transactions to TRACE. This new flag improved transparency in the market and also allowed us to directly test our algorithm to identify portfolio trades in TRACE. We confirmed that our methodology is well calibrated and found that more than 90% of the volumes identified by the algorithm were in fact labeled with the TRACE PT flag.

However, our algorithm is not trained to identify smaller PTs (less than 25 line items and/or less than \$10mn in total size) and PTs executed during popular reporting times (such as for example end-of-day) because the margin for error for these type of PT trades is very high. By analyzing a combination of the PTs tagged in TRACE and our Barclays proprietary PTs inquiry data, we also updated our historical PT volumes by "adding back" estimates for small trades and trades reported during popular delayed spotting times (eg, around 3:30pm or 4:00pm). While these adjustments did not have any material efect on volumes recorded during the early days of portfolio trading (2018-19), we revised more recent estimates of PT volumes upward, most notably in the IG market. However, we note that updating these historical volumes is an extremely challenging exercise, so we applied relatively conservative adjustments. Hence, volume comparisons before and after the TRACE PT flag went live in May 2023 should be interpreted with a degree of caution.

# Better together (2) - Enhancing a neutral stance through systematic views

In part 1 of this series, we showed how adding a systematic overlay to directional analyst ratings (OW/UW) can improve success rates and active returns. But what about neutral (MW) ratings? We find that similar dynamics apply. This should help analysts refine views where conviction is low.

This is a reprint of QPS and Credit Research: Better Together (2) - Enhancing a Neutral Stance Through Systematic Views, published on July 24, 2024.

## Three key points

• Fundamental and systematic views work well together: In Better Together: Fundamental and Systematic Views in Credit Investing (July 9, 2024), we used historical ratings of Barclays credit analysts and systematic strategies to demonstrate that fundamental and systematic recommendations complement each other. However, our analysis focused on analyst Overweights and Underweights only and excluded Market Weight ratings, although these represent over 50% of Barclays analyst ratings.

• A systematic overlay can help analysts refine their neutral views: Of the analyst Market Weight universe, a majority have directional systematic views. We show that systematic recommendations remain informative (57% success rate) in the universe of issuers with neutral (MW) analyst ratings and, therefore, can help analysts refine their views when their conviction is low and/or when a credit appears to lack a catalyst.

• Direction of travel matters: Following analyst upgrades (from UW to MW), the success rate and performance of systematic positive calls are higher than those of negative calls. The converse is true for analyst downgrades (from OW to MW): the conditional success rate of systematic negative calls becomes significantly higher than that of systematic positive calls. This reinforces the benefit of combining fundamental with systematic, given the information value contained in the direction of analyst rating changes (even to MW).

## Using systematic views to refine neutral ratings

In Better Together: Fundamental and Systematic Views in Credit Investing (July 9, 2024), we used historical ratings of Barclays credit analysts and of the systematic value-momentum strategy to demonstrate that fundamental and systematic recommendations complement each other. Adding a systematic overlay to analyst ratings improved analyst success rates and active returns. Our analysis focused on directional (ie, Overweight and Underweight) ratings.<sup>6</sup>

However, from the fundamental analyst's perspective, there are a variety of reasons to use a Market Weight rating. Analysts often focus on catalyst-driven situations to inform their OW and UW ratings by taking a view on "known unknowns." At any given time, these situations tend to represent a minority of an analyst's coverage universe, but often consume a majority of an analyst's time. After investing significant time in assessing potential outcomes, it is natural for an analyst to have greater conviction in these outcomes, pushing them toward either OW or UW ratings.

Moreover, analysts are often tasked with having a balanced portfolio of ratings, forcing them to sort ratings across UW, MW, and OW options. Directional calls naturally consume more time, both in forming the initial opinion and then from a surveillance perspective, as analysts monitor active portfolio positions. In the absence of a strong view, MW can be used as an analyst's default rating. Beyond this, there are some situations where an analyst may view a MW rating as the most appropriate, in order to balance upside and downside risks.

Figure 1 shows that analyst MW ratings, as an overall cohort, tend to perform roughly in line with the US investment grade index. However, as with any large basket of ratings, there are many individual tickers with MW ratings that out- or underperform.

Figure 2, borrowed from our previous publication, shows that more than 50% of issuers in the analyst universe have a neutral (MW) rating. However, such MW issuers often have directional systematic recommendations as shown in Figure 3. Indeed, each year, between 50% and 70% of issuers with a MW analyst rating have either positive (POS) or negative (NEG) systematic views.

## Performance of systematic views conditional on analyst rating change

Beyond looking at a MW rating as a static input, we want to consider whether the direction of travel of an analyst's rating matters, such as in cases where an analyst downgrades a rating to MW from OW, or upgrades a rating to MW from UW.

Intuitively, POS systematic views that coincide with analyst upgrades (UW to MW) might be more informative than those that coincide with analyst downgrades (OW to MW). Similarly, NEG systematic views are likely to be more informative when they coincide with downgrades and less informative when they coincide with upgrades.

In practice, positive (POS) or negative (NEG) systematic calls that coincide with analyst rating changes to the MW category are rare. Indeed, Figure 7 shows that many issuers tend to remain in the MW category for a considerable period of time.

Specifically for this analysis, with results shown in Figure 12, we introduce the concept of an active period, which we define as six months following the analyst rating change. We exclude conflicting systematic recommendations in active periods, meaning that we exclude an issuer from the systematic POS portfolio if it was downgraded by the analyst from OW to MW in the past six months. Issuers upgraded from UW to MW in the active period are excluded from the systematic NEG portfolio.

Finally, we double weights of issuers whose systematic ratings agree with the direction of analyst rating changes: upgraded issuers in the POS portfolio and downgraded issuers in the NEG portfolios.

## Investment Grade and High Yield Dog days of summer

The back half of summer has historically been unkind for credit, with trading volumes approaching their lowest levels in August and September. Both investment grade and high yield debt struggle to post positive excess returns in August, but after Labor Day, the tides tend to turn.

Trading volumes slow substantially toward the end of summer. For investment grade, August is the second slowest month of the year for trading volumes, with September also on the quieter side. The efect is less drastic for high yield, but both August and September have belowaverage volumes.

August tends to bring pain for credit returns, but the market usually turns after Labor Day. For investment grade, August excess returns historically hover around -0.10%, but after the holiday, September returns are around +0.12%. In high yield, August excess returns average +0.03%, but then pick back up to +0.16% in September.

CCCs tend to fare the worst in August, posting an excess return of -0.14% on average. The September rebound is substantial for CCCs, however, at +0.48%. September is also historically strong for single-Bs, which post average excess returns of +0.29%.

In investment grade, non-cyclicals fare better than cyclicals, and the front end posts the best returns. Although both cyclicals and non-cyclicals historically have negative returns in August, non-cyclicals usually turn the corner in September. The front end returns 0.05% in both August and September on average, while returns further out the curve experience large swings that are still net negative.

## Trading takes a break in the summer

As we progress through the summer, an increasing topic of conversation with clients has been seasonality and the idea of a "summer slowdown." Indeed, historical data show that not only do trading volumes decrease in the back half of the summer (see Figure 1 and Figure 2), but returns tend to sufer as well. With that in mind, some investors are derisking going into August to avoid these seasonality efects.

August is the slowest trading month for investment grade outside of December. Figure 1 shows that median August trading volumes from 2010 to 2023 have been \$410bn, roughly \$70bn less than the median monthly volume. September also tends to still be on the slower side, ranking eighth among months for volume.

Although not as drastic, August and September are slower for high yield as well. HY trading volumes do not come to as much of a grinding halt as IG, though a broader "summer slowdown" still exists, with July-September below average. The summer months trail only December (Figure 2), and the market technical still weighs on both credit performance and sentiment.

## Returns take a hit in August

The summer slowdown has historically been negative for credit returns. Since 2000, August has typically posted returns of about -0.10% and rallied less than half the time (Figure 3). Although the honor of worst month for IG excess returns goes to March, August is only marginally better. Furthermore, on a percentage basis, the IG market has rallied in August only 42% of the time, less than any other month. August supply over the past three years has been higher than the post-GFC median, at \$80bn versus \$58bn, which has not necessarily weighed on returns, but a similar surprise to the upside would likely keep spreads from rallying this time around. September has usually represented a bit of a rebound, with median returns of 0.08% and rallies a little over half the time.

The September rebound takes hold immediately after Labor Day. September returns are usually significantly better in the first 10 trading days of the month than in the rest of the month (0.11% versus 0.02%), and September returns pre-Labor Day (albeit based on very few trading days, on average) are historically negative (Figure 4). This suggests a constructive backdrop for returns as trading seats get filled following the de facto end of summer, which may come as a surprise given the negative supply technical. On average, September contributes roughly 11% to annual new issuance totals, but the relatively benign return totals for the month imply that other technicals tend to outweigh this.

## August and September are total versus excess return stories for high yield

High yield has its worst excess returns in August, and September doesn't fare much better. Although HY excess returns benefit from a better carry backdrop and thus avoid negative returns historically in any month, on a relative basis, August has a poor track record, with median excess returns of 0.03% (Figure 5). From this point of view, August seems less daunting, historically earning slightly more than the average month.

## Seasonality has mixed efects when looking more granularly

Seasonal efects are consistent across investment grade ratings, but high yield is more distinctive. From AAAs through BBBs, excess returns in August are consistently negative, but with a roughly equal bounce-back in September. Within HY, however, the seasonality efects on excess returns difer by rating. August is historically the worst month of the year for CCC debt, returning -0.14%, more than 0.8% of the average monthly excess return for the cohort. After this major drawdown for CCCs, September excess returns are, on average, over 0.6% higher, closer to the middle of the pack for monthly returns. BBs, on the other hand, face a much smaller month-by-month efect, returning 0.11% in August and 0.13% in September.

Investment grade utilities tend to sufer poor returns across both months. At the sector level within IG debt, industrials and financials tend to experience their worst or near-worst monthly returns in August, while September results in returns of at least 0.1%. August and September are both among the worst excess returns for utilities, and on an aggregated basis, the two-month stretch is over 0.2% worse than any other two-month period for the sector. The historical underperformance of IG utilities in August is likely related to duration efects. The average duration of this cohort is 8.4 years, 1.5 years longer than the broader IG index and the longest of the three major sectors, and the long-duration segment of IG typically underperforms in August (see Figure 10 below).

All sectors experience solid returns in September in high yield. Financials and utilities make up 12% and 2% of the HY market, respectively, and although these sectors tend to outperform industrials in September, this is likely more a result of single-name stories than broader secular trends within the sectors. After experiencing flat returns in August, industrials improve notably, with returns just below 0.2%.

## Data center securitization relative value

• Retail colocation data center ABS single-As (190 spread) trade in a similar-to-slightly wider context relative to retail fiber-to-the-premises (FTTP) ABS single-As (185 spread).

• Moving into assets associated with longer term contracts, there is more of a pick up in spread for wholesale data center ABS single-As (170 spread) and wholesale data center SASB CMBS single-As (155 weighted average spread of AAA, AA, and single-A tranches) compared to small cell ABS single-As (140 spread) and bulk FTTP ABS single-As (140 spread).

• At a spread of 115 for single-As, wireless tower ABS is the tightest trading digital infrastructure asset class. Wireless tower single-As also ofer the longest fundamental performance history (pre-GFC) and arguably the best liquidity profile. For capital bases that need more liquidity, wireless tower ABS single-As can make sense, but for investors who are able to give liquidity and pick spread, we think exposure to fiber ABS single-As ofers better relative value.

Broadly, we prefer CMBS over ABS data center deals because of their typically lower structural leverage metrics. However, there are key structural advantages and disadvantages to consider across ABS and CMBS deals:

• The static assets in SASB CMBS deals ofer an advantage for investors in underwriting deals as investors can express a view on specific data center profiles/locations, whereas ABS investors must rely upon the issuer's asset selection as master trusts grow. Given the hybrid CRE-digital infrastructure nature of these assets, they are not as fungible as assets like fiber (ie, there are meaningfully diferent risks based on region/market and the capabilities of each individual data center), which makes data center assets arguably less suitable for master trusts.

• However, larger ABS deals often ofer greater diversification benefits compared to CMBS that cannot be ignored. ABS deals funding large pools of assets for a business avoid some of the concentration risks inherent in SASB CMBS deals: in SASB CMBS, lenders are at risk of being exposed to an issuer's poorer performing properties, and don't benefit from the potential upside of properties an issuer is exposed to away from a singular SASB CMBS deal.

• Furthermore, ABS deals typically have rapid amortization provisions in the event of deteriorating performance, while these provisions are uncommon in CMBS structures, a negative for debt investors.

Compared to benchmark ABS and CMBS deals such as subprime auto and conduit, data center ABS and CMBS deals can ofer a meaningful spread pickup. In the near to medium term, we think that extremely strong demand technicals (ie, rising demand for cloud services, increasing internet trafic, AI growth) will continue to support data center fundamentals, and we think that allocations to data center securitizations can make sense, especially if consumer health continues to soften. But as we emphasized in the primer slide deck linked at the top of this report, these positive demand trends are already priced into data center asset valuations (and thus priced into LTVs). We think investors in the sector should stay vigilant with respect to data center demand technicals because if this demand softens, lenders will have lower cash flow streams available to pay down debt compared to competing digital infrastructure sectors.
