At first glance, this would suggest substantial room for yields to decline if the Fed is set to ease in June, given that in contrast to these historical episodes, yields have been largely range-bound over the last three months. Of course it<sup>’</sup>s important to note that 5-year yields are still 50bp lower than their peak in fall 2023, as this window misses the large rally over November and December. Overall, given that yields are significantly below their cycle peak, we look for yields to drift only modestly lower and the curve to steepen as we approach the first ease, and we have been recommending carry-efficient ways to position for this. We hold 5s/30s steepeners, but we also like mitigating the negative carry of these trades by also holding 2s/5s flatteners. We also continue to recommend 3s/5s/7s belly-richening butterflies as a positive carry trade (see Treasuries).

We also think front-end TIPS are an attractive, positive-carry way to position long-duration in the current environment, even with spot breakevens trading near 1-year wides. After adjusting for seasonality, the strength in commodity prices, and the repricing in money market curves alongside improving macro fundamentals, breakevens still appear cheap to our fair value estimate. Moreover, with the core PCE projection shifting higher while the median 2024 dot was unchanged, this implies a somewhat higher tolerance for a more gradual return to 2% inflation, presenting a more supportive backdrop for TIPS. Meanwhile, we think market-implied front-end real yields are too high relative to the Fed<sup>’</sup>s projections.

Even while OIS rates are priced for three eases this year, we estimate inflation markets are priced for core PCE to fall to roughly 2.3% q4/q4 in 2024. Thus, we hold longs in 5-year TIPS (see TIPS).

Away from the near-term path of rate policy, Powell also commented on balance sheet policy in his prepared remarks, indicating it would “be appropriate to slow the pace of balance sheet runoff fairly soon.<sup>”</sup> We believe this means they could decide to cut the monthly Treasury cap in half as soon as the May meeting, with implementation starting in mid-May. We reflect this adjustment in our updated Fed balance sheet projections in Figure 3. We still assume the Fed will want to stop balance sheet runoff when overnight RRP balances reach around \$300bn, suggesting QT is likely to continue through year-end. Powell also indicated that right now they<sup>’</sup>re mostly discussing slowing the pace of QT, and that issues like the composition and duration of the balance sheet are less pressing and can be debated later. We expect to learn more about their QT plans in the minutes to this Wednesday<sup>’</sup>s meeting in three weeks.

Hopefully we<sup>’</sup>ll get more clarity around how the Fed might adjust the caps on Treasury and MBS runoff at that point. We project Fed paydowns to edge slightly above \$20bn in the summer months, which might suggest wanting at least a \$25bn runoff cap on the MBS side given the preference for getting to an all Treasury balance sheet. Powell reiterated that goal in the presser, but stressed that the Committee doesn<sup>’</sup>t view this as an urgent consideration. We think that this aligns with market expectations that the Fed is unlikely to sell MBS in the near term, but that it could become a matter of more concern in 2025 (see Agency MBS).

More imminently, the upcoming April tax season bears watching. Typically, the tax season results in a concomitant drop in RRP and reserve balances as individuals and corporations draw down on their MMFs and bank deposits to fund their tax payments. In the decade leading up to and through 2021, the magnitude of these draw-downs around April 15 were on average around \$30bn and \$150bn for MMFs and reserves, respectively. However, in 2022 outflows were particularly sharp, reflecting meaningful capital gains taxes for the prior year (Figure 4). In contrast, 2023 saw much more muted outflows as negative returns dominated nearly every part of the financial markets the year prior. We think this year<sup>’</sup>s drawdown could reasonably fall somewhere in between \$40-140bn for MMFs and \$215- 465bn for reserves. There is risk that reserve balances bear a greater proportion of the tax-related outflows this year considering the attractive yields MMFs offer in the current interest rate environment relative to deposits. To that end, while reserves are still more than ample, it<sup>’</sup>s plausible reserves could fall to between \$3.0tn-\$3.275tn in the week following April 15, right within the range classified as the lowest comfortable level by primary dealers (see Short-Term Fixed Income).

Swap spreads are mostly wider on the week in response to shifting assumptions around the Fed's balance sheet. The earlier timing of taper alone does not materially affect our views on swap spreads. The decision of how to reinvest MBS paydowns and maturing USTs will matter, but this week brought little new information on this issue. For now, we maintain our widening bias on swap spreads across the curve. At the front end, this view is centered on upcoming negative T-bill issuance. At the long end of the curve, we remain biased towards swap spread wideners in the 30-year sector, given that spreads remain below fair value, VA hedging needs remain low, and bond fund inflows remain robust (as discuss further below). This is an important support for long end spread widening positions because bond funds - whether active or passive - are typically managed versus an index and AUM growth tends to produce somewhat price-insensitive demand for long end assets. We also maintain our widening exposure in the 10-year sector where spreads appear narrow to fair value (see Interest Rate Derivatives).

## More of the same, the same want more

Across spread products the general risk-on theme to start the year continues to persist, leading to continued spread tightening and compression across the stack. Indeed even amid heavy supply, demand has remained robust: Figure 5 shows bond fund inflows are currently tracking their most robust pace in over the past decade. More granularily, demand for IG credit has been the primary driver, with inflows into Treasury and Agg funds more in line with historical trends. Meanwhile, we look for issuance to moderate over coming months (see US Fixed Income Overview, 3/15/24). Overall, while spreads in some sectors have reached new decade tights and fundamental concerns still linger, it is hard to pinpoint what would send spreads wider absent a significant exogenous shock or the resumption of the hiking cycle.

In high grade credit, yields remain rangebound at the 5.50% sweet spot, while issuance has slowed, with \$112bn of supply MTD, and this should improve further as April has typically delivered one of the slowest months for issuance over the past four years, at around \$94bn. Meanwhile demand remains robust with 20 consecutive weeks of inflows into IG funds. Still, valuations looks rich at these levels, and we would prefer to look for relative value opportunities in this environment. We favor long Utilities versus US Banks and we think the 10s30s curve could flatten as issuance pivots back to Financials in April and long-end demand remains strong (Figure 6, see Corporates).

High-yield bond spreads tightened 9bp to the lowest level since October 2018, and are now only 4bp above their post-GFC tights of 325bp, against the backdrop of dovish Fedspeak and a solid labor market. While 1Q24 is already the most active primary market since 3Q21, we note capital market activity has moderated MTD. ABS spreads are holding firm despite healthy primary and secondary activity with new issues continuing to see robust oversubscription and pricing levels across the capital structure, sectors, and issuers. Indeed primary issuance has increased 45% oya to \$86.4bn in 1Q24 while in secondary YTD TRACE volumes are 27% higher versus the same period last year. 3-year AAA credit card ABS spreads have tightened 7bp YTD to Treasury +48bp, but we think they have more room to go given they were mostly flat at +45bp in 1Q23. On BBBs, subprime auto ABS spreads have tightened 55bp to +160bp YTD and, while at relatively tight levels historically, we do not anticipate widening pressure as issuance down the capital structure remains relatively light and spreads have more room to catch up with comparable unsecured corporates (Figure 7B subprime atolnAS f dck v). Overall, we expect to see strong demand and a steadier issuance pace in 2Q24 with ABS spreads biased flat to modestly tighter. The second half of the year remains potentially more volatile depending on rates and macroeconomic developments (and November elections) (see ABS).

While a reduction in Fed easing expectations over recent weeks have stoked more doomsaying on CRE, we see limited signs of concern in the CMBS space. To be sure, less rate cuts and higher interest rates are not good for CRE as that implies cap rates need to adjust higher to compensate investors for the higher cost of leverage, we need to keep in mind that loan spreads have tightened materially since late last year, more than compensating for the YTD selloff in rates. Some in the media point to the material refi wall that<sup>’</sup>s even bigger this year compared to last year<sup>’</sup>s due to substantial extensions as the catalyst for near-term doom. Looking through the CMBS lens, we don<sup>’</sup>t agree, as our view on rates and the recent spread rally portends easier conditions for refinancings and modifications this year versus last yea. In terms of relative value, we think there is value in seasoned AS and AA bonds versus REIT bonds, CRE CLO AAA versus BSL CLO AAAs, and FRESB A10Fs versus Freddie K A2s (see CMBS).

Finally, we have noted that capital relief trades have garnered increasing attention lately. The Fed<sup>’</sup>s statement on bank Credit Linked Notes (CLNs) in September 2023 created a buzz around the emergence of a new sector in securitized products. In the FAQ, the Fed stated that banks can use CLNs to receive capital relief on their risk-weighted assets. This announcement had long been awaited. However, it came with a caveat. The Fed imposed a \$20bn cap on the collateral a bank can transfer CLN on, and all trades are required to be approved by the appropriate Federal Reserve Bank for a bank to ultimately receive the capital relief. This has limited sector growth (see RMBS). However, CLNs are not the only capital relief trades banks can structure. Bilateral, or non-syndicated, capital relief trades, which do not count towards the \$20bn cap, have become more prevalent since the Fed<sup>’</sup>s announcement. This market is further developed in Europe. Our CLO and credit derivative colleagues provide an in-depth primer comparing SRTs, CLOs, and CSOs across a variety of metrics in a note published this week (see Three of a kind, Saul Doctor, 3/21/24).

In terms of regional flows, Euro-area investors bought \$33.6bn of Treasuries and \$9.4bn of Corporates in January, representing roughly half of net purchases in those two product types over the month. Indeed, Figure 10 illustrates that Euro-area investors have remained an important foreign pillar for US fixed income demand in recent months and we think this is likely to persist, as the relative value of US fixed income remains attractive (see HG Foreign Demand Monitor, Nathan Rosenbaum, 3/18/24). Investors domicilied in the Cayman Islands and the United Kingdom purchased \$17.5bn and \$11.8bn, respectively, representing the largest monthly purchases in approximately a year and we have previously noted that demand from these domiciles can often be representative of levered flows (see US Treasury Market Daily, Jay Barry, 3/19/24).

Turning to flows out of Asia, Japanese investors net purchased \$12.4bn of Treasuries in January, the strongest month of buying since October 2023. Looking ahead, the decision by the BoJ to exit NIRP and dismantle YCC this week raises the risk for higher JGB yields and a steeper Japanese sovereign curve, which could be a negative impulse for US fixed income demand. However, Japanese investors will likely continue to engage in the US IG credit market, as the depth and liquidity of the US corporate debt market dwarfs the Japanese market. Indeed we have seen robust inflows into corporate only funds domiciled in Japan while overnight TRACE flows, which we think are representative of Japanese buying, has been very strong recently with an average \$333mn/day in March (see BoJ policy shifts and US HG Credit: An update from the Land of the (Eventually) Rising Yield, Eric Rosenbaum, 3/20/24). Meanwhile China represented the largest net seller of Treasuries in January, shedding \$21.7bn over the month, the largest monthly net sale since March 2022. This selling comes amid a strong push for gold accumulation, which our Chinese strategists believe is motivated by FX reserve diversification, risk mitigation, and inflation hedging. Indeed February represented the sixteenth consecutive month of gold purchases (see China: FX reserves beat on strong exports, Tingtin Ge, 3/7/24).

Away from the broad foreign demand landscape, we note an important upcoming change in the data provided by the Treasury International Capital system, upon which we rely heavily for our analysis. Remember that in the spring of 2023 we saw the discontinuation of TIC Form S and the expansion of TIC Form SLT such that data collection of foreign holdings, transactions, and changes in fair value of domestic securities were consolidated into a single accounting framework and responsibility shifted away from traders (such as broker-dealers and principal trading firms) to end-users (such as investors, custodians, or issuers). Up until this point data on changes in fair value were not disclosed, leaving us to either rely solely on transaction volumes or, in the case of mortgages where transactions do not account for paydowns, make price-adjusted flow assessments from changes in the market value of holdings. However starting with the February 2024 data (to be released mid-April) Treasury will disclose this valuation data which reflect changes in the prices of securities held, with a historical look back to February 2023. While the introduction of this data will not completely explain the difference between net transaction and changes in holdings (as TIC still suffers from a reporting bias such that foreign-to-foreign transactions or transfers in domestic securities are not reported), the reporting of valuation changes will significantly close this gap and allow us to better approximate true foreign flows without necessarily leaning on price-adjustments which rely on likely too-broad assumptions.
