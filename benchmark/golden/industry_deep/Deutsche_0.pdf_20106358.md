## Introduction

When we first adopted a mild recession as our baseline forecast, a key element of our view was that, with an economy far from the Fed's objectives, the history of central bank-induced disinflations showed the path to a soft landing was narrow (see the latest USMPF paper here "Managing disinflations"). That history, plus the plethora of historically reliable recession indicators that began to flash red, led us to maintain a mild recession as a base case heading into 2024.

However, starting last summer, we began to flag upside risks to this expectation, noting that soft landing prospects were beginning to improve materially ("Recession or soft landing: Is disinflation different this time?"). Over the course of 2023, and especially over the back half of the year, the economy made quick progress on bringing the labor market into better balance and inflation closer to target while growth remained sturdy and financial conditions supportive. This performance was as good as the Fed could have hoped for when they kicked off their aggressive tightening cycle nearly two years ago.

We now see virtuous dynamics at play that could extend these positive developments through this year. As a result, we now expect the economy to avert a mild recession in 2024. This note details our updated forecasts.

## Reasons for optimism: Why this time looks different

In recent quarters we viewed the economy as slowing into a variety of headwinds that risked a temporary stalling out or decline in economic activity. Those headwinds were numerous: Financial and credit conditions had tightened sharply in response to the Fed's aggressive hikes, banking stresses and the spike in term premia. Households had faced pressures from higher interest rates, tight lending conditions, the return of student debt payments, and persistently elevated prices that led to downbeat sentiment and soft real income growth. Housing activity was still frozen with mortgage rates lingering near decade highs. Firm capex expectations were slipping under the weight of subdued global manufacturing sentiment. This backdrop made us cautious about the outlook.

Reasons have emerged for more optimism on each of these fronts in recent months. The pace of credit condition tightening has eased noticeably and financial conditions have become considerably more supportive (Figures 1 and 2). Households have weathered these conditions and the return of student debt payments and sentiment has rebounded as inflation declined, real income growth picked up, and risk assets remain buoyant (Figure 3). Lower mortgage rates have led to incipient signs of a pickup in mortgage purchase applications, housing demand and home sales. Finally, recent ISMs and PMIs show evidence that business sentiment may be in the early stages of recovering (Figure 4).

## Growth: Settling down after a sizzling 2023

As of the advance Q4 real GDP release, inflation-adjusted output expanded 3.1% (Q4/Q4) in 2023, a remarkable performance in light of banking sector stresses in the beginning of the year and the highest average nominal policy rate since 2000. Though the economy continues to face several headwinds – namely, still-tight credit conditions, rising consumer delinquency rates and a slowing labor market – the resilience to date points to a more benign slowdown in 2024 than we had previously projected. In turn, we now expect real GDP to expand 1.9% this year (Q4/ Q4), up from 0.3% previously. Our 2025 and 2026 forecasts remain little changed as we continue to expect inflation-adjusted output to grow around 2.2% for each of those years as the Fed eases policy back toward a neutral setting.

The reason for our upgraded outlook is the surprising strength of consumer spending which ended 2023 up 2.6% (Q4/Q4). Though the labor market is clearly slowing, real income growth remains healthy enough to avoid the sharp slowdown we had previously expected in H1 of this year (Figure 5). Indeed, most of the upgrade to our forecast is due to an upward revision to goods spending. To be sure, we still expect a notably diminished pace of goods expenditures (2.0% in 2024 versus 3.5% in 2023) with services spending projected to pick up slightly (2.3% vs. 2.2% in 2023). However, relative to our prior view that goods spending would eventually revert to its pre-Covid trend, the elevated level of goods as a share of overall spending appears to be the "new normal" (Figure 6). Hence, we now see real PCE growing a healthy 2.2% in 2024, up from a prior projection of 0.1%. Our 2025 and 2026 forecasts for inflation-adjusted consumer expenditures growth remain roughly unchanged at 2.3%.

Another sector where we have upgraded our view is residential investment. The housing sector was the first to feel the effects of the Fed's historically rapid tightening campaign as real residential investment declined a little over 20% from the Q1 2021 peak to the Q2 2023 trough. Though we previously projected slight growth for housing activity over the coming quarters, the Fed's dovish pivot and subsequent decline in mortgage rates argues for a stronger rebound in 2024. Hence, we now expect real residential investment to grow 6.0% (Q4/Q4) in 2024 compared to our prior projection of just under 1%. Given structural underinvestment in housing in the last cycle, we expect the sector to continue to outpace overall GDP growth in 2025 and 2026, growing 3.0% and 2.6% (Q4/Q4) respectively.

One sector of the economy that we remain less sanguine on is business fixed investment (capex), at least in the near term. Leading indicators of capex spending remain depressed and lending conditions remain tight (Figures 8 and 9). In addition, there is some potential for elevated policy uncertainty before the election to weigh on capex decisions ahead of that event. The upshot is that we project real nonresidential fixed investment to rise by only 1.1% (Q4/Q4) in 2024 (versus 4.1% in 2023) with the majority of the gain driven by a 2.4% uptick in intellectual property products (IPP). That said, one near-term upside risk to our forecast is the current tax package being considered by Congress. This bi-partisan proposal includes three provisions that temporarily increase business deductions through the end of calendar year 2025. Hence, there could be some pull forward of capex plans if the bill passes Congress. Even if the bill passes – saving business a roughly \$125bn in FY 2024 and \$60bn FY 2025 – the impact is likely to be minimal in terms of overall GDP growth, as shown by a recent scoring of the proposal by the Joint Committee on Taxation<sup>1</sup> .

The fiscal outlook is also highly uncertain given that Congress has yet to pass a FY 2024 budget. As we wrote last year (see "Gauging the size of the fiscal purse when power over it is paralyzed"), we project the FY 2024 deficit to range between \$1.6trn and \$1.9trn with our modal forecast being roughly \$1.7trn. Recent data has continued to point to a supportive fiscal posture, with the government sector contributing roughly three-quarters of a percentage point to real GDP growth in 2023, a level typically only seen around recessions (Figure 10). However, widelyfollowed estimates of the fiscal impulse point to a roughly 50bps drag on real GDP growth through Q4 of this year (Figure 11). To be sure, the \$1.1trn unexpected blow out of the deficit last year – largely due to one-off factors that will not be repeated this year – likely contributed to stronger-than-anticipated growth in 2023. Indeed, if Congress were to fail to agree upon a budget in early March, resulting in a federal government shutdown, the drag on growth could be substantially larger simply due to the how the BEA treats a government shutdown in the NIPA accounts.

Indeed, the fiscal outlook only becomes more clouded as we approach the November election. As we noted in our preview (see "2024 US election overview"), in a time of elevated fiscal deficits and interest rates, the policy stakes are high – particularly with the 2017 Tax Cut and Jobs Act (TCJA) beginning to expire in 2025.

The CBO estimates that keeping the TCJA in place would add roughly \$3 trillion to deficits through 2033. Indeed, with the Social Security trust fund projected to run out in 2033, fiscal policy decisions over the next decade only become more difficult.

Our updated forecasts are now more optimistic than alternatives, particularly in the first half of 2024, where consensus continues to anticipate a material slowdown (Figure 12). Our full year growth projection of 1.9%, is also meaningfully above the Fed's latest projection of 1.4% from the December Summary of Economic Projections (SEP). ## Labor market: Better balanced but still booming

Consistent with our more optimistic growth outlook, we now anticipate that the labor market will avoid recessionary dynamics and that the unemployment rate will only rise by a few tenths in 2024.

Entering 2023, there was considerable uncertainty about how the labor market would evolve following the Fed's aggressive tightening cycle. Despite these challenges, the labor market outcome over the past year was the best possible scenario. As relationships like the Beveridge curve show, imbalances were corrected primarily by lower demand as supply improved (Figure 14), and measures of labor market slack returned towards levels observed before the pandemic (Figure 15), taking pressure off of the Fed to be hawkish for labor market reasons. All of this occurred without a sharp deceleration in payrolls or sustained rise in layoffs. The labor market thus transitioned with limited pain from one Chair Powell labeled as tight to an "unhealthy" degree to one that looks tight but more historically normal.

We now expect this resilient labor market picture to continue in 2024. Our updated projections see the unemployment rate rising to only 4.1% in 2024, down from a prior peak of 4.6% (Figure 16). The expected mild rise in unemployment from current levels is driven both by a slowdown in payroll gains to more sustainable levels and a further modest increase in the labor force participation rate over the next year. These updated forecasts are now broadly in line with alternative projections from the Fed and Bloomberg consensus.

![](images/8bea5d619f5c5ad21997c24d53eff29e8f2631ee50ed2630c669e74f6e592e54.jpg)

Some elements of the labor market picture that worried us have improved recently. For example, job gains broadened most recently with the diffusion indexes reversing most or all of the decline over the past year (Figure 17). Temp help workers, which can tend to be a leading indicator for the overall trajectory of payroll gains, also returned to a positive reading for the first time in nearly two years (Figure 18). Finally, recent payroll numbers have actually been revised higher on balance, with the 6m average revision positive for the first time in a year (19).

That said, some of these positive developments were isolated to the latest jobs report, which is noisy and prone to revision. This argues for some caution in extrapolating recent strength. Indeed, there remain some reasons to be cautious on the labor market outlook. For example, recent data show that the private hiring rate has fallen to the lowest level since early 2014 (Figure 20). By extension, sturdy job gains are increasingly reliant on a historically low layoff rate. If the layoff rate were simply to normalize to 2019 levels, private payroll gains through December would have been close to zero or even negative (Figure 21).

Despite these lingering concerns, we anticipate that recent positive growth dynamics can also improve some of the worrying elements of the labor market. With housing getting a lift from lower mortgage rates, employment in construction and other housing-related sectors could remain better supported over the next year. Some modest acceleration in global manufacturing activity, as suggested by the PMIs, could also help to broaden employment gains in those sectors. As such, while there have been some worrying elements to the labor market picture, we anticipate that these signals could turn more positive over the course of the year as the labor market trends closer to its equilibrium levels.

## Inflation: All the Fed does is WIN, WIN, WIN, no matter what

## 2023 in review

The second half of 2023 was chock full of good news on the inflation front which has allowed the Fed to pivot away from its tightening bias. Indeed, four of the six core PCE prints in H2 were actually below 2% annualized, while the six-month annualized rate dipped below 2% (1.9% in both November and December) for the first time since September 2020 (Figure 22).

![](images/da5a73893c74de7bcd8b8ee699c3165e2ece37ad6e09338f19bb7c742cbda677.jpg)
Source : BEA, Haver Analytics, Deutsche Bank

That being said, with the year-over-year rate still almost a percentage point above target, Chair Powell emphasized that this progress is not yet enough to give Fed officials full confidence that inflation is moving sustainably to 2%. While the Fed is most concerned with the overall headline or core number for inflation, the composition of the recent inflation prints is one example for why the Committee has not achieved the desired level of confidence.

Looking at core goods, this is where most of the progress on disinflation has occurred, arguably too much progress to be sustained (Figure 23). Core goods prices fell by 2.5% annualized in the second half of 2023, the fastest rate in over 20 years. These price declines were pretty broad-based within goods. For example, furnishings and durable household equipment prices fell by 3.5% annualized, used motor vehicle prices fell by 7.4% annualized, and clothing and footwear prices fell by 3.5% annualized over the second half of the year (Figure 24). Medical goods and new motor vehicle prices are the only major categories actually adding to goods inflation, but even those pockets of strength are relatively modest compared to history (Figure 25).

## DB baseline forecasts and risks

In terms of our forecast going forward into 2024 and beyond, our baseline is that progress on disinflation will continue, with the Fed continuing to close in on its target. Specifically, we expect core PCE to end 2024 at 2.2% (Q4/Q4), before declining by a tenth to 2.1% by the end of 2025, and finally converging to the Fed's 2% target by the end of 2026 (Figure 28). Our forecasts are a little more optimistic than consensus, which is in turn a little more optimistic than the Fed's median projection from December (Figure 29). Core CPI should follow a similar trajectory; 2.6%, 2.5% and 2.4% in 2024-2026. The analogous numbers for headline are 1.8%, 2.0%, and 2.0% for PCE and 2.2%, 2.5%, and 2.4% for CPI. While these are our baseline forecasts, it is important to note that there are several risks around them, which are worth discussing given that the Fed "remains highly attentive to inflation risks."

The first is upside risks to core goods. As mentioned above, core goods deflation has been particularly extreme, so some normalization is to be expected. Indeed, we expect that the run rate for core commodities in the CPI should pick up from an average of -20bps a month in the second half of 2023 to -7bps per month in Q1 and to roughly unchanged through Q2. However, recent geopolitical events and disruptions to shipping in the Red Sea have increased shipping costs (Figure 30). This could increase pressures on supply chains, which could, in turn, result in a pickup in goods inflation above and beyond the normalization that we have penciled in. In terms of timing, increases in shipping rates seem to usually take about seven months (on a year-over-year basis), so this could begin to impact goods prices towards the middle of the year. We are, however, cautious about building this into the forecast at the moment, as there are reasons to think the passthrough will be more muted today than during the pandemic (e.g., less robust consumer demand).

Another set of risks to our forecast regards rental inflation. As mentioned above, the monthly run rates for rental inflation have moved sideways or even slightly upwards over the last six months. Our base case, as is the Fed's, is that slower increases in asking rents will filter their way into the CPI measures. Our forecast has primary rents decelerating from a run rate of 47bps a month on average in the second half of 2023 to 38bps in Q1 and 21bps in Q2. We have a similar trajectory for owners' equivalent rents (OER), with the average monthly increase slowing from 47bps a month in H2 2023, to 39bps in Q1, and 26bps in Q2. On one hand, there are upside risks to our near term inflation forecasts to the extent that rental inflation remains sticky and continues to lag their expected decline. That being said, there could be some downside risks towards the end of 2024 into 2025 given the collapse in the 2023 Q4 reading of the Cleveland Fed's New Tenant Repeat Rent Index (Figure 31). We would, however, express some caution in extrapolating this relationship, in part because the Cleveland Fed's gauge has tended to be revised higher in recent quarters.

In terms of core services excluding housing, there are a myriad of uncertainties. Focusing first on medical services, health insurance should continue to be a boost to CPI through at least April, when the BLS will incorporate more health insurance data. Our base case is that the April update will result in minimal changes and that health insurance prices will continue to grow by about 1% a month. To the extent that the April health insurance print surprises one way or the other, that would impact our outlook from May to September. It is important to note that this would not impact views on core PCE.

However, there are some uncertainties about the healthcare inflation outlook that would impact core PCE. We have previously flagged some upside to health care PCE inflation driven by the lead from past strengthening of wage inflation for hospital workers (Figure 32). That being said, the Medicare Physician Fee Schedule conversion factor for 2024 is set to fall by 3.4%, which is a downside risk for physician services, given that similar decreases in the conversion factor occurred in January of 2022 and 2023 and impacted those month's PCE readings (Figure 33). Hints on both of these risks would be evident in the January PPI data on February 16.

With respect to those core services other than housing and healthcare, the main driver is the tightness of the labor market and any resultant wage pressures. There are some differences between the CPI and PCE for this category, but for CPI we expect core services excluding housing and healthcare to decelerate from averaging 49bps a month in the second half of 2023 to 30bps in Q1 and 22bps in Q2.

One near-term upside risk specific to core PCE is from portfolio management and investment advise. We had flagged some upside risk to this category in December from the November equity rally given that it tends to follow moves in equities with a one month lag. While this category did post a roughly 2% gain, this was lower than we would have expected given the almost 9% November surge in the S&P 500 (Figure 34). Indeed, we could see another strong print in the January data given December's 4.4% gain in equities and the potential for some catchup from November's relative miss. As with healthcare, this would first show up in the January PPI data.

All together, our baseline forecast has inflation returning close to the Fed's target by year end, though with significant uncertainties around that outcome. Considering the numerous risks to the baseline view, it may not be surprising that the FOMC felt that it needed to specify in the January FOMC statement that it has not reached a sufficient level of confidence that inflation is sustainably heading down to its 2% target. However, with some of the aforementioned uncertainties set to resolve themselves in the near-term, the next couple months will be crucial in terms of the inflation outlook for the Fed.

## Fed: "Methodically" refilling the punchbowl

We continue to see scope for the Fed to cut rates this year, even with an improved growth outlook that avoids a mild recession. In contrast to our prior view, which anticipated that the Fed would cut rates more aggressively in order to actively ease policy in response to a weakening economy, we now expect soft-landing reductions to be motivated by concerns about passively overtightening. In other words, the Fed will look to normalize monetary policy to avoid jeopardizing the soft landing.

## Rate cuts: A shallower cycle

Following the January FOMC meeting and the strong January jobs report, we have maintained our baseline call for the first rate cut to occur in June. However, with an improved labor market and growth backdrop we now think that the Fed will deliver only 100bps of cuts over 2024, 75bps less than our prior expectation. After June, we see the Fed cutting by 25bps increments at the July meeting and then switching to a quarterly pace of reductions until the fed funds rate reaches 3.6%, a level we think is close to neutral.

The timing of the first rate cut is still uncertain. As noted earlier, we expect some modest uplift in near-term inflation prints, which could create enough caution by the Fed to not cut rates at the March and May FOMC meetings, particularly if coupled with sturdy growth and labor market outcomes and supportive financial conditions. As Chair Powell noted in his press conference, it is not currently the Committee's expectation to cut rates in March, as they seek greater confidence that inflation is trending sustainably to 2%. These comments clearly raised the bar for a March cut. He also referred to the decision to cut rates as "highly consequential", suggesting that the Fed is unlikely to rush to that decision without compelling reasons.

Beyond the timing of the first cut, the tactics around how to reduce rates are still uncertain. One question is will the Fed cut rates in larger than 25bps increments during the easing cycle. As we wrote recently, we think this is unlikely under a soft landing scenario (see "What could make the Fed go 50?"). This is consistent with Governor Waller's recent framing of Fed rate cuts, which he state would proceed "methodically and carefully". Larger cuts (e.g., 50bps increments) thus likely require that the labor market weakens more than anticipated, convincing the Fed they need to actively ease the policy stance. This outcome is possible but is no longer our base case.

A separate tactical question is whether the Fed will begin cutting at every meeting or at a slower pace. In recent work on this topic, we argued that starting with 25bps at each meeting is most likely ("How soon and how far? Alternative '24 cutting paths" and "The real deal about passive policy tightening") as it would ensure that policy does not quickly become too restrictive as inflation recedes (Figure 27). Another argument for starting faster is that, with policy restrictive in the Fed's view, it would make sense to slow the pace of cuts as they approach neutral, moving symmetrically to the tightening cycle.

Figure 36: Real monetary policy stance would hit historically restrictive levels on core PCE without cuts
![](images/371f8c427da0fc8390cb2a399addcf61425d752fbb42a199f605c3b3aacdc8b9.jpg)

On the other hand, there are arguments for starting more slowly. Financial conditions are supportive and cutting rates quicker could lead to an undesirable further easing as the Fed reinforces market pricing. In addition, the level of r-star is highly uncertain and estimates would likely rise in a soft landing scenario. It could therefore make sense to start more slowly if the destination is unknown. In addition, there is the potential for meaningful fiscal stimulus next year after the election, which could turn quicker rate cuts this year into a policy mistake next year. Finally, giving up valuable policy space by preemptively easing could limit the Fed's ability to respond to future adverse shocks. As such, while we assume the Fed would want to start with 25bps rate cuts at every meeting, we see reasonable arguments for starting slower and moving even more methodically.

As part of our Fed path revamp, we have also modestly upgraded our view on r-star. For some time we have argued that r-star has risen since before the pandemic, noting that a variety of estimates pointed to a range of 1-1.5% (see "How positive r(\*) you?"). With the economy continuing to prove resilient to the monetary tightening to date, we now see r-star likely sitting in the upper end of that range, consistent with a nominal neutral rate of 3.5%, rather than the bottom of that range. We would not rule out an even higher level for the neutral rate if growth continues to outperform. In this scenario, a 4% nominal neutral rate could be plausible. In fact, several of our estimates are close to that level, as is the estimate from the NY Fed staff's DSGE model.

## QT: Slowing down, but not necessarily stopping, in '24

Our new Fed view has important implications for the wind down of QT. Chair Powell and his colleagues have been clear that the motivation for reducing rates matters for balance sheet policy. If rate cuts are occurring under a soft landing scenario where the motivation is to normalize the policy rate, then QT can continue alongside rate cuts. In this scenario, both tools – rates and the balance sheet - are being normalized and there is no tension. However, if rate cuts are aimed at actively easing the policy stance because growth has slowed more than desired and the labor market has weakened more noticeably, then QT likely ends. In this scenario the two tools could be viewed as working at cross purposes, and the Fed would want to avoid sending mixed signals about the intended policy stance.

With the Fed now cutting rates to normalize policy in our baseline, we no longer expect QT to stop alongside rate cuts. Adjustments to QT parameters will thus be determined by how the Fed views the proximity of reserves to the ample level. Recent Fed communications have indicated a divergence of views about the urgency of slowing the pace of QT in the coming months. For example, Dallas Fed President Logan indicated her preference to slow QT when ON RRP is drawn down to "low levels", a threshold that could be reached around mid year. However, other officials, including NY Fed President Williams and Governor Waller, expressed less urgency around such an adjustment.

At the January FOMC meeting, Powell stated that officials would have an "in-depth discussion" on the balance sheet topic in March, which should most likely lead to a consensus on when and how to slow the pace of QT this year by the May meeting. Our baseline is that the Fed is likely to slow the pace of QT in June with QT continuing into 2025, but we expect to learn a lot more around the tactics for slowing and ultimately ending QT over the coming months.
