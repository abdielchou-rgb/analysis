## March 2024 Asia Trip Takeaways

Tom O'Malley, BCI, US | Scott Fessler, BCI, US | William Levy, BCI, US | Kyle Bleustein, BCI, US Simon Coles, CFA, Barclays, UK | Tim Long, BCI, US

Excerpted from Global Semiconductors & Hardware: March 2024 Asia Trip Takeaways, published on March 7, 2024

We spent the week speaking with ODMs, supply chain partners, and semi companies in Asia. Below are our key takeaways.

AI ASPs Driving Better July as Supply Chain Ramps Capacity: NVDA sold 1.4M H100 chips in CY23. Q4 volumes of H100 at \~720K moving to \~810K in Q1. These volumes should improve to \~850K in Q2, netting unit growth of \~5% seq. Test capacity is increasing by >30% by the end of Q2/Q3 from 300K to 400K per month, but will be gradual. In the 2H, total capacity for H/B series is moving from 400K to 500K/month. The supply chain hasn’t gotten initial forecasts for B100, but indicated that testing normally takes six months and they began in Q1. ODMs don’t expect material B100 shipments until late Q4 CY24/early Q1 CY25. In all, it seems like the B100 is coming a bit later than initial expectations of Q3 volumes. L-series, which previously was expected at 300K in Q4 and 600K in Q1 is now expected at \~100K per month and likely is more robust in the 2H as NVDA tries to find ways to sell to China. In aggregate, these unit numbers imply another \$1-2B of upside in April and potentially \$2B+ to July depending on the ASP uplift assumed for the H200.

AMD Higher Unit Share Forecast: ODMs talked about \~500K volume for AMD vs. \~4M for NVDA in CY24, representing 11% unit share for AMD. That number seemed a bit robust at first blush, but ODMs increasingly talked about new designs expected to launch 2H this year.

Traditional Server Downtick, AI Server Uptick: AI server market now expected to grow DDs with robust AI growth across the supply chain in CY24. AI server ASP is 20-25x general purpose server. Traditional server initially had expectations for strong DD growth, but now seems more like LSD at best for CY24. By Q, Q2 is expected up strong DD after a weaker Q1. Currently, Sapphire Rapids is used in almost all AI deployments, but as the market moves to Grace/Hopper it should see a modest headwind. Grace/Hopper form factor is 8 1 or 2 CPUs with 2 GPUs in a single blade server, 9 switches in the middle of the rack, and another 8 servers below. In aggregate, this is potentially 32 CPUs in a single rack. We think volume production of this type of deployment will be limited. ODMs indicated that Granite Rapids would be late CQ4 2024 at the earliest and more likely CQ1 2025, which is a delay.

Q2 PCs Likely a Bit Weaker, Adding Risk to Unchanged Full Year: In Q1, ODMs seem in-line down DD, but Q2 seems only up HSD vs. expectations of strong DD. The 1H of the year is likely down HSD H/H. Expectations for the full year remain at LSD, but the weaker start makes it much harder to get to the full year. AI PC expected at 1-2% of units in CY24 (AI PC described as APU), which is 4M vs. INTC 40M. Pricing on AI PC expected to start around \$800, which is below Gaming PCs at \$1000+. 2H PC sales are expected to benefit from a Windows refresh, but this is likely more geared towards Q4.

Updates on AI Chips/ASICs/Switch Silicon: GUC is making ASIC for Cobalt and Maya for MSFT with the ramp starting in Q2 and contributing more materially in '25. INTC 18A interaction with MSFT largely assumed to be Cobalt. This would mean that production would move away from TSMC, which seems unlikely. Gaudi 2 volumes seem well below initial \$1-2B pipeline and likely are in low hundreds of millions in aggregate. Marvell Innovium platform looking to intersect the market at 800G but feedback is that the product is still too expensive for current performance.

## All Aboard the Equities Express

Venu Krishna, CFA, BCI, US | Japinder Chawla, CFA, BCI, US | Rex Feng, BCI, US | Madhuresh Rai, CFA, BCI, US | Young Chen, BCI, US

Excerpted from Long & Short of It: All Aboard the Equities Express, published on March 5, 2024

LO equity exposure rises to a post-COVID record; global macro HFs flip from short equities to long; CTAs near max-long EU equities and indiscriminately long US equities; retail options activity suggest heavier upside buying vs. institutional; retail flows to MM slowing; both real and fast money heavily OW Tech vs. all else.

The world goes long equities. The scramble for equity exposure picked up steam over the last month. Real money investors continue to quickly ratchet up their equity positions, pushing long-only institutional equity exposure to a post-COVID record. Long equity futures positions are hovering near 3-year highs. Global macro hedge funds completely unwound 3Q23 equity shorts and are now quite long equities, joining their multi-strategy equity HF counterparts, which maintain the long equity exposure they built up over 2023.

Systematic positioning in equities remains stretched, while China shorts also pared back: CTAs appear to be near max-long EU equities, more so than their US counterparts, which is in line with their YTD outperformance that has almost gone unnoticed amidst the Big Tech/AI euphoria (see 'Follow the 'magnificent' leaders). Notably, although CTAs are still short Chinese equities, shorts have been pared back significantly and the funds appear to be least short since August last year. Interestingly, within the US, they appear indiscriminately long SPX, NDX (Tech) and RTY (small caps), seemingly subscribing to the broadening of the rally thesis. On the other hand, CTAs have likely turned short US Treasuries amid a resilient US economy and paring back of rate cut expectations. Coming to Risk Parity and Vol Control, equity allocation is quite elevated but there is arguably room to add further.

Institutional investors push down + retail pull up = skew near 20yr flatness: while S&P skew has been relentlessly flattening YTD, NVDA blockbuster earnings catalyzed the skew capitulation to levels hardly seen in the past two decades, as earnings stole the Fed's thunder in driving stocks. Pressure for both downside + upside skew suggests a mix of lighter protection buying + pickup in upside demand, as the put-to-call OI ratio remains historically balanced. Drilling further, retail investors surpassed institutional ones as the most active option users for the first time in 1.5 years, suggesting heavier upside buying from the former, and lighter protection buying from the latter.

Retail inflows to money markets slowing, with IG credit (and to a lesser extent equities) seeing the benefits. Retail equity flows turned positive but are still dwarfed by inflows to credit; DM and specifically US credit remains the heavy favorite while US government bond funds saw muted overall flows. Retail money market inflows remain positive but have tapered of on a rolling monthly basis. Notably, retail flows to US IG credit funds have picked up at the same time that money market inflows have downshifted. This looks to be consistent with past trends, whereby drawdowns on money market allocations tend to favor credit over equities after yields on cash relative to risky assets hit historically wide levels.

Funding the Tech overweight with (almost) everything else. MFs and HFs are almost in lockstep as far as sector preferences. Tech, Energy and Industrials are the biggest consensus overweights, while Healthcare has become the biggest consensus underweight after a significant cutback in exposure over 4Q. Two exceptions: 1) HFs went overweight the consumer complex in 4Q while MFs remain underweight both Discretionary and Staples, and; 2) HFs were quicker to chase the upside in Big Tech relative to mutual funds, continuously extending their overweight in contrast to real money investors who trimmed Big Tech exposure in 3Q23 before re-adding to the overweight to close out the year.

Outside of equities, some unwind of 2023 favorites. Asset managers built up extremely long 10Y Treasury futures positions over the last year, and slightly unwound some of this exposure over the last month. Positioning in long USD speculative futures is back to neutral as markets await better clarity on the path forward for the Fed, while long speculative positions in AUD futures are being trimmed amid weak China data.

Europe is also enjoying a brightening economic outlook, with its economic surprises (CESI) rebounding recently, while they are peaking in the US. We have already seen a mild pick up in European soft data indicators like PMI ytd, albeit from depressed level, and we think the positive momentum can extend into H2 (see Reflation fizz – March Chart Pack, 6 Mar). Policy may also be about to get more supportive, as if ECB ends up cutting rates more than the Fed in H2, as per our economists' view. This would likely at least cap material EUR upside despite the boost from stronger growth. This is consistent with our FX strategist's view that EURUSD should trade in a 1.00-1.10 range, absent a large improvement in China (see  Don't miss the forest for the trees, 3rd Mar). In short, we see the improving growth/policy mix for Europe giving legs to the reflation trade, while low valuation and positioning provide some downside cushion.

Credit has been soft, driven by record issuance rather than credit risk. CCCs have performed the best and A industrials the worst. The US economy remains robust. The latest data indicate that US growth remains above trend. The most recent ISM data imply a diference in new orders between manufacturing and services: contracting and weaker than expected for the former but close to the best in a year and higher than expected for the latter.

In particular, the consumer is remarkably resilient. Our research team finds that US consumers remain a pillar of the economy, with spending patterns shifting toward services (which aligns with the ISM new orders data). There are some signs that borrowers are stretched, but more data are needed to determine if they indicate material cracks (see here).

CEOs are the most constructive about the US economy since mid-2022. That is the result from the latest CEO survey by the Business Roundtable published earlier this week. According to the chair, "This quarter's survey results underscore the resiliency of the U.S. economy and suggest accelerating economic activity over the next six months." (see here).

Inflation data are noisy, but lower inflation remains the base case. CPI and PCE have both been firm, with some potentially worrisome data in the core and supercore subsets. However, inflation data have been noisy. For instance, the latest ISM Services Prices Paid data came back down from very high levels in January. This tends to be a good predictor of supercore inflation (see Figure 6).

We expect central banks to stay put in March, except for the BoJ. The Fed is likely to keep rates unchanged at the next FOMC meeting (March 20) given the combination of robust growth and noisy inflation data. Powell did not indicate otherwise in his testimony to Congress this week. Interestingly, we will have an update of the dot plot at this meeting. The ECB decided to stay put this week, and we expect the BoE (March 21) to do the same. However, we believe that the BoJ (March 19) will exit NIRP in March rather than April (see here).

Credit is a bit softer, driven by issuance and not by credit risk issues. US investment grade cash has widened by 9bp, to 97bp, since reaching two-year+ tights on February 22. At the same time, high yield cash has also widened by 9bp, to 315bp. This corresponds to an 18bp outperformance for HY adjusting for beta. The CDX indices have outperformed cash over this period: CDX IG is unchanged at 51bp and CDX HY is 4bp tighter at 332bp.

CCCs are the best performers across credit. CCCs have tightened by 59bp since January 17, which was the last time investment grade cash was at its current level. Note that this is a bit of a catch-up, as CCCs had been lagging materially. During this period, the lower the rating, the better the performance has been in both absolute and beta-adjusted terms. The relative performance since then clearly implies that the credit softness is driven more by record supply in higher-quality IG than worries about credit risk: year-to-date single-A issuance is the heaviest since 2016 in relative terms, and single-A industrials have fared the worst.

We believe that credit yield demand remains strong and that issuance should slow, helping spreads stay tight. Conversations with investors continue to indicate that yields of 5.3% in IG and 7.8% in HY remain attractive even though spreads are tight. In the short term, the market expects issuance to slow soon from the hardly sustainable year-to-date pace, which would mean positive technicals again.

We update our sector views across investment grade and high yield post-earnings after this beta compression. In IG, we downgrade banks and tobacco from Overweight to Market Weight (see IG section). In HY, we downgrade electric utilities from Overweight to Market Weight (see HY section).

## Economic Outlook

## Workers of the world, sit tight!

Christian Keller, Barclays, UK | Mark Cus Babic, Barclays, UK | Akash Utsav, Barclays, UK

Excerpted from Global Economics Weekly, published on March 8, 2024

Labour markets are the focus of central banks globally. The ECB believes only by June will it know enough about wages to justify a first cut, the BoJ is expected to hike in March due to robust wage demands, the Fed received a green light for June cuts from a favourable labour market and the BoE awaits next week's wage data.

## Labour markets remain critical for central banks

Payrolls are setting the stage for the Fed to cut in June...

The US labour market data released this week were ambiguous. Nonfarm payrolls grew by 275k in February, more than we (225k) and the consensus (200k) were expecting, with the 3mma printing at an elevated 265k and rising for the third month in a row. However, the print came alongside an almost 170k downward adjustment to the prior two months, essentially revising away January's big upside surprise. In addition, average hourly earnings were adjusted lower in January and surprised to the downside in February, leaving our underlying wage growth tracker at 3.8% m/m saar. Meanwhile, the household survey painted an even weaker picture, as it suggested employment contracted for the third consecutive month and showed the unemployment rate ticking up by more than expected, to 3.9%.

Combined with decelerating but still-firm job openings reported in the JOLTS, we read this week's labour data as pointing to a healthy and robust market, but not one that is running away and risks generating substantial upward pressure on prices. In this regard, it is also important that a part of the job gains can be accounted for by labour force growth, which returned to positive territory in February.

Similarly, other US data hint that activity is resilient but not reaccelearting in an unsustainable way. For example, services ISM ticked lower, but nearly all of this was driven by a downswing in delivery times, which is actually good news that January's disruptions did not persist into February, while business activity improved. Pulling together the available hard data, our Q1 GDP tracker now stands at 2.6% q/q saar, and we mark down our Q1 GDP forecast from 3.0% q/q saar to a still very solid 2.5%.

On the margin, such data makes us more confident in our baseline that the Fed will begin cutting in June, as the indicators reduce the likelihood of a "no landing." This is also supported by Chair Powell's Congressional testimony this week, where he noted that the Fed is "not far" from gaining the confidence it needs to implement cuts.

That said, next week brings more crucial data, most importantly February CPI and retail sales. For the former, we expect the headline to have accelerated to 0.5% m/m from 0.4% m/m due to higher energy prices, while core CPI might have eased to 0.33% m/m from 0.39% m/m. Underlying this forecast is an expectation of a broad-based, modest disinflation in core services prices as the run-up in January for some of the services categories appeared idiosyncratic. Whether this materialises likely will be critical to judge whether the surprise acceleration in inflation at the beginning of the year was just a bump or a change of trend. Meanwhile, we expect a strong rebound in retail sales, growing 1.7% m/m in the headline and 1.0% m/m in the control group, as the efects of January's weather-related disruptions and questionable seasonal adjustments reverse.

## ...while wage growth kept the ECB cautious...

The labour market also remains pivotal for policymakers in Europe. The perceived upside risks to inflation due to elevated wage growth kept the ECB cautious and noncommittal at this week's meeting. The Governing Council (GC) held rates steady, as expected, but the most notable feature of the meeting was the dissonance between the staf forecasts and communication at the press conference.  Namely, the macroeconomic projections for growth and inflation were again revised lower, including a significant downgrade in price growth forecasts, which show inflation undershooting the target at the policy-relevant horizon. Importantly, these downward revisions include core inflation, which is now seen at or below 2% from Q3 25, compared to 2.1% before.

Despite this, President Lagarde took a cautious tone, signalling that the debate about dialling back policy restriction has begun, but the GC is not yet confident enough in meeting its inflation objective in a timely manner. Both the President and the monetary policy statement cited domestic price pressures, driven by wages, as the primary risk to disinflation.

The upcoming data should resolve these uncertainties, but likely not imminently, as the President commented that "we need more data, we will know a little more in April, but a lot more in June." This supports our baseline for the first cut to happen in June, although April remains live. That April should not be completely ruled out was also confirmed by the comments of centrist governors Villeroy and Rehn, who spoke of rate cuts beginning in either April or June. After June, we continue to expect consecutive 25bp cuts until the ECB reaches the terminal rate of 2.5% in January 2025.

Besides the rate path, our focus is also on the new operational framework, which the ECB should present next week. In it, we expect the GC to institutionalise a demand-driven "floorsystem," where the suficient level of reserves is determined endogenously by banks' demand, while the ECB maintains a structural bond portfolio. We also think the ECB will narrow the corridor between the deposit facility rate and the main refinancing rate to 25bp by cutting the latter rate, potentially as soon as next week. Meanwhile, we do not expect any changes to the minimum reserve requirements.

On the data front next week, we are watching for country final February HICP prints and euro area January industrial production, which should contract by 2.1% m/m, although this is skewed down by the volatile Irish data and excluding that country, production might edge slightly higher.

## ...and strong "shunto" settlements should prompt the BoJ to hike in March

Meanwhile, in Japan, the incoming labour market data supports an early exit from negative rates. Most importantly, this week kicked of the annual cycle of data on "shunto" wage negotiations, beginning with the survey of the unions' wage demands. This showed the labour unions on average requested an increase of 5.85% (of which 4.30pp in base pay), sharply exceeding not only last year's demand of 4.49% but also the JTUC-Rengo's target of 5% or more. In fact, this is the largest demand in 30 years.

The results of the "shunto" negotiations will now be released in stages, with the initial survey released next week, followed by several more surveys culminating in the final outcome published in July. As in the past, we expect the initial results to show higher wage growth than later surveys as the latter incorporate more smaller companies, where the earnings environment is less favourable. Even so, based on the higher-than-anticipated union demand, we revise up our forecast for wage hikes and expect even the final survey to show an increase of 4.6% (we previously forecast 4.1%), significantly above last year's hike of 3.58%.

On balance, we think this outsized wage demand and likely strong initial "shunto" result next week will be enough for the BoJ to exit negative rates already in March instead of waiting for April as we had thought before, although this remains a very close call. This baseline is also supported by media reports, which point to the BoJ leaning toward a March hike.<sup>1</sup> In addition, the strong wage request may influence the pace of rate hikes after the exit of NIRP, and we now see a stronger upside risk to our baseline expectation that the hiking cycle will end when the policy rate reaches 0.25% in July.

## The UK's labour market data is up next

Similarly, next week will bring important labour market data in the UK. We expect the whole economy regular average weekly earnings growth to moderate slightly, to 6.1% 3m/y in January from 6.2% 3m/y in December, while a base efect could push up the private sector counterpart, giving the impression of a mild reacceleration from 6.2% 3m/y to 6.3% 3m/y. Separately, the UK's Spring Budget this week does not alter the macro outlook. Its core feature was a 2p cut to National Insurance contributions (costing c.£10bn per year; NIC) and a freezing of the fuel duty, which was slightly more than we had anticipated (we had expected a 1p cut to the NIC), but overall this is no game changer for the UK's fiscal policy, growth or inflation. On inflation, freezing the fuel duty will probably lower price growth in 2024 by 20bp, but this was already incorporated into our forecasts.

## China's policy efort not commensurate with growth ambition

The policy package revealed during China's NPC this week remained conservative.  It mainly consisted of a 3% budget deficit and CNY1trn of special treasury bonds (c.0.8% of GDP), which combined (ie, 3.8% of GDP) present the same deficit size as last year. Besides this, the government also incrementally raised the local government special bonds quota to CNY3.9trn from CNY3.8trn in 2023.

This package, on the whole, largely in line with our expectations, suggests no change in the government's reluctance to provide a more-meaningful stimulus or ofer any new policy easing in the housing market. In our view, this will likely be insuficient to achieve the announced 5% growth target for 2024. Even though the target is unchanged from last year (when it was achieved), the annual 2024 growth number will no longer be flattered by low base efects, making 5% more dificult to reach.

This is especially true as domestic demand remains soft, held back by a deep contraction in the property market and weakness in consumption on the back of negative wealth efects, high household debt, and a subdued labour market. This week's better-than-expected trade data suggest the external sector may partly ofset these domestic headwinds, but we remain cautious both on the magnitude of this efect and on the sustainability of a rebound in exports, given weakened new export orders and possible tarif escalation.

In our base case, the announced policy should prevent a further sequential deceleration in the economy, and, as the measures gradually kick in, allow for quarterly growth to improve gradually. However, even such a path, with a peak of 5.5% q/q saar in Q3, would leave the annual 2024 GDP growth at only 4.4%.

In the coming days, we will watch for the release of February credit and inflation data. We expect total social financing growth to ease to 9.3% y/y in February, from 9.5% in January, while we think CPI will increase by 0.2% y/y after falling 0.8% y/y in January. The latter would be driven by the diferent timing of the Lunar New Year holiday, and without the LNY distortion, we expect the Jan-Feb average CPI to remain in deflation at -0.3%. We see PPI to falling 2.5% y/y in February, unchanged from the previous month.

## Global outlook: varying flight patterns

The first two months of 2024 have already brought significant swings in the expected macro developments, especially as US growth surprised (again!) on the upside, the pace of disinflation suddenly slowed and rate cut expectations were reduced accordingly — a good time to take a comprehensive look at our growth and inflation forecasts across regions, reassess our predicted policy paths and formulate our global outlook for the coming quarters.

Much has been made about how economies would eventually "land," and indeed the metaphor of aircraft flight describes the global macro situation quite nicely.

## Soft landing in global aggregate

In aggregate, the global economy is experiencing a softish landing. It is no longer flying as high and fast as it did it in pre-GFC times, but the predicted slowdown from 3.2% in 2023 to 2.9% in both 2024 and 2025 is less pronounced than previously thought. Moreover, with some exceptions in smaller EM economies, there have been no notable output contractions, and unemployment in advanced economies remains historically low. All this, while aggregate inflation in advanced economies is predicted to settle back to a 2.7% average in 2024 and 2.3% in 2025, down from 4.7% in 2023 (and 7.6% in 2022!).

However, these global aggregates hide very varied regional flight patterns, especially in growth performance.

## US: a soft no-landing

First, the US economy continues to refuse to land, having surprised with a much higher Q4 GDP and since has shown few signs of any significant slowdown. That said, while activity remains robust, inflation does not seem to be reaccelerating. The upside surprise in January CPI disrupted the faster-than-expected disinflation process of previous months, but does not seem to have changed the trend of slowing price increases.

Overall, the US economy seems still on track to glide towards its 2% inflation target, albeit with much higher growth (and an only marginally higher inflation) in 2024 than earlier expected. We predict US growth at 2.6% and 1.5% in 2024 and 2025, respectively, and core PCE to end 2024 at 2.6% and 2025 at 2.3%. As part of this scenario, we expect the Fed to start easing in June and

reach 4.75% policy rate (upper-end) by end-2024 and reach a final rate of 3.75% in December 2025. Given that this implies the US economy returning to target inflation, not only avoiding recession but without even significantly slowing below its trend-growth at any point, the scenario may perhaps be most adequately described as a "soft no-landing."

## Europe and UK: trouble lifting of

If concerns for the US economy were around its landing, in case of Europe and the UK, the question is about an eventual take-of. Held back by the weight of much higher interest rates and facing structural headwinds from energy costs, tight labour supply, and lower demand from China, the euro area has remained stuck on the runway. Although the economy likely troughed in Q4 2023 and the recession has been shallow, all indications are for the euro area economy to accelerate but barely lift of the ground. We forecast EA growth to reach 0.4% and 1.3% in 2024 and 2025, respectively, and HICP to end 2024 at 2.3% and 2025 at 2.1%. As part of this scenario, we expect the ECB to start easing in June and reach 2.75% policy rate by end-2024 and reach a final rate of 2.5% in Q1 2025.

Similarly, we predict UK growth at 0.1% and 0.9% in 2024 and 2025, respectively, and headline CPI to end 2024 at 1.8% and 2025 at 1.7%. As part of this scenario, we expect the BoE to start easing in June and reach a 4% policy rate by end-2024 and reach a final rate of 3.25% in Q2 2025.

## China: continuing turbulence

China’s economy continues to experience turbulence as one of its main engines, the property market, has failed. The flight continues at reduced speed, but oficial announcements about a desired reacceleration are not matched by the eforts to fix the broken engine. The plane remains safe, but further turbulence and an even slower flight speed cannot be excluded. We predict China to grow 4.4% (vs oficial target of 5%) in 2024 and 4.0% in 2025, and we see selfreinforcing debt-deflation dynamics as a looming downside risk.

## EM (ex-China): flying circus

Emerging markets remain an even more varied bunch, ranging from India predicted to reach growth as high as 6.8% and 7.0% in 2024 and 2025, respectively, to a 4% contraction in the case of Argentina (2024). On a regional basis, high growth in economies like India and Indonesia lift the growth for EM-Asia (ex-China) to 5.2% and 5.3% in 2024 and 2025, respectively. That is about double the 2.6% growth rate predicted for the EEMEA region (for both 2024 and 2025), which includes a slowing but still surprisingly robust Russia and Turkey, a reaccelerating Saudi Arabia and Poland. In LatAm, Mexico’s predicted growth rate of 3.0% (2024) stands out, while Brazil’s 1.9% estimated growth remains disappointing. We initiate coverage of SCA with an UW rating and a price target of SEK 105.  We think that SCA's stock price incorporates a significant component linked to the valuation of its forest assets, which in turn is linked to a market-transaction-based methodology used by various Swedish forestry companies, viz. Sveaskog (public limited company solely owned by the Swedish state; not covered), Billerud (not covered), Holmen (not covered), SCA and Stora Enso. Initiating Coverage on U.S. Mid-Cap Banks with a Positive View

However, growth is still elusive as funding growth is settling into a slower rate, and customers have slowed demand for loans given economic uncertainty and higher rates.
