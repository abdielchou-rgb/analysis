# Japan Economic Perspectives What is "underlying inflation" for the BoJ and JPY's role?

## Why does the BoJ remain dovish despite JPY depreciation?

Because the BoJ judged recent JPY depreciation would not affect underlying inflation. The Bank probably expects the FX move per se and its impact are temporary (we disagree) or the impact could be mixed, leaving limited impact on net (we agree). In addition, the Bank may see a greater hurdle for further rate hikes than we do, fearing unexpected tightening effects (we are uncertain). We note that there was no direct analysis on the impact from JPY depreciation to underlying inflation in the latest Outlook report.

## What is underlying inflation?

In our understanding, it is long-term inflation expectations that are not observable. In other words, it is trend inflation that is not affected by temporary factors, such as exogenous shock. We (and the BoJ, we believe) are not certain what drives the expectations, but actual inflation in the past and output gap (including the expectation in the future) probably have some effects.

According to Box4 (page 55) in the Outlook report, the Bank notes that "in forecasting consumer price inflation, it is important to capture the underlying inflation by examining the factors that fundamentally drive price changes and excluding the effects of temporary fluctuations, such as in input prices." It implicitly suggests that the underlying inflation is basically long term inflation expectations, in our view. The Bank underscores the positive feedback between wages and prices is a force to raise the underlying inflation, but our understanding is that the positive feedback is a phenomenon and a consequence of higher inflation expectations.

Regardless, it is not easy to capture the underlying inflation (or genuine inflation expectations), especially in timely manner. Thus, the Bank presented three approaches to examining underlying inflation. First, constructing indicators from price data that (1) are less susceptible to temporary factors and (2) reflect underlying developments, such as labour costs. Second, focusing on indicators related to inflation expectations. Third, constructing econometric models and then estimate indicators of trend inflation using statistical methods. Some of these outcomes are shown in the following pages.

## Has underlying inflation risen?

Yes, the outcome of all approaches supports the BoJ's judgment that underlying inflation has risen recently, but to varying degrees. However, as the Bank underscored, all of the approaches presented here have limitations. We should recognise that the Bank's assessment of underlying inflation does not rely on just a few indictors, but is based on more comprehensive data analysis and inputs, including anecdotal information from firms. In other words, the broad range over which the Bank can exercise its judgement can make it difficult for market participants and public to understand.

## How will JPY depreciation impact underlying inflation?

In our view, JPY depreciation has mixed effects. Since Japanese inflation expectations are adaptive to actual inflation (see BoJ staff's analysis at comprehensive assessment of the monetary easing (the back ground) in September 2016), higher and longer inflation would push up the expectations (i.e., underlying inflation). Moreover, higher corporate profits for firms that face external demand (including inbound tourists' spending) can raise wages, strengthening the positive feedback between wages and prices.

On the other hand, higher living costs (energy and food) for households with JPY depreciation (especially with higher international commodity prices with supply-side shock, such as geopolitical events) could see consumers strengthen their defensive attitudes toward spending, weighing on underlying inflation through a negative effect on the output gap.

Which factor matters more in current phase is hard to tell, but the BoJ seems optimistic on consumption (see Box 1 page 45 in the latest Outlook report).

## BoJ policy normalization to proceed as long as underlying inflation rises

In any event, we think that the BoJ will adjust current accommodative financial conditions because we expect underlying inflation to rise further with recovery in consumption and a pick up in service prices. That said, it is understandable that the BoJ is cautious to make the next rate hike at this stage as a negative impact to the economy is hard to predict, especially without evidence of recovery in consumption and service prices in CPI.

Indeed, the Q1 real GDP and consumption data due on 16 May are expected to contract (our current forecasts look for a -0.4%q/q, saar and -0.1%, respectively, but risk skews to the downside), and Tokyo CPI in April showed no clear sign of faster service CPI even at the beginning of new fiscal year (see Japan Macro Watch "MoF to move first, then BoJ?").

We thus expect the Bank will raise its policy rate on 31 October from the current 0-0.1% to 0.25% after confirming a pickup in real consumption and service price inflation in summer. Underlying inflation is unlikely to be judged to have risen without confirming these two, regardless of JPY move, in our view. By the end of October, the BoJ can also obtain some clues on wage growth in 2025. Our basic thought is unchanged from the note BoJ and Japanese economy: New Journey to slow normalization, presented on 22 March.

## July rate hike is possible, but JPY depreciation per se is unlikely to be the trigger

Of course, a July rate hike cannot be ruled out, especially with further JPY depreciation and pressure from the public. Even in this scenario, the BoJ would likely present evidence that underlying inflation has risen. But without recovery of consumption, it is probably hard to convince the public of this assessment, in our view.

The reduction of JGB purchases may come sooner rather than later, but that depends on bond market conditions, not JPY moves. Governor Ueda stated in the press conference after the April meeting that the Bank has no intention to use JGB purchases as a monetary policy tool (overnight rate is the prime tool). Also, he said the reduction will be decided by the Board, although operational flexibility is given to the Financial Market Department.

According to the Cabinet Office's econometric model, a 10% depreciation of JPY against USD would push up real GDP level by 0.43%pt relative to the case without the change in the value of currency in the second and third year, while consumption would be boosted by 0.2 to 0.3%. Consumption deflator (close to CPI) to rise 0.2%.

In the same econometric model, a 20% rise in oil prices in USD terms weighs on real GDP by 0.1 to 0.2% in the second and third year and consumption by 0.3%. Consumption deflator rises 0.2% in the first and second year, then moderates to 0.15% in the third year.

Our CPI inflation forecast looks for a success of the BoJ's price mandate, sustained and stable 2% inflation, in 2025 and 2026, but a further rise in service CPI inflation to close to 3% by middle of 2025, which is driven by continued 3% wage growth during the year, is necessary.

Our base case BoJ call looks for the next policy rate hike in October to 0.25% from current 0-0.1%, followed by three 25bp hikes in 2025, and two hikes in 2026 (terminal rate is 1.5%, the middle of our view on neutral rate between 1.0% and 2.0%). We thus expect the policy rate at 0.25% at end of 2024, which is close to current OIS market pricing. However, our call at 1.0% at end of 2025 is about 40-50bp higher than the pricing. It suggests that market participants are sceptical of the success of the BoJ's policy mandate.

Our economist colleagues in the US and Europe look for consecutive rate cuts by the Fed (September and December this year and seven 25bp cuts in 2025 to reach 3.25%) and the ECB (the rate cut starts from June this year and 25bp per quarter cuts continue until 2026 to reach 2%).
