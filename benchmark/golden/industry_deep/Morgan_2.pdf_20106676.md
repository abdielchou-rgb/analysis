## United States

Fed rhetoric continues to signal it will take an unhurried approach to cutting rates

● Low jobless claims suggest a healthy labor market through mid-February

Housing demand looks to have formed a bottom and picked up modestly

• Expect firm PCE price data next week

The holiday-shortened workweek didn<sup>’</sup>t deliver much news to change opinions on the business cycle. We continue to believe that real GDP is expanding at around a 1.75% annual rate this quarter, which would be a slowing from last quarter, but still a pace that is at or modestly above-trend. So far this year the most favorable development has been the generally strong January employment report. This week<sup>’</sup>s jobless claims report—which covers the survey week for the February jobs report—fell to 201,000, a low level consistent with ongoing strength in the labor market.

Over the last year almost all the business surveys have sent a more cautious signal about the economy, and that was the case again this week. The February national manufacturing PMI edged up, the services PMI edged down, and both were in territory notionally consistent with positive but modest growth. The Philly Fed non-manufacturing survey (like we said, slow week) slipped further.

Recently most indicators of housing demand have shown modest improvement. This week existing home sales in January picked up 3.1% to a 4.0mn sales pace. That sales pace is still below that of any month between 2010 and late 2023, but a journey of a thousand miles begins with a single step (and expectations for rate cuts). By contrast, last week<sup>’</sup>s January housing starts report pointed to permits stabilizing over the second half of last year at a relatively high level, consistent with the structural supply-demand imbalance in housing.

While the data calendar was light this week, the Fed-talk calendar was full. The key message was patience. The minutes to the late January FOMC meeting didn<sup>’</sup>t add much to the rate discussion that wasn<sup>’</sup>t already conveyed in Powell<sup>’</sup>s postmeeting press conference. Moreover, the discussion around balance sheet reduction suggests there<sup>’</sup>s no urgency to adjust that process in the near term. Accordingly we now look for adjustments to the monthly runoff caps to be announced in June instead of March. Fed vice chair Jefferson stuck close to the Committee communications. Governor Cook sounded a little more optimistic on inflation and dovish on policy. Meanwhile, Governor Waller sounded a little more hawkish, as summed up in the title of his remarks: “What<sup>’</sup>s the rush?<sup>”</sup>

Powell<sup>’</sup>s semiannual monetary policy testimony on March 6 is unlikely to depart too far from these themes, as there are no more major data releases between now and then.

Next week is going to be much busier in terms of data releases. The January PCE price data will be important to watch and expectations are for firm prints to reflect strength in the related price data already shown in the CPI and PPI releases. We estimate that the headline PCE price index rose 0.4% in January (2.4%oya) while the core PCE price index jumped 0.5% (2.9%oya). We will also get a variety of January GDP source reports that we expect to generally be weak, with declines anticipated for real consumer spending, construction spending, and durable goods orders. January should set up a weak starting point for economic activity in 1Q. While we do believe that growth is slowing, we think that the January weakness likely exaggerates the cooling trend given harsh winter weather during the month and potential issues with seasonal adjustment. Automaker reports on February sales should give an early signal that activity picked up that month following the weak January, with the sales rate expected to firm in February after it dropped off in January.

## Fed staying patient

The minutes to the late January FOMC meeting reinforce the patient approach to cutting rates that Powell voiced in the post-meeting press conference. While only “a couple<sup>”</sup> of meeting participants noted the downside risk to growth from waiting too long to cut, “most<sup>”</sup> participants noted the risk of moving too quickly. Consistent with the statement released after the meeting, participants noted that risks to achieving their employment and inflation goals are coming into better balance, but they are still highly attentive to inflation risks. Indeed, much of the policy discussion in these minutes repeated themes already mentioned in the statement and press conference. If that weren<sup>’</sup>t enough to make these minutes less useful, the discussion is a little dated given the subsequent news on employment and inflation. In any event, there<sup>’</sup>s nothing here to make us rethink our call for a first cut in June.

There was a short discussion of QT in which it was mentioned that balance sheet reduction was running smoothly, and that at the March meeting they would “begin in-depth discussions of balance sheet issues<sup>”</sup> to guide an “eventual<sup>”</sup> decision on when to slow the pace of run-off. Given this leisurely-sounding schedule, we pushed back our expectations for the announcement of a reduction in the monthly caps from the March meeting to the June meeting.

Fed speakers from the past week have had time to digest the most recent news on inflation and the labor market and, like the minutes, suggested no urgency to announce any changes to QT or to start cutting rates. It appears that officials are still anticipating cutting rates this year, but that these cuts won<sup>’</sup>t happen in the immediate future.

## Jobless claims staying low

Many economic indicators have been noisy lately and we expect volatility to continue in several of the upcoming reports. While a variety of key indicators seem to be fluctuating between periods of strength and weakness, labor market data generally have been noisy but showing only varying degrees of strength. There wasn<sup>’</sup>t a lot of related news this past week but initial jobless claims filings moved down to the low end of the range of figures seen over the past several months, which already were looking favorable by broad historical standards (Figure 1). Continuing claims filings also have generally been low in recent months, although with the latest news not quite as upbeat as what we see for initial claims filings.

## Home sales perking up

Homebuyers likely have benefited from recent declines in mortgage rates and a number of home sales measures have firmed lately off of low levels. This week we saw that existing home sales rose 3.1% to 4.0mn saar in January (Figure 2). January was the strongest month for home sales since August, although activity was still quite low relative to the historical norms. Limits in inventories and increasing prices likely are restraining home sales even though lower rates help somewhat with affordability.

Existing home sales are counted when transactions are completed so they lag some other related metrics. And some of the timeliest news on homebuying activity points to the recent lift in home sales being temporary. Mortgage purchase application volumes have now fallen in four straight weeks (into mid-February), though they remain higher than their recent lows (Figure 2).

## Surveys mixed and decent

The flash PMI data for February showed mixed changes regarding activity in different sectors, with the manufacturing survey improving and the services survey softening. The manufacturing survey's headline composite increased from 50.7 to 51.5 and many of the report<sup>’</sup>s key details firmed between January and February, including the output index rising from 49.3 to 52.3. The February data in the manufacturing survey were not particularly robust, but the latest figures for the headline and several key details were the strongest seen in a while (the headline came in at its highest level since September 2022). The link between the business survey data and the “hard data<sup>”</sup> on manufacturing is not particularly tight, but the recent firming in the PMI signals that we may see some upcoming improvement in other indicators related to manufacturing activity that generally have been weak recently (Figure 3).

Meanwhile, the services PMI survey's headline activity index declined from 52.5 to 51.3, with many of the survey's other details also weakening between January and February. The headline had been drifting up over the prior several months before this latest move down and overall there have not been very dramatic changes in the services survey data lately that point to big changes in economic activity.

The flash PMI data for February showed mixed changes regarding activity in different sectors, with the manufacturing survey improving and the services survey softening. The manufacturing survey's headline composite increased from 50.7 to 51.5 and many of the report<sup>’</sup>s key details firmed between January and February, including the output index rising from 49.3 to 52.3. The February data in the manufacturing survey were not particularly robust, but the latest figures for the headline and several key details were the strongest seen in a while (the headline came in at its highest level since September 2022). The link between the business survey data and the “hard data<sup>”</sup> on manufacturing is not particularly tight, but the recent firming in the PMI signals that we may see some upcoming improvement in other indicators related to manufacturing activity that generally have been weak recently.

Meanwhile, the services survey's headline activity index declined from 52.5 to 51.3, with many of the survey's other details also weakening between January and February. The headline had been drifting up over the prior several months before this latest move down and overall there have not been very dramatic changes in the services survey data lately that point to big changes in economic activity.
