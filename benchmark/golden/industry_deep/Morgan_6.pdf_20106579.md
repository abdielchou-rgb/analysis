Skeptics argue that not much has changed. We see things differently. Macro policies will stay accommodative and engender a decisive shift in wage and price-setting behaviours. Japan will transition to a new phase of moderate, sustained inflation with a return of corporate sector dynamism.

# Japan is thawing from the deep freeze of prolonged lowflation

Transition to a new equilibrium of moderate inflation underway: For almost 30 years, Japan faced the challenge of persistent lowflation. Nominal GDP, which grew at a healthy rate of 5.4% during the 1980s slipped sharply to an average of 1.9% in the 1990s and further to -0.6%Y over the 2000s. Nominal GDP growth started to inflect higher post 2013, averaging 1.6% over 2013-19, and this has since risen further to 3.5% over 2022-23. Our chief Japan economist Takeshi Yamaguchi has highlighted that Japan is witnessing a momentous shift in its nominal GDP path. He is forecasting that nominal GDP growth will hold up at 3.3% in 2024 and will be sustained at 2.5-3.0% over the medium term.

Skeptics argue that not much has changed in Japan...: Skeptics would argue that what we have experienced so far is just the result of exogenous shocks in the form of global inflationary pressures and higher commodity prices. As the effects dissipate, inflation in Japan would descend back towards its old low equilibrium. Moreover, skeptics contend that Japan still faces the structural challenges from its weakening demographics and elevated debt levels. From our perspective, however, deflation is the crux. Getting the management of deflationary pressures right is crucial to alleviating the headwinds of debt. While demographics will still constrain potential growth, managing the debt and deflationary headwinds will allow the economy to grow closer to its underlying potential.

...but we see things differently: To be sure, it was a combination of fortuity in the postpandemic global reflationary environment and right policy choices that have given us the current backdrop in Japan. Just like every other economy, Japan faced a rise in inflation driven by mismatches in goods demand and supply and gains in commodity prices. This global rise in inflation in the post pandemic era led to a sharp tightening of monetary policy by the Fed and other DM central banks. In contrast, the BoJ kept its monetary policy accommodative even as interest rates in the US rose to a 23-year high. The ensuing rate differentials led to yen depreciation, which further added to imported inflationary pressures. The upshot of this backdrop was that the real interest rate and real GDP growth (r-g) gap reached a historically wide level and has since been maintained at accommodative levels.

Entering the next phase of the inflation cycle: Sustaining the r-g gap at these levels has rekindled animal spirits in the economy, in effect helping to reflate the economy. The policy setup has helped drive a meaningful decline in total debt to GDP. Corporates and households are responding to the stimulatory effects and are changing their behaviour. Companies are moving away from the previous status quo of not changing prices and not lifting wages. There has also been increased participation as well as churn in the market. Workers and unions are asking for – and getting – higher pay. We think this sets into motion the onset of a virtuous cycle.

The journey ahead: While we are optimistic that Japan will achieve sustained moderate levels of inflation, we don’t necessarily think that the current juncture represents a mission accomplished moment for policymakers. In fact, the opposite is true. The latest weak print of 4Q23 GDP is a reminder that the prolonged low-flation episode will require the patience of policymakers to be able to keep policies as accommodative as they can be to ensure the new equilibrium of moderate inflation is entrenched. With many uncertainties still lingering, we think a gradual approach to removing accommodation will be warranted. We will still need to see a full-fledged revival of dynamism in the corporate sector and the labour market. For instance, corporates should be turning up the capex momentum and spending on R&D to innovate and lead in new and emerging sectors.

## Has anything in Japan changed?

Skeptics argue that not much has changed in Japan: In our conversations with investors about Japan, we still find a good number of skeptics who’d argue that not much has changed in Japan that would warrant a fundamental reassessment of the macro outlook. They cite the fact that debt remains very elevated, and the demographic situation looks set to worsen. As for the inflation outlook, the input cost driven nature of it means that inflation would slip away from target once the effects fade.

But we see a return of moderate inflation and a dynamic corporate sector: We argue that there have been changes to Japan’s macro and that more changes are afoot. In fact, we think that Japan is transitioning towards a new equilibrium where moderate levels of inflation are sustained and dynamism returns to the corporate sector. Based on what we already know, Japan’s nominal GDP growth had already inflected to average 1.6% during 2013-19 and, in 2022-23, nominal GDP growth averaged 3.5%. This is a far cry from the - 0.03% nominal GDP growth that Japan witnessed between 1993 and 2012.

## What has driven the change

The long and arduous exit from deflation: The number one issue that has been affecting the Japanese economy for much of the past 30 years has been the damaging effects of prolonged deflation/lowflation.

Our framework: Our framework in thinking about Japan is shaped by the debtdeleveraging dynamics and in turn, we have preferred to view these dynamics in the context of a real rates and real GDP (r-g) framework. In this context, our analysis on historical successful deleveraging episodes showed that the key to reflate and delever an economy hinges on maintaining an adequate r-g gap. Specifically, for the median debt reducer economy, an r-g gap of -200bps should be maintained for about 10 years to stimulate aggregate demand and rekindle animal spirits. As an example, we would highlight that the US maintained this gap at an average of -340bps from 4Q09 to 4Q18. To achieve that gap, reflationary policies in the form of monetary and fiscal easing must be employed forcefully in tandem. For more details, please see Global and US Economics: Debt Sustainability: r-g Is Key, July 2021.

## Phase One: 1990-2012

Stuck in low growth low-flation loop: Policymakers kept the macro policy stance too restrictive. Real rates stayed above real GDP growth for the first four years after the bubble burst. Fiscal expansion effectively also only materialised from 1993 onwards. This slow initial pace of easing and the lack of policy coordination thereafter (i.e., monetary and fiscal easing were employed but rarely in tandem) eventually led the economy into deflation from 1994 onwards. With this backdrop, the economy was hobbled by balance sheet recession dynamics and debt to GDP kept rising from 1992-2002.

Over 2004-07, there was a period of temporary reprieve in that the global boom led to a sustained rise in Japan’s exports and this helped to widen the r-g gap to the -200bps that was required for debt deleveraging. During this period, debt to GDP in Japan delevered from a peak of 309% in 1Q05 to 296% in 2Q07.

But from 2008 to 2012, the r-g gap narrowed to an average of just +150bps. Persistent lowflation at zero-lower bound on rates meant that real interest rates remained too high to revive aggregate demand and engineer an exit from deflation. During this period, we also had a situation where global factors in the form of slower trade growth weighed on Japan’s growth prospects, while global lowflation also weighed on Japan’s inflationary dynamics.

Corporate sector capex was curtailed…: Faced with the persistent slowdown in nominal GDP growth and deleveraging pressures, the corporate sector stayed risk averse and did not take up capex, resulting in a stagnation in capital stock accumulation. Regulatory forbearance in the initial stages also sustained zombie entities, hampering productivity growth. Moreover, the Japanese yen also kept appreciating against the US dollar all through this time.

… and resulted in a decline in competitiveness: These factors combined to erode the competitiveness of the Japanese corporate sector. A case in point would be how the Korea corporate sector invested in R&D to improve its competitiveness and gain a presence in emerging sectors at the expense of Japan during this period. Indeed, the gap between Japan and Korea’s R&D spending as % of GDP began narrowing more aggressively from 2005 onwards, overtaking Japan in 2010. Reflecting this, Japan’s global export share steadily declined from a peak of 10% in 1994 to 3% currently, largely the same as Korea’s current export share. This is despite Japan’s working age population being twice as large as Korea’s. While arguably auto-makers were still globally competitive, we saw the decline in many other industries in Japan as well as the fact that we did not see many new globally competitive leaders emerge in new industries.

## Phase Two: 2013-2019

<table><tr><td colspan="3">Period averages</td></tr><tr><td>r-g gap: -180bps</td><td>GDP deflator: 0.6%</td><td>Nominal GDP growth: 1.6%</td></tr></table>

Abenomics marked a turning point: The introduction of Abenomics from 2013 onwards led to the first change in Japan’s macro dynamics, albeit still not to an optimal situation. Aggressive monetary easing was employed but, unfortunately, fiscal easing was still not always employed in tandem. BOJ implemented aggressive quantitative easing from April 2013, but the government initiated fiscal tightening with a consumption tax in April 2014 and again in 2019. Through 2013-2018, the cyclically adjusted primary balance narrowed. We think this measure to tighten fiscal policy worked counter to the overall objective of reflating the economy.

But fiscal policy and the global backdrop were still not conducive: The effectiveness of Japan’s reflationary policies were hampered by the fact that many economies also were facing lowflation and deleveraging headwinds post the global financial crisis. Moreover, the Fed was, for the most part during this period, at the zero lower bound and its balance sheet contracted only from 2018 onwards. Other DM central banks were also employing accommodative monetary policy for much of this time and it meant that Japan was not

able to generate additional inflationary pressures from what should have been a weaker JPY during this period. Nonetheless, these partial efforts did lead to Japan’s deflator averaging 0.6% during 2013-19.

## How does the continued weakness in 4Q23 GDP print impact our views?

Skeptics would point to the continued weakness in Japan’s GDP – which registered a second consecutive quarter of %QoQ contraction in 4Q23 to mark an unexpected technical recession – as a pushback to our constructive views. In our view, the weaker-than-expected GDP prints reaffirm that the transition towards a moderate growth and inflation equilibrium will take time and requires sustained support from accommodative policies.

Additionally, our Japan economics team still sees some possibility of upward revision to the 4Q data into positive %QoQ territory in the 2nd preliminary reading due out on March 11 (see Japan Economics: Unexpected Technical Recession: Impact on BoJ, Feb 15). They also note that leading indicators are showing signs of bottoming at the margin, and they expect hard economic data to begin recovering from February. As such, they see sequential growth turning marginally positive in 1Q23 in the base case.

## The next phase of Japan’s reflation journey

Positive changes in wages and price dynamics: In the next phase, we need to see a decisive and sustained shift in how firms set wages and prices. There are signs that this is beginning to happen, with firms shifting from the past social norm of no price increases. Our Japan economics team has previously discussed the initial signs of these changes in behaviour in two reports (see Inflation Norms Are Changing Even in Japan, April 2022, and Momentous Shift in Japan’s Nominal GDP Path, June 2023).

Firms are changing their price-setting behaviour: BOJ analysis indicates that in the past firms tended to avoid increasing selling prices if their competitors did not, even in the face of rising costs. Moreover, BOJ Governor Ueda has also indicated that in the past, “menu costs” in Japan – which are costs firms incur when changing prices – became larger as the lowflation environment became prolonged. This led to the further entrenchment of lowflation. However, there is more evidence that there is now a shift in firms’ price-setting behaviour. An analysis by BOJ of Tankan survey data of output prices indicates that even firms that have been cautious on changing their selling prices in the past are becoming more willing to pass through cost increases to customers. In that sense, menu costs may be declining, and firms may raise prices more easily going forward after a first round of increase.

Workers are demanding higher wages too: As prices rise and inflation expectations for households also rise, workers are demanding higher wages. Reflecting this, scheduled cash earnings growth (common sample basis) has risen to a record high of 2.2%Y in Dec-23, while on a non-common sample basis it has reached a 26-year high. Our Japan economics team also expects a headline wage hike of around 4%Y at the upcoming spring wage negotiations, with the base-up portion after accounting for seniority pay increases to reach mid-2%Y. This base-up portion growth would be the highest since 1992 and follows on from 2.1%Y in 2023.

At the same time, there are structural developments in Japan’s market that are supportive of higher wage growth going forward. While Japan’s prime working age population has continued to rise, in part supported by increase participation by female workers and foreign workers, its old age participation rate – which had increased from 20% in 2012 to 26% in 2021 – has now stagnated. BOJ estimates that overall room for additional supply is expected to decrease over the medium term. Moreover, there is increasing evidence that labour mobility is rising, with the share of employed individuals who have changed jobs over the past year rising to a post-Covid high of 5.2% in 4Q23, above the long-term average of 5.0%.

Early indications that a virtuous cycle of rising wages and prices could take hold: In this next phase, firms would need to be able to pass on their increased labour costs to selling prices. On that front, BOJ interviews with companies indicate some initial progress has been made, though there are still firms who indicate difficulties in passing on labour costs to output prices, suggesting that this transition is not complete. Policymakers have also issued guidelines for SMEs in negotiations to appropriately pass on labour costs. As firms increase their ability to pass on labour costs, this would generate a virtuous loop of rising wages and prices and bring about more sustainable reflation.

## What will sustain this change

All about r-g again: Again, we are viewing this through the lens of our r-g framework. Based on our forecasts, the r-g gap would gradually narrow but still be sustained at wider than -200bps over our forecast horizon of up to 2025. We think that the policy stance will remain accommodative enough to keep real rates negative, while the push to improve corporate governance, a revival in corporate sector dynamism, and the increase in capex will lift productivity growth to sustain overall GDP growth.

## Managing real rates trend

Domestic and external uncertainties warrant a very gradual approach to removing accommodation: While we are seeing signs of inflation dynamics shifting and we don’t see a return to deflation, we are mindful that there remains a need to actively manage the real rates trend. We’d highlight the following uncertainties:

## 1) Domestic factors – wage pass through and capex momentum

BOJ deputy governor Himino has noted that BOJ analysis shows the pass-through from a 1% rise in wages to prices is still relatively low at 0.2% and has not turned statistically significant yet, though the estimated effect now (2Q23) has risen as compared to the early 2010s. In another BOJ working paper, the authors suggest that the pass-through could strengthen in a non-linear fashion when wage growth exceeds 3%. However, we are currently not at this juncture, suggesting that we will need to see further evidence of wages strengthening with adequate pass-through effects. It is in this context that the Shunto wage negotiations this spring will be key to assess.

Another thing that we are tracking is the momentum in corporate capex. While there are nascent signs of a recovery as animal spirits have been rekindled to some extent with the accommodative policy setup and exuberance in asset markets, we have yet to see a fullfledged recovery in the capex cycle yet.

## 2) External factors – China’s deflationary pressures and potential rise in trade tensions

As we have been highlighting, China is facing extensive deflationary pressures and is pushing its excess capacities to the rest of the world. We had also cautioned that the continuation of these trends could risk a renewed rise in trade tensions, especially on the tariff front if Republicans win the White House in the upcoming elections. Against this backdrop, there is still a risk that the global backdrop could yet turn unfavourable in supporting Japan’s reflation.

## Managing growth

Sustaining an improvement in productivity growth is key: Beyond the near-term cyclical recovery, we will need to see a sustained uplift in productivity growth. As it is, our Japan economics and strategy teams have noted a trailing trend of rising productivity and increased corporate ROEs. At the current juncture, there is a policy push to focus the corporate sector’s attention on improving returns and capital efficiency via taking up micro reforms. Our equity strategists are projecting a sustainable rise in Japan’s ROE to 12% by 2025, up from 9% currently. The continuation of this focus on corporate reforms should bode well for an improving productivity trend, and in turn overall GDP growth.

## The return of dynamism

A return to a dynamic economy: In sum, we believe that dynamism is returning to the Japanese economy. Consumers are tolerating higher prices and the latest data points show that domestic consumer demand remains robust (see Weaker Manufacturing vs. Solid Information Services and Retail: January Reuters Tankan, Jan 17). Workers are demanding higher wages and churn has returned to the market. Corporates are witnessing record profits and changing their pricing behaviour, as well as innovating new strategies to grow. This marks a clear departure from the past 30 years, in which there was little dynamism in Japan’s corporate sector. To be sure, these are the initial signs and, as we have argued, there still needs to be policy support to help engender these trends. But at this point in time, we believe the future for Japan’s economy looks more promising than at any time in the past 30 years.
