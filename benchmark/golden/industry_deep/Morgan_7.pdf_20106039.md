Since our last publication a fortnight ago, long-end Treasury yields have continued to rise and mortgage spreads widen on the back of stronger-than-expected domestic economic data, pressure from DM rate moves, rising inflation expectations, and somewhat hawkish Fedspeak. Meanwhile HG exceptionalism extended into the new year with credit spreads tightening further despite heavy corporate issuance as demand remains robust. At these levels, we think it is appropriate to add duration in the belly while we recommend fading the richness in intermediate breakevens. Concurrently we remain neutral on mortgages but hold a more bearish view on HG credit and CMBS spreads.

Turning first to the data, retail sales rose by a strong 0.6% in December, with the important control group rising 0.8%, and even with these numbers buoyed by favorable seasonal factors, the unadjusted data is still supportive of a healthy household sector. Nonetheless, the front end of the rates curve has remained remarkably well anchored: OIS forwards are pricing in approximately a 50% probability of a 25bp ease in March, less dovish than two weeks ago, but are still pricing in more than 130bp of easing this year. The front end has been relatively insulated from the sell off, in our minds, because both the December FOMC meeting, as well as the minutes of this meeting, have indicated that the Fed has made clear progress toward its inflation target, and that the next move is likely to be an ease. Meanwhile, stronger economic data have biased longer-maturity Treasuries higher, with the broad curve reaching its steepest levels since early summer 2022 (Figure 2). Looking ahead, we recommend adding 5-year duration longs, given the considerable backup in yields from their late 2023 lows, an expectation that TIPS breakevens will reverse lower, and a more favorable technical backdrop. According to our Treasury Client Survey, net long duration positions have been reduced, while the share of neutrals has risen to its highest level since May 2023. Additionally, despite the steepening observed in recent weeks, we maintain 5s/30s curve steepeners, as the curve still appears too flat after adjusting for its drivers, and the curve tends to steepen as markets transition from Fed tightening to easing (see Treasuries).

Turning to inflation markets, 10-year TIPS breakevens have widened nearly 11bp, net of carry, since reaching local lows late last year, moving back to their widest levels since early November. This move has occurred even as our broad commodities index has declined, leaving 10-year breakevens appearing nearly 20bp too wide versus our fair value model. The dovish shift from the Fed over the past month, despite resilient labor market and growth data as well as still elevated core CPI inflation, has supported the outperformance of longend TIPS. However, we expect Powell will emphasize patience at the upcoming FOMC meeting, while we expect core CPI inflation to cool in coming months, supported by falling used car prices, and the core CPI/PCE wedge to narrow. Meanwhile, the potential implications from the Red Sea trade disruptions are likely to be limited, and do not appear to be a significant driver of the breakeven widening observed in recent weeks (see Shipping disruptions reignite supply chain woes, Nora Szentivanyi et al., 1/18/24). Instead, we think much of the recent widening has been technical in nature, due to the record extension in the TIPS Index this month. While this dynamic may keep TIPS supported over the next week and a half, we expect breakevens to cheapen as we move past month-end. Thus we recommend adding 10-year breakeven narrowers (see TIPS).

Swap spreads have widened over the last two weeks across the curve, except at the long end, in line with our prior expectations and despite heavy financial corporate supply. Looking ahead, we maintain our widening bias on swap spreads in intermediate maturities. First, seasonal headwinds are behind us as financial issuance should subside. Second, last week we brought forward our expected timeline for the tapering and conclusion of QT, and comments from Governor Waller this week support this view. An earlier end to QT is supportive of spread wideners as the Fed is expected to reinvest MBS into Treasuries via secondary market purchases once QT ends, and a higher terminal level of reserves should mean increased asset swap demand from banks, on margin. Third, the steady rise in SOFR towards IOER increases the relative attractiveness of asset swapping USTs for banks. SOFR is likely to continue to remain elevated given a growing stock of Treasury supply and ongoing balance sheet constraints (see JPM Mid-Week US Short Duration Update, Teresa Ho, 1/4/24). Finally, valuations remain supportive as the 5Y sector continues to remain too narrow by our fair value measurement. To take advantage of wider swap spreads in the belly of the curve, while benefiting from the slide in the term structure, we recommend paying 2.625% Feb<sup>’</sup>29 matched maturity swap spreads overlaid with a long vega position to hedge the potential for increased implied volatility (see Interest Rate Derivatives).

High grade corporate credit spreads continued to tighten over the past two weeks, reaching 108bp on our JULI, which is only 3bp wide to the tightest levels post-GFC. This has occurred despite \$136bn of issuance MTD, already \$6bn above recent January averages, with more than 10 days left in the month. Demand is clearly overwhelming supply as yields remain elevated, and this can be seen in the aggressive flattening of the 10s30s curve beyond model-implied levels as money managers are raising cash in the belly while seeking longerdated and high yielding paper. Fund flows in particular have been strong to start the year and growing ETF trading in particular is contributing to greater liquidity and tighter spreads. As expected, the last couple of weeks have seen fund inflows rival levels we witnessed this fall, in particular among IG credit-specific and Agg funds (see Figure 3). We expect fund flows should moderate in the weeks ahead according to seasonal patterns, but historical cyclicals suggest they could resume once the Fed enters an easing regime (see Figure 4.and whilefos vutpc y r, g  m k and Overview, US Fixed Income Markets Weekly, 12/15/23).

Looking ahead, we are more bearish on HG spreads as at these levels. While yields remain attractive, there is still a significant portion of the market that does care about spreads and can pivot accordingly into other asset classes such as MBS when corporate spreads are too tight. We think we are at or near levels where this will become more of a concern. Moreover, as earnings season moves from Financials to Non-financials, supply will become less predictable, opening the possibility of more price impact. We also note that HG spreads are tighter YTD even as equities are lower and HY spreads are wider. In other words, HG exceptionalism is fully on display, and we are not convinced this will persist. A near-term test of our more bearish stance will likely be earnings season, which, if disappointing, opens the door to investors reassessing their views of the odds of a soft landing and ensuing impact to spreads (see Corporates).

Mortgage spreads widened by a staggering 10bp across the stack in tandem with higher yields, with most of the conventional 30yr stack finding a home in the 25-30bp Tsy OAS range. We think these are fairer valuations given that money managers and banks should be relatively spread sensitive. Strong bond fund inflows to begin January have been marginally supportive for mortgages as it relieves some of the overweight pressure off of money manager demand, but these buyers should still remain overall spread sensitive given the weighting disparities (see fund flow Figures above). Meanwhile fourth quarter bank earnings reports were surprisingly robust in regards to securities demand. Disclosures from Bank of America in particular indicate that some large banks may be looking to add securities to AFS, hedged with swaps, as they look to offset a growth in shorter-duration time deposits. Overall demand for MBS may be more robust in 2024 than we previously thought on the back of growing deposits, though we expect banks will demand greater OAS pickup vs. Treasuries than in the past. Meanwhile this better demand is likely to be met by an increase in origination volumes, as we have likely reached the seasonal low in turnover. As such we remain neutral on mortgages and with a flat OAS stack, continue to prefer higher coupon conventionals, as well as favoring Ginnie premiums (see Agency MBS).

Meanwhile we think spreads in mortgage credit are likely to remain rangebound in the nearterm, and this may be the best time to add ahead of the seemingly inevitable decline in longer-term yields in 2H24. We generally have liked CRT M2/B1 but the recent spread range is challenging to recommend adding more exposure. Instead we prefer non-QM A2s over CRT M2s (Figure 5). Lower rates allow mortgage credit to delever faster, but volatility is likely to remain high with energy price pressures and a looming presidential election. We do note a pickup in delinquencies among recent non-QM loans, especially in the <sup>‘</sup>22 vintage, and this could be in part related to a higher WAC. We think a majority of delinquent borrowers will prefer modification or choose to sell the property and thus do not anticipate material principal writedowns (see RMBS).

CMBS spreads have continued to march tighter, likely in the wake of the CREFC conference which rang optimistic and hopeful with a desire to put money to work (see Thoughts from the CEFC conference, Chong Sin, 1/10/24). However with 10yr LCF AAA spreads now at J+104 — more than 50bp tighter from October 2023 local wides and 5bp inside 7-10yr single-A corporates (JULI ex-EM) — we are more cautious on further outperformance in CMBS spreads (Figure 6). While we cannot rule out further compression given the positiv tone from investors, we note that LCF AAA spreads are now at levels not seen since before the 2023 regional bank stress while the fundamental picture has not improved materially. Indeed our analysts note that the latest December 2023 remit period shows 30d+ delinquency rates among FRESB multifamily loans reached 2.2%, the highest level since the program began a decade ago and a 100bp increase from the prior month. While this spike was likely caused by hardship faced by a single sponsor within the New York loan pool, we think we could see ongoing stress among FRESB loans and CRE CLO, as borrowers, in particular from New York, face elevated borrowing costs and induced valuation deterioration (in part due to rent control regulations) (see CMBS).

## Update on foreign demand for US fixed income

This week, Treasury released its monthly TIC report on foreign transactions and holdings of US securities for November, which shows that foreign investors bought \$131.3bn longterm fixed income securities, a pickup from \$76.7bn from the prior month, and represents the strongest month of foreign purchases since April 2023 (Figure 7Foreign vstpuchad \$13bf l-m US N, 7 O.). The strength was driven primarily by foreign private investors, who purchased \$167.1bn of securities, the most since August 2022, while foreign official investors sold \$35.8bn, the greatest month of selling since January 2023. Across products, foreign investors increased the pace of Treasury purchases from \$54bn to \$90bn over the month of November, also driven by private investors. Meanwhile foreign interest in MBS products waned, with foreign private investors reducing the pace of purchases from \$18bn to \$12bn, while foreign official accounts sold \$6bn of MBS (Figure 8.driven byaotl cug fTs p  ). Finally, foreign demand for corporates was strong, purchasing \$34.1bn of long-term corporate debt, and this was almost entirely concentrated among private foreign investors.

Diving into the country level details, we note that Euro area investors were once more strong buyers of Treasuries and corporates in November, adding \$32.5bn and \$6.9bn, respectively; investors from France were particularly heavy buyers of Treasuries at \$23.3bn. Investors from the United Kingdom also were prolific purchasers, buying \$17bn of longer-term Treasuries and \$6.6bn of US corporate debt (Figure 9). The strong buying from this investor base should not come as a surprise as our European colleagues have recently highlighted the attractiveness for Euro area investors to buy Treasuries over domestic sovereigns on both a matched maturity and rolling 3M FX hedge basis, and as such we would expect buying from this cohort to continue (see Cross currency basis 1Q24 Outlook, Fabio Bassi, 1/12/24). Meanwhile the attractiveness of US corporate bonds for foreign investors (in particular from Europe) peaked in November and has since declined, suggesting that we should expect to see still robust, but moderated, purchases going forward (see HG Credit Foreign Demand Monitor, Nathaniel Rosenbaum, 1/16/24).

Turning to Asia, TIC data shows that net purchases from Japan were virtually flat in November, and this trend holds across the three major fixed income categories. This result is corroborated by MoF data which show Japanese investors sold a modest 0.12tn JPY of USD bonds in November, while they turned to buying roughly 1.27 JPY long-term foreign bonds in December (see Japan Flows in Pictures, Takafumi Yamawaki, 1/12/24). Meanwhile China sold roughly \$17.5bn of long-term US fixed income securities in November, concentrated in Treasuries. Our Chinese colleagues continue to highlight growing gold purchases, which have risen for 13 consecutive months through November (see China: The largest monthly gain in a decade for FX reserves, Tingting Ge, 12/7/23). Overall, we think there is room for a pickup in foreign private investor demand for US fixed income as hedging costs decline while we expect weak demand from the foreign official sector (see US Fixed Income 2024 Outlook Overview, 11/21/24).
