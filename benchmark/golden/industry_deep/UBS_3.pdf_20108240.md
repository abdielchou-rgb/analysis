# China Property The potential destocking models

## Acquisition of unsold properties from distressed developers

On 15 May, Bloomberg reported that the government would consider acquiring unsold properties from distressed developers at a deep discount to convert to social housing. This is in line with one of the two destocking models discussed in our Politburo Meeting note on 30 April. In our view, this would help: 1) ensure project completions, 2) stabilise the market, and 3) provide social housing supply; however, a potential bottleneck is that certain properties, if located in the suburb districts/lower-tier cities, might not be suitable for conversion to social housing. We estimate a Rmb1.1-2.4trn range in funding size would be required, based on: 1) the remaining social housing investment quota for 2024-25 in the 14th Five-Year Plan, and 2) funding required to destock inventory months to a normal level (Figure 1). Despite the recent rally, we still expect 30-40% share price upside from current levels if property prices are stabilised, as developers' current profitability is below cycle. Our top picks are CR Land, Longfor, Yuexiu Property and BEKE.

## How much funding is needed?

We estimate the required funding based on the three goals mentioned above. From a project completion perspective, we estimate the funding required to complete the suspended projects on a national basis is Rmb1.5trn, based on 7.0m suspended units and Rmb2,000/sqm extra construction costs; see our project whitelist note. From a market stabilization perspective, we estimate the funding required to bring down the inventory months to a normal level is Rmb2.4trn for the 35 tier 1-2 cities, assuming a 35% discount (wiping out the land premium that the developers had paid). We consider 35 tier 1-2 cities instead of a nationwide acquisition as the government aims to provide social housing for the 35 cities only. The previous 14th Five-Year Plan on social housing aimed to invest Rmb3.0trn to build 8.7m units, and this implies the remaining investment quota is Rmb1.1trn for 2024-25. In order to justify the economics, the cost and tenure of funding have to be lower than current tier 1-2 rental yield of 1.7% (Figure 9), and long enough (30 years), similar to the Rmb100bn PBoC's rental housing loan provided in January 2023.

## Another model: Acquisition of secondary home inventory (trade-in policy)

Another model is the trade-in policy; ie, the local government acquires the existing home from upgraders who can only use the proceeds to buy a primary property of higher value, while the local government then converts the existing home to social housing for rental purposes (see Figure 2 case study). We believe this policy measure could be effective if rolled out on a larger scale; ie, with more funding support from central government. This would be effective because: 1) it would release upgrade demand by providing liquidity in the secondary market; 2) it would result in simultaneous destocking in both primary and secondary markets; and 3) the household sector would also expand its balance sheet. This was our reason for turning more bullish in our January 2024 upgrade report (APAC Focus: How to end the deleveraging cycle). As per UBS Evidence Lab survey (see note) and developers' feedback, upgrade demand becomes the largest demand driver now. According to the seventh national census, 28% of urban housing stock (29.5bn sqm) was built before 2000 (Figure 8), and thus 8.1bn sqm (8.5 times 2023 national new home sales) of housing stock was built over 20 years ago as of end-2020, which could be the source of upgrade demand. Taicang, Zhengzhou, Nanjing, and Wuhan launched this policy in 2023. In 2024, Zhengzhou launched the trade-in target of 5,000 units (5.6% of its 2023 new home sales), and Nanjing targeted 2,000 units or 4.5% of 2023 new home sales.

## Model 2: Acquisition of existing properties

## Three types of trade-in schemes; government acquisition is key

So far, three types of trade-in schemes have been established to help home owners sell existing housing and purchase new housing. The first scheme is conducted through the support of local governments. SOEs or local government financing vehicles (LGFVs) would acquire eligible existing housing to convert to social housing, and then home owners could use the sales proceeds to purchase a replacement in the primary market (see case study on Figure 2). In the second scheme, some local governments have offered subsidies to home owners selling existing housing and purchasing a new one. Thirdly, the government partners with agencies to prioritise the transaction of existing properties for upgraders. See details in Figure 3-5. We think the latter two schemes might not be effective, and the first one (ie, with government balance sheet) is essential to unleash upgrade demand.

According to seventh national census, 28% of urban housing stock was built before 2000 (Figure 8). We estimate existing urban housing stock at 29.5bn sqm in total, and thus, as of end-2020, 8.1bn sqm was over 20 years old, which is 8.5 times 2023 national residential primary sales. As the government acquires the existing inventory to convert to social housing, we expect the trade-in scheme to kick in for the 35 major cities under social housing programme. As of 2023, the 35 cities sold residential primary properties worth Rmb5.0trn, or 48% of national sales (Figure 6). Among the 35 cities, we estimate 242m sqm of inventory need to be removed to return to the historical average level of inventory months. Assuming ASP at Rmb15,000 per sqm, the total funding needed is Rmb3.6trn (Figure 7), and this could help boost 72% of the 35 cities' primary sales. However, we think the actual amount will be less than Rmb3.6trn, as the upgraders will also have to put in capital for upgrades.

## Nanjing trade-in scheme case study

On 27 April 2024, Nanjing Anju Construction Group, an LGFV responsible for social housing construction, launched a trade-in programme of 2,000 units. The eligible existing properties have to be located in the six core districts, and the value of an existing property cannot exceed 80% of the value of primary housing that the homebuyer plans to buy (ie, homebuyers need to put down another 20% at least of the new home value). Details in Figure 2. The available new housing includes 14 projects under Nanjing Anju Construction Group, scattered in core and non-core areas, and the unit sizes range from 70sqm to 387 sqm. In the first two days, over 2,100 residents applied for the programme and 42 units of new home purchases were settled. After acquisition, Nanjing Anju Construction will convert those existing properties for rental purposes.

## Valuation Method and Risk Statement

We base our price targets for China property developers on PE or P/BV multiples.

We believe key downside risks related to the China property market include: 1) government administrative policies that restrict demand and mortgage lending; 2) tight financing for China's developers; and 3) lower-than-expected residential growth in China's economy.

We believe key upside risks include: 1) a material policy loosening that effectively boosts residential property sales, investments and prices to positive YoY growth; and 2) large-scale asset disposals at fair prices by some developers to ease liquidity pressures.

CR Land: Our price target is based on a PE multiple. We believe the key risks to CR Land include: 1) a slowdown in China's property market, especially in lower-tier cities; 2) increasing competition in the M&A market leading to higher land costs and lower margins; and 3) delays in the conversion progress of its urban redevelopment projects.

BEKE: We use a PE multiple to value KE Holdings (Beike). We think key downside risks for Beike include: 1) failure to retain and attract management team and high quality agents; 2) potential competition pressures, especially in the longer term; 3) failure to diversify its regional revenue mix as over 30% of Beike's revenue came from Beijing and Shanghai in the past few years; 4) failure to maintain good relationships with top developers; and 5) government intervention, especially anti-monopolistic measures or public platforms.

Longfor: We use a PE multiple to value Longfor. We believe key risks to Longfor are: 1) government administrative policies to restrict demand and mortgage lending; 2) tighter financing for China developers; 3) increasing penetration of e-commerce business in retail distribution, which could affect retail landlords; and 4) lower-than-expected growth of China's economy.

Yuexiu Property: We base our price target on a PE multiple.

We believe key downside risks related to Yuexiu include: 1) government administrative policies that restrict demand and mortgage lending; and 2) tight financing for Chinese developers; and 3) lower-than-expected growth of China's economy. We believe key upside risks include: 1) a material policy loosening that effectively boosts property sales, investments and prices to positive YoY growth; 2) large scale asset disposals at fair prices by some developers to ease liquidity pressure.
