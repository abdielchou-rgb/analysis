## You Can't Always Get What You Want When You Need It

In The Linchpin Got Pulled, we noted that as weaker data drag down growth expectations, the performance of risky assets could suffer unless real rates fall faster than growth expectations. In order for real rates to fall faster than growth expectations, Fed guidance needs to become more dovish than the data would otherwise suggest.

August employment report weakness encouraged Fed Governor Waller to suggest "that the balance of risks is now weighted more toward downside risks to the FOMC's maximum-employment mandate." But he also said that he does "not believe the economy is in a recession or necessarily headed for one soon."

His comments about the pace of rate cuts did not convince investors that the Fed would deliver a 50bp cut in September. Rather, he suggested that he would need to see more labor market data to act "forcefully":

As of today, I believe it is important to start the rate cutting process at our next meeting. If subsequent data show a significant deterioration in the labor market, the FOMC can act quickly and forcefully to adjust monetary policy.

Our economists also believe the data support the start of an easing cycle. Still, they didn't think the August employment report would be enough to encourage the Fed to deliver 50bp in September (see Modest rebound in jobs, easing coming … probably 25bps).

Investors increasingly want the Fed to respond more forcefully with each weak employment report. The more time it takes the Fed to commit to quick and forceful action, the more that weaker employment data increase investor concerns.

Risky asset prices reflect these growing concerns. If investor growth expectations – generally forward looking – deteriorate faster than FOMC participant plans to address them – more recently backward looking – risky asset prices will remain under pressure, in our view. This framework comes from the Gordon Growth Model.<sup>1</sup>

We think investors need much stronger labor market data in the months ahead in order to calm budding fears. Indeed, the employment-to-population ratio (EPR) indicator favored by our economists took another step toward flagging a recession – moving from 0.3pp to 0.4pp. Similar to the Sahm Rule, the EPR indicator flags a recession at 0.5pp.

In Exhibit 1 and Exhibit 2 , we combine the EPR indicator, the Sahm Rule, and the vacancy rate indicator into a Triumvirate Rule (see Did Someone Say 'Sahm'-thing About a Recession?). The Triumvirate Rule takes the minimum of the three indicators, but uses a lower threshold to begin calculating non-zero recession probabilities.

That said, despite a shift in risks, we're not yet sold the DXY will break below 100 — it may instead stay in its roughly six figure range. Consider:

• Global growth remains anemic and shows no signs of improvement;

• Foreign central bank pricing has ample scope to follow the Fed, which would rewiden rate differentials in USD's favor; and

• Market pricing from elections in the US and Germany presents asymmetric USDpositive risks.

Data in Europe and China have continued to disappoint. If anything, there is even further scope for euro zone growth to roll over thanks to increasing downside risks to Italian growth from the expiry of Superbonus. "Push" factors like a dovish Fed reduce upside risks, but we need "pull" factors as well to draw capital out of the USD and that requires a clearer and compelling alternative.

The dovish Fed repricing and slowing US data pushed US yields down relative to its peers, weighing on the USD. But the next shoe to drop could be foreign central bank pricing catching up. The more central banks deviate from the Fed, the higher their currencies go, weighing on growth and inflation. The ECB curve could prove a key candidate for repricing into its upcoming meeting.

Finally, we can't ignore politics. This Sunday's German elections showed us that parties with heterodox economic policies continue to gain steam. Markets are pricing the US

Presidential election as effectively a coin flip, but we think a Republican administration is more likely to be USD-positive than a Democratic administration (see here).

How does one trade this? The clearest expression is short USD/JPY. Markets could tussle between the USD Bear and Defense Regimes in the near term as the debate between a US hard vs soft landing continues. The beauty of USD/JPY is that, unlike nearly every other pair, it tends to fall in both Regimes. Thus, as long as one is confident real rates are going down, independent of why they're falling, USD/JPY is the cleanest trade.

In terms of FX RV, we still see GBP and AUD as the cleanest longs and EUR and CAD as the clearest funders. Local stories suggest markets may be less willing to price in deep cuts from the BoE and the RBA, while the BoC and ECB both stand out to us as candidates for a dovish repricing. However, given their risk sensitivity, RV may be the cleanest expressions and we continue to recommend long GBP/CAD. Short EUR/USD via options remains an attractive global macro and local hedge.

What about the market-implied path for Fed policy further into the future? The market implies the trough rate for Fed policy to match the longer run dot from the Fed's June dot plot (see Exhibit 6 ). The market price reflects the average of all outcomes considered.

If the average outcome of Fed policy in this cycle results in a policy rate back at neutral (or what the consensus of FOMC participants deem neutral), then the market price must also reflect outcomes with lower-than-neutral and higher-than-neutral policy rates.

Put differently, market prices could imply some probability of a hard landing, as well as some probability of a soft landing in which either (1) policy rates don't return to neutral or (2) neutral policy rates rise more than expected.

In general, we believe current market pricing – a relatively swift move down to a neutral policy rate – reflects the idea that, on average, the Fed will be able to "stick the soft landing", i.e., ease policy quickly enough such that the economy avoids a recession. These prices remain consistent with our economists' views.

## Are super-long JGB ASW still cheap?

This content was originally published in "Japan Macro Strategy: How to Position After the Recent Volatility?"

Long-end JGB ASW have been heavily sold off since NIRP removal, as the market seemingly started to price in the upcoming QT. Cheapness has indeed been partially corrected of late (see Exhibit 29 ), as the medium-term QT schedule was already clarified, and the seemingly now almost certain prospect of Fed rate cuts has fuelled some renewed interest in cash bonds. However, just how much scope might there be for further richening?

Below, we construct a fair value model for super-long JGB ASW with a view to assessing current valuations. As we discussed in Japan - Estimating ASW Fair Values, February 8, 2020, an ASW spread can be expressed as the sum of the expected future GC-TONA spread and the JGB-OIS risk premium differential.

With regard to the latter, we believe that it should be acceptable to focus mostly on JGB supply/demand factors for modelling purposes given that the OIS market tends to be much freer from such influences.

Super-long JGBs lack liquidity and carry significant duration risk, meaning that they may be difficult to hold in size for investors other than life insurers (who can hold policy reservematching bonds that do not need to be marked to market) or pension funds (who invest for the long term and are accordingly willing to ride out fluctuations in market value).

Exhibit 29: JGB ASW spread performance across the curve since NIRP removal
![](images/37209abc2d29ff50ba1f2c42eb382f9308bc63b85c142368a3c45b93e69ca86f.jpg)
Source: Bloomberg, Morgan Stanley Research

Exhibit 30: Estimated change in dealers' inventory in the long end versus 30y JGB ASW
![](images/eb3dbd0200af10118159938fc7871d614cefb3db70967756d0b83b822bc53848.jpg)
Source: BoJ, Japan MoF, JSDA, Bloomberg, Morgan Stanley Research

Dealers (such as securities firms) facing onerous risk-management requirements are liable to sell off super-long JGBs when their inventories are building, thereby generating cheapening pressure. For the purpose of our model we have looked to approximate the change in dealer inventories by subtracting investor purchases of super-long JGBs from monthly net issuance (MoF issuance of 10y+ JGBs minus BoJ purchases) (see Exhibit 30 ).

The JGB liquidity index (GVLQJPY Index) – which displays the average yield error across the universe of Japan government notes and bonds with remaining maturity one year or greater, based on the intra-day Bloomberg relative value curve – should also serve as a useful gauge of dealer risk-tolerance levels.

The basic idea is that liquidity is 'worse' when valuations differ significantly across issues, signifying that arbitrage mechanisms are not functioning properly (for whatever reason). Poorer liquidity is liable to signify impairment of securities firms’ financial intermediation capacity, thereby leaving super-long JGBs more vulnerable to cheapening pressure owing to their comparatively high interest rate risk (see Exhibit 31 ).

There are of course cases where JGB supply/demand may be distorted by BoJ purchases, but the central bank has already dialled back its absorption in the super-long sector to the point where its operations should have only minimal impact (see Exhibit 32 ).

## 1. GC-TONA spread;

2. Six-month moving average of the change in dealer inventories of 10y+ JGBs (MoF issuance of 10y+ JGBs - BoJ purchases of 10y+ JGBs - investor net purchases of 10y + JGBs, 10y JGB DV01 equivalent term);

3. JGB liquidity index (GVLQJPY Index).

We obtain a roughly 85% determination coefficient when running our data for 30y JGB ASW using monthly data (see Exhibit 33 ), with all three of our variables having statistically significant explanatory power.

Up until recently our model had been estimating fair value at between (roughly) 10bp and 20bp. This year, however, we appear to have been seeing more of an impact from both a rise in GC-TONA spreads as the BoJ ended its negative interest rate policy and reverted to a single-tier reserve structure (see Exhibit 34 ).

Moreover, there has been an accumulation of dealer positions as the commencement of BoJ rate hikes seemingly caused by investors' reluctance to purchase JGBs in anticipation for further BoJ's normalization.

We have also seen off-the-run issues cheapen within the super-long sector as the curve has bear-steepened sharply and some investors have looked to sell off less liquid bonds (see Exhibit 35 ). Dealers saddled with such issues in their inventories might be in less of a position to buy at auction, which might in turn serve to generate additional cheapening pressure on 30y JGB ASW.

30y JGB ASW has cheapened even further than can be explained by such factors (see Exhibit 33 ). However, we do see potential for this cheapness to be gradually corrected over time.

As we discussed in Japan Money Market after NIRP Removal, March 14, 2024, the GC-TONA spread is likely to remain somewhere in the vicinity of -2bp as long as the BoJ sticks with a single-tier reserve structure. Moreover, investors may start showing gradually more interest in super-long JGBs now that Fed rate cuts (and declines in US interest rates) are seemingly imminent.

Furthermore, life insurers might be looking to buy more super-long JGBs as 1H FY2024 draws to a close, and we can also expect to see a certain amount of support from pension funds. The net upshot should be a lightening of dealer inventories as well as an improvement in the liquidity index.

We therefore expect fair value itself to start falling to some degree. We are also looking for super-long JGBs to be supported over the medium-to-long term by a reduction in MoF issuance that could commence as soon as next year (as has been telegraphed by the Advisory Council on Government Debt Management; only Japanese is available so far).

We obtain similar results for 20y ASW showing the current spread level quite some distance below fair value (see Exhibit 36 ). That said, we favor buying 30y JGB ASW given that they look particularly cheap. The main risk to this trade is that of investors becoming (or remaining) reluctant to buy super-long JGBs due to (say) a rebound in US interest rates and/or USD/JPY that serves to revive concerns about downside risk to JGB prices (or hopes for better buying opportunities).

• Foreign central bank pricing has ample scope to follow the Fed, which would rewiden rate differentials towards USD's favor;

In addition, uncertainty over the US election, which could encourage investors to pare back risk-taking, makes a cash allocation even more appealing.

As of August 29, MMF AUM has increased by \$100bn in the month, this is tracking for the strongest monthly inflow of the year ( Exhibit 97 ). This is also significantly above seasonal trends and only slightly below the prior year in which negative sentiment around the banking industry led to strong inflows into MMFs. Looking ahead, typical seasonal trends suggest inflows continue into the month of September, albeit at a slower pace.

Institutional funds will have sizable outflows around the September corporate tax date, as corporations redeem cash invested with MMFs to make payments to Treasury. Yet, September is also a very active month for US corporate bond issuance – with \~\$125bn expected from investment grade companies – this is a tailwind for inflows into institutional MMFs.

Even though market pricing implies that the Federal Reserve will cut rates at its September 18 meeting, retail inflows will likely continue. As highlighted above, inflows into retail funds have begun to show sensitivity to the lower yields on cash alternatives which have fallen in anticipation of Fed rate cuts.

Daily retail fund inflow trends show the strongest day of inflows for the month is the first day of the month, as investors re-invest the interest income earned from the prior month. Although there were indeed strong inflows on the first day of August, the strongest day of inflows of the month occurred on the sixteenth day ( Exhibit 98 ).

We observe that on August 22 there was \$210bn in publicly held bills that matured. Around 70% of the maturing proceeds were 3-month and 6-month bills, whose yields had now begun to reflect investor expectations for expanding probabilities of a faster/larger Fed easing cycle. On August 8, \$240bn in publicly held bills matured, where in addition to 3-month and 6-month, a 52-week bill matured.

Provided inflows on these dates were well above typical daily trends, this suggests that instead of simply rolling over proceeds and locking-in potentially rich 3-month and 6- month bill valuations, some investors likely viewed the yield pick-up MMF yields offered as more attractive ( Exhibit 99 ). Hence, inflows into MMFs could remain supported in September, despite Fed rate cuts, as cash rotates into MMFs from other cash alternatives.

Despite the further growth in MMF AUM, MMF weighted average maturity (WAM) has recently been in a downward trend since a local peak of \~35 days on August 2. Yet, the all-around weak July nonfarm payrolls report released on August 2 marked the local WAM peak and brought about conversations of whether “unexpected weakness” in the labor market could push the economy into a recession or hard landing. This led to a sharp repricing of the Fed policy rate path as the skew of risks now favor a more aggressive Fed easing cycle, with increased downside risks to employment.

Hard landing concerns and the aggressiveness of Fed policy easing priced into longerdated bills are two primary factors which have contributed to the recent steady downtrend in WAM. The theme of the week was weak labor market data. Wednesday's jobs openings data for July came in materially below expectations, though the layoff rate data remain below pre-pandemic averages. Friday's jobs report also surprised to the downside on non-farm payrolls, with pronounced downwards revisions to the already-weak July reading. In both cases, the data emphasize the increasing risks to the labor market.

Rates rallied, as the market increased both the speed and the depth of the cutting cycle (see Exhibit 106 ). The trough of pricing through '26 is currently \~2.8%, below (1) 5y5y OIS-implied levels of neutral, and (2) recent Fed speak estimates of r\* (see Harker 8/23/2024 or Bostic 8/29/2024). In other words, rates markets are pricing a policy path in which the Fed not only removes restriction, but actually stimulates activity.

Vol markets dropped on Friday, in part as event risk was priced out, making us think that the path forward is for vol to move lower. CBOT breakevens were wide for this past week, with NFP deemed largely influential for the path of policy. However, we think the move lower in vol following the jobs report was not only a removal of event risk, but also:

• Increasing confidence that the short-term path is for rates lower: In our view, the only path to persistently higher vol is through a repricing of right tail risks. As labor market data weakens, and inflation remains in line with the target, investors are increasingly confident on the need for policy easing.

• Hesitancy to further fatten left-tail risks: The market is pricing a non-trivial probability (\~20%) of the 1y SOFR rate in 1m being 30bp below spot

## Trading Strategy 1 – "Trade Longs/Fade Shorts"

When the 5-day moving average crosses above the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract (long duration) and hold for a 25-business-day period. When the 5-day moving average crosses below the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract and hold for a 25-business-day period. In short, this strategy buys futures when the Simple Moving Average Crossover (SMAX) generates both a long and a short signal, given the historical outperformance of long signals traded long and underperformance of short signals traded short. Given that the SMAX could generate both a long and a short signal within the predefined holding period, an investor may have a 200% long position since each of the two signals would be traded in separate portfolio sleeves.

## Definition of terms

Buy/Long: The analyst expects the total or excess return (depending on the nature of the recommendation) of the instrument or issuer that is the subject of the investment recommendation to be positive over the relevant time period.

Sell/Short: The analyst expects the total or excess return (depending on the nature of the recommendation) of the instrument or issuer that is the subject of the investment recommendation to be negative over the relevant time period.

Selling protection or Buying Risk: The analyst expects that the price of protection against the event occurring will decrease over the relevant time period.

Buying protection or Selling Risk: The analyst expects the price of protection against the event occurring will increase over the relevant time period.

Pay: The analyst expects that over the specified time period the variable rate underlying the swap agreement that is the subject of the investment recommendation will increase.

Receive: The analyst expects that over the specified time period the variable rate underlying the swap agreement that is the subject of the investment recommendation will decrease.

Unless otherwise specified, the time frame for recommendations included in the Morgan Stanley Fixed Income Research reports is 1 - 3 months and the price of financial instruments mentioned in the recommendation is as at the date and time of publication of the recommendation.

When more than one issuer or instrument is included in a recommendation, analyst expects one part of the trade to outperform the other trade or combination of other trades included in the recommendation on a relative basis.
