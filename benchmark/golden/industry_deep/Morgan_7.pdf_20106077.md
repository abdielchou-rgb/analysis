# FX & EM Fixed Income Strategy: Fiscal Fears Remain

Simon Waever

2023 on track to see a broad deterioration in fiscal balances: As we enter 2024, fiscal data for most countries are near complete for 2023. We use the same data set of countries we use in our fiscal risk premia monitor (see EM Strategy: Fiscal Risk Premia Update, January 11, 2024), which covers 29 countries with reasonably timely monthly fiscal data released. Most now have November data released.

In general, 2023 has broken the trend of improving fiscal results since the wides hit in 2020 due to Covid ( Exhibit 1 ). This means that both the average and median, and even the top quartile, move further away from reaching the more stable pre-Covid levels. On aggregate, the median country ran a nominal fiscal deficit of 2.7% in 2016-19 whereas the latest number is now 3.7% compared to 3.2% in 2022.

In general, most countries are far better off today than the wides in 2020 as a result of Covid. Notable exceptions are Turkey and Mexico, which are at the widest fiscal deficit levels today, with Indonesia and Kenya on the other hand running the smallest deficits compared to the 2016-22 period ( Exhibit 2 ).

• Improving fiscals mostly due to expenditure rationalisation: Six of the seven countries seeing significant improvement in their fiscals all cut expenditures, some materially so. Colombia was the key exception that benefitted from much higher revenues instead.

## • Worsening fiscals mostly due to lower revenues, specifically commodity

revenues: At the other end, the clear driver behind those with wider fiscal deficits has been a fall in revenues with no offsetting revenue gains. In particular, for most of the countries it's the negative impact that falling commodity prices/production has had on revenues. Key exceptions to this trend were Turkey and Ukraine, where much higher spending, due to the earthquake and the conflict, respectively, were not offset by higher revenues. Beyond 2024, Turkey's fiscal outlook is poised to improve as fiscal consolidation continues and earthquake-related spending fades.

• Interest expenditures up across the board: A final notable trend has been the rise in interest expenditures across the board. Only El Salvador and Colombia saw falls in interest expenditures as a share of GDP, which in the case of El Salvador was largely due to the pension reform. For all others, it's clear that the higher funding costs both domestically and externally are increasing debt-servicing costs.

## Market implications

• Fiscal risk premia to stay relevant, and elevated in many countries: Our fiscal credit risk premia flags the risk premia as very elevated in Panama, Ecuador, Colombia, Romania, Nigeria and Hungary. While this in theory suggests that the moves are overdone, we also see the fiscal outlook as challenged in most of these countries, meaning that they are likely to stay cheap (see EM Strategy: Fiscal Risk Premia Update, January 11, 2024). Countries we are outright bearish on include Colombia and Nigeria, yet we also see downside risks in both Panama and Ecuador despite the cheap valuations.

• Elections are key for outlook: Elections will have a big impact on the fiscal trajectories. For 2024, elections in Panama, Romania and South Africa are among the most important for markets, but with Mexico, Uruguay, Venezuela, Ghana, Tunisia, Turkey, Sri Lanka and Pakistan also important (see Elections on the Horizon, November 17, 2023).

• Rating risks remain to the downside: Fiscals will unsurprisingly always be a key driver of the sovereign ratings. For the coming year, we see downside risks to ratings coming from fiscals for Colombia, Panama, Ecuador and Romania and reducing the likelihood of upgrades for Uruguay. On the other hand, Argentina is the one that could see significant improvement in its rating in case of a successful fiscal adjustment.

• Hard currency issuance to increase year on year for EM, both gross and net: In part due to the still-wide fiscals and thus high financing needs, we expect sovereign hard currency gross issuance to increase to US\$164 billion in 2024 as market access opens up further. Net issuance increases by US\$19 billion to US\$87 billion. IG issuance at US\$125 billion would be the second highest on record (see EM Sovereign 2024 Financing Outlook, January 10, 2024).

While primary issuance should remain high through January, it should then slow in February, in accordance with historical trends. While we still are not expecting inflows to EM, there is now a case to be made that the spread differential between EM IG and US IG is large enough that the diverging flow outlook could ease: A key reason for US IG's continued outperformance versus EM IG has been inflows. Move Ecuador to a dislike stance: While most see 2026 as a difficult year for Ecuador due to the high debt repayments, we see risks as more front-loaded. So far already subdued bond prices kept us from moving to an outright dislike stance, but the recent rally looks overdone and leaves risk/reward negative, in our view, and we thus move to a dislike stance. Key reasons include:

• Security issues to impact macro negatively: Ecuador has been in the global headlines since the elections last year due to the unrest related to security issues. First and foremost is the negative impact this is having on Ecuador's population. Unfortunately, there is also an important spillover to macro. Growth is likely to be hit negatively as investment slows, in turn impacting tax revenues. Security spending will also need to go up at the same time. On the external accounts, there is likely to be a hit to investment, financial flows but also to tourism. Net travel receipts contributed 0.6% of GDP to the current account surplus of 1.6% as of 3Q23 on a rolling 4Q basis.

• A significant financing gap already in 2024: As per our financing outlook, we already foresee a financing gap of US\$2.3 billion, a number that an IMF deal alone would not solve. This assumed a fiscal deficit of US\$3.1 billion or 3.7% of GDP, yet all indications, including from the Finance Ministry, are that it could come in much wider at around US\$5 billion. While we agreed with the downside risks at the time of writing our financing outlook, we simply saw no way of financing such a wide deficit. The government is aware of the challenge and is attempting to both raise revenues and cut expenditures. This includes a 3pp VAT hike to 15%, which is expected to gain around US\$1 billion. While it's far from clear that this can be approved in the first place, the aim is also that such new revenues would mostly go towards increased security spending. Oil revenues also see downside risks due to both prices and production. On the cost-cutting side, fuel subsidies have been brought up. However, given the historical issues the government has had in

reducing these, we see no reason why today would be any different. Reversing the oil referendum would also be risky. Finally, arrears already stand at around US\$4.5 billion, likely limiting the space to increase it much further.

• FX reserves suddenly look very low: 2023 ended the year with FX reserves of US \$4.5 billion or only 1.7 months of imports, having fallen by 47% in 2023. While not a unique problem to Ecuador across EM, it's a clear worry that reserves fell to such an extent against a backdrop with still-supportive oil prices and while not having large FX debt repayments yet.

Move Argentina to a like stance: While far from the best entry point given the rally, bond prices are nonetheless not extended when factoring in the macro changes expected from our economist. The following are key points that we think still warrant a more positive view than we have had so far, and in particular versus Ecuador.

• A significant fiscal adjustment under way with a 0.8% primary surplus possible already in 2024: In terms of an actual budget for 2024, the government will use the 2023 version, which to start with means a large decline in spending in real terms. For the budget targets, the aim is a balanced nominal budget. Given that this would be a significant adjustment of 5-6pp GDP, as reviewed by our economist (see Argentina: A Good Start, December 20, 2023), a more realistic target is a primary surplus of 0.8% of GDP for a nominal deficit of 1.5% of GDP. However, even achieving this would be a huge change and would surprise most market participants, in our view.

• FX reserve build possible: Away from fiscals, the other focus point of markets is the FX reserves, given the very weak starting position. Our economist forecasts a US\$15-20 billion increase in grain exports this year with an improved energy trade balance and a weaker FX adding another US\$5-7 billion delta to exports (see Argentina: Digging Through External Accounts, December 7, 2023). With a positive financial account of around US\$5 billion, with FDI for instance improving to US\$2.5 billion, in the end the forecast is for an FX reserve build of US\$13.7 billion in 2024.

• A strong willingness to pay debt: Given the precarious fiscal and FX reserve situation, worries have been high on FX debt repayments. However, so far the government has been very clear that it intends to make all payments. While this was also our expectation early on, it has gone further in directly being willing to borrow more from the BCRA in order to make such payments. Making US\$1.6 billion in payments in July 2024 will be helped by the income from the harvest, while the expectations outlined above for the fiscal result and FX reserve build would suddenly increase the likelihood of also paying US\$5.6 billion in 2025. To be clear, these are still huge numbers and we don’t expect any standard eurobond market access in 2024 to help to address them. Risks are thus far from removed that some restructuring is needed. However, it's lower than we anticipated earlier and we suspect that sentiment will improve further in the coming months.

• Broad reforms are possible: Finally, our economist is also optimistic on the significant reforms that have already been proposed via an executive order and an Omnibus bill (see Argentina: On the Sweeping Reforms, January 16, 2024). In particular, our economist expects a slightly diluted version of the bill to be approved already by month-end or early February in the lower house (see here).

• Plenty of risks but prices not extended either, plus we are not the only ones who have been sceptical: The commitment, size and speed of adjustment have surprised us positively. While bonds have clearly performed well, it's not been excessive. An average price across the bonds of 36 today compares to 32.6 the day before the second-round results and an average price of 29.2 in 2023 up until the second round. We think that a key reason for bonds not extending further is that there is still a lot of scepticism in the market, for very good reason given the scale of the challenge and historical mistakes. However, if the data and reforms come in as expected above, we suspect that dips will be shallow and there may not be that many better entry points, especially as we judge positioning by EM real money to be close to neutral still.

Buy ARGENT 2046 versus ECUA 2040: Combining the two views above makes for a specific trade recommendation as well to switch out of Ecuador into Argentina. In Ecuador, the 2040 bond outperformed in the past week, which means that any reversal would likely see it underperform despite its second-lowest cash price. For Argentina, while we have so far preferred ARGENT 2030, this has also outperformed materially due to its more front-loaded amortisation schedule. For adding risk today, we suggest ARGENT 2046 instead. While it's not the bond that has lagged the most recently, ARGENT 2046 is among the lowest cash price bonds but actually has a better amortisation structure by end-2025 compared to all bonds apart from the 2029 and 2030 bonds.

ARGENT 2046 is currently around 2 points below ECUA 2040 but has a higher coupon and higher payments until end-2025 and beyond. We target ARGENT trading 5 points above ECUA, reaching the highs in December, with a stop at -6 points. For Argentina, a key risk is governability, including potential larger protests. In Ecuador, the risk to the upside is that the VAT hike is agreed together with an IMF package in the very near term.

## 1. Why are you bearish on MXN and when do you expect it to start underperforming?

Since the start of the year, MXN has been the focus of our client conversations. For most of 2023, MXN had a relatively straightforward narrative which did not require extensive debating or analysis. However, in our view and in line with our expectations laid out in our 2024 outlook, sentiment is slowly starting to turn.

We expect 2024 to be a trickier year for MXN, given a fairly rare temporal intersection of four types of risk events, and we maintain a medium-term bearish bias:

1. Political noise stemming from the Mexican and US general elections (which only intersect every 12 years).

2. Potential rate cuts around the Mexican election (which have not occurred in the last 20 years), and around the US one too. As we explain below, we think that in theory MXN can digest a 400-450bp interest rate differential versus the US, all else equal. However, we worry that in practice the currency could react faster to rate cuts, given the overall negative balance of risks ahead.

3. Higher fiscal noise in Mexico (given a 2024 nominal deficit at 30-year wide levels and the need for tax reform in 2025).

4. An ongoing slowdown in the US economy: MXN is arguably the only EM currency that likes strong US data, given much better integration between the two economies in recent years via nearshoring and remittances.

To be clear, we think that any of these four risks would be manageable on a stand-alone basis. However, taken together, they should create non-negligible headwinds for MXN, likely starting in March (three months ahead of Mexico's election, as explained below).

## 2. How stretched does MXN positioning look now?

In short, positioning looks cleaner now, but still mildly long. As we highlighted in our previous note on January 8, our indicators suggested that MXN longs looked quite extreme as of the start of the year, making MXN vulnerable to a potential correction in risk appetite.

Our sentiment indicator has also shifted lower very mildly, from roughly 80% implied bullishness to \~70%. We still like to hold our short MXN position versus BRL as we think that longs in the latter have more room to grow and Brazil's narrative into end-2024 looks much smoother for now.

## 3. How many rate cuts can MXN digest?

This is a topic we explored in more detail here. In short, we think that MXN can likely digest a 400-450bp policy rate differential versus the US, given some structural improvements in its fundamentals over the last few years. Back in 2017 when the 600bp policy differential was introduced by Banxico as the "appropriate" level, foreign ownership of local bonds was at historical highs (\~65%), but dropping at a fast speed given political uncertainty. The current account deficit stood at \~2% of GDP and uncertainty around Mexico's trade relationship with the US, its largest partner, was very elevated.

Fast forward to 2024, a renegotiated NAFTA deal (and so far no mentions about it from US presidential candidates), a narrower current account deficit (supported heavily by US remittances) and much lower foreign ownership of local bonds (\~30%) point to much better integration between Mexico and the US.

In turn, we think that, all else equal, this would support a narrower policy rate differential, of roughly 400-450bp, which MXN has also been able to digest at other points in history.

However, while we think that in theory MXN can digest a 400bp interest rate differential versus the US all else equal, we worry that in practice the FX could react faster to rate cuts, given the overall negative balance of risks ahead.

## 4. How are you thinking about Mexico election risk premia?

MXN risk premia starts to increase 90 days prior to the Mexican general election: As we highlighted in our 2024 outlook, MXN tends to underperform in spot terms in the 90- day period ahead of election day. This has been the case in the last three elections, with MXN depreciating by 5-10% regardless of whether the result was perceived as orthodox or unorthodox by markets. At least in the last two elections, the presidential election results were also accurately captured by polls in advance.

The 90-day pattern can be explained by the fact that it corresponds to the official campaigning period in Mexico. As the election will take place on June 2 this year, the campaign period will extend from March 1 to May 29. If history is any guide, this implies that USD/MXN should begin to price in more local election premia starting on March 1.

The dates of the three official debates between presidential candidates, which could directly impact MXN volatility, are as follows:

• April 7, 2024: Candidates will answer questions received via social media platforms;

• April 28, 2024: Candidates will answer questions received from a public audience;
• May 19, 2024: A direct debate between the candidates.

Two notable 2014 changes in the electoral laws taking effect in 2024: We note that unlike in previous years, the new president will be sworn in on October 1 (versus December 1), ahead of the US election. We also note that senators and other representatives elected in 2018 will be eligible for re-election.

Fiscal reform comments and Congress composition will likely drive MXN price action: So far, the polls point towards policy continuity beyond 2024, but the reality is that both candidates remain somewhat of an unknown for markets, especially in terms of potential fiscal policies. Prudent fiscal policies should become crucial in 2025, given Mexico's need for fiscal consolidation amid structurally higher spending patterns.

Based on our investor conversations, we think that at this point markets would feel most comfortable with a status quo outcome (i.e., policy continuity on the presidential front, with a simple (not qualified) congressional majority).

Additionally, we see some risks around more unorthodox announcements from the outgoing president as well, as he looks to finalise his legacy before his term ends. Developments around the energy sector or comments around Banxico's policy path (unlikely at this point) could also bring about significant volatility over the next five months.

## 5. How are you thinking about US election risk premia?

There is no clear historical pattern for MXN around US elections: As shown below, MXN reacted quite strongly to the 2016 election won by the Republican candidate, and it did not seem to be affected by elections won by Democrat candidates. As Mexico ultimately navigated the 2016 US regime change quite smoothly, we think that some investors might be tempted to fade potential MXN noise around the US election. We think that this could be a mistake, as Republican debates already suggest a hawkish stance towards Mexico.

Trade-related comments should matter most: That said, Mexico lost some of its 2016 election spotlight to China in 2020, at least as captured by the number of news articles on each topic. So far, the campaign website of the leading Republican candidate as per national polls does include references to Mexico on subjects such as immigration, border security and illegal substances, but not on the trade front so far.

The latter would be most impactful for MXN price action, as they could signal risks to the recent economic integration between the two countries.

## Has Korea's NPS used up its FX swap line with the BoK?

The context: The BoK introduced a US\$10 billion FX swap line with Korea's National Pension Service (NPS) in late 2022 and increased its size to US\$35 billion in April 2023. As the NPS needs to buy foreign assets every month to increase its strategic allocation to foreign assets, the monthly USD buying/KRW selling activity puts downward pressure on KRW. The purpose of the FX swap line is to reduce the pressure on KRW.

Quite often, investors ask us the following questions: 1) How much foreign assets does the NPS need to buy every year? 2) Will such purchases increase annually? 3) How does the FX swap line affect USD/KRW demand? 4) How much of the swap line has been used? and 5) What does it mean for USD/KRW? We address all the questions below.

## The NPS's strategic and tactical asset allocation

Korea's balance of payments: Korea's current account surplus has declined sharply in recent years, mainly due to Korea's increasing energy imports and, to a lesser extent, a smaller transport services trade surplus ( Exhibit 52 ). The good news is that Korea's primary income surplus has been increasing thanks to Korean corporates' repatriation of foreign dividends, providing an offset.

Despite the deterioration, Korea's current account remains in a surplus. The excess savings over investment has allowed Korea to invest those savings abroad, leading to decent-sized outflows in the financial account ( Exhibit 53 ).

## How much does the NPS need to buy annually in the future?

There are two sources of buying foreign assets: First, the NPS would continue to increase its allocation to foreign assets at a steady pace. As discussed above, the annual increase could be around 1.6% of AUM assuming there is no valuation effect of foreign assets versus domestic assets. This could be evenly split between foreign bonds and equities. This indicates US\$717 billion (current AUM) \* 1.6% = US\$11.5 billon purchase of additional foreign assets every year.

Second, the NPS also receives new contributions from pensioners every year. Our calculation suggests that its overall assets have been increasing by about 10% every year, which is a reasonable assumption for future growth. Meanwhile, the NPS' long-term return target is 5.2%. This implies around a 5% annual increase in AUM due to new contributions. Given that the NPS targets at least a 50% allocation to foreign assets, this suggests US\$717 billion \* 5% \* 50% = US\$18 billion.

Overall, our calculation suggests that the NPS needs to buy around US\$30 billion of foreign assets in the next few years in order to achieve its target allocation. This amount is consistent with the amount of foreign purchases seen between 2019 and 2022.

## Why did the BoK introduce the FX swap line with the NPS, and how does it work?

The BoK-NPS FX swap line was initiated in September 2022, when broad USD strength pushed USD/KRW towards 1,400, a level not seen since March 2009. To alleviate the KRW depreciation pressure, the authorities rolled out a slew of measures, which included a US\$10 billion FX swap deal between the BoK and NPS that ran until end-2022.

In April 2023, the swap deal was renewed and expanded to US\$35 billion. In December 2023, when it was due to expire, it was renewed again and is currently scheduled to run until the end of 2024.

How does the FX swap work? The FX swap deal allows the NPS to obtain the USD it needs for its foreign investments from the BoK's FX reserves instead of the FX spot market, in exchange for KRW. The FX swaps have a maturity of six months or one year, so after the stipulated period of time, the NPS would need to return the USD to the BoK's FX reserves.

Until the end of this year, it is also allowed to temporarily increase its FX hedge ratio by up to 10% in the event of unusual FX moves, but this has not been utilised so far.

NPS flows may have a larger impact when KRW is already under pressure: This shows that the NPS' foreign investment is just one of the many flows that drive KRW, and can be outweighed by other factors. The NPS outflows may have a larger impact on the currency when KRW is already under depreciation pressure from other factors.

BoK-NPS FX swap could help to ease depreciation pressure: That said, for this year at least, with a minimum of US\$22 billion of the BoK-NPS FX swap line still available, the NPS could tap on it when KRW is under pressure to avoid exacerbating the move. Therefore, we think that the broader USD and risk environment will be more important in determining the KRW direction.

USD is key for the USD/KRW trend: Given that we still see room for USD upside tactically, we remain neutral on KRW for now. However, USD/KRW is approaching attractive levels to sell for the medium term, with the top end of the recent range at \~1,360. When we start to see catalysts for USD weakness, we'd expect KRW to outperform despite the NPS outflows.

Watch for maturity of the BoK-NPS FX swap deal: For next year, though, if the BoK does not renew the FX swap deal with the NPS, then KRW could face more downward pressure as the NPS would need to buy USD not just for its new foreign investment, but also to repay the BoK when the swaps mature. For this reason, the BoK-NPS FX swap deal may be terminated only when KRW is on an appreciating trend.

## Stay neutral on SGD amid MAS status quo

MAS to keep policy unchanged: After moving to a quarterly meeting schedule, the MAS will announce its next decision on January 29. Policy is likely to remain unchanged. Since the last meeting, Singapore's inflation has continued to decelerate, imported inflation is still declining by 1%Y, oil prices have fallen by 13% and S\$NEER has remained at \~1.4-1.6% above the midpoint of the MAS' policy band. At the same time, core inflation remains relatively elevated at 3.2%Y, limiting the scope for easing.

S\$NEER valuation looks rich: Based on our estimates, S\$NEER is currently trading \~1.5% above the midpoint of the MAS’ policy band. Considering that the MAS is likely to keep policy unchanged, the current S\$NEER valuation looks rich to us.

However, this rich valuation has been sticky. S\$NEER has been staying \~1.4-1.6% above the midpoint of the band since late October. This came as the significant USD weakness propped up S\$NEER, largely offsetting downward pressure on the basket from the SGD depreciation against the risk currencies ( Exhibit 72 ).

USD strength, preferably with equities rallying, is most conducive for S\$NEER weakness: This suggests that the best environment for S\$NEER to weaken is one where USD and equities rally simultaneously. Indeed, this is what historical moves suggest when we look at the weekly changes in S\$NEER versus moves in equities (S&P 500) and USD over the past five years ( Exhibit 73 ).

While USD and equities rallying simultaneously may sound rare, it has happened in 52 out of the past 260 weeks (five years), or 20% of the time. One environment in which this could happen is where the US economy outperforms the rest of the world, driving flows into US assets.

However, until we see a clear catalyst for this to happen, or signs of the MAS turning dovish, we stay neutral on S\$NEER. Ultimately, the MAS’ current policy setting puts S \$NEER on a path of gradual appreciation, so timing is crucial for any plays for a correction lower in S\$NEER.

We don't think that the upcoming MAS meeting will provide the catalyst for S\$NEER to correct lower as policy and tone should remain unchanged. The key points to watch for are any potential revisions to GDP and inflation forecasts, and whether the MAS still sees both upside and downside risks to the inflation outlook.

In rates, we are biased to look for higher SGD yields: The MAS stepped up its bill issuance significantly in 4Q23 to \~S\$5-6 billion per month, the highs seen in 2021/22, as US rates rallied aggressively ( Exhibit 74 ). This contributed to keeping SORA largely stable within the \~3.2-4% range despite the rally in US yields.

At the same time, the SGD loan-to-deposit ratio has stabilised, edging higher from 69.3% in August to the current 70%. This is mainly driven by an improvement in loans as SGDdenominated deposits held steady ( Exhibit 75 ), which points to less easy liquidity on the margin.

## Definition of terms

Buy/Long: The analyst expects the total or excess return (depending on the nature of the recommendation) of the instrument or issuer that is the subject of the investment recommendation to be positive over the relevant time period.

Sell/Short: The analyst expects the total or excess return (depending on the nature of the recommendation) of the instrument or issuer that is the subject of the investment recommendation to be negative over the relevant time period.

Selling Protection or Buying Risk: The analyst expects that the price of protection against the event occurring will decrease over the relevant time period.

Buying Protection or Selling Risk: The analyst expects the price of protection against the event occurring will increase over the relevant time period.

Pay: The analyst expects that over the specified time period the variable rate underlying the swap agreement that is the subject of the investment recommendation will increase.

Receive: The analyst expects that over the specified time period the variable rate underlying the swap agreement that is the subject of the investment recommendation will decrease.

Like: Based on current market conditions as of the date of this report the analyst expects that the relevant securities of the issuer that is subject of the recommendation will perform favorably over the relevant time period as compared to the overall market of comparable securities by other issuers. Dislike: Based on current market conditions as of the date of this report the analyst expects that the relevant securities of the issuer that is subject of the recommendation will perform unfavorably over the relevant time period as compared to the overall market of comparable securities by other issuers. This is not intended to be, nor should it be interpreted as a formal fundamental rating of the issuer or its creditworthiness.
