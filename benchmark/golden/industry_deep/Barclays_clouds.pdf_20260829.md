## Scattered clouds

Tech weakness in the US, soft EA PMIs and idiosyncratic risk has disrupted the summer grind. We do not recommend fading HY weakness vs IG cash, or Main weakness vs IG cash, and we retain our cautious stance on cyclicals as well as Autos across IG and HY.

The summer quiet has been disturbed by clouds on both the macro and micro front, leading to general weakness across markets.

On the macro side, away from tech weakness in the US causing equities to slump, EA PMIs painted a subdued growth picture, softening in July, with weakening seen across activity, price and employment metrics. Notably, the manufacturing sector continues to struggle. Our economists point out that the levels of the July PMIs would be consistent with a flat to slightly expansionary growth print in Q3 which, at face value, would suggest downside risks to their forecast of 0.3% q/q. Similarly, China growth has surprised negatively as well.

Partly reflecting the mediocre economic outlook, and the slowing US economy, a swathe of companies have surprised negatively during earnings announcements, and been penalised in both equity and credit markets. Although the negative news has been a focus, if we look at spread moves in €-IG from 9-24 July, 24 tickers widened on average by more than 10bp  – while 19 tickers tightened on average by more than 10bp. As such, the by-ticker spread reaction (up until COB 24 July) had been fairly balanced.

European credit markets as a whole have traded with a growth-concerns footprint ( Figure 1), with high-beta and HY credit underperforming IG, and CDS underperforming cash.

What do we think this means in terms of positioning? With growth softening, both in Europe and abroad, we have little desire to fade the underperformance of HY cash vs IG cash. In fact, the HY-IG cash underperformance and news around names such as Auchan, supports our view that Xover should decompress to Main.

Main, in turn, has widened vs cash amid low supply and contagion from US equities. We have no desire to fade this underperformance either. In fact, in our view Main could keep widening vs cash were CTAs to de-risk meaningfully from current levels. This is more so the case given how tight Main is looking vs SX5E ( Figure 2). In short: own IG cash, hedge with Main.

Digging further in, the current market dynamics support our cautious view on cyclical sectors (which still trade tight, adjusted for the rating of cyclical companies) in IG and HY, as well as our Underweight rating on Autos in both IG and HY.

With more volatility, also at the single-name level, it is comforting that in cash space across IG and HY, turnover for cash bonds has increased YTD – the topic of our IG, HY and Focus sections.

In IG, both caused by market growth and increased turnover, trading volumes have risen meaningfully y/y, led by financials. The increase in volumes has occurred in a very uniform fashion: large cap stack do not appear to constitute a disproportionate fraction of the overall trading volumes.

In HY, trading volumes have been increasing and excluding new issues, turnover is the highest in PE HY since at least 2012 and the number of bonds which do not trade at all in a given month is also very low. While this paints a very positive picture of HY liquidity, the flipside is that the trading activity has become highly concentrated: the top 10 tickers account for just north of 25% of all volumes – the highest proportion since 2016.

On the derivative side, the picture is more mixed. CDS index volumes are lower y/y across the board – in the US more than Europe, and HY more than IG. In contrast, credit ETF and TRS volumes are generally up, with the notable exception of US HY products. Single-name CDS volumes are unchanged across Europe and the US, although net risk of US single-name CDS is meaningfully higher. In contrast, net risk in European HY single-name has dropped, which we attribute to the tail of illiquid entities in Xover which prevents index-intrinsic arbitrage trades from supplying liquidity to the single-name market.

## US and European macro products and single-name CDS activity - ETF and TRS on the up

CDS index volumes are lower y/y across the board, the US more so than Europe, and HY more so than IG. In contrast, credit ETFs and TRS have seen sizable increases y/y, with the exception of US HY. Single-name CDS volumes are unchanged, with net notional up for US entities, but down for European HY.

## CDS indices: Lower volumes amid a general lack of volatility, with CDX.HY down the most y/y

Aside from short-lived bouts of volatility in mid-April and in European markets in June, markets remained mostly calm in 1H 24, which we think contributed to lower CDS index volumes y/y across IG and HY CDS indices ( Figure 1 and Figure 2).

CDX IG and HY volumes showed sharper drops vs 1H 23 (-9% and -17%, respectively) than iTraxx Main and Xover volumes (-4% and -10%, respectively), with the latter two benefiting from the bout of French election-induced volatility in Europe. In general, CDX HY and Xover have shown sharper drops than CDX IG and Main, which we attribute to two factors. First, economic growth has surprised positively in both regions, reducing the desire to express negative views on growth-sensitive risk. Second, idiosyncratic risk has picked up meaningfully (in particular in Xover, which also has fewer constituents), and this could also serve to reduce the desire to express macro views using these indices, as single-name news can drive the performance of a macro trade.

Despite the small y/y drop across indices, activity levels and liquidity remained healthy, which, based on anecdotal evidence, was supported by continued strong interest in relative value trading and significant participation in the CDS index market by CTAs. <sup>1</sup> We expect both of these to remain important drivers of activity in H2 24, which, when combined with the more traditional uses of CDS indices (placeholders for cash, expressing directional views, hedging), should support volumes, especially with the US election still ahead.

# Credit ETFs: Sizable y/y increases for US IG and loans and IEAC and IHYG in Europe, while US HY ETF activity continued to decline

To assess activity in the US credit ETF market, we aggregate turnover data for the five largest investment grade and high yield ETFs and the three largest loan ETFs by AUM. For European activity, we use aggregated IEAC volumes for investment grade and aggregated IHYG volumes for high yield.

In the US, high yield ETF volumes declined again, by 11% y/y ( Figure 3). Lower HY ETF activity is consistent with the decline in CDX.HY volumes that was previously discussed, and in-line with lower HY TRS volumes (which we discuss in the next section). US high yield is the only market where we observed a decline in activity across all three portfolio products - CDX, ETFs, and TRS. We think that this is likely due to a continued shift away from macro concerns and toward single-name selection to generate returns, especially with high yield index spreads historically tight but dispersion elevated. The decline in HY ETF activity was not uniform, however. HYG volumes were down a more modest 6%, JNK and SHYG were down more than 40%, and USHY and SPHY volumes were actually up, 20% and 381%, respectively, with the latter starting from a low base.

In contrast to the decline in overall US HY ETF volumes, investment grade and loan ETF volumes increased, by 23% and 22%, respectively ( Figure 3 and Figure 4). For loans, the rebound in activity is an encouraging sign, but volumes remain nearly 40% below 2022 levels. The rebound in IG ETF volumes has taken them to a new high, with activity levels supported by increased portfolio trading activity and what appears to be a shift in investor allocations, with the two short-term ETFs in the top five by AUM, VCSH and IGSB, experiencing nearly \$3bn in outflows year to date and the two intermediate-term ETFs, VCIT and IGIB receiving \$4.5bn of inflows (LQD, whose holdings tend to skew more intermediate to long-term, had \$1.5bn of inflows). Consistent with the fund flow trend, volumes for intermediate and long-term ETFs within the top five by AUM were 11-36% higher while volumes for the short-term ETFs were 26-29% lower.

For Europe ( Figure 4), IEAC has seen significant growth, with volumes up 57% in H1 vs the same period in 2023 - and a near doubling of trading volumes since 2022. We attribute this to the

continued adoption of portfolio trades in Europe, which motivates the use of ETFs to manage risks, in turn creating more liquidity in a virtuous circle. We note, however, that IEAC volumes, at an average of \$2.8bn per week, are less than an 8th of the weekly volumes of the large IG ETFs in the US: there is still ample space for growth. The IHYG ETF has also seen meaningful growth, up 27% y/y, balancing the reduction in Xover volumes.

## TRS: IBOXHY TRS activity continued to slide, while other TRS are up - especially QW5A (EU IG)

In the US, TRS activity picked up for both IG (+7%) and loans (+34%), but declined for HY (-20%). As previously discussed, we think that HY portfolio product volumes have declined amid a greater focus on single-name selection. Even with the decline, IBOXHY TRS remains the most active TRS contract, averaging more than \$1.1bn/week in volume, while the gains in the other TRS have come from a lower base ( Figure 5).

For Europe, the QW5A IG TRS has seen significant growth - both compared to the lull of H1 23, but also relative to 2022. The increase in trading volumes in both the IG TRS and the IEAC ETF is noteworthy in the context of broadening adaptation of portfolio trading: there has been no sign that this has cannibalized volumes in cash portfolio products; if anything, the opposite has happened. IBOXXMJA volumes are little changed vs 2023, in contrast to the continued decline in IBOXHY trading volumes.

## Single-name CDS: Flattish volumes for corporates after 2023's surge

Single-name corporate CDS volumes were mostly unchanged in 1H 24 in both the Americas and Europe, after the meaningful increase in 2023 ( Figure 6). European sovereign CDS volumes were also flat, while Americas sovereign volumes declined but remained 11% above 2022 levels.

For the US, anecdotal evidence suggests that bank hedging flows (whether new hedges or unwinds of existing ones) remain an important driver of corporate CDS volumes for both index and non-index names. Index arb has also been active this year, more so recently in CDX.HY than in CDX.IG, and we expect it to continue to support single-name CDS activity, especially in the less liquid names.

As in the US, European corporate CDS volumes <sup>2</sup> are mostly unchanged relative to H1 2023. We expect idiosyncratic risk - both in terms of refinancing concerns and the focus on potential LBOs - to continue, allowing single-name volumes to stay at these elevated levels.

## Single-name net notionals point to increased risktaking

Another angle to gauge the activity in single-name CDS is to look at the evolution of singlename CDS net notional, or open interest <sup>3</sup> . Looking across European and US single-name CDS, net notional declined meaningfully from 2015 (and earlier) to 2018, after which net risk was sideways for years ( Figure 8). This has changed with year-on-year increases in both 2023 and 2024.

We see this as a healthy sign for the single-name CDS market: away from volumes in general having increased since 2022, the amount of risk being taken (long and short) has generally increased as well. It would appear that 'the reports of the single-name CDS market's death are greatly exaggerated'.

Where has the increase in single-name net risk come from? Dissecting the change from July 2022 to now by region and type of single-name CDS ( Figure 9) the answer is clear: the US singlename CDS market has driven this, with increases north of 20% across IG, HY, and financials. In contrast, European IG and financials have seen more modest growth - and European HY singlename CDS have even seen a decrease in net risk.

We believe that the stagnant European HY single-name CDS market can be attributed to the near-absence of index-intrinsic arbitrage trades being executed in Xover, owing to a long tail of highly illiquid entities. The fact that European HY single-name CDS have "underperformed" in a net risk evolution sense vs the rest of the market is, to us, another reason why the Xover index rules should be changed: either a reduction in the number of entities, or by changing the rules to allow more 'cross-over type' names to become eligible.

## Trading volume trends

€-IG trading volumes are up, driven by both market growth and increased turnover. Looking under the hood, activity looks balanced, with only a tiny fraction of the market not trading at all in a given month, and no major single-name concentration.

Trading activity in €-IG picked up significantly in the first half of the year, with monthly volumes comfortably surpassing the highs of 2020/21 for both seasoned bonds and new issues ( Figure 1). In line with seasonal patterns, volumes peaked in January and have gradually declined since. In spite of the subsequent drop-of, volumes have still been notably higher than in previous years. Although this has coincided with heavy supply volumes in primary, trading activity hasn't just been a function of market growth as turnover has picked up meaningfully – putting an end to a multi-year downward trend ( Figure 2). As with volumes, turnover has also fallen since January but remains above 2021/22 levels.

Trading volumes in H1 24 picked up across sectors, led by Financials, as sector turnover rose meaningfully. Volumes in Banks, Other Financials, REITs and Other Utilities increased the most relative to previous years.

Greater volumes and higher turnover point to a more active and liquid €-IG market. However, these metrics don't measure the breadth of activity in the market as a small number of very actively traded names may 'hide' a long tail of illiquid bonds. Digging into this, the top 10 names by YTD volumes account for 15% of all trading volumes. While this sounds high, those 10 issuers account for 12% of the index. More generally, the biggest cap stacks see greater trading volumes but it doesn't look disproportionate to index weight: the top 20% of issuers by notional saw 21% of the H1 24 trading volume. That's not to say that single-name volatility has not been elevated or doesn't impact trading volumes – a number of story names have recorded outsized volumes, such as THAMES and PBBGR  – but these aren't significant enough in size to drive the overall market, unlike in HY  (see the HY section for more).

We also measure how many bonds in the market don't trade at all in a given month. Whether we do this by count or notional-weighted, the result is consistent – only a tiny fraction of the market falls into this category. After peaking above 3% in 2020, this measure has come down meaningfully and currently stands at 0.1%, well below 2019 levels. We think that there being fewer bonds that never trade is a function of increased portfolio trading – a finding that is echoed in the \$-IG market (see More shocks in corporate bond volumes, 14 June 2024).

## £-IG

We see a similar pattern in the £-IG trading volumes but with a smaller magnitude than in €-IG. As in the Euro market, volumes started to pick up in the second half of 2023, peaked in January this year and have steadily declined since. While this means H1 24 volumes are notably up vs H1 23, the decline in trading activity since the January peak means that monthly volumes are returning to levels seen last year, rather than remaining elevated versus history ( Figure 4  & 5).

Dissecting the trading volume data, we find that trading in the £-IG market is slightly less balanced than the €-IG market. The top 10 names by YTD volumes account for 20% of the H1 volumes, while making up 14% of the index. THAMES unsurprisingly is a large contributor to this as it accounts for c.1% of the index but 3.5% of the H1 24 trading volumes. More broadly, the largest 20% of names by weight account for 25% of volumes – slightly higher than in €-IG, but not significantly over concentrated.

Finally, we also look at how much of the market doesn't trade at all in a given month, and see that this number has fallen. While there is some month to month volatility, such as in September 2023,  the proportion of bonds that haven't traded in a given month has come down and currently stands around 1% ( Figure 6).

Despite late July usually being associated with a summer wind-down, a number of idiosyncratic situations in recent weeks - whether earnings related or restructuring related - has kept activity higher than perhaps expected for this time of year. With this in mind, we take a look at recent trends in trading volumes and turnover and show that, after two years of declining volumes and tepid turnover, 2024 has bucked that trend with a stark improvement in both.

Indeed,  Figure 1 shows that total trading volumes over the past 12 months have jumped by \~€60bn relative to the lows in 2023, and in fact 2024 has seen the second highest trading volumes through June - at €190bn - over the past 10 years ( Figure 2) and up 24% and 8% versus 2023 and 2022, respectively. Note, for the purposes of definition we utilise Traxx data to track trading volumes, with our analysis suggesting that this covers on average 65% of all trades (we do not scale accordingly, with our focus instead on the trend rather than the absolute volumes).

## Liquidity appears healthier: fewer bonds (ex. CCCs) never trade

Consistent with what we find above, we would also argue that liquidity appears to have improved based on the percentage of bonds that never trade in a given month, which as  Figure 6 shows, has been consistently low for much of the past two to three years, having been structurally higher in the pre-2018 period especially. On average, in 2024 to-date, the

percentage of bonds that do not trade in a given month is less than 1% of the index, which is consistent with what we have seen in the past few years, versus 3-6% consistently for much of the pre-2018 period. We would view the decrease in bonds that don't trade from 2018 onwards as consistent with the increasing use of portfolio trading especially.

FIGURE 6. The percentage of bonds that never trade remains very low

Looking at that breakdown by rating in Figure 7, perhaps unsurprisingly almost all BBs trade - in fact the average per month that have not traded in the past 12 months is a miniscule 0.5%. For single-Bs that number is 0.9% - also very low but up slightly in the past year, while for CCCs that number is comparatively much higher at 3.6% and has been on the rise since the start of 2023 to rival the peak from 2021. In our view, this speaks to the high number of restructuring situations currently in the market - of which most are rated CCC - which subsequently means trading activity can be constrained until there is some sort of outcome.

## But the concentration of volumes is the most since 2016

A pushback to the above as it pertains to improving liquidity is that we have seen a higher concentration of total index trading volumes in a small number of topical situations. And this is true when we look at the data - the top three names this year by volumes have, and by some distance, been SFRFP, GRFSM and INTRUM - all of which have had very idiosyncratic credit stories attached ( Figure 9). And when we look at how much the top 10 accounts for total trading volumes year-to-date, we estimate that to be 27%, with the equivalent amounts for top 3 and 20 being 13% and 38%, respectively ( Figure 10). What perhaps makes this more interesting is that in pure notional weight terms within the index, the top 3 and 10 account for only 4% and 15%, respectively. In other words, these names are punching well above their weight in terms of trading volumes.

## Rising redemptions

With 33% of deals out of their reinvestment periods, 30% of AAAs are currently amortising (including a few static deals) while 24 deals have paid down/called so far this year. On a cumulative basis, 10% of CLO 2.0 AAAs have paid down. We expect more call activity given 13.5% of AAAs are factored down to 80% or below.

New issue activity continues to keep pace, with more than €30bn priced year to date, which is higher than the full-year issuance in both 2023 or 2022 and we raised our issuance forecast to a record €40-45bn, supported by new loan issuance ( Forecasting record issuance, 25 June 2024). In spite of record supply from CLO managers, spreads continue to tighten. AAAs are now printing at 125-130bp in the primary market and 96bp in the secondary market (according to PSL) and some shorted-dated AAAs could trade inside 90bp in secondary. Therefore, demand remains strong and we see continued warehouse creation. Even as new deals are issued, more deals are exiting their reinvestment periods (RP) such that 33% of deals by count are out of their RPs currently. Hence, demand is also supported by the redeployment of capital returned to investors in the form of paydown and call activity of deals outside their RPs. We have observed €13.7bn of redemptions in 2024, either through amortisation or calls.

Call activity increased: 24 deals have been fully paid down in 2024, significantly higher than in any other prior year of CLO 2.0s ( Figure 2). These deals add up to €7bn of paydown from the start of the year. With €65bn out of RP at the start of 2024, YTD call volumes represent 10.8%, an increase from 7.2% just two months ago ( A long wait, 24 May 2024). Historically, 10% of all CLO 2.0 AAAs issued have paid down. This has risen sharply in 2024, in spite of the increase in the overall CLO 2.0 universe ( Figure 1).
