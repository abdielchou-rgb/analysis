# Citius, Altius, Fortius

The Olympic motto reads "Faster, Higher, Stronger – Together." It could have been used as the motto for central bank policies, inflation, and growth in 2022 and 2023. "Slower, Lower, Weaker – Together" seems a more appropriate description in 2024, unless recent weakness in equities extends further.

## Will the Linchpin of Global Risk Sentiment Get Pulled?

The last two-and-a-half weeks saw a marked decline in risky asset prices. The Nasdaq 100, which fell 9.5% from high to low, and the Nikkei 225 Index, which fell 11.4% from high to low, led the decline among major indexes.

The declines occurred alongside more severe price action in semiconductor stocks. The Philadelphia Stock Exchange Semiconductor Index fell 17% from high to low – the most acute decline in semiconductor stocks since their major lows in 2022.

The eye-opening price action did not appear connected to any particular macro economic factor. Yes, China and Germany delivered disappointing growth data during the rout. But those data seemed coincidental, not explanatory or reactionary.

Importantly, these equity market declines occurred alongside easier monetary policies across a large number of central banks year-to-date, with the most recent easing in China and Canada. If the equity sell-off continues, investors may start to question their prevailing concern that easier central bank policies would support risky assets.

The wobbles in risky assets shines the spotlight most brightly on US economic data – particularly data describing growth – given the exceptional strength shown by US economy year-to-date. In many ways, the health of the US economy is the linchpin of global risk sentiment.

Our economists expect the upcoming US employment report to show a "still-strong labor market" with total payroll growth at 195k and private payroll growth at 155k. At the same time, they flag important downside risks:

There are important downside risks from Hurricane Beryl, from seasonal factors, and from teacher employment. Hurricane Beryl interrupted activity but we expect it slowed hours more than payrolls. Seasonal factors for July have shifted since before the pandemic; we think the shifts are appropriate but if they are not, there's downside risk. We have allowed for some drag from public sector education payrolls, but it's possible that the drag is larger (or smaller).

The downside risk from seasonal factors our economists discuss comes from private payrolls. Another source of risk may come from government payrolls. Exhibit 1 shows that the seasonal adjustment for private payrolls in July averaged only +39k over the past decade – the smallest adjustment in absolute value terms for any month of the year.

Exhibit 2 shows that, for government payrolls, the seasonal adjustment averaged +1.12mm over the past decade – meaning, the BLS expected just over a million government employees to go off the payroll in July, so it added them back to its nonseasonally-adjusted estimate of payroll growth.

• The July seasonal adjustment to government payrolls represents the largest adjustment of the year and the most volatile over the past decade due to a sharp decline after the pandemic.

If one of these risks to private or government payrolls realize this coming week, we think investors would take more signal from the downside miss in private payrolls. Nevertheless, if both risks realize at the same time, we would expect a larger market reaction than if only one realized.

• A downside surprise in both private and government payrolls would mean lower US Treasury yields, a steeper US Treasury curve, and a weaker US dollar. We think risky assets would suffer as well, given that we think investors would lower their growth expectations more than their inflation expectations.

Our economists also discussed undocumented immigration in their employment report preview. They suggested that the payroll benchmark revision – delivered on August 21 and based on the QCEW (Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages) – would revise lower employment lower, but understate employment growth.

QCEW probably undercounts the number of unauthorized immigrants who are working. Many of the unauthorized immigrants are not authorized to work and are ineligible for unemployment insurance. They are therefore not part of the unemployment insurance system's records and cannot be counted in the QCEW.

Our economists believe the divergence between the payroll (establishment) measure of employment and the household measure of employment also comes down to

undocumented immigration.

## Expect more JPY outperformance vs CNH

This past week, investor debate has centred on the unwind of various of carry trades in FX markets. Alongside rotation in equities, with semiconductors underperforming relative to small- and mid-cap stocks, there has also been rotation from cyclical or growth sensitive currencies into low yielders, primarily JPY, with markets more generally exhibiting risk-off tone with major stock indices declining alongside a rally in government bonds.

While each of these drivers has had its own dynamics and idiosyncratic drivers, the weakness in activity data coming from Eurozone and China raised some concerns about global growth, following the softer data seen from the US in the first half of July. Our economists see downside risks to their forecasts for both the Eurozone and also China.

Yet, these data misses were relatively small and don’t on their own explain the extent of the decline in risk assets. JPY strength likely added to investor anxiety over the performance of risk assets and global economic activity, given the JPY's reputation as a safe haven, even though JPY strength has largely been driven by domestic factors or factors unrelated to the global business cycle.

The weakness in data in the rest of the world that we have seen supports our general expectation for the USD to rebound following the weakness earlier in the month. We continue to hold this long USD bias.

Admittedly, there is an inherent tension between our generally upbeat view on USD and the risk that carry trades are unwound on the back of global growth concerns, since the USD is generally a high carry currency versus much of global GDP.

Long USD vs Asia is after all a carry trade. Yet the cross should also gain some support as growth in China starts to slow.

Since concerns about China growth are a key factor behind FX behavior, we think it is contradictory to see USD/CNH actually fall as growth weakens and onshore equities fall. Staying long USD/CNH is the appropriate position, in our view, reflecting continued expectations of US outperformance and having the benefit of also being high carry, though the latter is incidental rather than motivating.

This week we saw USD/CNY fall in sympathy with USD/JPY, alongside an unwinding of carry trades. In the end though, we expect to see some divergence between USD/CNH and USD/JPY, and see JPY continuing to outperform CNH.

In the short term, we expect to see a rate hike from the BoJ at the upcoming policy meeting. This should boost JPY, by helping to compress yield differentials relative to the US. The FOMC also meets next week on the 31st July, and should signal it is close to cutting rates. The team expects to see lower 2y UST yields, helping the yield compression with JPY rates.

In China, the PBOC has been easing policy and onshore CNY FX-implied yields have dropped sharply to around 1% in the past week and are at now at the bottom end of the range of the last 10 years ( Exhibit 17 ). Hedging onshore FX risk in China has thus rarely been cheaper, particularly given the high yields on offer in the USD. The yield differential of long JPY vs short CNY position has also substantially moved in JPY’s favour over the past four years ( Exhibit 18 ).

How the authorities in Tokyo and Beijing engage with the risk of tariffs is also likely to influence the performance of their currencies relative to the USD and each other. We expect this dynamic to result in JPY outperformance vs CNY/H.

## United States

Coming full circle: In March 2022, we noted that the 2s10s yield curve was going to invert, without necessarily suggesting that a recession would follow. Nearly two and a half years later, the 2s10s yield curve has been steepening recently and appears about to dis-invert again. And we still expect the US economy to stage a soft landing. Decomposing the yield curve, rate expectations led the flattening while term premiums have led the steepening, opening the door for the curve to be steeper versus the last 10 years.

July FOMC preview: We see a bullish bias to short-term yields driven by the Fed meeting, and some bearish election and seasonality-driven bias higher into long term yields – increasing the chance that the yield curve can continue to steepen. We maintain 2s20s steepeners into the July FOMC.

Treasury refunding preview: On Monday at 3:00pm ET, Treasury will release its updated privately-held net marketable borrowing estimates for 3Q24 and 4Q24. For 3Q24, we estimate \$649bn relative to the \$847bn estimated by Treasury before May refunding, following the lowering of the run-off cap for Treasury securities to \$25bn per month in June. At August refunding announcement on Wednesday 8.30am ET, we expect Treasury will keep both nominal coupon and FRN auction sizes unchanged, in accordance with its regular and predictable issuance strategy.

## Euro area

This week we saw further evidence of downside risk to growth in the EA. In addition, we found it interesting that the release of the PMIs evidenced some compression in margins and hints at a reduction in labour productivity, which could make the outlook for cuts more uncertain, considering the front-end-led rally seen.

Besides that, we note a normalization of duration valuations, but point out that, at least historically, at current residuals' levels any upside from fading the rally is likely to be limited in the context of still favorable seasonality for the first half of August and a slowdown in issuance.

## United Kingdom

The August MPC is eventually approaching, with the market pricing \~10bp of cuts. We expect a first cut to be delivered (5-4 voting split) but the messaging to lean to the hawkish side in order to reflect the dissenters' view, coupled however with dovish inflation projections. We recommend Sep'24-Feb'25 MPC flattener which provides a better risk-reward than receiving August outright in our view.

Investor attention will also turn on the fiscal news flow, as the FT reported that

Chancellor Rachel Reeves will outline the results of an audit of finances and possibly hint at tax rises this year. As there are already some modest upside risks to this fiscal-year gilt supply, any further increase remains a relevant downside risk for valuations, as the market could reprice in fiscal slippage risk.

That said, we expect some fiscal consolidation in the next Budget, which should bring tax hikes to the tune of at least \~£10 billion, and the fiscal framework to be changed only afterwards, during the course of this Parliament, with limited implications for near-term borrowing and gilt supply.

## Japan

We continue to see a flatter curve ahead of the July BoJ MPM. Media headlines have suggested that a July hike will be a close call. If the BoJ hikes the rate at this meeting, the market will likely speculate that the government and BoJ are now on the same page with regards to FX. We expect further hikes to be priced in if the BoJ delivers the hike, in line with our economists' forecast.

On the other hand, external developments suggest global carry unwinding. We believe that falling US interest rates should—along with falls in USD/JPY— contribute to a narrowing of the term premiums priced into super-long JPY rates. Overall, we expect the curve to face flattening pressure in the event that the BoJ does opt to hike its policy rate at the end of this month.

In the JGB space, we see a risk that Japanese lifers may accelerate their purchases of long-end JGBs post the BoJ MPM. We see particular scope for super-long JGBs to outperform during “risk off” phases, as players look to lock in carry ahead of the summer vacation period.

Given our expectation that the super-long sector will continue to be supported by a mix of BoJ rate hike speculation and “risk off” sentiment, we close our JGB 20s30s steepener, part of our long JGB 5s20s30s fly recommendation, and shift to a JGB 5s20s flattener. We also maintain our 5s10s JGB ASW box flatteners in anticipation of 10y ASW valuations richening towards fair value, to at least some degree, after the BoJ’s “detailed plan” for QT is unveiled.

## Yield curve comes full circle

In March 2022, as rate hikes were being priced in, the yield curve was flattening quickly, heading toward inversion. Collaborating with our US banking and economics teams, among others, we noted that the 2s10s yield curve was going to invert, without necessarily suggesting that a recession would follow (see Living with Yield Curve Inversion). Our economists have maintained a call for a "soft landing" in the US.

Nearly two and a half years later, the 2s10s yield curve has been steepening recently and appears about to dis-invert again, having spent the longest time being inverted. And we still expect the US economy to see a soft landing. The yield curve inverted, indeed – without a recession this time.

Exhibit 19: 2s10s curve in the last 20 years
![](images/dcdf548266961c8abfe814afa11528fa63caaac33252452e148c28e8029e1b21.jpg)
Source: Bloomberg, Morgan Stanley Research

So why did we think in 2022 that curve inversion was different at the time? Two reasons. First, we saw the Fed rate path inverting as a sign of the Fed staying above neutral and ultimately going to neutral. This is in contrast to past inversions that suggested a Fed path for easing in response to a weak economy. Second, we noted that the depression of term premiums post-GFC – for a multitude of reasons including QE – had made the curve artificially flatter. The framework has broadly played out in the last two and half years.

The curve can be analyzed by looking at the 2s10s curve broken down into: 1) the rate expectations (RE) curve, and 2) the term premium (TP) curve. Notably, the RE curve drove the bulk of the curve flattening, while the TP steepening has brought the curve near dis-inversion ( Exhibit 20 ). This means that, as the RE curve will steepen with rate cuts, the spot curve will be able to steepen to higher levels than in the last decade since \~2015.

As we have noted recently, term premiums could stay elevated especially as the focus on the fiscal picture remains heightened during the US election season in the coming months ( Exhibit 24 ). And as rate cuts start, the RE curve, by design, will steepen ( Exhibit 21 ). We continue to suggest positioning in steepeners via 2s20s steepeners.

## July FOMC preview: From timing to pace

Market context into the meeting: The US economy has shown some signs of cooling, and inflation in 2Q24 has looked much cooler than what we saw in 1Q24 – a reminder of the possible residual seasonality that may be affecting the progress on core PCE inflation. Additionally, cooler rent inflation in June has opened the door to continued cooler CPI prints going forward, and should have added to confidence on inflation by both investors and the Fed.

This backdrop has allowed the market to gravitate toward a September 25bp cut as a given. The market has generally been skeptical of pricing a series of continuous cuts, and even now the focus has shifted from the timing of cuts to the pace of cuts after September.

Our base case remains for there to be three 25bp cuts in 2024, starting in September, through November and December. The market currently prices a shallower cutting cycle, spaced in a pattern that shows markets pricing a higher probability of a cut every alternate meeting ( Exhibit 22 ) – a once-a-quarter pace. On the other hand, our base case is that the Fed delivers seven back-to-back cuts through June 2025 – opening a gap between our pricing of rate cuts and the market's ( Exhibit 23 ).

Our economists do not expect the Fed to deliver much guidance on the pace of cuts, even if the Fed shows readiness for cutting rates in September. And the absence of a dot plot makes it harder for markets to tease out the right pace. Investors will look for key phrases like "mid-cycle adjustment" or "insurance cuts" – words that were used during the rate cuts in 2019, when the Fed delivered three back-to-back 25bp cuts from July through November.

Elections are also likely to loom large heading into the meeting, and we do not expect the Fed itself to make any comments about the presidential election as is the historical practice. However, markets will continue to grapple and consider the elections – and we think this will keep the long end of the Treasury curve under pressure to price higher yields ( Exhibit 24 ). Adding to that, the risk of August/September seasonality in which investor focus on positive carry (i.e., short duration currently) ( Exhibit 25 ) drives long-end yields higher and the curve steeper.

Overall, we see a bullish bias to short-term yields driven by the Fed meeting, and some bearish election and seasonality-driven bias higher into long-term yields – increasing the chance that the yield curve can continue to steepen. We maintain 2s20s steepeners into the July FOMC.

## More on the STGRDI index

The ten sub-indices of the GRDI monitor the daily price change of the following assets:

• GRDI1: Compares the performance of a broad EM debt benchmark index (EMUSTRUU Index) with an one for developed markets (SBWDMUU Index).

• GRDI2: Compares the performance of an industrial metals index (SPGSIN Index) to one for precious metals (SPGSPM Index).

• GRDI3: Compares the performance of a global equity index (MXWO Index) with an investment-grade DM government bond index (SBWDMUU Index).

• GRDI4: Compares the performance of two stock indices, namely a large-cap US growth stock index (RLG Index) to one for large-cap value stocks (RLV Index).

• GRDI5: Tracks daily changes in the volatility of the US stock markets (VIX index).

• GRDI6: Tracks daily changes in the implied bond volatility of the US Treasury market (MOVE index).

• GRDI7: Compares the daily changes in average G10 FX 1 month and 3 month ATM implied volatility, both in terms of level changes and the

## Maintain long 1y1y CPI swap

• The volatilities of the constituent rates

• The correlation between the constituent rates

As Exhibit 117 shows, for a fixed correlation assumption, curve volatility is locally positively correlated with constituent vanilla vols – especially if vanilla vega were to drift lower. This begs the question: What is stopping correlations from further driving a divergence between curve vega and vanilla vega? From purely a technical perspective, implied

5y2s10s correlations are at the 90th percentile over the past year, suggesting it might be more likely to observe lower, rather than higher, implied correlations going forward. This is especially true for investors who hold twist-steepening outlooks given:

• Weak economic data could lead the front end of the curve to rally;

• Fiscal and political outlooks could lead the back end of the curve to sell off

However, forward curves have historically been more correlated than what is currently priced. Exhibit 119 shows the 6m rolling correlation between 5y forward rates since 1993 (yellow), as well as the median correlation observed (dark blue). Our takeaways are:

• Current pricing (87% on 5y2s10s) is well below the median (95%), indicating that we are still rich to a historical fair value

• While it is not uncommon to see a drop in correlations during cutting cycles (COVID and GFC), our economists' preferred historical comparison (1995) saw above-median correlations

Thus, while curve vega has been cheapening versus vanilla vega, we think that a correction of the trade should only be considered as a tactical short-term trade for investors with a twist-steepening bias. Investors could set up the trade via the following package:

• Long \$100mn notional 5y2s10s straddles

• Short \$16.8mn notional 5y2y straddles

• Short \$2.4mn notional 5y10s straddles

We think this weighting would constitute a vol neutral proposition, under the assumption that 5y2s10s vega is bucketed as \~60% 5y2y and \~40% 5y10y. This trade can be entered by collecting 3.5bp up front, and has near-zero theta and vega upon entry.

# G4 Smarter (beta) Trading Strategy

MORGAN STANLEY & CO. LLC

Matthew Hornbach, CMT Matthew.Hornbach@morganstanley.com

+1 212 761-1837

Enhancements to a G4 10y government bond futures momentum strategy have produced higher Sharpe ratios and stronger returns, relative to total return government bond indices for the G4, US, Germany, Japan, and the UK since 2000. See A "Smarter" (Beta) Way to Trade G4 10y Futures Duration? for more information on these strategies.

## Trading Strategy 1 – "Trade Longs/Fade Shorts"

When the 5-day moving average crosses above the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract (long duration) and hold for a 25-business-day period. When the 5-day moving average crosses below the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract and hold for a 25-business-day period. In short, this strategy buys futures when the Simple Moving Average Crossover (SMAX) generates both a long and a short signal, given the historical outperformance of long signals traded long and the underperformance of short signals traded short. Given that the SMAX could generate both a long and a short signal within the predefined holding period, an investor may have a 200% long position since each of the two signals would be traded in separate portfolio sleeves.

## Trading Strategy 2 – Trade "Longs Only"

When the 5-day moving average crosses above the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract (long duration) and hold for a 25-business-day period. When the 5-day moving average crosses below the 20-day moving average, do nothing. In short, an investor ONLY trades long signals initiated by the SMAX given their historical precedent to outperform.

## Definition of terms

Buy/Long: The analyst expects the total or excess return (depending on the nature of the recommendation) of the instrument or issuer that is the subject of the investment recommendation to be positive over the relevant time period.

Sell/Short: The analyst expects the total or excess return (depending on the nature of the recommendation) of the instrument or issuer that is the subject of the investment recommendation to be negative over the relevant time period.

Selling protection or Buying Risk: The analyst expects that the price of protection against the event occurring will decrease over the relevant time period.

Buying protection or Selling Risk: The analyst expects the price of protection against the event occurring will increase over the relevant time period.

Pay: The analyst expects that over the specified time period the variable rate underlying the swap agreement that is the subject of the investment recommendation will increase.

Receive: The analyst expects that over the specified time period the variable rate underlying the swap agreement that is the subject of the investment recommendation will decrease.

Unless otherwise specified, the time frame for recommendations included in the Morgan Stanley Fixed Income Research reports is 1 - 3 months and the price of financial instruments mentioned in the recommendation is as at the date and time of publication of the recommendation.

When more than one issuer or instrument is included in a recommendation, analyst expects one part of the trade to outperform the other trade or combination of other trades included in the recommendation on a relative basis.
