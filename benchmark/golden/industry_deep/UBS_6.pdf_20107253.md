In our Spotlight calls, we pose a series of questions to sector analysts on relevant ESG considerations in their sector. Key discussions from our Cap Goods call are summarised below.

## What ESG considerations are driving investment theses in your sector?

ESG considerations in Cap goods fall largely into two key areas:

1. Companies as "enablers" of ESG – primarily the E – energy efficiency, electrification, energy transition, broader production efficiency through automation driving down usage of resources and materials (efficient designs, processes, better quality etc).

2. ESG as a differentiator – particularly interesting where otherwise comparable companies can be differentiated in terms of relative ESG progress or exposure – eg comparing a Kone vs Schindler or Schneider vs Legrand. The focus is on the ‘enablers’ – ie where a push to higher ESG standards drives demand or poses risk to demand or business model.

As discussed in the Cap Goods ESG Company Radars, overall we see the global capital goods sector as a net beneficiary, as the trends derived from the energy transition will in most cases provide an incremental growth opportunity for the sector. We see electrical equipment manufacturers as one of the earliest beneficiaries of this trend, as growth in investment towards renewable generation as well as associated infrastructure is likely to accelerate first (over the next 10-15 years), though the overall impact on the sector is likely to be quite broad-based. As the sector provides equipment and components that drive increasing efficiency in operations for manufacturing companies globally, we see it as an "enabler" for the energy and sustainability transitions. This is particularly true for companies whose offering is capex related, as there is rarely a conflict between investing in new, more efficient, capital goods equipment and reducing CO2 emissions, or transitioning to electricity as a source of energy. On the contrary: driving efficiency improvements in customer operations via innovative products and solutions goes to the core of what drives top-line growth, pricing power and value creation in the capital goods industry. There are many examples of how the energy transition is likely to increase both overall investment levels in capital goods equipment and offer capital goods companies an opportunity to increase their share of customer wallets.

Of course, the impact is not all positive; we think companies/businesses exposed to fossil-fuel end-markets are most at risk of declining business (e.g. companies exposed to oil & gas capex, fossil-fuel based generation), though we believe this now forms less than 10-15% of the sector's overall revenues (but would be higher/lower at a company level).

## Segment Performance

Energy efficiency is a long established trend which became more urgent in 2022, and electrification is now coming to drive demand hand in hand with that. Obviously electrification is impossible to enable by just adding renewables; energy efficiency improvements are required to really switch away from gas. We have seen clear outperformance including at the relevant divisions of players like Schneider Electric, ABB, Siemens Smart Infrastructure business, but also from players like Siemens Energy and Vestas, and players like Alfa Laval, Atlas Copco, and Legrand.

If we look at electrification overall, on organic growth basis, in 2023 revenues in that space have ended at about 50% above 2019 level. That's with about 20 percentage points of price, but nearly 30% points of volume growth clearly outgrowing the broader buildings space which is up about 15-17%, and the sector average is at about that level too.

Other areas of interest are automation and digitalization, as a key driver. We look at manufacturing, industrial automation and digitalization equipment, and digitalization that comes in the form of industrial software. Also of interest is industrial IOT: connectivity for monitoring remotely and eventually enabling true smart buildings i.e. own decision making for various equipment that surrounds us in buildings, but also is deployed in all sorts of manufacturing facilities. Companies with strong automation offerings include Hexagon (Manufacturing Intelligence business), Siemens (Digital Industries), Schneider (Industrial Automation), and ABB (Robotics & Discrete Automation, Process Automation and Motion), as well as players like Andritz, Valmet, Krones and KION.

The other area to flag on this is energy transition, where the sector is very thematically exposed. The main play is through wind, with Vestas as a pure play, and Siemens Energy (with c35% revenues coming from Siemens Gamesa). We also have other areas of that value chain such as Wärtsilä with battery energy storage, Andritz with hydro storage, Alfa Laval with heat pumps, and equipment related to hydrogen production, and Spirax-Sarco, who have a plan to decarbonise industrial heat generation with Medium Voltage heat-exchangers. The other two areas to flag are electrification of mobility: electric vehicles as part of that, but it's much broader than just cars, and also water; select exposures in companies like Rotork, Halma, Geberit in Switzerland, Krones, Andritz, GEA, and Alfa Laval that contribute with their equipment to savings and efficiency.

Water is a topic of perennial interest (examples of published research include The Last Compounder: Upgrade to Buy and Showing that Oil & Water do mix).

Datacentres in particular are a (relatively) new but key area of interest. As discussed on the Spotlight call, the most contentious areas are fulfilling power demand, and cooling. Very strong demand expectations will be subject to power supply, and equipment providers being able to ramp up to fulfil those demands.

On the cooling side, a move to GPUs increases the power intensity & density of a rack by c.5x, which obviously increases heat production, which then becomes difficult to air cool. As a result, a move towards liquid cooling is required; one solution is to put a heat exchanger on the back of a server rack and feed cold liquid (typically water) through it. We are also seeing an increase in discussions about feeding cooling solutions directly through the chips.

In terms of coverage exposure, Schneider Electric has the largest exposure within our coverage at c.20% of sales into data centres and networks. Legrand follows with c.15% exposure to networks. Schneider is present all the way from power supply, power security, distribution and then all the white space as well. In terms of electrification - transformers, medium voltage switch gear, that's where pricing was been the strongest with Siemens and ABB, we believe, being the best positioned in this area amongst UBS coverage. Halma has some interesting niche exposure to photonics which enable more data, more quickly. Additionally Alfa Laval also has some exposure via liquid cooled heat exchangers.

 US EPA rulings on methane emission penalties

 Shipping decarbonization – European and Global Maritime Organisation carbon tax potential.

On IRA in general, we haven't really seen the impact coming through yet. IRA also subsidizes hydropower, but again we haven't seen the positive impact yet. The same holds true for Wärtsilä, one of the global market leaders in battery storage with significant exposure to the US market. The company has seen exponential growth in the last few years and is expecting significant IRA impact (which hasn't come through yet).

Another clear driver is shipping decarbonisation. At a significant recent meeting in London of the International Maritime Organization we expect discussion and possible passing of a global carbon tax (the MEPC 81 meeting saw broader agreement on a global carbon levy and fuel standard, with draft outline of legislation agreed & approval expected in 2025, see here). We've written at length about that topic last year in the UBS Q series: Shipping decarbonisation where we have said that we think a carbon tax is the most likely outcome in terms of fuel surcharge for bringing down CO2 consumption and bringing the fossil fuels on par with the prices of the renewable fuels. We see the shipping companies are adopting new fuel types. Methanol has been the most adopted fuel type so far, spearheaded by Maersk. We also see more action from the shipping companies on efficiency measures. And that's why also companies like Alfa Laval and Wärtsilä benefit in their order intake from this despite the fact that shipyard orders overall are relatively down. So they benefit content wise, the barriers to entry go up because those systems are more sophisticated. Also here, we can see the benefit already.

## Where do you see the grid space moving in the next few years? Ageing infrastructure, increasing demand and electrification require significant investment – which names in your coverage do you think are best placed to benefit?

A clearly positive outlook on both Transmission and Distribution grids. Distribution Grid spend is one of the key angles on the Electrification theme – electrification of vehicles (EVs, trucks, off highway), other transport (rail, vessels, aircraft), process industries (mining, green steel), new industries (hydrogen, batteries production), data centres growth. Buildings electrification is a further substantial driver that has accelerated post 2022 European energy crisis.

Space is sold out at the distribution grid level. On the transmission side players have been pushing to add capacity because they were sold sold out two or three years forward, and that is also becoming quite a bottleneck on the distribution side. You've got multiple drivers there and it's back to that electrification trend where it's vehicles, but just not just cars, it's the trucks and buses and all sorts of, of highway vehicles that are becoming electric that need charging. Then you've got all sorts of other transports like rail, like aircraft, like ships are becoming increasingly electric. We now have electric ferries which would have been unimaginable only a few years ago.

We also have some of the very large energy intensive process industries trying to electrify; mining is probably the brightest example. There are examples of projects in green steel. And then new industries like hydrogen and battery production, data centres, etc. These all need grid scale substations to connect them to supply power to them. The question is how are we going to generate that, and how are we going to be able to deliver power?

Another key consideration is lack of investment into the grid. Most investments in this energy transition thematic have been more on the generation side, they've seen significant build up of renewable power. But if you look at average investment in transmission and distribution in the last five years in real terms, it's been pretty flat globally. We've seen a slight uptick in the last two years and we would then expect this to further accelerate over the next seven years. On top of the increased demand, you also have an aged grid network. The European Commission recently put out a report along with the grid action plan showing that about 40% of Europe's distribution grid is 40 years old or older (see our note Not Fit for Purpose - Grid needs €584bn invested). Similarly, in the US, probably about a quarter to 30% of the grid is 40 years or older.

Then you have a changing generation pattern as well where there is increasing share of renewables, and one of the biggest issues with renewables is the variable nature of the resource and correspondingly there is an increased need to invest further in grid stability as well, and these aged structures will not be able to take on such a variable load. So there is a lot of investment that needs to go into the grids. We see the fastest growth in the medium voltage segment, followed by high voltage and then then on the low voltage side.

In terms of exposure, companies well positioned include:

 Schneider (70% of revenues from Energy Management),

 ABB (about half of revenues from Electrification Products),

 Siemens (25% of revenues form Smart Infrastructure division) and

 Siemens Energy (c.20% revenues from Grid Transmission).

## Have you seen any evidence of a green premium in the sector?

We believe any potential "green premium" can only be achieved if the thematic exposure is overlaid with company-level execution. For example:

 Schneider Electric at nearly 2x of the market multiple now vs just over 1x in 2015-2016 (1.4 vs 0.8-0.9x vs sector); similar picture but to a lesser extent ABB, Legrand.

 Alfa Laval premium vs other mid cap and Wärtsilä.

Though when companies face execution/ operational challenges, the ESG premium ascribed to valuations don't hold up (as recently seen in the case of Siemens Energy with the quality issues in their wind turbine business and for Alstom with their recent cash flow issues in 1HFY24, both of which saw sharp negative share price reactions and shares remaining under pressure since then).

Circular economy – have you seen much development here in your coverage? Is there capex being spent or planned to move towards a more circular economy? Or perhaps from a customer perspective, key sales? Any key hurdles?

 Buildings – clear shift towards remodelling – outright positive for Cap Goods.

 Industrial software penetration – product or asset lifecycle management, asset performance management.

 Machinery – Repair / refurbish – SKF for bearings and RecondOil.

In terms of circular economy exposure, there are several angles. One is buildings which is actually one of the largest sub verticals in terms of demand for capital goods companies. There is everything from the electrical inputs of supplying power to the building and all the way to the consumption points of sockets and switches, but also companies supplying elevators, locks, lavatories, lighting systems, smoke detectors in residential applications, swimming pools, equipment and appliances, etc. And here, the focus is clearly shifting towards remodeling rather than new construction which has some interesting implications because you don't consume the concrete and rebar steel, but you do need all the equipment.

There are also areas like glass insulation that get replaced. We think the future of multistorey construction is taking the existing building, stripping it down to bare concrete and metal structures, and building them out. Also changing purpose potentially from office to multi use, from office to residential, creating recreational spaces, etc. Within that, the entire suite of cap goods products is involved and often with a higher density than what the original construction was. Some companies are more exposed to others, eg in the electrification or insulations space. But this looks to be a rising tide that lifts all boats.

We also thought about it from the perspective of industrial software that is designed for product life cycle management. So that's when you design the product and think about all the stages of its life through usage upgrades. Maybe remodeling if that product is a building, and all the way to eventual decommissioning and how all the pieces that have gone into it can be reused. It's the same with asset life cycle management: When you have a number of assets, there are interesting software offers that allow you to manage that with better efficiency with predictive maintenance. That industrial software space is growing at double digit pace, and has shown some really interesting resilience vs in the past where in a down cycle like COVID or great financial crisis, the growth rate would slow towards zero. Maybe you have like a tiny single digit dip, but really growth slows and then re accelerates back up as opposed to having actual volume downturn. We're also seeing a broader move towards repair / refurbish.

For most names we can find examples where they have offers geared more towards rebuilding, refurbishing a product as opposed to trying to push for full replacement or scrapping and, and fully replacing, examples that come to mind are SKF (recently double upgraded to Buy), where they have a bearing reconditioning of very large bearings that go into massive mining machines or conveyor belts that collect lubricants that can be treated and reused. The thematic touches quite a lot of companies across our sector, but where it really drives incremental demand it's buildings remodeling and industrial software.

## Oil & gas exposure is highlighted as a risk, can you explain that & how it’s evolved & how you see it progressing?

Up to 2015 this was the largest growth area, in some cases reaching into high twenties or even 30% of companies' exposure. Since then, most of the sector has de-emphasized investments in these businesses, and we've also seen natural attrition as spend levels came down. We have not necessarily seen regulatory scrutiny as a key factor here.

An interesting area of growth is methane reduction; we see this as a driver of growth and margins for Rotork. This is a key example of how the sector can act as an enabler towards decarbonisation, cleaning up the operations of an otherwise / previously carbon-intensive / emissions heavy industry. Other companies with exposure include Alfa Laval (last year saw a record high order intake on offshore). We've definitely seen the aftermath of higher energy prices on oil and gas resulting with a certain delay. Then also in much higher orders for offshore platforms for drill ships, which is still going on as we speak. So that's the usual cyclical element and we have obviously seen 10 years of hardly any investment. So there's been quite some pent up demand and no kind of regulation related pushback just as yet.

## Valuation Method and Risk Statement

The immediate risk in relation to the subject matter covered by UBS's Global Sustainability Team arises from a lack of definition in the field, reflected in the many names and acronyms in use by practitioners: sustainability; responsible investment (RI); socially responsible investment (SRI); ESG (environmental, social and governance) investment; ethical investing, impact investing, etc.

The field covers an enormous range of potential issues and, over time, their importance fluctuates. At the time of writing, we believe the issues raised in this research to be relevant to investors, but this may change. Additionally, this research should not be read as a complete or definitive account of all relevant issues for firms. Although we attempt to address all significant or nascent issues, these may not always be apparent, and these may change over time.

Finally, this document should not be interpreted as meaning all the issues addressed in our research have a financial impact. Whether or not ESG issues have a financial impact remains an open question as there is no accepted financial model that can determine whether any given issue (ESG or otherwise) is already reflected in share prices.
