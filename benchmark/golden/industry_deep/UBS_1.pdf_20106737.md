## A monthly synthesis of all our views in a single document

We summarise our views on the main economies and investment strategy on the following pages. The aim is not to be comprehensive, but rather to provide a succinct summary of the main changes in our views and recommendations over the past month. The contributing analysts are listed in the relevant sections of the report—if you have any questions on any particular area, please reach out to them directly.

## Waiting for the data pendulum to swing back

The gap between US real GDP growth and the rest of the G7 over the last 6 months is at a 20-year high. The central thesis of most of our trading calls is that this can’t last, but the data so far disagrees. Given what is now priced, however, the risk reward over the next few months suggests taking a cautious view on equities, re-establishing received positions in fixed income, and being long volatility. When markets make new highs, 88% of the time they rise over the next 6 and 12m, but we’ve never seen markets make new highs when unemployment and intra-index volatility are this low.

The danger the next few weeks, however, is that we expect another hot US CPI print, which could perpetuate current price action. We likely need to get that print out of the way, and see weaker consumption/employment data, before many of our more defensive trades can work. But with that caveat, we like receiving the US front end, and in equity prefer defensive positioning through software, healthcare, transmission and distribution utilities and defence stocks. In FX, we like being long high-yielders such as NZD and CAD against CHF . In credit, we don’t see an immediate trigger for spread decompression so stick with carry trades (favouring HY/LL and in Europe Fins vs Nonfins). In EM, risk premia are too compressed; we largely stick with local market receivers in Brazil/Mexico/Korea, while being generally short EMFX (we identify 7 currencies).

## Economics Summary

## Key Messages

•The gap between US real GDP growth and the rest of the G7 over the last 2 quarters is at a 20-year high (ex-pandemic). We expect it to close by Q2.

•Global disinflation is intact but central banks are wary of easing too soon.

The divergence between US resilience and weakness elsewhere continues. In the 2nd half of 2023, GDP growth in the US averaged +4.1% annualized, while in the rest of the G7 it averaged -0.5% (simple average). Indeed, the UK and Japan were technically in recession while both France and Germany were a rounding error away from posting two negative quarters. Global growth fell from 3.9% in Q3 to 2.3% in Q4, and EM ex-China growth fell from 5.2% to 1.7%. Into year-end the impact of global monetary tightening was clearly still reverberating in the data.

However, there are some signs of a pick-up in global momentum in some of the Jan/Feb data. The global January manufacturing PMI data moved up over a full point and (at 49.7) is just a few ticks short of starting to expand—its highest level in 17 months. Most of that improvement comes from developed markets and it is showing up in forward looking components like new orders, even if some of the PMI strength is exaggerated by delivery delays due to the Red Sea attacks (PMI data interpret those as signs of demand strength; the delivery time component accounts for roughly a third of the European PMI improvement). The PMI improvement also mirrors some of the stabilization in the trade data we’ve seen in the last few months. Although most of that improvement so far had been concentrated in Asia (the tech cycle turning up), there had been a narrowing of the import demand weakness, which largely now sits with Europe. It’s possible we are starting to see the impact of improving real disposable income, as inflation recedes. Having said all that, we’d be wary of overstating the improvement. The flash service sector PMIs for Feb deteriorated for the US, and the Feb flash manufacturing PMIs showed no follow through of improvement for Europe and some deterioration for Japan. Things are gradually improving but not at any great speed.

The key conundrum for the global outlook remains what to make of the US data. Taken at face value the strength in employment and consumption is at odds with our forecast of a sharp slowdown in Q2/Q3, and if we are wrong on the forecast the outlook for the global economy is a lot rosier. But we expect a lot of the employment strength to be revised away in the next few releases. The quarterly census last week confirmed that, once again, payroll strength was substantially overstated in Q2 and Q3, and retail sales for January (as well as revisions for Q4) came in much weaker than expected. That could be the beginning of the slowdown in consumption we have in the forecast, and it would mirror the weakness in real income growth (6m annualized we estimate it has fallen from about 5% to close to 0%, with a large part of the delta coming from diminished fiscal support). A lot hinges on the next retail sales print for Feb and the revisions that we think are coming. Those could either validate or invalidate our forecast.

China’s data is difficult to interpret at this time of year given the shifting Chinese new year calendar, as well as last year’s reopening base effect. But on balance it looks as if, outside the property sector, things are getting better. Travel, shopping and spending on services (e.g. movies) improved considerably over last year and in some cases are back above 2019 levels (though outbound travel still at only 57% of those levels). Property activity (25% of the economy) remains weak, however, with sales falling vs last year’s CNY holiday and in level terms sit at only 1/5 of 2019. We’ve seen a flurry of policy measures to stabilize property which is showing up in improving existing home sales, but new home sales/construction remain largely moribund.

Global disinflation momentum remains intact but hit a roadblock in the US. Residual seasonality in January is a known problem, so we don’t take too much signal from the 40bp CPI print, but we are forecasting something similar for Feb (including for PCE) which does change the contour of what the FOMC will be looking at. That raises the risk that the 1st cut does not come in May but in June, but there is plenty of data before then to undo the ‘what’s the rush’ sentiment.

The pendulum of market pricing has swung back to validating a slow approach to central bank easing. We suspect that extrapolates too much from the US data (inflation in Europe came in below central banks forecasts, and wage and profit growth in Europe are slowing, for instance). For Japan we have scaled back our expectation of monetary tightening: we think NIRP ends in April but expect no hikes after that given the weakness in demand.

## United States

## Key Messages

 CPI inflation and non-farm payrolls surprised on the upside in January, but retail sales fell, prior months were revised lower, and manufacturing production fell in January.

We expect real GDP expanded \~3.1% (Q4/Q4) in ’23. GDP then expands just 0.4% in '24E before reaccelerating to 2.4% in ‘25E.

The slower GDP growth should lead to a slower labor market and contraction that should raise the u-rate to 4.7% in ‘24E, helping to push core PCE inflation down to 1.9%, before rising to 2.1% in 2025E.

We expect the FOMC’s first cut at their May meeting with some risk of this slipping to June. With inflation back to target this year, we think the Fed will have considerable leeway to respond to weaker growth, returning the funds rate to 2.75 to 3% in 2024.

January’s inflation surprise doesn’t undo the broader progress on inflation. The core CPI rose 0.4% in January, surprising on the upside. We now expect some follow-through strength in the February data too. Together, that may slow the improvement, but we expect core PCE inflation to fall to 2.5% in the April data, material progress toward the FOMC’s 2 percent inflation target from the over 5% core inflation seen during 2022. We estimate core PCE inflation to fall below target to 1.9% at the end of 2024. With the mid-point of the target range for the federal funds rate at 5.4% since last July, the real policy rate continues to rise.

Our baseline still expects an economic slowdown. The January employment report was strong, but we expect was flattered by measurement complications like last year, which was revised down later (the divergence in the two main employment series is shown below; truth is likely somewhere in between). Looking at the driver of the expansion, the consumer, retail sales fell 0.8% in January, a weak start to 2024. Manufacturing production fell in January and is down 0.9% over the year. Most surveys still point toward contraction. The outstanding 2023 growth in construction of manufacturing plants has slowed considerably. The federal deficit is set to shrink this year too, as fiscal support for growth wanes. It remains to be seen if the expansion’s exceptional resilience continues. We expect the level of higher interest rates, ongoing erosion in the strength of household balance sheets, and uncertainty surrounding the Presidential election that weighs on business investment, to lead to weak 2024 growth of roughly only 1/2% (Q4/Q4), and an increase in the unemployment rate to 4-3/4% at the end of 2024.

We expect a first ‘calibration’ cut in May, with more to follow. Following the signal from Chair Powell and incoming data, we pushed out our expectations for a first rate cut to the May FOMC meeting, with risk of June. Financial markets are pricing a 25 bp cut by June and between 3 and 4 for this year. However, with our forecast for inflation nearing 2% and moving below, we expect that gives the FOMC considerable leeway to lower nominal rates should growth concerns arise. We think growth concerns will rise in the coming months, prompting the FOMC to move to more neutral policy to support economic expansion. As a result of the low inflation and economic slowdown, we expect the funds rate to end 2024E in a range of 2.75% to 3%, move down to 1.5% in early 2025 to kick start growth again, and as downturn risks subside, end 2025 at 2% to 2.25%.

## Key Messages

 We forecast Eurozone GDP growth of 0.6% for 2024 (with downside risk) and 1.2% for 2025. Germany is underperforming within the Eurozone.

Eurozone labor markets remain strong, with record-low unemployment and strong nominal wage growth.

Eurozone: Following 0.5% in 2023, we forecast GDP growth of 0.6% for 2024 and 1.2% in 2025. We expect restrictive monetary policy, the weak external environment and fiscal consolidation to continue to exert headwinds to growth over the coming quarters. However, low unemployment and the recovery in real wage growth should support household consumption and prevent outright contraction. Following GDP growth -0.1% q/q in Q3-23 and 0.0% q/q in Q4-23 – implying that the Eurozone narrowly avoided a technical recession in H2-23 - we forecast +0.2% q/q for Q1-24. However, the latest indicators (e.g. January/February PMIs) suggest downside risk for Q1-24.

Germany at the core of Eurozone weakness: German activity and sentiment, including forward-looking indicators, underperform the Eurozone aggregate with few signs of a pick-up so far. German manufacturing remains particularly exposed to global demand weakness and fiscal policy is tightening in response to a constitutional court ruling.

Tight labor markets and elevated wage growth likely to slow down the decline in core inflation: Despite sluggish growth, Eurozone labor markets remain tight, with unemployment at a record low of 6.4% in November, 80bps down from pre-Covid levels. The demographic transition is intensifying the structural shortage of qualified labor and many companies are hoarding labor, despite the uncertain growth outlook. As a result, nominal wage growth remains at elevated levels, implying risks of a slower disinflation, particularly in labor-intensive services.Inflation fell 0.1pp to 2.8% y/y in January, after a temporary increase in December. Overall, we expect inflation to average 2.3% in 2024, 2.1% in 2025, and 2% 2026. ECB: With the deposit rate at 4%, the ECB has signaled that rates have likely peaked and the discussion when the first cut should and will be delivered is now in full force. We forecast the first ECB rate cut for April 2024, with cumulative cuts of 100bps in 2024 and 100bps in 2025, which would bring the depo rate back to the broadly neutral level of 2% by end-2025. However, we acknowledge a risk that the first rate cut might only come in June (not April) and think that the upcoming ECB meeting on 7 March might perhaps provide a bit more clarity on the timing.

## Japan

## Key Messages

Japan’s economy was in a technical recession in H2 last year and UBS expects a further contraction of real GDP in Q1.

However, we also expect a pickup in private domestic demand, consumption and capex with a rise in real wages and high profits from Q2, offsetting the moderation in external demand, including inbound tourists’ consumption.

CPI inflation is likely to slow with a fall in import prices but could then firm with higher wage growth.

The BoJ’s communication signaled the end of the current accommodative policy framework, especially negative interest rate policy and the 10yr yield target (YCC), to end soon (we expect April), while further rate hikes look unlikely through this year now.

Technical recession but not an outright recession. Real GDP unexpectedly contracted in Q4 last year, leaving two consecutive quarters of contraction, a technical recession. The weakness in private domestic demand was notable, falling three consecutive quarters. Private consumption was particularly weak with a fall in real wages (income). We now expect the contraction continues this quarter and lowered our 2024 annual real GDP growth forecast from 0.6%y/y to 0.3%, lower than Japan’s potential growth of around 0.5%. However, on a Q4/Q4 basis, growth is essentially forecast at 1%. We expect private domestic demand to recover while external demand, including inbound tourists’ consumption, slows. If the economy can sustain 1% real GDP growth despite the population shrinking by 0.5% per year, we believe 2-3% nominal GDP growth to be sustained (“nominal renaissance.”) Meanwhile, the Nikkei 225 index approached its record high reached at the end of 1989.

CPI inflation to slow but wage growth to accelerate. CPI inflation slowed from a peak of around 4% to close to 2% as a result of government subsidies in energy and a decline in import prices, especially in foods. It looks likely that disinflation continues with some volatility given cross-currents of subsidy policy changes and the lagged effect from the fall in import prices, ending 2024 at below 2%. However, higher wage growth after the strong outcome of the Spring wage negotiations should lift service prices with a lag, which would stop the disinflation. Through this process, we expect inflation expectations would rise and stabilize at around 2%, but uncertainty is high.

End of NIRP in April still looks likely, but no more hike through 2024. The BoJ deputy governor Uchida made a speech on 8 February, offering various hints on what the Bank is considering regarding the near term start of policy normalization. The most important takeaway for us was that the end of negative interest rate policy (NIRP) will not be the end of (the very) accommodative policy stance. We maintain our call that the BoJ will raise the policy rate from current -0.1% to 0.0% and end yield curve control (0% long term policy rate) on 26 April (not on 19 March), but with the weak domestic demand trajectory, we no longer expect a second hike (by 25bp) in July, especially with our continued UBS house view on the US recession in Q2 and Q3, which would prompt the Fed to cut the policy rate aggressively by 50bp per FOMC in H2.

## High dispersion with mega cap leadership

Developed market equity indices continued to rally sharply over the last month. The largest companies in the US and Europe rallied the most and often in contrast to notable weakness in much of the rest of the market and particularly small caps. This strong momentum-quality-growth factor bias over value and volatility factors led to even lower levels of realised correlation in both regions. Weighted pairwise correlation in both markets recently fell under 15%. This has kept index volatility levels very low too.VIX and V2X have been in a 13% to 15% range all year but there is a noticeable difference in the average stock level volatilities. In the US, the largest companies price volatility at 25% on average - still well above pre-covid lows of 20%. In contrast, the largest companies in Europe price 20% volatility on average - back to the pre-covid levels already. Much of the EU/US difference is due to the sharp gap moves in US mega cap tech stocks and the ongoing concerns about systemic risks in US regional banks.

## The volatility regime still points higher

Our Macro Cookbook connects S&P 500 volatility with US macroeconomic variables across 256 regimes back to the 1960s. It takes in the full market return distribution rather than just averages. We therefore get richer insights on the level and trajectory of equity volatility based on economic forecasts for the next couple of years. Based on an expectation of softening US growth, falling inflation and interest rates, and modestly rising unemployment, we think S&P 500 volatility will average 15-25% in the coming quarters – a higher level than we experienced in 2023 and modestly higher than the market currently implies.

## Opportunities in scenarios

Our Market Cookbook helps us identify the relative sector returns and volatility for specific short-term forecasts for real yields, breakeven inflation, credit spreads and leading indicators.

For our baseline view (above) we favour upside in European banks through call options funded by selling US bank calls. The ratio is favourable too at 1.5x EU bank calls for each US bank call sold.

For a scenario of higher for longer - a risk as we continue a period of positive economic surprise - our models suggest energy stocks are among the highest beta. Other materials and cyclical companies also have exposure to this scenario.
