# Global Strategy

# Will China export deflation to the rest of the world?

## China’s weak property, strong manufacturing poses global disinflationary risks

While most of the world has been struggling with inflation and weak trade, China has seen the opposite: deflation and an export surge. Overcapacity has increased across several sectors. Weak domestic demand is a factor, but strong supply appears to be playing a key role. Cyclically, things may improve somewhat: demand is bottoming out, and energy, food & tech should generate modest inflation. Beyond this, however, we see a rising likelihood of China exporting disinflation and challenging growth in other manufacturing economies over the next 1-2y. To be clear, there isn’t robust evidence of this today, but several symptoms are lining up in a manner that suggests China’s rising manufacturing investment may have global consequences. This is in addition to the weak growth impulse radiating from China’s slower property investment and imports. A weaker RMB is a third potential conduit for global disinflationary spillovers.

## Is there clear evidence of China exporting disinflation? No, but risks ahead

The data clearly shows China exporters gaining global share in recent years. These gains, which initially came as other supply chains collapsed through the early days of Covid, are proving persistent. The fall in China’s export deflators (and potential 'export' of disinflation) is much more recent, starting about 12m ago. We would admit similar export price declines have also been seen in other Asian exporters. Worryingly, however, a product decomposition of China’s export prices shows that deflation has been broad based, not only in Tech. We would not yet conclude that China is exporting major goods deflation to the rest of the world, but, if it was, this is what it would look like in the early stages. US data currently shows just a 3% y/y drop in import prices from China, though the 11% drop in China's price deflators indicates larger disinflation risk in other parts of the world.

## Who loses? Manufacturing economies, primarily

We present a scorecard along six dimensions that points to Europe, Japan, Thailand and Korea being most exposed to potentially rising trade competition from China. Mexico, Brazil & the US appear less at risk. Commodity exporters such as Indonesia, Australia, S. Africa and Colombia also face challenges as China’s investment in the more commodityhungry property sector remains weak. Higher weight for core goods in CPI baskets and much larger imports from China as a proportion of consumption suggest that EM inflation is more exposed to a potential disinflationary impulse from China ahead.

## Asset implications

We see EM FX as the most directly exposed asset class. China export price disinflation would essentially exert the same pressure on other currencies as a weaker CNH. CZK, HUF, THB and COP, and CNH via options, are our preferred shorts. PLN, INR, BRL and, tactically, KRW should hold up better. Potential deepening of China export disinflation is likely to pose bigger challenges for EM equities than DM. Korea has been one of our key Overweights within EM for the last 6m but, longer term, it appears relatively vulnerable, as does Indonesia. Latam and India appear more insulated. We see tactical value in parts of Chinese equities, and remain long index calls over the next 3-6m in an anticipation of a modest cyclical upturn. Sectorally, global ex China autos appear most vulnerable. See inside for our list of companies potentially positively / negatively affected by China exporting disinflation. The main beneficiaries are likely in local debt amid disinflation. 2- 5y debt in Brazil, India, Korea, and Mexico should outperform the US, FX neutral.

![](images/fea2b914470289a9cc495ce5920441f43b64258105232f88ccdebd0d79ede641.jpg)

Figure 9: China: selected inflation measures
![](images/b52743b13d40bce5cf5d42e047631a31e7cfa79d29867118bf582379f8c6150a.jpg)
Source: Haver, UBS

China's GDP deflator fell 0.6% last year, its largest drop since the late 1990s. Aside from an atypically large contraction in the primary sector (- 2.2% y/y), where pricing is volatile, the industrial sector GDP deflator (- 2.7%) posted its largest drop since 2015 and tertiary sector inflation was its joint weakest on record (1.2% y/y, similar to 2020). Weak pricing was broad-based, therefore.

Our China CPI diffusion index is recording the lowest proportion of items within the basket above 2% since at least 1995. At 7%, this is the lowest proportion across all major EM economies we track. This points to the breadth of China's disinflationary challenge.

Housing has seen relatively strong deflation, with the 70-city existing house price index -5.7% y/y in Feb / - 9.5% on a 3m saar basis, their weakest on record. Y/y consumer goods deflation has been only more intense in the GFC. Falling house prices and continued weakness in manufacturing PPI likely don't bode well for core CPI and services CPI - both of which have remained in positive y/y territory thus far. Our economists expect headline y/y CPI to weaken again in March and Q2, before recovering moderately to post full-year CPI around 0.5% y/y.

Country A is said to have a revealed comparative advantage in a given product i when its ratio of exports of product i to its total exports of all goods (products) exceeds the same ratio for the world as a whole:

That is,

$$
R C A _ { _ { A i } } = \frac { \overline { { \sum _ { j \in P } X _ { A j } } } } { \overline { { \sum _ { j \in P } X _ { w j } } } } \geq 1
$$

Where

P is the set of all products (with iEP),

XAi is the country A's exports of product i,

Xwi is the world's exports of product i,

∑jEPXAj is the country A's total exports (of all products j in P), and

∑jEPXwj is the world's total exports (of all products j in P).

When a country has a revealed comparative advantage for a given product (RCA >1), it is inferred to be a competitive producer and exporter of that product relative to a country producing and exporting that good at or below the world average. A country with a revealed comparative advantage in product i is considered to have an export strength in that product. The higher the value of a country’s RCA for product i, the higher its export strength in product i.

Screening across 971 sectors in total, we filtered to only include this in a) which China has an RCA of 0.7 or higher (1.0 indicates a clear global comparative advantage in that sector) and b) where China's annual exports in that sector are \$10bn or higher. This reduces the number of sectors analysed to 52.

## Valuation Method and Risk Statement

Risks of multi-asset investing include but are not limited to market risk, credit risk, interest rate risk, and foreign exchange risk. Correlations of returns among different asset classes may deviate from historical patterns. Geopolitical events and policy shocks pose risks that can reduce asset returns. Valuations may be adversely affected during times of high market volatility, thin liquidity, and economic dislocation. All options recommendations related to FX are 'over-the-counter'.

Risks to Mercedes-Benz and the auto sector include, but are not limited to: (1) Commodities: Oil and raw material prices; (2) interest rates; (3) overall macro growth and consumer sentiment; (4) currency fluctuations; and (5) new entrants in the OEM space (Tesla, Chinese EV makers). Our price target is based on a SOTP model using cycle-average target multiples.

Risks to Stellantis and the automotive sector include, but are not limited to: (1) volatility in global demand driven by macro trends including consumer sentiment, unemployment, interest rates, credit data etc.; (2) the company's ability to master the transition to electric cars; and (3) execution risks related to the merger. Our PT is based on a SOTP model using cycle-average target multiples.

Risks for VW and the auto sector include, but are not limited to: (1) Commodities: Oil and raw material prices; (2) interest rates; (3) overall macro growth and consumer sentiment; (4) currency fluctuations; (5) new entrants in the OEM space (Chinese OEMs, tech players, ride on- demand services). Our price target is based on a sum of the parts model.

We value Volvo using a two-stage DCF for the core activity, to which we add the associates (for Polestar we use the market value to which we apply a holding discount, for other associates we use the auto industry's current average EV/EBIT multiple of 6x). The auto industry is a cyclical sector. Earnings can be highly sensitive to macro, interest rates, commodity prices, etc.

SKF has historically been very cyclical and dependent on the development of global industrial activity. In addition changes to FX rates can have a material impact on group earnings. We value SKF using a SOTP.
