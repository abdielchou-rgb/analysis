# Tracking Asia's sovereign debt path

Elevated rates and an increasing turn to populist policies have left investors more wary of fiscal risks. Although Indonesia has come under scrutiny, post a BBG article, our analysis suggests that even under a more pessimistic alternative scenario, we do not see such a rapid rise in government debt. That said, Indonesia and the Philippines face a twin deficit challenge, thereby rendering them vulnerable to FX weakness. Although it is no longer struggling with a dual deficit, Thailand's large fiscal stimulus plan has prompted some concerns, albeit a more limited fiscal push looks likely. Malaysia and South Korea have seen relatively limited increases in government debt, with their foreign investor share at over 20%. In contrast, China and India's dependence on foreign capital is very low, albeit with the highest government debt-to-GDP ratios in the region, with the former seeing a relatively faster rise in recent years.

While the risk of a sovereign debt crisis remains low, performance across the region has varied, requiring closer tracking, especially in the context of a rather sharp rise in maturing debt this year and elevated (re)financing costs, pressed further by high US rates. Adding to the pressure, Asia FX is facing significant risk of a tariff war. That said, the risks are not one-sided, with a sharp reversal likely in the case of earlierthan-expected rate cuts by the Fed, albeit perhaps not as sharp as last December, given trade policy risks.

In this report, we take a closer look at the evolution of Asia's sovereign debt and related indicators, while detailing China's growth challenges, India's macro outlook post the general election, Indonesia's alternative debt scenarios and Korea's long-term flow prospects, among other topics related to ASEAN.

In a previous Asia Thematic Analysis report, we contemplated the potential impact on Asia of a tariff war between China and the US (imposing additional 50% tariffs on each other). While the significant impact of this on China was no surprise, its greater impact on Vietnam was noteworthy – resulting in a 3ppt drag on growth, with negative effects lasting over five years, due to the country's large exposure to both economies (while India was least affected). At the onset, a significant external shock would cause Asia's FX volatility to surge again, limiting the capacity of central banks to ease and provide a buffer for the economy. Although capital controls might be employed, they stand as either the least favored (or the last) policy option. Although advanced Asia's external balance would also suffer, ASEAN central banks may be more wary in this regard, given their more challenging external fundamentals.

While reserve adequacy ratios vary within Asia, we also refer to international investment positions when assessing external risks. China's reserve adequacy ratio, as defined by the IMF, is the lowest in Asia, at 65% of recommended reserves – although that determination assumes that the authorities aim for an essentially fixed exchange rate. Allowing for greater currency variability would lead to the conclusion that China also has a more-than-adequate level of reserves. The IMF's reserves adequacy framework does not take into account other foreign assets held by these economies that could be used to service their external debt. China is a net creditor to the world, with net foreign assets at 16% of GDP. South Korea's net creditor position is even better, at 42% of GDP, with a reserve coverage ratio of 93%, with 7.3% of FX reserves in currency and deposits. However, there is the matter of sustained weakness in the yen, which hovers around as a drag on the rest of Asia FX. Asian currencies are now either nearing and testing their recent highs or crawling ever higher against the US dollar amid elevated US rates. The pressure on Asia FX and rates could worsen ahead, especially with the risk of a more serious tariff war seemingly nearing, rendering risks to our FX view heavily tilted to the upside. On the other hand, any sign of earlier rate cuts by the Fed could result in a rather sharp reversal (like that of last December). This would provide relief for EM Asia in particular, amid sharp increases in maturing debt.

## Economy

China's growth outlook has improved since the beginning of the year, thanks to a rebound in external demand. We raised our 2024 China growth forecast to 5.2% in April (half a percentage point higher vs. our initial forecast of 4.7%). Similar upward revisions were also seen among market economists at around the same time. The improvement was largely due to (and will likely continue to be led by) a rebound in external demand. Net exports contributed 0.8ppt to China's headline GDP growth in Q1 2024, a big swing from its negative -0.6ppt contribution in 2023. Exports will likely sustain positive growth over the next few quarters, despite recent trade frictions. The negative impact from a new round of US tariffs (impacting 0.5% of China's total exports) and EU tariffs on Chinese EVs (another 0.5% of China's exports) will likely be limited, while exports to emerging markets and developing countries are expected to increase further. Higher export orders also helped China industrial output, which grew by c.6% in the first five months of 2024.

Meanwhile, the recovery of domestic demand continued to lag, mainly dragged by the property sector. The sector saw another 26% YoY decline in sales and a 4% YoY decline in prices in May, despite a set of new policy supports. The outlook for the property sector is still unfavorable for H2 2024, owing to the sector's large inventory. Finished home inventory has increased by 70% since 2020, and it is now equivalent to 5.5 months of new home sales. There is potential for an even larger stock of unfinished home inventory; according to our estimates, total home inventory likely exceeded annual home sales in 2022-23, equivalent to almost 15 months of new home sales today. In addition, the number of secondary market home listings in big cities has almost doubled since 2022. The size of the government's inventory purchase program is only equivalent to about 10-15% of finished home inventory, and even less, if unfinished homes and secondary market listings are included. Thus, we believe that the supply of new and secondary homes will likely remain excessive relative to demand in H2 2024. This will likely lead to further decreases in home prices, reinforcing the "wait-and-see" sentiment among homebuyers.

The services sector and the consumption recovery also lost some momentum, as consumer sentiment was still mixed. Over the past few months, the domesticdemand driven services sector saw a decline in growth to below 5%. Domestic demand for goods did not improve much either, as retail sales growth has stayed low in the 3-5% YoY range over the past few months. Our dbDIG team has regularly surveyed Chinese consumers in large and medium-size cities on their spending behavior. The latest survey in Q2 2024 pointed to an improvement in income expectations as well as a perception of less economic uncertainty. Consequently, the proportion of consumers that want to save more also declined to 39% in Q2 (from 42% in Q1 and 46% in 2023). Yet, the property sector's negative impact on consumption seemed to have increased again. The proportion of consumers answering that they plan to reduce spending because of property volatility jumped to 52% (from 39% in Q1).

These developments have led us to slightly decrease our 2024 GDP forecast to 5.0% (from 5.2% previously). This is driven by a 0.4ppt downward revision to Q2 GDP growth to 0.5% QoQ (from 0.9% previously). In the second half of the year, domestic demand will likely see some support from an acceleration of government spending and bond issuances, including incentives for household car and home appliance purchases and equipment upgrades by corporates. We expect QoQ growth to rebound to 1.3% in Q3 and 1.2% in Q4. Under these assumptions, YoY growth would be 5.2%, 4.7% and 4.7% in Q2, Q3 and Q4 2024, respectively. We expect growth to slow to 4.5% in 2025 as the impact from fiscal spending fades.

Meanwhile, inflation is gradually improving, but a return to 'normal' inflation rates of around 2% could still take time. Food (especially pork) and energy prices are improving, but the drag on inflation continued to come from lower housing prices and car price cuts. We expect consumer inflation to continue to improve to slightly above 1% by end-2024 and further to 1.5% in 2025, albeit staying below its 2% longterm average level. PPI inflation is also expected to rebound to positive territory by end-2024, supported by global commodity price increases, better management of domestic production capacity, measures to boost car and home appliance sales and an acceleration of equipment replacement.

## Policy outlook

The PBOC's monetary policy framework is undergoing significant changes. Credit growth has slowed sharply in recent months, with the PBOC downplaying the importance of money quantity. And it has taken multiple measures to make bank lending more effective. The 1-year MLF policy rate is also becoming less relevant, and the PBOC is considering making the 7-day OMO rate its new policy rate. If this transition takes place, we think the MLF rate could be allowed to move to a level that is closer to market rates. Meanwhile, because the OMO rate is more closely linked to market rates, cutting it could put greater depreciation pressure on the renminbi. Given the stronger USD so far and the PBOC's continued efforts to stabilize the bilateral USD/CNY exchange rate, we believe the PBOC will only cut the OMO rate once this year by 10bps in Q4.

The renminbi is still facing depreciation pressures, owing to a strong US economy, a sustained interest rate differential between USD and CNY, and continued weak investor sentiment on China assets. We believe that depreciation pressure may ease toward Q4 and next year, once the Fed enters a rate-cut cycle. Thus, we expect the CNY to stay at around 7.2/USD by end-2024, appreciating to

6.9/USD by end-2025. Our forecast implies that CNY will remain stable against a trade-weighted basket in the next two years.

Keeping up the pace of fiscal spending has turned out to be more challenging than expected, owing to a decline in fiscal revenues (especially from land sales). The government's bond issuance is back-end loaded this year, which will likely help boost spending growth to positive territory in H2. Nevertheless, we forecast total fiscal spending to only grow by 1.5% this year (down from 5% previously). We believe the government may need to revisit its budget mid-year in order to compensate for revenue shortfalls.

The Politburo meeting in late July will be good timing for reviewing China's growth situation and the macroeconomic policy mix. China's growth has decelerated somewhat after the April Politburo meeting. If the recent softness continues, the July Politburo meeting would be the opportunity for the government to reassess the situation and consider additional policy support. We believe the government may need to introduce additional fiscal measures, as revenue shortfalls are again constraining local governments' capacity to spend. It may also discuss how the PBOC can reform its monetary policy framework by switching to a short-term key policy rate. Last, but not the least, it will likely take stock of property sector performance on the back of recent measures and consider future steps.

In addition, the Central Committee of China's Communist Party is set to hold its third plenum in July to discuss critical reforms that will shape China's policy trajectory over the next 5-10 years. Historically, these sessions have primarily focused on structural reforms rather than short-term macroeconomic policy. The upcoming meeting will likely focus on important economic issues, possibly including: a) further de-leveraging of debt within the system; (b) a more level playing field for the private sector; (c) bolstering technology and advanced manufacturing in order to be more self-reliant; and (d) resolving headwinds to the property sector (see our Strategy team's preview here).

## India's macro outlook post the general elections

After holding an absolute majority for the last ten years, the Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP) has formed a coalition government at the center (with the help of its ally, the National Democratic Alliance [NDA]) for a third consecutive term, under the leadership of PM Modi. The election results came as a surprise, as the BJP secured only 240 seats, thereby failing to cross the 272 halfway mark, contrary to bullish predictions from various exit polls that indicated an even stronger turnout for PM Modi's party in the 2024 elections compared to the 2019 results (the BJP had secured 303 seats and the NDA tally was 353 in 2019). This naturally raises a number of questions about India's near- and long-term reforms and the macro outlook under Modi 3.0. We answer all of these questions to the best of our ability, with the inherent assumption that, despite a slimmer victory mandate, the current NDA government will likely be a stable government and last for the next five years.

## We discuss key macro-relevant questions post the election results:

1. Will reforms take a back seat? In our view, a slimmer mandate for the NDA is unlikely to derail or reverse the reforms that were undertaken during the incumbent administration's last ten years. These include (among others) the PLI scheme for the manufacturing sector, the Make in India initiative, fiscal consolidation, GST, privatization, the smart cities mission, the push in public infrastructure building, digitisation, and liberalization of financial markets. However, what could be more at risk is a push – at least in the near term – toward the more difficult phase of reforms, particularly in factor markets (labor and land), which require a wider political consensus for implementation at state levels. The results of this election have arguably strengthened the position of regional political parties, and we believe that significant political capital would be required for the new government at the center to make progress on some of these intended reforms.

2. Will the long-term structural growth outlook be impacted? Despite a downside surprise to the tally of BJP and NDA seats relative to opinion and exit poll expectations, we see no material risk to India's long-term growth prospects. We continue to expect average 6-6.5% real and 10-11% nominal GDP growth for India, which should allow it to double in size and become a USD7trn economy by 2030. In the past few decades, irrespective of various business cycles and several external shocks, the Indian economy has delivered 6%+ real GDP growth on average, and we expect this trend to continue in the coming years. Before 2014, a coalition government was the norm for India, and even in those decades, India delivered strong growth. Hence, we do not expect the long-term growth outlook to be materially impacted, as the BJP has not been able to cross the 272 mark on its own. We believe that the structural tailwinds (India's demographic dividend, the rise of its aspirational middle class, its status as a favorable investor destination, and its resiliency) that characterize the Indian economy are strong enough to help the country deliver 6-6.5% real GDP growth for the rest of the decade, as long as there is a stable government at the center.

3. Will the capex recovery be derailed? There is risk that the private corporate sector could become more cautious about broad-basing the capex recovery, given a slimmer margin of victory for the NDA. However, we believe that at worst, those investment decisions may be delayed to some extent, not derailed altogether. We note that corporate sector investments are guided by capacity utilization and profitability potential, and as long as there is a stable government at the center (even with a lower tally of seats compared to the past two elections), we expect corporate sector capex to pick up, probably after a period of a wait-and-watch approach. India's private corporate sector has not invested materially in the last decade, and with capacity utilization having picked up, we believe that further capex recovery traction is inevitable, albeit perhaps with a slightly delayed start.

4. Will policy priorities change in favor of a consumption boost? We believe this is the most likely scenario. Given the outcome of the elections, we believe it is likely that the new government will see the need to boost private consumption – particularly for lower-income individuals – in order to ensure better performance in the upcoming assembly elections. We note that, despite real GDP growth coming in at 8.2% yoy in FY24, private consumption was much weaker at 4.0% yoy, particularly due to weak rural demand, thanks to a patchy monsoon in 2023. With private consumption being close to 58% of GDP, we believe that a greater push to boost consumption demand could help maintain real GDP growth in the range of 6.5-7.0% yoy in FY25 and around a 6.5% average in the subsequent years (even if the pick-up in private capex recovery is delayed and lower than what would have been expected with a significantly stronger mandate from the NDA). Hence, a slimmer margin of victory for the NDA may not be unambiguously negative for growth prospects, although policy priorities could change, based on the need to support a stronger and inclusive consumption boost, in our view.

5. Is fiscal consolidation at risk? We don’t think so. The central government's target is to bring the fiscal deficit down to 5.1% of GDP in FY25 and further to 4.5% of GDP in FY26. If the NDA had obtained a stronger mandate, then perhaps the pace of the fiscal consolidation would have accelerated from FY25 itself. However, given that the new government may choose to provide more subsidies and welfare schemes in order to cater to the lower-income and rural sector of the economy, we believe the fiscal deficit target may be maintained at 5.1% of GDP for FY25. Nevertheless, we believe it is unlikely that the fiscal deficit will surpass the current budget estimates or that the FY26 target of 4.5% of GDP will be missed. Two factors have improved the center's fiscal outlook: i) the FY24 fiscal deficit has come down to 5.6% of GDP (from the 5.8% of GDP revised estimate), making it significantly easier to achieve the 5.1%-of-GDP fiscal target for FY25, ceteris paribus; and ii) the RBI has transferred a INR2.1trn dividend to the government (appreciably higher than the budget estimate of INR900bn), with the extra proceeds allowing the possibility of lowering the FY25 fiscal deficit to below 5.1% of GDP. Given these two factors, we believe that it will not be very difficult for the government to meet the 5.1%-of-GDP fiscal deficit target for FY25, even after accommodating for more welfare proceeds and a consumption boost, which we believe can be compensated via the extra RBI dividend and increased tax collection. Moreover, with bond index inclusion likely to start from 28 June, it is unlikely that the new government will prefer to increase the fiscal deficit target, as this may adversely affect the hard-earned fiscal consolidation credibility of the last several years.

6. Will financial sector stability and resiliency be compromised? We believe this is unlikely. Over the last several years, the RBI and the government of India have been building buffers and putting in safeguards, whether in the form of an Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC), an inflation targeting framework or tightening of macro-prudential norms (to cite a few examples). The Indian economy and its capital markets remain far more resilient today than they were a few decades ago, and the RBI is in a strong position to manage FX and interest rate volatility. Gross NPAs in the banking system have fallen to low single digits, the FX reserves buffer remains comfortable at USD652bn and corporate sector balance sheets remain healthy; broadly the fundamentals of the financial sector remain resilient. We acknowledge that equity markets reacted negatively to the election results, but they recovered swiftly thereafter. We note that in April-June 2004, the NIFTY fell 15% vs. the previous quarter (when there was an unexpected change in government), but it was again up 15% qoq by the next quarter. With financialization of household savings gaining traction, we expect the prospects of Indian capital markets to remain strong (as long as there is political stability at the center).

7. Will inflation and inflation expectations become unanchored? We prefer to wait for and watch the budget proposals and other policies announced by the new government in order to come to a definitive answer on this topic. Nevertheless, at this stage, our best guess is that inflation will not be compromised, in order to provide a likely boost to consumption or to support rural demand – particularly because inflation negatively affects the poor the most. In addition, in order to provide a bigger boost to consumption, we believe it would help if the RBI were to embark on a rate-easing cycle from the end of this year or early next year. Yet, in order for the central bank to cut rates, CPI inflation would need to remain closer to the 4% target. Therefore, we believe that any consumption push would likely be done while keeping the broader fiscal deficit and market borrowing targets in check, reorienting expenditure in a way that supports consumption (but in a noninflationary way). We still expect the RBI to begin cutting rates from the Oct-Dec’24 quarter and ease by 50-75bps in this cycle. If fiscal deficit targets are met, and the inflation trajectory evolves in line with RBI's current projection for FY25, we would not be surprised to see monetary easing from the RBI later this year, in order to support consumption growth in the quarters ahead. We believe that fiscal and monetary coordination may reach a better balance in order to boost consumption growth in a non-inflationary manner, rather than depending solely on the fiscal lever, which may prove to be inflationary in the coming years, in our view.

8. Will the rupee depreciate sharply? We believe this is unlikely. We expect the RBI to maintain its preference for minimising volatility in the currency markets while likely gradually allowing INR to weaken to near 85 by the end of the year. We expect political stability at the center and India's positive structural story (even under a slimmer NDA mandate) to continue to attract growth-critical foreign capital into the country, which should further strengthen India's Balance of Payments position and its forex reserves position – thereby helping to keep the rupee stable, amid various global macro and geopolitical uncertainties. We forecast India's gross FX reserves (currently around USD650bn) to rise to USD700bn and USD750bn by the end of FY25 and FY26, respectively. We believe the RBI will continue to buy USD as and when the opportunity presents itself, which should set a natural floor to INR and eventually allow a gradual shift higher of the USD/INR range, while the RBI continues its strategy of maintaining low volatility.

## Inflation and monetary policy outlook

India's May CPI inflation came in at 4.75% yoy, (+0.48% mom), a bit lower than the April turnout of 4.83% yoy (+0.48% mom). While India has been facing a severe heatwave during the last few months, this has not yet affected vegetable prices as severely as in 2023. However, last year, inflation was well contained until May. Then, tomato prices began spiking in June and July. Hence, while overall food inflation momentum remains contained at present (but elevated in yoy terms), there is no guarantee that it will not worsen significantly in June and/or July. In FY25, we forecast CPI inflation to moderate further to a 4.6% average (the RBI's forecast is 4.5%), from 5.4% in FY24, assuming a normal monsoon.

Core inflation (CPI ex food and fuel) increased by 0.24% mom (2.97%yoy) in May vs. a 0.52% mom increase (3.19% yoy) in April. On a yoy basis, core CPI fell to a record low – although we believe the May print is likely to mark the bottom in this cycle. We forecast FY25 core inflation of 3.6% yoy, which if achieved, will constitute a 70bp decline from FY24 (4.3% yoy). Core CPI is likely to average 3.2% between April and Sept 2024; thereafter, the core CPI average is likely to rise to 3.7% yoy in Oct-Dec 2024 and to 4.2% yoy in Jan-March 2025.

## South Korea: A timid net creditor to the world

n South Korea's pension reform would result in a more significant rise in NPS AUM and larger increases in foreign assets, also underscoring the importance of attracting foreign investment, although any positive energy supply shock would also help to mitigate the BoP impact in the long run. Despite its (ever so strengthening) net creditor position, South Korea's policy makers remain concerned about potential effects of rate cuts on the Korean won.

Juliana Lee, Chief Economist, APAC

## Working to attract more foreign investment

South Korea's net creditor position strengthened further in Q1, rising to USD831bn from USD810bn at end-2023. In particular, portfolio assets surged to USD905bn from USD856bn, largely due to rising overseas equity investments. That was mirrored on the liabilities front, albeit lagging behind. That said, South Korea's efforts to open up its markets further may result in a sizable rise in foreign investment share in local securities going forward, helping to counterbalance overseas investments by locals. Beyond retail investors' growing stake in overseas securities, foreign investment by the NPS will continue to rise as its AUM increases. The NPS sees its AUM rising from KRW1101tn in March 2024 to KRW1755tn in 2040 – an increase of KRW720tn. That would translate into a potential outflow of KRW360tn over the 17-year period, if we assume that at least half is allocated to global investment.

There are obvious concerns over National Pension Service (NPS) depletion beyond 2055–given South Korea's demographic headwinds, with its birth rate now down to only 0.72 – as the country's rapidly ageing population suggests that the NPS could be in deficit by 2041. To delay its depletion by almost a decade, a special National Assembly committee on pension reform reached a consensus in May to raise the contribution rate from 9% to 13%, but it could not reach an agreement on changing the income replacement rate to 43-44% (advocated by the ruling PPP party, conditional on structural reforms) vs. 45% (pushed by the DPP), up from the current level of 40% – resulting in a further delay in its reform.<sup>2</sup> Any increase in the contribution rate would point to a more rapid rise in NPS AUM and even larger portfolio outflows ahead.

Meanwhile, the Korean government's efforts to improve accessibility to its capital markets, point to significant capital inflows ahead. For example, the WGBI inclusion represents a potential inflow of about USD70bn (assuming the mid range of estimates) in debt investment inflows. Other than the MSCI upgrade the government is seeking, its corporate value-up programme hints at larger equity investment inflows ahead. If the foreign share of domestic equity markets returns to its peak of 35%, it points to an inflow of about USD72bn (KRW100tn). These two factors would bring about USD140bn of inflows, representing about 60% of the overseas investments from rising NPS AUM noted above. As for the corporate value-up programme, we consider it as an inevitable phase of South Korea's corporate life cycle, especially as the third generation of family ownership matures.

## External balance at risk due to potential tariff shocks, not rates

South Korea is not a risk of a BoP crisis and a total of 50bps rate cuts by the BoK (our prevailing view) would not be a catalyst for it, for that matter, especially in the context of improvement in its external balance. Not only does South Korea remain an important net creditor to the world, but its goods trade balance has notably improved since last year. In fact, it is well on its way to reporting a larger amount of exports of USD670bn this year. Exports rose 9.5% yoy 3mma in May (vs. 8.5% growth in Q1), largely due to semi exports (export volume rose 5.4% in April vs. 4.7% in Q1). Meanwhile, imports continued to be weighed down by fuel and chemical goods, albeit contracting at a slower pace of -3% in May vs. 11.1% in Q1 (import volume fell 0.9% vs. a 4% fall in Q1). South Korea's good trade balance averaged USD3.2bn in April/May, with the semi exports average of USD10.7bn, up from USD2.9bn for all goods in Q1. Our forecast for CA remains at USD58.6bn for the year, up from USD35.5bn in 2023.

That said, the Korean economy, along with its peers in Asia, faces significant risks. In the event of a tariff war between China and the US (imposing additional 50% tariffs on each other), this would not only leave their growth sharply lower, but it would also weigh on South Korea's growth. We estimate that a serious tariff war between the US and China would drag on South Korea's growth for three years, by -0.6ppts in 2025, 1.2ppts in 2026 then 0.2ppts in 2027, then turning positive, adding +0.2ppts in 2028 and onward. This would be due to the uneven impact of a tariff war on US and China growth, with the latter likely continuing to struggle through 2029, while we believe that the impact on US growth may be limited to the first year. South Korea continues to run trade deficits with China, at -USD1.2bn (3mma) in May, while running larger trade surpluses with the US, at USD5bn in May.

On a positive note, South Korea's growth and its good balance may get a boost if the report of gas and oil reserves of up to 14 billion barrels (75% and 25%, respectively) off the coast of the south eastern city of Pohang turns out to be true. The result of the exploration could be provided as soon as 1H25, with commercial production starting as soon as 2035. If successful, it may be able to cover South Korea's gas needs for 29 years and its oil needs for four years. In 2023, it imported

1.06bn bbl of crude oil and 44.3mn tons of natural gas, according to KNOC data.
Over the last decade, oil and gas represented about a quarter of imports.

## Why ease and when?

The minutes underlined MPB members' concerns over the potential effect of rate cuts on the Korean won. Although it is not at a risk of a BoP crisis, the Korean won clearly remains the top risk to the BoK's easing, despite South Korea's improved external position, as the memory of the IMF crisis continues to haunt its policy makers. From our end, we see the Korean won's weakness as an automatic stabilizer – especially in the context of sustained depreciation of the yuan and yen. While the euro's weakness reflects the broadening of political and fiscal policy uncertainties around the world, South Korea's commitment to fiscal discipline stands firm.

Our baseline view continues to be that a notable payback in GDP growth in Q2 (for which data will be made available on 25 July) and a sustained decline in inflation should prompt the Bank of Korea (BoK) to deliver its first rate cut on 22 August, barring any significant, idiosyncractic shock to the Korean Won. We expect a total of 50bps of rate cuts in this cycle, with the BoK policy rate lending at 3%, leaving the real rate at around 1%. In fact, the latest MPB minutes revealed that the staff also expects a payback in private consumption (as the positive shock from the new mobile model release fades) and construction in the second quarter. In response to the stronger-than-expected Q1 GDP, the Bank of Korea (BoK) significantly raised its 2024 growth forecast by 0.4ppts to 2.5%, but lowered its 2025 forecast to 2.1%, in line with our forecasts, noting that much of the upward revision in growth (0.3ppts out of 0.4ppts) was driven by (IT) exports.

Domestic demand weakened in recent months. Retail sales growth fell to - 2.8%yoy 3mma in May, vs. -0.5% in Q1, while equipment investment fell 3.8%, worsening from 0.5% fall in Q1, and construction reported a 2.4% contraction vs. 4.8% growth in the same period. Worse still, construction orders point to sustained weakness at the national level through end-2025, notwithstanding an earlier recovery in certain areas. On the production front, services growth stood at 1.8% in May, vs. 2.1% in Q1, while IP growth slowed to 3.6% from 6.2% and construction growth deteriorated to -2.5% from +4.8% in the same period. In contrast, public administration growth improved to 0% in May, from -1.2% in Q1.

Meanwhile, headline inflation moderated slightly to 2.9% from 3% in the same period. We expect the latter to fall to the low 2%s by August, also depressed by high base effects. Consumer inflation expectation has also fallen back to 3% in June. Despite a stronger near-term growth outlook, the BoK left its inflation forecast unchanged at 2.6% for 2024, anticipating a decline to 2.4% in 2H from 2.9% in 1H, before falling further to 2.1% in 2025. Although noting a high level of uncertainty with respect to the future path of inflation, the BoK attributed "sticky" inflation to structural factors which cannot be addressed with monetary policy. Without rate cuts, the economy will face further passive monetary tightening, with the real policy rate rising to 1.1% in 2H from 0.6% in 1H, when using the BoK's outlook.

We remain concerned over credit risks amid elevated borrowing rates – which we see as an underlying reason for the BoK to develop more tools to deal with market volatility ahead. With South Korea's growth recovery concentrated around the semi sector, there are concerns over the financial health of other sectors. In its last financial stability report, the BoK noted its concerns over the deteriorating asset quality of Korean FIs. In particular, it noted Korean corporates' "weakened" profitability and interest payment ability. Although Korean banks' corporate delinquency rate stood at 1.7% in Q3 last year, that of NBFIs rose to far higher levels of around 4.2%. Worse still, like most of its peers in Asia, South Korea is facing a rather high wall of debt maturing this year and the next (as reported earlier) at far higher refinancing costs. According to the latest BoK report on the financial statement of non-financial corps, although, as a group, their sales growth and profitability indicators improved compared to those of the same period last year, their stability indicators worsened, with the debt-to-equity ratio (89.2% to 92.1%) and the bonds payable-to-total assets ratio (25.4% to 25.7%) increasing in Q1 2024, reversing the improvement shown in Q4 2023. There was a notable divergence between SMEs and large companies, with SMEs actually reporting a further deterioration in sales (not improvement), with SMEs in the services sector performing far worse than those in the manufacturing sector. Worse still, both manufacturers and non-manufacturers reported worsening expectation, as their concerns over weak domestic demand grew.

## ASEAN: Keeping it constructive

n Indonesia tries to assuage market concerns, while BSP turns more dovish. Singapore's labour market starts to cool; more tempered fiscal stimulus looks likely in Thailand, while a weak dong keeps liquidity tight by the SBV.

Juliana Lee, Chief Economist, APAC Junjie Huang, Economist, APAC

## BI holds steady amid fiscal concerns

Bank Indonesia (BI) maintained its policy rate at the June meeting, with Governor Warjiyo stating at a parliament hearing at end-June that it sees “no need to increase the BI rate yet because inflation is low”. The latest inflation reading in May declined to 2.8% YoY from 3.0% in April as food prices declined and remains well within BI’s 2.5%±1% target corridor.

BI’s policy decision was made amid pressure on the rupiah as there were some market concerns on the “current perception of fiscal sustainability moving forward”, referring to a possibility that the incoming administration might target an increase in the government debt-GDP ratio to close to 50% over the next five years. To allay concerns, the Indonesian government has since announced that there were no plans to increase the debt-GDP ratio as such, and the budget deficit for this year and the next will remain below the 3% ceiling. The President-elect’s proposed food programme was allocated funding of IDR71tn (USD4.3bn; 0.3% of 2023 GDP; 2.6% of government revenues) for the 2025 draft state budget, lower than the IDR120tn initially estimated earlier this year.

We simulated Indonesia’s fiscal outcomes over 2025-29 under alternative scenarios, taking the latest announcements as our central assumption. In all scenarios, including one under stress, we found it highly unlikely that the government would breach the 50% debt-GDP ratio. In the first two scenarios of alternate revenue growth paths (government-projected growth, and less-thanexpected growth) the average fiscal deficit over 2025-29 stays within the 3% limit, and debt-GDP within 40%. Even in the stretched scenario which assumed no revenue growth and higher-than-expected spending, debt-GDP rises to only 43.7% by 2029, although the average fiscal deficit rises to 3.84%. The last time Indonesia’s debt-GDP ratio had risen above 50% was during the Asian Financial Crisis, spiking from 26.4% in 1997 to 72.5% in 1998. We do not think such an event will occur.

Consequently, with the incoming administration’s awareness of the market reactions to its expansionary spending programmes, we think there is a non-zero probability that these policies could be implemented in a somewhat reduced form. We await more details on the implementation of the plans, which is expected to be announced in October.

## BSP more dovish as it lowers inflation forecasts

Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas (BSP) kept its policy rate unchanged in the June meeting, as expected. Despite the rise in headline inflation to 3.9% YoY in May (Apr: 3.8%) nearing the 4.0% upper end of the BSP’s target range, the central bank lowered its baseline inflation forecasts for the third consecutive meeting, by 0.2ppts each in 2024 and 2025, to 3.3% and 3.1%, respectively. The revisions partly took into account the downside risks stemming from lower rice import tariffs, which “could have significant second-round effects”. Nonetheless, BSP maintains that the prospects for output growth “remain largely intact over the medium term”.

Consequently, we expect Philippines’ GDP growth outturn for the coming quarter to moderate as the lagged impact of tight monetary policy dampens economic activity, before picking up in the later part of the year. Consumer spending, the key support for growth, is likely to taper in line with lower consumer confidence and the uptick in unemployment from 3.5% in Feb to 4.1% in May, alongside higher borrowing costs. This would be balanced out by a stronger exports picture— exports grew by 9.6% in Jan-Apr compared to the same period last year. Our baseline GDP growth forecast for 2024 stands at 5.6%, lower than the government’s target of 6-7%.

## Thailand may see a more limited fiscal stimulus, while housing concerns rise

Thailand's parliament recently approved the first reading of the FY25 budget draft (THB3.8tn), which includes THB153bn for the digital wallet (DW) programme - with an overwhelming 311 votes in favour, hinting at an easy approval for its second and third (final) readings before October - while the cabinet has agreed to a FY24 supplementary budget of THB122bn for the DW. In case only THB275bn are spent, from the FY24/25 budget, vs. the THB500bn envisioned earlier, Thailand's public debt may be at least 1.5ppts lower than the government's own projection of 67.5% of GDP.

Another growth upside surprise appears to be in the pipeline, with the BoT recently noting that a 1%QoQ sa growth is likely in Q2, before slowing to a 0.6% quarterly growth in 2H, justifying its policy position. That said, it is not clear that the BoT has assumed the DW impact. The latter could add 0.3ppts to Thailand's growth this year and the next. While the BoT has maintained its neutral stance, additional regulatory policy may be in the pipeline to deal with potential "oversupply" in the housing market, from the supply front. As for the sharp rise in Thailand's CPI inflation to 1.5% in May, from 0.2% in April, about two-thirds was due to the temporary utility adjustment and fuel subsidies in May and June last year, which will drop out of the data by Q3. That said, we have pushed out the timing of rate cut by the BoT to Q4 from Q3, to coincide with the DW implementation and closer to the Fed's cut.

## Vietnam's public investment slows, but wage hikes are sought

The dong's weakness continues to push the SBV to keep liquidity tight, as shown in the chart above. With its CPI inflation now well above 4%, its real rate hovers just above zero, raising the risk of rate hikes by the SBV to defend the dong.
