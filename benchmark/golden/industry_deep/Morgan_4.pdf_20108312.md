## Factor-based call overwriting strategies for Japanese equities

Call overwriting strategy has been gaining popularity as a method for generating stable income. Given the robust performance of the stock market in recent years, balancing the income-generating potential of call overwriting with the opportunity for upside participation has become increasingly important. In this report, we examine the effectiveness of factor-based call overwriting strategies on Japanese equities. The goal is to identify which factors can enhance the performance and stability of call overwriting strategies in this specific market context.

A review of factors for call overwriting in Japan: Our analysis covers a wide range of factors, including volatility and risks, spot price, fundamentals and valuation and media. By group, spot price and volatility and risks show the highest effectiveness in enhancing call overwriting strategies. Recognizing that factor effectiveness varies across market regimes, our review of topperforming factors allows investors to adapt to changing market dynamics. Specifically, while higher volatility boosts option premiums, caution is needed as it can signal an oversold condition followed by a market rebound. Improved earnings revision breadth serves as a bullish indicator for stock price, leading to better outcomes when selling more OTM calls during such periods. On the other hand, 6-month return serves as a bearish signal, suggesting the avoidance of selling or selecting more OTM calls during periods of weak spot returns. This strategy was particularly effective before end-2022, but a shift in market dynamics since 2Q23 has reduced its effectiveness.

Introducing a three-factor model for call overwriting strategies : To enhance call overwriting strategies, we propose a three-factor model that integrates top-performing factors. This approach leverages the strengths of each factor to provide a more adaptive strategy. Using a max voting scheme, the model aggregates signals from each factor to decide whether to sell a call or to refrain from doing so. The three-factor model shows higher total return and return/volatility ratio compared to single-factor models and the benchmark strategy. Backtesting highlights the model<sup>’</sup>s advantages, consistently performing close to the best single-factor models while significantly outperforming the worst ones, thus mitigating substantial underperformance risks. We test a variation of our three-factor model by selling more ITM calls when a call writing recommendation is triggered. The approach further boosts income potential and enhances risk-adjusted performance of the strategy.

Screening for attractive call overwriting candidates: Based on the model<sup>’</sup>s outputs, we screen for attractive call overwriting candidates within the TOPIX core 30 index. Currently, the top ranked candidates for overwriting 1M calls are Mitsui & Co (8031 JP), Honda Motor (7267 JP), and FANUC (6954 JP).

# Factor-based call overwriting strategies for Japanese equities

Call overwriting strategy has been gaining popularity as a method for generating stable income (see here). AUM in US-listed option-based ETFs have grown to approximately \$100 billion across around 300 funds, representing an increase of about 700% over the past three years (Figure 1). Within this category, call overwriting funds form the largest segment, comprising approximately 61% of the total assets.

Given the robust performance of the stock market in recent years, balancing the income-generating potential of call overwriting with the opportunity for upside participation has become increasingly important. Our EMEA team recently conducted a study focused on mitigating drawdowns in index call overwriting strategies (see here). This research aims to refine techniques that allow investors to generate consistent income while still capturing a portion of the market's upward movement, thereby enhancing the overall risk-adjusted returns of their portfolios.

In this report, we examine the effectiveness of factor-based call overwriting strategies on Japanese equities. The study explores a diverse set of factors that investors typically consider, including momentum, fundamental, and volatility factors. Our research utilizes the TOPIX Core 30 index as the underlying universe, providing a focused analysis on the most prominent stocks in Japan. The goal is to identify which factors can enhance the performance and stability of call overwriting strategies in this specific market context.

## A framework for evaluating factors for call overwriting

A factor-based call overwriting strategy involves dynamically adjusting the specifics of call options—such as strike price, tenor, and timing of selling—based on various factors. This approach tailors the call writing process to align with specific market conditions and specific characteristics of the underlying assets.

A major challenge in evaluating the effectiveness of a factor is determining whether it signals bullish or bearish price action for the underlying assets. For some factors, the signal is straightforward. For instance, improving earnings revisions generally indicate a positive outlook for a stock<sup>’</sup>s price. However, this is not always the case. For example, price returns can be interpreted in different ways: strong returns might be seen as a bullish signal (where strong returns are followed by even stronger returns) or as a bearish signal (where strong returns are followed by weaker returns).

To address this challenge, we develop two strategies for each factor: a bullish factor strategy and a bearish factor strategy. These strategies are designed to assess the influence of factor signals on call overwriting. When a factor is used as a bullish signal, we sell more out-of-the-money (OTM) options when the factor is above its 2-year rolling median. Conversely, when a factor is used as a bearish signal, we sell more inthe-money (ITM) options when the factor is above its 2-year rolling median. For each factor, we determine whether to apply the bullish or bearish strategy based on historical performance and fundamental rationale.

Our benchmark strategy involves systematically selling 1-month 105% calls on a monthly basis. For all strategies—the benchmark strategy, the bullish factor strategy, and the bearish factor strategy—we hold the short call positions until maturity. Upon expiration, we then sell new calls with strike prices determined by the prevailing signals.

## Introducing a three-factor model for call overwriting strategies

To enhance the performance of call overwriting strategies, we propose a three-factor model that integrates the top-performing factors: earnings revision breadth, 3M ATM implied volatility 1-month change, and 6-month return. The rationale behind this model is grounded in the understanding that the effectiveness of individual factors can fluctuate across different market regimes. This multi-faceted approach leverages the strengths of each factor. By combining these factors, the model aims to provide a more adaptive strategy, offering investors a comprehensive tool to navigate varying market conditions and achieve better risk-adjusted returns.

We employ a max voting scheme to aggregate signals for call overwriting decisions. At each rebalance point, each factor in the model provides a recommendation to sell either 105% strike calls or a more out-of-the-money (OTM) options (110% strike calls). Each factor's recommendation acts as a "vote" for the type of call to be sold. If the majority of factors recommend selling more OTM calls, the strategy will sell 110% strike calls. Conversely, the strategy will sell 105% strike calls if the majority recommends that. This max voting scheme ensures that the call overwriting decision reflects a consensus of the most influential factors. This method enhances the reliability of the strategy by leveraging the combined insights of the top-performing factors to optimize call overwriting decisions.

The three-factor model serves as a simplified approach to illustrate how factors can be combined to further improve the performance of call overwriting. More sophisticated methods can be employed to yield even stronger results.

## Risks of Common Option Strategies

Risks to Strategies: Not all option strategies are suitable for investors; certain strategies may expose investors to significant potential losses. We have summarized the risks of selected derivative strategies. For additional risk information, please call your sales representative for a copy of “Characteristics and Risks of Standardized Options.<sup>”</sup> We advise investors to consult their tax advisors and legal counsel about the tax implications of these strategies. Please also refer to option risk disclosure documents.

Put Sale: Investors who sell put options will own the underlying asset if the asset<sup>’</sup>s price falls below the strike price of the put option. Investors, therefore, will be exposed to any decline in the underlying asset<sup>’</sup>s price below the strike potentially to zero, and they will not participate in any price appreciation in the underlying asset if the option expires unexercised.

Call Sale: Investors who sell uncovered call options have exposure on the upside that is theoretically unlimited.

Call Overwrite or Buywrite: Investors who sell call options against a long position in the underlying asset give up any appreciation in the underlying asset<sup>’</sup>s price above the strike price of the call option, and they remain exposed to the downside of the underlying asset in the return for the receipt of the option premium.

Booster : In a sell-off, the maximum realized downside potential of a double-up booster is the net premium paid. In a rally, option losses are potentially unlimited as the investor is net short a call. When overlaid onto a long position in the underlying asset, upside losses are capped (as for a covered call), but downside losses are not.

Collar: Locks in the amount that can be realized at maturity to a range defined by the put and call strike. If the collar is not costless, investors risk losing 100% of the premium paid. Since investors are selling a call option, they give up any price appreciation in the underlying asset above the strike price of the call option.

Call Purchase: Options are a decaying asset, and investors risk losing 100% of the premium paid if the underlying asset<sup>’</sup>s price is below the strike price of the call option.

Put Purchase: Options are a decaying asset, and investors risk losing 100% of the premium paid if the underlying asset<sup>’</sup>s price is above the strike price of the put option.

Straddle or Strangle: The seller of a straddle or strangle is exposed to increases in the underlying asset<sup>’</sup>s price above the call strike and declines in the underlying asset<sup>’</sup>s price below the put strike. Since exposure on the upside is theoretically unlimited, investors who also own the underlying asset would have limited losses should the underlying asset rally. Covered writers are exposed to declines in the underlying asset position as well as any additional exposure should the underlying asset decline below the strike price of the put option. Having sold a covered call option, the investor gives up all appreciation in the underlying asset above the strike price of the call option.

Put Spread: The buyer of a put spread risks losing 100% of the premium paid. The buyer of higher-ratio put spread has unlimited downside below the lower strike (down to zero), dependent on the number of lower-struck puts sold. The maximum gain is limited to the spread between the two put strikes, when the underlying is at the lower strike. Investors who own the underlying asset will have downside protection between the higher-strike put and the lower-strike put. However, should the underlying asset<sup>’</sup>s price fall below the strike price of the lower-strike put, investors regain exposure to the underlying asset, and this exposure is multiplied by the number of puts sold.

Call Spread: The buyer risks losing 100% of the premium paid. The gain is limited to the spread between the two strike prices. The seller of a call spread risks losing an amount equal to the spread between the two call strikes less the net premium received. By selling a covered call spread, the investor remains exposed to the downside of the underlying asset and gives up the spread between the two call strikes should the underlying asset rally.

Butterfly Spread: A butterfly spread consists of two spreads established simultaneously – one a bull spread and the other a bear spread. The resulting position is neutral, that is, the investor will profit if the underlying is stable. Butterfly spreads are established at a net debit. The maximum profit will occur at the middle strike price; the maximum loss is the net debit.
