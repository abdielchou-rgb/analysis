## Urban village renovation and social housing are potential game-changers

Despite numerous policy easing measures, China's property market has been stuck in a negative feedback loop where falling property prices cause weak price expectations, which results in delayed property purchases and even further selling in the secondary market. However we think the recently announced urban village renovations and social housing programmes are potential solutions to create demand and reduce inventory in the market, accelerating the inventory digestion cycle. We estimate urban village renovation may contribute 5% of national new home sales, while social housing could be used to harmonise housing supply to mitigate further price cuts by developers or homeowners. PBoC announced Rmb350bn Pledged Supplementary Lending (PSL) funding increase in Dec 2023, the first time in the past 13 months, suggesting the funding support may be coming.

## Faster inventory digestion leads us to reverse our 3 year bearish view

We previously expected the inventory digestion cycle to be complete in December 2025. However our detailed analysis of these two schemes leads us to conclude that inventory digestion will now be complete by January 2025 at the latest - almost a year earlier than our previous estimates. As a result we turn more positive on the sector, having been bearish for the last three years. Since urban village renovation and social housing mainly target tier 1-2 cities, the potential property sales recovery is likely to be limited to these cities. We have upgraded COLI (now our top pick) and Longfor from Neutral to Buy due to their high exposure to tier 1-2 cities, and in Longfor's case, its business transformation.

## A negative feedback loop

Despite numerous policy easing measures, China's property market remains weak due to weak property prices and low income expectations across household sectors. The property market is now in a negative feedback loop where falling property prices cause weak price expectations, which result in delayed property purchases and even further selling in the secondary market. Secondary listings for 50 cities have increased by 30% in 2023 to 3.8m units, putting further pressure on new home sales. This also explains why secondary transactions have fared better than those in the new home market. See "China Property: APAC Focus: Deleveraging Series 4: Secondary Listings" for more details.

## Any solution? Three major projects

In November 2023, the Minister of MOHURD explained the new development model is: 1) to meet different living needs (first homes and upgraders) by providing properties of good quality; and 2) to avoid a volatile property market. In terms of execution, three major projects–urban village renovations, social housing, and “both leisure and emergency” public infrastructure projects–have been kicked off.

We think the recently announced urban village renovations and social housing programmes are the solutions to create demand and reduce inventory in the market, accelerating the inventory digestion cycle. Based on our inventory model, we previously expected the inventory digestion cycle to complete in December 2025, based on 2024 property sales and a 10% decline in forecasted new starts. However, if the two programmes are going to contribute 5% of national property sales in 2024, and assuming new starts still decline 10%, we now believe the inventory digestion cycle will finish earlier in January 2025. This is based on national inventory, and we believe the inventory digestion cycle in tier 1-2 cities will end earlier than January 2025.

## Urban village renovations

## What are urban village renovations?

Due to rapid urbanisation over the past two decades, cities have expanded to the villages which were originally located on the outskirts of the city. With ongoing urbanisation, these villages have been surrounded by high-rise buildings, ie villages within the city. They are usually categorized by: 1) high population density, 2) low efficiency of land usage (due to low plot ratio), 3) the inclusion of illegal buildings occasionally, 4) a lack of basic infrastructure facilities, 5) the existence of business activities due to low rental prices, and 6) low-cost rental housing for low income families or migrant population.

There are three types of renovation: 1) the demolition of existing buildings and construction of replacement buildings, 2) aesthetic changes to improve living conditions without demolition, and 3) a combination of 1 and 2. For the purposes of our discussion, in this note we only refer to the first one - demolition of existing buildings and construction.

## Reasons of urban village renovations:

1. Support property investments

2. Increase the efficiency of land usage in core districts

3. Improve residents' living conditions

4. Achieve the 14th Five Year Plan of 6.5m units of social rental housing in 40 major cities

5. Inventory digestion through the monetary or housing voucher compensation

## How does it work?

Urban village renovation is about demolishing existing properties and constructing properties with higher plot ratio (usually 2-3x, compared with an original plot ratio of less than 1x), such that a higher land usage efficiency is achieved. Considering the prime location of these villages, land prices are higher than construction costs on per sqm basis. Hence, the excess increase in plot ratio can be used to subsidise the compensation paid to the original residents, either in terms of resettlement housing or cash, public facilities' construction costs, local government fiscal income and developers' profit (if developers are involved). Historically, relocation housing has formed the majority of the subsidy, as villagers typically prefer to live in the same location as before, and building new resettlement housing is less expensive for local government than paying cash for nearby commodity housing of the same size. However, renovations may increase housing supply. Below is an example of Lirendong village in Panyu district, Guangzhou, redeveloped by Yuexiu Property.

## Case study of Lirendong urban village renovation

The urban village has an original site area of 1.6m sqm and existing buildings with GFA of 2.26m sqm; ie, a plot ratio of 1.40x. After renovation, it will be redeveloped into property with 3.91mn sqm GFA; ie, a 2.41x plot ratio. Among the 3.91mn sqm, 2.27m sqm is resettlement housing for the original residents and the village committee, 1.24m sqm is private commodity housing for sale to fund the project, and the remaining 0.40mn sqm includes the public facilities.

This project's nearby primary property price is Rmb49,000/sqm and the nearby land transacted price in 2023 was Rmb32,805/sqm. Hence, we estimate the increase in plot ratio of 1.02x (2.41x minus 1.40x) caused this piece of land to appreciate by Rmb54.1bn (1.62m sqm site area x 1.02 x Rmb32,805/sqm), which is used to fund the whole project.

## How does it help the property sector?

In the case study illustrated above, if the compensation is changed from providing resettlement housing (which is 2.27m sqm) to monetary compensation or housing

voucher, this could create housing demand of 2.27m sqm. In 2022, Guangzhou new home sales were only 10.27m sqm. Hence, just one village renovation can create a 22% impact on new home sales.

## Potential challenges:

Higher cost burden: This means a higher cost burden to local government, since it is cheaper to build than buy nearby commodity housing, due to higher land prices as a percentage of property prices. Assuming Rmb5,000/sqm construction cost, the 2.27m sqm implies a construction cost of Rmb11.35bn. If the government provides cash to compensate villagers and assumes villagers buy the same size property in the same location, we estimate the cash compensation cost will increase to Rmb36.8bn and the initial cash outlay will be Rmb111bn, based on a cash compensation price of Rmb49,000/sqm (nearby commodity housing price) x 2.27m sqm GFA, minus future land sale revenue of Rmb32,805/ sqm x 2.27m sqm. To address this, the local government prefers to use housing vouchers as compensation, to reduce its cash burden. However, we think funding support from central government / Peoples Bank of China is crucial to address local government funding needs.

Funding source: Given local governments' tight fiscal capacity, they need the support of central government or PBoC's pledged supplementary lending (PSL). This is similar to the 2015-18 shantytown redevelopment programme when, during the peak period, funding was mainly sourced from: 1) local general public budget (around 10%), 2) policy banks (50-80%), supported by PSL from the central bank, and 3) commercial banks (10-40%), driven by policy banks.

 Acceptance of relocation compensation: Development down cycle could be extended if the villagers disagree with the relocation compensation. As such, Guangzhou city is the first city to announce lowering the minimum requirement of the renovation plan approval from 80% of villagers agreeing to renovate to 67% (see note on 23 November 2023 for details).

 Future supply increase: Under the above case study, housing supply will eventually increase from 2.26m sqm to 3.91m sqm; ie, an increase of 1.65m sqm. However, we are less concerned about this, as local government monopolise land supply and can coordinate supply to avoid the market crashing.

Land reserved by local government: In the past, some cities allowed the developer to conduct primary development and secondary development together; ie, the land will belong to the developer without the need for a public land auction market. However, under the latest announcement, local government will have to purchase the land back after primary development and sell it on a public land auction market for secondary property development. This reduces the profitability of the developer involved in the renovation, and hence reduces motivation and requires government funding.

## Comparison between urban village renovation and shantytown redevelopment

Shantytown redevelopment in 2015-17 was successful in turning around the property market at that time, as the PBoC injected capital via pledged supplementary lending (PSL) directly to policy banks which lent to local government to compensate residents for shantytown redevelopment. During the period, PSL increased by almost Rmb3.0 trillion.

There are some similarities and differences between the two programmes. First, urban village renovation is smaller in scale (focused on the 21 mega and supercities), while shantytown redevelopment was nationwide. Second, the purpose of urban village renovation is not just about destocking, but also to provide social housing, drive property investment and improve living conditions. Third, shantytown land is stateowned, whereas urban villages are owned collectively by the villagers, and hence obtaining villagers' approval for renovation may be more challenging. Fourth, shantytown redevelopment was all about knocking down and rebuilding, while urban village renovations include not only knocking down and rebuilding, but also partial and even no demolition. Fifth, funding sources for urban village renovations come from multiple sources including developers, whereas shantytown redevelopment was mainly funded by from PSL.

## Estimated scale of urban village renovations

We estimate the total addressable market (TAM) of urban village renovations in the 21 mega and super cities based on number of urban residents living in self-built houses (proxy to urban village) and the living space per capita, as per the 7th national census data. We estimate the annual urban village renovation scale based on the local governments' announced near-term renovation target. Conclusion: We estimate the total addressable market of urban village renovation is 1.06bn sqm, and annual renovation is 52m sqm, which contributes to 23% of new home property sales of these 21 cities, or 5% of 2023E national residential new home sales. Assuming an average selling price of Rmb25,000/sqm in these 21 cities, we estimate the required investment amount for urban village renovation will be Rmb1.25trn.

## Methodology:

1) Urban residents living in urban villages: We use urban self-built houses as a proxy for urban villages. Based on the share of self-built houses over total housing stock, which is sourced from the seventh China National Census, we calculate there are 27.6m residents living in urban villages among the 21 cities. Using the city's average living space per capita, we estimate the total area of urban villages of 21 cities at 1,059.9m sqm.

2) Urban village renovation GFA per capita: According to the disclosure of local governments' working plan on urban village renovation (see Figure 14 for details), we expect the average government target for urban village renovation in GFA is 1.60 sqm per urban villager per year. The 21 cities could contribute to a total of 52m sqm of urban village renovation per annum.

## Social housing

## History

Since housing reform in 1998, China's social housing can be largely divided into two types: 1) for sale, including economically affordable housing, housing with price and unit sizes caps, shared-ownership housing and shantytown redevelopment housing; 2) rent only, including low-rent housing, public rental housing and social rental housing.

Economically affordable housing was the first type of social housing, launched in 1994. It targeted low-income families and was priced at a discount to market prices due to free land cost. According to NBS, the cumulative completion area of economically affordable housing was c551m sqm, or 7m units, during 1994-2010.

Houses with price and unit size caps refer to commodity properties with caps on prices and unit sizes. The goal is to provide cheaper (below market price) and smaller (less than 90sqm per unit) properties to residents. Home owners can resell their houses after five years but have to pay 35% of the difference between the market price of the commodity housing in the same location at the moment of resale and the original selling price of these properties. Shared-ownership housing is jointly held by home buyers and governments. These two types of social housing were only launched as pilot schemes in a few cities and were relatively small in scale.

Shantytown redevelopment housing was the main focus of social housing during 2014-218, with the purpose of destocking. According to MoHURD, we estimate the total housing resettlement of shantytown redevelopment reached 46 units until 2022, of which we estimate 31% (14mn units) was compensated by cash and 69% (31m units) was compensated by relocation housing.

 Low-rent housing was first launched in 1998 and targeted the lowest income families. Public rental housing was launched in 2010, with more relaxed application requirements than low-rent housing but higher rents. According to MoHURD, we estimate the total housing stock of low-rent and public rental housing is around 17m units.

 Social rental housing was first mentioned in the Central Economic Work Meeting in 2020 and is one of the key essential social housing programmes in the 14th Five Year Plan. It mainly targets citizens new to the city (ie, migrants) and young talent. In 2021 and 2022, governments created 0.94m and 2.40m units of social rental housing, respectively (see Figure 21).

## We estimate social housing accounts for 18% of total housing stock

Based on the discussion above, we estimate the total social housing stock including and excluding shantytown redevelopment monetized resettlement portion at 73m units and 59m units by 2022, respectively. This accounts for 23% and 18% of total urban housing stock in unit terms.

While government counts shantytown redevelopment as social housing, we think redevelopment by subsidy scheme should not be counted as social housing, because: 1) those residents receive cash and buy commodity housing, and 2) it is more comparable to Hong Kong and Singapore, in terms of social housing stock as a percentage of total housing stock (see Figure 17).

In mainland China, social housing stock as a percentage of housing stock is only 18%, compared with 46% in Hong Kong and 79% in Singapore.

## New build or conversion?

The policy document #14 specifies that, in order to lower costs, social housing supply can come from land allocation, only paying the respective land cost. Also, local government can 1) claim undeveloped land back from developers (there is a rule stating that if a developer does not develop land for two years, the government can claim it back without compensation) and 2) convert the forced disposal of properties/ land from developers' bankruptcy to social housing. Finally, if the market has a high inventory, local government can acquire existing commodity housing and convert it to social housing, to avoid repeated construction.

## How does it work and help the property sector?

We see two main avenues could benefit the property developers leading us to reverse our three year bearish view. 1) By shifting excess inventory from a comparatively weaker balance sheet (developers) with limited holding power, to a stronger balance sheet with longer holding power (government), this allows for a more orderly work through of the inventory issue, lessens downside risks and could help restore confidence to the market. 2) The second is through helping absorb excess inventory through the various schemes we’ve outlined. Our analysis suggests the inventory digestion cycle will end earlier than January 2025 – almost 12 months earlier than our previous more bearish view.

There are two models so far:

## 1. Direct purchase of commodity housing inventory and convert to social housing

Multiple cities (including Suzhou, Fuzhou, Jinan, Zhengzhou, etc.) have implemented the purchase of existing commodity housing to convert to social housing, see Figure 23. However, we believe the impact on the market is limited due to tight local government funding. While the total inventory remains unchanged, the government coordinates the social housing supply after the commodity housing market stabilises. Hence, similar to urban village renovations, local government has a stronger holding power than homeowners/developers to avoid further price cuts causing an increase in panic selling.

## 2. Taicang model: Facilitate upgrade demand

 In November 2023, Taicang local government financing vehicle (LGFV) announced residents can sell their existing commodity housing units to the local government in exchange for newly built commodity housing developed by them. There are two requirements: 1) the existing property has to be smaller than 144sqm, and 2) the existing property value cannot exceed 60% of the new property's value, (ie, the home owners need to pay at least 40% price difference for the price gap). In early November, 100 units had been successfully exchanged and the LGFV has subsequently kicked off a second batch of 500 units for exchange.

Our view: Given the record high units of secondary listings in the market, the LGFV acquisition of commodity housing could digest the inventory in the secondary market and the LGFV's newly built property inventory, and also lowers local government's burden as upgraders pay the 40% price difference. The LGFV then converts the existing housing to social housing for rental or talent housing later. We think this is the right direction to destock by moving the inventory from households' balance sheets to the government's balance sheet, which has a stronger holding power. However, this model is somewhat limited by the local government balance sheets, and needs support from central government/PBoC funding to expand the scale.

## How much inventory needs to be destocked?

The current 35 cities have inventory (defined as inventory with presale permit) of 316m sqm or 21.2 months of past three-months sales. Assuming property sales remain unchanged, inventory needs to fall by 115m sqm or 36% in order to return inventory months to 10-year historical average of 13.4 months. The average selling price for these 35 cities is around Rmb20,000/sqm, and hence, it requires Rmb2.3trn to destock. However, we think the actual amount required will be less, as the government can potentially acquire inventory at a discount to market prices, especially from the bankrupted developers. Assuming a 60% discount to the market price, it may require Rmb1.38trn to destock the inventory. We used a 60% discount, as we reference Taicang's case of acquisition of second-hand market inventory, where home owners existing property value cannot exceed 60% of the new property's value (see below).

## B. Let commodity housing return to its commodity attributes

Positioning of commodity housing: In the 35 cities' housing supply mix we expect social housing will increase, whereas commodity housing will decline. Government specifies social housing supply cannot be circulated back to commodity housing. Hence, we believe social housing meets the living requirements of medium or lower income residents, while commodity housing targets higher income residents, and the price premium of commodity housing over social housing implies upside appreciation risk to future property prices, reflecting the former's prime location and product quality.

Removal of price cap: We think more restrictive measures on commodity housing will be removed, especially price cap measures. In October 2023, the Ministry of Natural Resources announced the scrapping of price caps in land auctions, which were introduced in 2021. We think the removal of land price caps suggests property price caps could be removed in future. This would allow developers to freely price their products based on the market and product quality, motivating developers to build better products. We believe the removal of price caps is likely to have less of a negative impact on nearby secondary prices, as new home price caps historically caused disruption to nearby secondary market transactions. Also, this may restore homeowners' expectations on future property prices.

Similar to Singapore's housing model: We think this is similar to Singapore's housing model. Singapore house prices are known to be among the most expensive in the world in terms of price-to-income ratio. With the majority (79%) of residents living in public housing, this created a limited impact on the social instability on the expensive property price expensive property prices have not led to social instability.

## Factoring in the urban renovation and social housing projects

 Bull case: We estimate urban village renovations may contribute 50m sqm annual new home sales; ie, 5% contribution to new home sales, and hence 2024 new home sales will decline 5%. New home starts decline 7% in 2024E and are flat in 2025E.

 Base case: We assume half of the 50m sqm contribution; ie, 25m sqm contribution to new home sales, and hence, we raise our forecast for new home sales from -10% to -7% in 2024. Similarly, in 2025, we forecast new home sales to decline 5% YoY, vs our previous published forecast of -7% YoY. New home starts decline 7% in 2024E and are flat in 2025E.

 Bear case: Urban village renovation and social housing do not contribute any demand, and hence, we assume new home sales decline 10% and 7% in 2024 and 2025, respectively. New home starts decline 10% in 2024E and are flat in 2025.

## Still a destocking cycle in 2024

Based on new home starts and sales, included in our inventory, we estimate current inventory in the pipeline at 2.6bn sqm or 26 months; above the historical average of 24 months. In our base case, we estimate inventory months may return to the average level since 2010 by June 2025, and we should expect stabilisation in property prices and new starts by then. In our bull case, we assume inventory months will return to the historical average level earlier by January 2025, compared with December 2025 in our bear case. Under the three scenarios, we still expect a destocking cycle in 2024, and hence we estimate a negative YoY decline for new starts under all three scenarios.

## Completions upcycle to end by mid-2024

Urban village renovations and social housing have little to do with completions. We maintain our completions forecasts and expect GFA completions to decline 3% YoY in 2024 and 15% YoY in 2025, reflecting the substantial decline in new starts since 2021.
