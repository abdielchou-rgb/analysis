## Global Credit Playbook – April 2024

## Loans, Loans on the Range

Fundamentals – the data remain good: While ‘soft landings’ are rare (1995, 2019), DM data continue to track one, with PMIs improving and inflation easing. We don’t see credit as sensitive to when the Fed or ECB start easing, so long as either can ease if needed. Overall balance sheet health looks solid, but there remains significant dispersion.

Technicals – inflows and front-loaded supply: Higher rates (but lower rate vol), US\$6 trillion in US money market funds and central bank easing should all be good for bond demand (including credit). We think that heavy year-to-date supply is partly driven by front-loading (and a desire to get ahead of elections). We continue to think that EU financial supply could be much lower than expected.

Valuations – float on: While spreads are tight, richness varies significantly by cohort. We like loans (and CLO equity), where spreads have lagged and stronger data (with less easing) are highly supportive of total returns. Valuations for CCCs and EUR IG are also more ‘average’ than ‘extreme’: we continue to like both. Prefer AT1 over EU HY.

Topical – rate weakness, M&A strength: Duration is suffering under multiple headwinds: 1) Strong economic data; 2) Poor April seasonality; 3) Bad carry/roll; and 4) Heavy net supply. All four issues look very different for credit, and we are relaxed about the credit impact of the current rate sell-off. Meanwhile, we continue to see the ‘return of M&A’; we don’t yet think this activity is aggressive enough to be credit-negative, but it should be another driver of (heavy) dispersion.

## Japan & China | The ‘Hot’ and ‘Cold’ Porridge in the Goldilocks Tale

• While the macro in US/EU is ‘just right’ for credit (moderate growth, less inflation), the story is different in Asia.

• We expect subpar China growth and inflation in 2024 and 2025. Too-little inflation (i.e., deflation) remains a risk, and we expect further easing. Deflation can be good for credit spreads (i.e., Japan in the 1990s).

• Japan, in contrast, is ‘hot’, with rising core inflation, a strong stock market and the BoJ set to raise rates on our forecasts.
