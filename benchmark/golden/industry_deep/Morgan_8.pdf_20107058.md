## United States

Core CPI surprised to the upside again, up 0.3% in February

Retail sales disappointed, but 1Q GDP still likely around 2.25%

• Jobless claims stayed low, manufacturing stayed soft

• We now think the FOMC cuts 3 times this year; we think March median SEP dot shows 2 cuts

Core inflation rates surprised to the upside again in February following up the firmer-than-anticipated January prints reported last month. Core consumer price inflation still looks to have stepped down somewhat between January and February, but the inflation trend has firmed over the past two months despite year-ago inflation rates continuing to drift lower. The core CPI increased 4.2% saar over the most recent three months while the related year-ago figure slipped to 3.8%; for the former, this marked the strongest run rate since last May, while for the latter, this marked the softest print since April 2021 (Figure 1).

Fed chair Powell did not seem particularly troubled by the January inflation data in his recent Congressional testimony. And we don<sup>’</sup>t think that the subsequently released February numbers will derail the FOMC<sup>’</sup>s plans to start lowering rates this year. But the recent firmness in inflation combined with signs of continued labor market strength could lead to the Fed doing fewer rate cuts this year than we had previously been anticipating. We now look for three rate cuts this year and for the FOMC to continue with one 25bp cut per quarter through the end of 2025. The median FOMC dot SEP at the December meeting already showed three cuts penciled in for this year and we think that could be adjusted at the upcoming meeting to show two cuts now anticipated for 2024. We don<sup>’</sup>t expect major changes in the FOMC statement<sup>’</sup>s forward guidance or the median economic forecasts.

A weaker economy likely would give the Fed more confidence that inflation will moderate over time and generate a desire for the Fed to lower rates. Key related indicators this week looked mixed. The February retail sales report disappointed, with a lower-than-anticipated 0.6% increase in the headline measure along with a flat print for the important control group. It still looks like real GDP will be decent overall in 1Q (forecast: 2.25%) with solid growth in real consumer spending during the quarter, but consumers seem to have lost momentum early this year relative to a strong end to 2023. This is consistent with a cooling economy.

Jobless claims filings, meanwhile, continued to come in at low levels through early March, which points to continued strength in the labor market despite the expected slowdown in GDP growth. Manufacturing indicators have continued to look weak on net, through some noisy monthly reports. However, the broader economy (and labor market) has been able to perform well despite softness in manufacturing.

## Core CPI looks firm again

The CPI<sup>’</sup>s headline rose 0.4% in February, in line with expectations and supported by a 2.3% increase in energy prices. Away from food and energy, the core index rose 0.4% in the month, although at 0.358% to three decimals, it was close to rounding down to the nearest tenth. The monthly change in the core index was a little above expectations, and core inflation has been solid lately (increasing 4.2% saar over the most recent three months).

But the February data support our view that the firmness in January likely was not the start of an accelerating trend for inflation, and in year-ago terms the core inflation rate continued to moderate into February (moving from 3.9%oya in January to 3.8%). For the “supercore<sup>”</sup> CPI measure (core services ex. tenants<sup>’</sup> rent and owners<sup>’</sup> equivalent rent), the monthly inflation rate stepped down from a boomy 0.8% in January to a softer but still solid 0.5% in February. Tenant<sup>’</sup>s and owners equivalent is continuing to moderate, though at a slower pace than occurred early in <sup>’</sup>23 (Figure 2).

We saw a pattern in the key details of the February PPI and import price reports similar to what was evident in the key details of the CPI release. “Core<sup>”</sup> inflation rates have stepped up in recent months relative to the earlier trends, but there was some downshifting in inflation generally evident between January and February (apart from energy prices). We still think that the underlying inflation trend will keep moderating over time, but this will depend in part on 1) whether rental inflation continues to cool over time as suggested by some industry figures and 2) if the labor market softens like we expect. We think core PCE inflation will be running fairly close to the FOMC<sup>’</sup>s 2% inflation target on a sequential basis in the second half of the year. But for the nearer-term, we think core PCE inflation is tracking a 0.32% monthly increase for February, with this measure up 2.8%oya.

## Retail sales off to a tepid start

Total retail sales increased 0.6% in February, which was a decent gain, but it reversed only part of the 1.1% drop reported for January, and it also came out below expectations for a larger increase (Figure 3). Sales of the important control group—the total excluding autos, gasoline, building materials, and food services—also came up short of expectations, with a flat reading reported for February (and mixed revisions for earlier months).

Despite the softer-than-anticipated retail sales data, the rebound in auto sales last month likely supported an decent 0.2% increase in real consumer spending last month. Overall we still think that real consumption growth will be solid in 1Q, likely coming in close to 2.25% saar during the quarter. But all that said, it does look like there has been a loss of momentum for consumers early this year relative to the strong end for 2023.

## Manufacturing still going nowhere

The level of manufacturing industrial production has been effectively unchanged since late <sup>’</sup>21 and moving in a very tight range. That didn<sup>’</sup>t change in February, as total industrial output increased 0.1% while manufacturing output jumped

0.8%. This strong increase in manufacturing output was above expectations but it reversed only part of the downwardrevised 1.1% drop reported for January. Some of the recent volatility in the data looks to be related to the weather, but through these recent swings the trend has been soft—manufacturing output declined 0.8% saar over the most recent three months and 0.4% saar over the most recent six.

## Fed ahead

At next week<sup>’</sup>s meeting we expect the FOMC will leave rates on hold and make few changes to the post-meeting statement. In the dot plot we think there are better than even odds that the median dot for this year moves to showing two 25bp cuts by YE24 vs. the three such cuts in the December dot plot. We also think there<sup>’</sup>s a good chance the median longer-run dot moves up from 2.5% to 2.625% or 2.75%. We expect little change in the median economic forecasts, though the median core PCE forecast for this year could move up a tick (Figure 4).

As for our call, we are comfortable with looking for a first cut in June. It was less than six weeks ago when March was being priced in. Just as we thought that was an overreaction to soft November-December inflation readings, we similarly view recent commentary as overreacting to stronger January-February readings. Further afield, plugging the expected median SEP forecast into a standard balanced-approach Taylor rule would predict over 200bp of rate cuts by year-end. Nobody<sup>’</sup>s December dot was close to this. It<sup>’</sup>s hard to explain this discrepancy with a higher neutral rate (it would require an r\* of around 2%; in December, 15 of 18 participants had an r\* 1% or lower). Instead, we suspect many on the Committee share Governor Waller<sup>’</sup>s recent opinion that, irrespective of inflation developments, they should go slow on rate cuts unless growth shows worrying weakness. Considering their apparent reluctance to reduce rates more quickly we are now looking for a quarterly tempo of rate cuts into next year, with one 25bp reduction at each SEP meeting.

The consumer price index (CPI) rose 0.4% in February, a gain that was in line with expectations and one that was supported by a 2.3% increase in energy prices. Away from food and energy, the core index rose 0.4% in February, although at 0.358% to three decimals, it was close to rounding down to the nearest tenth. The monthly change in the core index was a little above expectations, and core inflation has been solid lately (increasing 4.2% saar over the most recent three months). But the February data support our view that the firmness in January likely was not the start of an accelerating trend for inflation, and in year-ago terms the core inflation rate continued to moderate into February (moving from 3.9% oya in January to 3.8%). For the “supercore<sup>”</sup> CPI measure (core services ex. tenants<sup>’</sup> rent and owners<sup>’</sup> equivalent rent), the monthly inflation rate stepped down from a boomy 0.8% in January to a softer but still solid 0.5% in February.

The underlying details were mixed across the CPI report. Core goods prices rose 0.1% in February, which marked the first monthly increase for this grouping since May 2023. Core services prices, meanwhile, rose 0.5% in February, an increase on the high end of the range of figures reported over the past year apart from the robust 0.7% jump reported for January. The step-down in core services inflation was due in part to owners<sup>’</sup> equivalent rent, which moderated from a surprisingly firm 0.6% January increase to a 0.4% increase in February that was in line with our expectations. While we think that the broad trend for rental inflation will keep moderating over time, tenants<sup>’</sup> rent surprised to the upside in February, moving up 0.5% that month.
