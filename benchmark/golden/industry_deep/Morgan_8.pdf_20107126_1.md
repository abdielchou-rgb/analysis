# The Viewpoint: Trade Tensions, Supply Chain Diversification, and China

Trade tensions have already led to the US shifting away from China and China has shifted away from the US. If trade tensions emerge in a meaningful manner, it would lead to an acceleration in supply chain diversification efforts.

## Key Takeaways

Trade flows into the US have become more diversified since the onset of trade tensions in 2H18.

■ Mexico, Vietnam, and India have since emerged as new manufacturing hubs, supplying goods for the US and the world.

China has, however, not lost out. It has gained market share in global exports, aided by stronger exports in new segments and to new geographies.

■ Recent developments suggest that trade tensions are coming to the fore again, which could be another catalyst for a further rewiring of supply chains.

■ In particular, trade tensions are likely to emerge in the new sectors where China had made progress.

## Follow the flow of trade

Supply chains – from diversification to disruption to diversification again: It has been a little over five years since trade tensions emerged. Since then, supply chain diversification efforts have ebbed and flowed. The onset of trade tensions led to initial efforts to diversify the supply chain. However, the pandemic created a severe disruption to global supply chains, which meant that China, which had faced minimal lockdowns in 2020 and 2021, was able to keep production activities on. We even saw that China had gained market share in global exports during that time period. The subsequent inflationary pressures meant that trade tensions took a back seat as policymakers focused on normalizing supply chains and minimizing disruptions.

Fast forward to more recent times, with the supply chain disruptions behind and as the inflation normalization process proceeds, diversification efforts have gained fresh momentum.

## How trade flows are getting rewired

Key observations of how trade flows have shifted since December 2017: In tracking the trade flows, we note the following key and noteworthy trends:

• The US is buying less from China and buying more from Mexico, the Euro Area, Vietnam, Taiwan, Canada, Korea, and India. The Euro Area and Mexico are now the two biggest suppliers of goods into the US, accounting for 16.5% and 15.4% market shares, overtaking China.

• The trade surplus of Mexico and Vietnam with the US (based on US-side data) has gone up US\$148bn since December 2017, but 45% of that has been offset by a wider trade deficit with China (based on China-side data).

• It is not just rerouting from China. Our granular analysis shows that for Mexico and Vietnam, only about 30% of the increase in their trade surplus with the US may have been sourced from China.

• These economies are showing overall trade surplus improvement and making some gains in global market share, suggesting broader-based gains in their competitiveness.

• China’s market share in US imports has declined from 21.6% in December 2017 to 13.9% in December 2023, but its market share in global exports has risen from 12.9% to 14.4% in the same period.

• China has lost US import market share to new manufacturing hubs in select sectors like electrical machinery and machinery and mechanical appliances. However, this is more than offset by its gains in global export market share by selling new products and to newer geographies. Data up to 2022 indicate some market share gains in vehicles and parts and chemicals, where China had relatively lower market shares previously. By geography, China has also sold more to Russia, Vietnam, Mexico, Saudi Arabia, Brazil, and the rest of Asia.

## The US: Partial diversification of import sources

Limited traction in onshoring... If trade policy was intended to bring manufacturing back onshore into the US, then most of the metrics would indicate that there has been limited traction so far. The US trade deficit has widened even further since 2017 and its share of global imports has remained at around 13%, suggesting that there has been very limited onshoring.

...but some success in diversification: While there appears to be limited success on the onshoring front, there are some initial signs of success in diversification. Since 2017, there has been significant shifts in US trade partners. From the US imports data, the US is now importing much less from China and has diversified its import sources. China’s import share in the US has dropped from 21.6% in December 2017 to 13.9% in December 2023. Over the same time period, the Euro Area has gained the most market share in US imports, after Mexico, and alongside Vietnam, India, and Canada.

## China: Still the most dominant exporter

China has gained export market share since 2017: Since the onset of trade tensions in 2017, China has actually gained market share in global exports, which would have run contrary to expectations at that point in time. Its global export share rose from 12.9% in 2017 to a peak of 15.6% in March 2021 amid Covid disruptions to global trade and supply chains. Even as supply chain disruptions have normalized post-Covid, China has managed to retain some of these gains, with export shares settling into a range of 14.0-14.5% over the past year, which remains higher than its pre-Covid and pre-trade tensions era.

New segments and new geographies: From our analysis, by looking at the export destinations and export categories, we find that China has been able to maintain its leading competitive edge in global exports in the following ways:

1) New segments: China has reinvented itself by being at the forefront of emerging sectors like renewable energy – EVs, batteries, solar panels, and older generation semiconductors. Over the past five years, these new segments have grown at an average of 22.2% vs the 7.7% growth in the rest of the export basket. Consequently, they now account for 8.5% of China’s export basket, up from just 4.5% five years back.

2) New geographies: While China is now selling less to the US, it has managed to carve out new export markets via a number of routes. First, it is selling to economies which are geopolitically friendly with China. For instance, China’s exports to Russia on a 12-month trailing sum basis have grown 157% since 2017 and Russia’s share in China’s exports has risen to 1.9% in 2017 vs 3.3% in 2023. Second, there are some elements of rerouting in which China sells components to companies in other trade partner economies before they eventually sell to the US – we can observe this via the shifts in trade balances with the US and China (more on this in the latter section). On a separate note, Chinese companies have been setting up operations in other trade partner economies and that has opened up avenues for Chinese companies to protect their revenues.

## The new manufacturing hubs – Mexico, Vietnam, and India

How we have identified these economies: We looked at which economies have gained market share in US imports, supplemented by a sizable increase in the economy’s trade surplus with the US. On these metrics, we note that Canada, the Euro Area, and Mexico, which had already been significant suppliers to the US before trade tensions, have only seen their shares move up. These economies are the three largest suppliers to the US, having overtaken China. Vietnam and India have both increased US import shares: 1.7ppt and 0.6ppt, to 3.7% and 2.7%, respectively. In particular, Mexico and Vietnam together have accounted for 54% of the widening in the US’s trade deficit since December 2017.

Some rerouting detected… To be sure, there has been some rerouting of the trade flows, as we detected from looking at the trade flows. For instance, the economies of Mexico, Vietnam, and India have all increased their trade surpluses with the US – but at the same time, their trade deficits with China have widened. Since December 2017, their collective trade surplus with the US has risen US\$168bn and they have also seen a cumulative widening of their trade deficit with China of US\$114bn. In other words, 68% of the increase in trade surplus with the US has been offset by a rise in trade deficit with China. On the face of it, it may appear that there has been significant rerouting.

…but a look at the details reveals gains from local production too: For Mexico and Vietnam, we have dug deeper into the granular data to see the trade balance details by products to understand if and where potential rerouting could have occurred. From the categories analysis, we calculate that about 30% of the incremental trade flows may have been sourced from China, with the rest likely filled by local production activities. We think the supply chain diversification efforts that are happening have initially been filled by assembly, with the components imported from China. But over time, further shifts in the supply chain have taken place, such that these economies are able to increase domestic content, thereby reducing their dependence on China for the components in the segments where they are taking market share from China for the US market. Moreover, part of the reason their trade deficit with China has widened is because they are the markets for China in the emerging sectors, for instance in renewables.

New manufacturing hubs are gaining competitiveness more broadly: At the margin, we also note that these new manufacturing hubs have gained in competitiveness too, not just with the US. First, they have seen increases in overall export market share gains. Moreover, the magnitude of the improvement in their overall trade surpluses has been greater extent than with just the US alone.

FDI flows corroborate the gains: Looking at the trends in FDI flows, we find that the new manufacturing hubs of Mexico, Vietnam, and India have also similarly gained market share in global FDI flows. At the same time, FDI inflows into China have slowed meaningfully. On the flip side, we are also seeing that economies like Korea and Taiwan have also increased their FDI outflows into the US – as a reflection of some of the limited traction in US onshoring trends.

## Which product categories have potentially seen rerouting via Mexico and Vietnam?

• For Mexico: Machinery and mechanical appliances, electrical machinery, vehicles and parts and furniture are among the product categories which have seen a simultaneous sizable increase in trade balance with the US and decrease in trade balance with China. Specifically, machinery and mechanical appliances include items such as computer and computer parts, home appliances such as air conditioners and refrigerators, capital goods such as forklifts and bulldozers, etc. Electrical machinery would include mobile phones, monitors, semiconductors and batteries, etc.

• For Vietnam: Products classified by materials, textiles, clothing and footwear, metals, stone and glass, and machinery and mechanical appliances are the product categories we have identified. Specifically, products classified by materials include plastic and rubber products, leather, wood and paper products, as well as printed books, etc.

## The outlook for trade flows and trade tensions

The loop between trade flows and trade tensions: As we highlighted, trade flows have been altered since the last round of trade tensions emerged. With the supply chain disruptions behind, we are now seeing another round of trade tensions emerging (recent investigations into China by both the European Commission and US and also the agreement between the US and Mexico to monitor the origin of investments into Mexico).

In launching the investigation into Chinese EVs in early March, President Biden noted that “China’s policies could flood our market with its vehicles, posing risks to our national security.” This adds to the earlier 2022 ban on the sale of advanced chips meant for use in AI technology. Ahead of the US presidential elections in November this year, former President Trump has already been stating in press interviews that he will likely impose more tariffs – whether on imports from China, across the board tariffs and also the prospect of significant tariffs on Chinese-made cars which could make their way into the US via Mexico.

Given the dynamic nature of trade flows (as we had witnessed in the data), this also raises the prospects of more onerous non-tariff barriers like potential rules on sourcing content. We see the risk of trade tensions broadening out in both tariffs and non-tariff barriers. If these tensions materialize, it would likely accelerate efforts to further shift supply chain diversification.
