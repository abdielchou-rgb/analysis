# Global Equity Strategy Equities: Stick with it, 6% upside by year-end

We lift our year-end forecast for the MSCI AC World to 830 from 800 (this gives 6% upside potential by year-end). There are 7 key reasons behind this.

1) Risk Appetite/tactical signals: UBS' Quant team has now reintroduced our Global Risk Appetite index which is currently 0.8 std above its norm but at levels that give an average return of 10.2% in the next 12 months (risk appetite correlates closely with ISM and is discounting an ISM reading that equates to only 1.5% US GDP growth). Other positioning/sentiment indicators that had warned of temporary consolidation in mid-March no longer do.

2) US wage growth likely to slow sharply: The best lead indicators of US wages are consistent with wage growth slowing to 3%-3.5% from 4-4.5%. Low wage growth indicates the inflation shock is temporary, helps profit margins and lengthens the cycle (ie full employment is probably c3%, not c4.1% as Fed officials have indicated). UBS US economists see 15-17bp per month of core PCE post June.

3) Reacting positively to weaker data: The market seems to be transitioning much more quickly than we had expected to reacting positively to weaker data.

4) Equities still modestly cheap, if Gen AI pushes up productivity growth: If Gen AI pushes up productivity growth by 1% from 2028 (we have become even more convinced of this), then the equity risk premium is 4.7%. The warranted ERP (ie where it should be) is determined by credit spreads/ISM and is now at 4.4%. Hence, we believe equities have 6% upside potential. The key to the warranted ERP is credit spreads— default rates, interest cover and the behaviour of CLO mezzanine tranche spreads suggest spreads will not widen near term.

5) Earnings revisions and performance are now in line: 2 months ago, this was not the case. Lead indicators of earnings revisions (ISM prices paid in the US) and US results (Q1 4% ahead of consensus) are now in line with the current positive level of earnings revisions. When earnings are revised up and PMIs rise (the phase we are in), equities typically give monthly returns of 2.9%.

6) Extended Fed pause is a positive: Historically, 12 months after a Fed pause, equities have been up 25% versus just 15% so far.

7) Scope for defensive-led bull market: Bottom up, we can get, though unusual, defensive-led bull markets.

Upside risk: We have 6 out of the 8 preconditions for entering a bubble that we are not in (helped by some signs that G4 central bank balance sheets are stabilising and retail buying).

Downside risk: Profit margins are abnormally high, but there are good idiosyncratic reasons why macro models temporarily don’t work (namely, tech and energy which supply 65% of the margin improvement are less sensitive to unit labour costs and rates than GDP profits).

## 2) Best lead indicators of US wage growth show slowdown to c3%-3.5%.

In our opinion, wages are key because they account directly and indirectly for nearly half of inflation. Inflation becomes embedded if it feeds though to higher wage growth. Wages are the largest component of corporate costs.

The key news of the past month is that three of what we view as the best lead indicators of wage growth have come in lower than we had expected. These are the voluntary job quit ratio (the measure that Fed chairs Yellen and Bernanke used to focus on), the percent of firms looking to raise compensation on the NFIB data and the Indeed survey of wages of new employees.

## 3) Valuation. Still 8% upside on the ERP model if we assume (as we do) that Gen AI pushes up productivity growth by 1% from 2028.

Currently, the equity risk premium (ERP) is 4% based on consensus EPS and consensus trend GDP growth numbers. If we assume that Gen AI pushes up productivity by 1% from 2028, then it rises to 4.7%.

Clearly the key issue is whether Gen AI pushes up productivity growth. Gen AI in our colleague Sean Simonds' model continues to be the critical driver of the market.

## 4) The US election.

Our central view is that there will not be a 'clean sweep' by either party and that the risk of import tariffs will be watered down.

However, in the event of a clean sweep by either party, we see a risk of substantial fiscal easing at a time when the fiscal position looks difficult and the US economy is still suffering from the effects of inflation.

On the fiscal side, the cyclically adjusted primary budget deficit was 5.5% of GDP in 2023, according to the IMF, and (state and federal) government debt to GDP is 124% of GDP on IMF data (this is c20% higher than in 2016). In other words, to stabilise government debt to GDP, fiscal tightening of c4.5% of GDP would be required (if the Fed own none of the debt and it is all rolled over at the long end). The CBO has estimated that if former President Trump were able to renew the tax cuts due to sunset in 2027, that could cost \$4.6trn.

In the event of further substantial fiscal easing, in theory the fiscal deficit could rise to a level where it affects bond markets' confidence; in turn, this could result in one of three events:

1. A sharp rise in bond yields that causes a sharp correction/bear market in equities. We estimate that each 1 percentage point rise in the bond yield takes c1%-1.5% off GDP growth and a bond yield above 5% would also leave equities looking expensive. Any significant fall in equities would also hit growth, as 35% of household financial assets are in equities. Ultimately, a sharp rise in bond yields would likely cause either a fiscal or a Fed response, we believe.

2. A U-turn on fiscal policy (i.e. tightening) that brings with it both slower growth and (if President Biden were to win a clean sweep) higher corporates taxes (with Biden looking to raise the corporate tax rate by 7% and the tax on buybacks rising from 1% to 4%).

3. A potential US credit downgrade that again could push up the risk premium on US assets and the dollar.

An additional risk of a clean sweep by either party is more inflation via import tariffs (and unlike in Trump's term, this would be unlikely to be absorbed by profit margins), and any fiscal stimulus by either candidate would be inflationary.

Sharply reducing immigration would also likely be inflationary, as would appointing a more dovish Fed Chair when the current term expires in 2026. Any inflationary boost would likely force a Fed response, with the 10-year breakeven today at 2.4% versus 1.68% in November 2016.

Another concern is that corporates might postpone investment decisions in October while they await the results of the election (for example, to have more certainty on tax credits associated with IRA). At the same time, bond markets might be wary of more spending post the election; hence, bonds could sell off as growth slows.

## Details of Risk Appetite Index:

The aggregated risk appetite indicator is calculated using a weighted linear regression of a six-month returns measure (“return”) on a one-year risk measure of volatility (“riskiness”) on a series of equity and bond indices. The slope of the linear regression represents the Global Risk Appetite. For more information, see the note here.

## Details of ERP:

Our ERP uses consensus EPS growth numbers for the first 5 years, which are linearly faded out to 15 years to long-term consensus GDP growth rates (provided by Consensus Economics). We also fade margins back to their 10-year average over a 10-year period after year 5 (i.e. in years 6-15). We believe that this adjustment helps us to make more reasonable estimates on the future growth of earnings and therefore the implied ERP. We take a 45% payout ratio (which has been the average seen in the US in recent decades). We then work out the discounted value of dividends using the risk-free rate and solve for the additional discount rate required on top of the bond yield (a proxy on risk-free rate) to make the discounted value of future dividends equate to the current market level.
