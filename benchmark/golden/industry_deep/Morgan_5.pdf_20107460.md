# Emerging Markets Outlook and Strategy

# Broadening growth helps EM weather higher core rates

## Key topics

An improving global growth outlook helps provide offsets for EM assets to higher US yields.

Our recommendations remain OW EM FX, OW EM local rates (in smaller size) and MW in EM credit, with a bias towards the higher spread components.

## Macro developments

Near-term growth prospects for the US and China have improved and could prompt upward revisions to our growth forecasts for the rest of EM.

Recent inflation prints have surprised to the upside on higher food prices, but core disinflation remains intact.

EM easing continues but upside inflation surprises and fewer expected cuts by the Fed have led us to reassess the timing and depth of easing cycles for some.

## Executive Summary

## Higher US yields once more, but this time with some growth

An improving global growth outlook helps provide offsets for EM assets to higher US yields (Figure 1). While EM assets are usually driven by a global growth factor and a global liquidity factor (i.e., US rates), the past couple of years saw markets more focused on US rates, as the global growth outlook was being significantly lowered (Figure 2). This process was already bottoming last year and since October growth indicators have been leading to upward revisions, with positive EM Economic Activity Surprise Indices also showing that the latest data are coming in above expectations. The rates implications of this have already been seen in the 60bp move higher in 10y UST yields from end-2023 lows and repricing to fewer and later Fed cuts. But the growth implications should support EM assets which have been reluctant to embrace this driver returning, with commodity prices also rising and providing support. This is an environment which we view as net positive for EM assets, given rates have already repriced and growth is broadening ahead of expectations. But we acknowledge that EM bond yields will depend on the path of the Fed which is very data dependent, with another strong US inflation print likely enough for a rethink. We cut our local duration size last month acknowledging this and may need to respond further to the upcoming data. We still hold OWs in EM FX and higher spread EM sovereigns.

Our recommendations remain OW EM FX, OW EM local rates (in smaller size) and MW in EM credit, with a bias towards the higher spread components. Our broad market views remain the same and are generally constructive on EM assets, preferring local markets to hard currency. Within local markets, the environment looks better for EM FX, which has lagged the improvements in the growth outlook and commod ities prices. The slight hawkish shifts from EM central banks will also add currency support. Our conviction on EM duration is lower – and we cut the OW size last month. The fundamental picture for duration is less supportive as growth firms, inflation risk is less to the downside and central banks are slightly less dovish. But this is countered by valuations as markets have cheapened. EM bond real yields, for example, are now at the highest levels since 2009 given the inflation fall (Figure 3), and DM rates have also already repriced higher. We have cut Asia duration (moving UW THAIGBs) but are adding some duration in EMEA EM (cutting UW ROMGBs to MW). We will need to stay attentive to the incoming US data as this valuation buffer may not be able to withstand another hot US inflation print. In EM credit, we remain MW in Sovereigns and Corporates, given already low spreads at the asset class level, but have benefitted from the rally given our OW stance in the higher spread and distressed EM sovereigns which have seen material improvements. In EM Sovereigns our monthly rebalancing reduces the extent of this HY OW position given levels.

Near-term growth prospects for the US and China—the biggest external drivers for the rest of EM—have improved. Despite the ongoing tightness in monetary and credit conditions, broader financial conditions in the US have eased in recent months. This has reflected in large wealth gains for households and, in turn, in strong nominal GDP growth and buoyant corporate profits. At the same time, rising immigration flows and surprisingly strong productivity gains are also boosting growth and employment on the supply side, while keeping a lid on wage pressures and hence protecting profit margins. We now see the US economy expanding at 1.5%ar in the current quarter (0.5%ar previously) and have marked up full-year growth to 2.4%oya from 2.1%oya earlier. Growth momentum is also holding up better than expected in China. Broad-based strength in activity data suggests that the Chinese economy grew at 6.6%ar in 1Q24, 1%-pt higher than previously thought. While we expect this pace to step down in 2H24, risks to current quarter growth— and authorities<sup>’</sup> annual 5% target—are tilted to the upside.

Our forecasts for EM ex China have remained broadly unchanged but could be revised higher in view of stronger US and China growth. Notwithstanding better growth momentum in the US and China, we continue to see EM ex China (EMX) growing at a steady clip of around 3%ar (Figure 4). Led by a sharp reacceleration in Mexico, Latam is on track to shake off its recent recession-like performance and rebound to an average 2.5%ar growth pace over 2Q-4Q24. Conversely, an expected sharp slowing in Türkiye following past six months of aggressive monetary tightening will weigh on growth in EMEA EM. But with an offsetting boost from South Africa and the CE4, the region should nonetheless deliver just above 2%oya growth this year. Meanwhile, growth in EMAX should hold steady on continued recovery in tech output while non-tech production has remained lacklustre (Figure 5A difernta covy s\*mug). A further uptick in the March global output PMI to 51.9—consistent with a 2%ar gain in production— gives us confidence that this weakness should be short-lived, notwithstanding the recent earthquake in Taiwan.

China’s economic activity surprised with a strong start at the beginning of the year, despite the market’s worries about insufficient policy support. Key economic indicators in China in January/February (Figure 6Figure 1), along with March PMI readings, point at a broad-based improvement in economic activities except in the housing market. The biggest upside surprise in Jan-Feb was from industrial production, which rose 7%oya and average was 1.6%m/m sa higher than the December level. Strong production activity was attributable to strong exports (related to a stronger-than-expected global economy) and manufacturing investment (related to policy support, e.g. equipment upgrade and new economy sectors). Consumption and services activity also picked-up benefiting from the LNY effect (average retail sales in Jan-Feb is 2.4%m/m sa higher than the December level), and March non-manufacturing PMI readings suggest that business and production-related service activities picked-up while the LNY-related boost to consumption may have faded. Taken together, the risks to our growth forecasts in 1Q (already revised up recently to 6.6%q/q saar) and 2Q (4.7%q/q saar) appear to be biased toward the upside.

Additional stimulus looks unlikely in China, while deflation pressures remain. The above-trend growth in China has two implications. First, the probability of additional stimulus (vs. policy announcement in the March NPC meeting) is low. The pace of government bond issuance and credit extension is slower than the previous year (when policy support was significantly front-loaded), and the PBOC has been cautious in rate cuts (concerns about CNY deprecation pressure and NIM compression for banks). Recent speculation on possible PBOC purchase of Treasury bonds in the open market, even if implemented, is more of an addition to the PBOC<sup>’</sup>s liquidity management toolbox rather than a signal of additional stimulus or a shift to China<sup>’</sup>s QE. Second, despite strong and broad-based activity improvement, the supply-demand imbalance remains unchanged, raising the concern about a return of deflation pressure on CPI, PPI and export prices. In particular, China<sup>’</sup>s export prices declined notably in 2Q and 3Q in 2023, a source of global consumer goods disinflation. Export prices stabilized in 4Q in sequential terms but may return to a downward path or stay soft. This will affect the inflation dynamics for the rest of the world. Meanwhile, Chinese exporters<sup>’</sup> price competition strategy may trigger escalation in trade tension.

## EM Asia

## Macro Outlook: Cyclical tech lift into 2Q24

A cyclical lift is at hand in EM Asia, watching breadth. The March manufacturing PMI data this past week depict a region that looks to be in the midst of the cyclical upswing and if it continues, should present upside risk to growth in late 1Q24 and Q2. However, the breadth of the lift remains a concern, with the production data through February suggesting that it is tech-led, though the recent stabilization in nontech is a positive sign that things could bottoming out there. Indeed, the manufacturing output in the auto sector remains soft and has actually contracted in through February. The hope is that the breadth of the manufacturing lift should expand beyond tech and if so, would provide us more confidence that this would also percolate into domestic demand as well and thus engender a virtuous cycle that extends the manufacturing cyclical lift into the rest of the economy.

China<sup>’</sup>s exports depict shifting trade winds. Aside from the cyclical lift apparent in the PMIs, China<sup>’</sup>s recent manufacturing PMI expansion has been particularly impressive and is also evident in its exports, which through February rose a strong 23.4%3m/3m, saar, juxtaposing with an export contraction of 2.4%3m/3m, saar for the rest of the region. The details in direction of trade suggest that non-traditional destinations contributed to much of China<sup>’</sup>s recent export strength, including other Emerging Markets (EMEA EM, LATAM and the Middle East). These markets now account for 30% of its exports last year from less than 23% in 2010, while the G3 has reduced its share to 35% from 45% over the same period. To the extent that these markets continue to remain resilient, China<sup>’</sup>s relative export outperformance could continue.

China<sup>’</sup>s demand remains lopsided. Although China<sup>’</sup>s strong activity data in the first two months of the year point to a positive surprise in China<sup>’</sup>s near-term growth outlook, it still does not detract from fact that the current pace of growth remains 3%pts lower than implied by its pre-pandemic trend growth of 5.5%. This is partly due to a cyclical component from the pandemic scarring, and partly due to a structural component reflecting a faster deceleration in potential growth. In our latest estimates, we expect China<sup>’</sup>s potential growth has come down from 6% in 2020 to 4-4.5% this year, and 3-4% in 2025-30, compared to our 2019 estimates that looked for a gradual structural slowdown (from 6% in 2020 to 5% in 2025 and 4% in 2030). The lower potential growth is mainly attributable to a sharp decline in productivity growth, driven by changes in external and domestic environments. ## Local Markets Strategy: Resilience can only last for so long

Rates: Turning MW (from OW) duration in the GBI-EM Model Portfolio as the region continues to be hit by external shocks. The first quarter of 2024 has seen EM Asia duration well and truly get put to the test, having to grapple with a mix of higher external rates, a meaningful Fed repricing, FX pressures and now materially higher oil prices. Through all of this EM Asia rates have remained remarkably resilient, outperforming most of their EM peers. We have put this largely down to the fact that coming into the year, the pricing of rates cuts were broadly shallow, which has meant the region has acted as somewhat of a safe harbour amidst the global volatil ity. However, the latest oil move has the ability to have deeper repercussions. Hence we see it as a prudent step to further neutralize our duration stance. We have done this by turning UW THAIGB<sup>’</sup>s in the GBI-EM Model Portfolio, and adding a fresh THB 10Y NDOIS payer. Fiscal risks are now coming back into focus in Thailand, which against still expensive valuations and cleaner positioning, should see the back end of the curve underperform (see here). We hold this against an OW on INDOGBs.

FX: More headwinds than tailwinds. With global macro markets trading towards a late cycle extension, weaker external balances and a lack of meaningful idiosyncratic pull factors, Asia FX has been more reliant on global financial conditions. A firmer global business cycle has begun to hurt Asia FX, making regional regional currencies ideal funders in the late cycle, led by the JPY, feeding though to the rest of Asia. The one exception is the SGD where rising imported energy prices are likely to keep MAS hawkish in April. With volatility rising from the east, NEER basket weights and historical betas to the JPY and CNH suggests JPY weakness is likely to hurt EM Asia FX in any scenario, but softness is likely to be much larger if the RMB also weakens in tandem. Currencies can generally be grouped into three clusters – KRW, IDR and THB (with the highest exposure and betas), INR (with the lowest weights and betas), and TWD, MYR, SGD and PHP (with high weights but lower betas). Ongoing dividend payments also compound Asia<sup>’</sup>s woes, with Korea, Thailand and Indonesia seeing potential outflows of up to 4%, 3% and 1% of GDP, lasting till Apr, May and Jul, respectively. Given these dynamics, we remain underweight EM Asia FX in the GBI-EM Model Portfolio, and outright short in KRW (vs USD), THB (vs SGD), and CNH (vs basket and INR).

China: Growth is not the only game in town. Having been a growth underperformer for the better part of the last year, China is finally seeing some light at the end of the tunnel in a wave of upward growth revisions globally. While this has led to a repricing in Chinese equities and commodities, it has

offered limited support to CNY FX and rates. The domestic cyclical lift for the yuan has been neutered by the ongoing repricing in US rates that has particularly weighed on low yielders. Starting valuations also look unfavorable for CNY FX, with CNY TWI looking overvalued vs BoP fundamentals and USD/CNH tracking below rate differentials. This owes in a large part to PBoC<sup>’</sup>s relentless FX smoothing since last year. But pressure is increasing on the PBoC to loosen their grip with onshore USD buffers eroding and large basket currencies (JPY) starting to weaken. Some flexibility in the fix and lighter-than-expected state bank smoothing recently are early signs of a PBoC shift. We stay UW CNY FX in the GBI-EM Model Portfolio and hold onto a short reduced CNH basket (vs USD, INR, SGD, AUD). For CNY rates, recent upside data surprises have reduced hopes of any imminent rate cut from the PBoC. While rate cut expectations are unlikely to be fully priced out over the long run, the fact that 10y CGB yields are already more than 15bp too low vs PMIs suggests that recent consolidation could have legs. Meanwhile, accelerating issuance in the coming months could also weigh on bond market sentiment. We stay neutral, waiting for better levels to add duration.

## EMEA EM

## Macro Outlook: Prudence is the name of the game

Central banks and inflation dynamics remain at the forefront in EMEA EM. Regional central banks have been leaning hawkish, largely on the back of domestic factors. This is the case especially among high yielders like South Africa and Turkiye, but also low yielders in CE4 and Israel. As well as the idiosyncratic factors at play, global inflation persistence and the re-pricing of Fed rate cuts on the back of strength in the US economy also call for caution from central bankers.

The CNB cut 50bps to 5.75% and accelerated the pace of easing. However, as CZK came under weakening pressure, this was followed by a flurry of hawkish commentary, despite the fact that Czechia remains the clearest case of easing among CE4 central banks. This shows that even where the economy is weak (Czech still operates below pre-COVID levels) and inflation is at the target, global factors can play a role to limit easing. In Hungary, the NBH delivered a hawkish cut, with the message highlighting the need for caution given changes in investor sentiment (and weak Forint), and that further cuts will come at a smaller pace. This is in line with our call for a reduction in the pace to 50bp/meeting from April, which after a couple of meetings should be slowed even further to 25bp. The year-end rate remains at 5.5% (assuming the ECB and the Fed will be cutting in line with JPM expectations).

The other two CEE central banks remain prudent and kept rates unchanged last week, Poland at 5.75% and Romania at 7%. The NBP is bent on risks stemming from April<sup>’</sup>s reinstatement of a 5% VAT rate for food, as well as adjustments to regulated energy prices mid-year. Assuming the ECB and the Fed cut rates, we see scope for modest cuts (25bps in November and 25bps in December). In Romania, underlying inflation momentum is around 9% versus the policy rate of 7%. This environment is hardly supportive for rate cuts and last statement does talk about less disinflation than expected. But in February the governor pre-announced easing after two confirmed months of disinflation – on over-year-ago rates – so we continue to see NBR starting rate cuts in May. Only limited cuts are possible – we see NBR stopping at 6.25% this year with more cuts possible in 2025.

Like Czechia, the economic picture provides space for continued policy easing in Israel, mainly as inflation is low and inflationary risks are rather limited, though there was an increase in momentum in core inflation. But increased geopolitical tensions and moves in DM rates pricing complicate the BoI mission. The exchange rate lost more than 3% in NEER terms in the past month while yield curve shifted high er. We continue to expect the BoI to cut by a total of 75bp by year-end, but think risks are getting increasingly skewed toward a shallower cutting cycle.

In South Africa prospects of policy rate cuts this year seem to be fading rather than firming as the balance of inflation risks is increasingly skewed to the upside. Last month, the SARB held the policy rate steady at 8.25% with the overall message moderately hawkish. A sustained return to the inflation target is probably further delayed with El Niño adding concerns of a re-acceleration in food inflation. In addition, services inflation surprised to the upside in February, raising core inflation projections into next year, while inflation expectations have remained sticky. The currency remains a risk to the outlook even as the SARB currently assesses the rand to be undervalued. This is as the political arrangements post-May national elections are far from certain. We postponed the start of the easing cycle to September from May but still expect same terminal rate, 7.50% to be reached in 1Q25. Risks are skewed toward fewer cuts because of firming inflation and shallower Fed easing.

In Turkiye, facing FX pressure ahead of local elections, the CBRT hiked the policy rate by 500bps to 50%, a month sooner than expected. It also widened the interest rate corridor from ± 150bps to ± 300 bps and set overnight lending rates at 53% (previously 46.5%) – effectivel 650bp in tightening. We still expect rate cuts over November-December to bring the key rate to 45% at year-end. The forward guidance was hawkish as the CBRT kept the door open for further rate hikes. The result of local elections showed the main opposition CHP (Republican People<sup>’</sup>s Party) collecting 37.8% of national votes, exceeding President Erdogan<sup>’</sup>s AK Party at 35.5% for the first time in the last two decades. Despite that, President Erdogan stated that the medium-term economic program aiming for disinflation will continue to be implemented and highlighted that Türkiye will have four years without elections to fix the economy. Populist economic policies were ruled out.

## Latin America

## Macro Outlook: Easing act, part two

Balancing data points. Though to different degrees, we saw disinflation in progress at the end of last year in all major economies of the region. The recent data, however, suggest that these synchronized dynamics may no longer apply. In Chile, Brazil and Peru—where inflation is lower than in its northernmost neighbors—core inflation has surprised to the upside recently. Meanwhile, in the laggards of the easing cycle, Mexico and Colombia, the slowdown of core inflation is clearer. While we still look for a consistent disinflation across all Latin American countries, the recent numbers raise a yellow flag, particularly in the Peru and Chile. Marginal information also raises questions on activity. Over the several weeks JPM<sup>’</sup>s nowcaster for the region hovered above our forecast for an 1.0% q/q SAAR result. The balance of risks to our forecasts, however, is not homogeneous across countries. Despite cutting our 1Q GDP growth forecast for Mexico markedly, marginal data suggests that actual results could be even lower than that. Peru is a particular case where our forecast and qualitative assessment are more constructive than suggested by our nowcaster.

Easing cycle continues. On average, the Central Banks response to the aforementioned dynamics was to choose a more cautious approach. Once again, different realities imposed different strategies, with clear differences between central banks that started easing earlier and those which are only now initiating their cycle. Chile<sup>’</sup>s CBC decelerated the easing pace and removed their guidance for policy rate to converge to neutral this year, just as Brazil<sup>’</sup>s BCB shortened its forward guidance, committing to only a 50bp cut in May – instead of doing so for coming meetings. In both cases we see no need to adjust our forecast for rates of 5.25% and 9.5% by the end of 2024. Colombia<sup>’</sup>s BanRep decided by a 5-2 majority to accelerate the pace in the last meeting, while Banxico suggested that their base-case scenario is for a cautious but steady easing cycle ahead. The later was a surprise, leading us to change our forecasts and accommodate back to back cuts over the year, although recognizing that a May meeting cut is still a close call.

Global decisions matter. Though focused on its own idiosyncratic cycles, central banks in the region think uncertainty on the global outlook matters. Here there are a couple of things to keep in mind. One is that we see incoming news as consistent with JPM<sup>’</sup>s view that inflation will prove persistently high this year and limit the room for much DM central bank easing. The other is that although our confidence in a high-for-long path for policy stances remains strong, several developments suggest that the heat generated by these stances will not prove sufficient to “boil the frog<sup>”</sup>. As a result we have become more receptive to scenarios in which US and global expansions are sustained for longer. This combination suggests that Latam central banks concerns with the impact of global financial conditions should continue to lurk in the background in the near term, with the risks to global rates looking more balanced than they seemed a while ago. All considered, we expect rates to be lower than priced in for countries such as Brazil, Mexico and Colombia by year end, yet to stay above central bank<sup>’</sup>s estimates of neutral rates. Chile stands out as the Latam country poised to disappoint by cutting less than suggested by the yield curve.

## Still bullish despite the quick and strong rally

Furthermore, frontier FX valuation continues to provide a substantial buffer to global ebbs and flows in risk sentiment, after substantial devaluations in recent months. In addition, front-loaded and substantial policy rate hikes should result in positive real rates on an ex-ante basis. Finally, support from multilateral development banks, mainly the IMF, remains strong as lending to lower middle income countries is now sitting around historical highs. We expect these themes to continue playing out as interest in Egypt local markets are set to continue, but with a shift to Nigeria local markets. KES remains the main outperformer.

Conditions have aligned for Frontier FX but real rates could be even higher. After significant currency depreciation in recent years, most recently in Egypt and Nigeria, Frontier FX generally offers value relative to historical REER as well as our FEER and BEER model estimates. Ongoing policy and market rate hikes in some places have also created a path towards positive real rates by year-end, although Nigeria likely will remain in negative territory. That said, it hasn<sup>’</sup>t proven to be an obstacle to attracting capital inflows even though rate cuts from global central banks are yet to materialize. In any case, on a forward looking basis real rates in frontier should be positive given the disinflation process underway and elevated policy rates. Furthermore, real market rates are actually in positive territory for most frontier markets and should provide a buffer for investors.

Making progress on sovereign debt restructuring processes. In spite of the challenges of the common framework, Zambian authorities appear to have reached an agreement on terms of a restructuring with a group of core bondholders, right after reaching agreements with the Paris club, China and other bilateral creditors. This agreement could pave the way for others such as Ghana and Sri Lanka as the different creditors reach a consensus on previously contentious topics, such as Net Present Value of relief provided and comparability of treatment. ZMW has strengthened substantially against the USD since the announcement was made.

Rise in commodity prices should help accelerate the reopening of some frontier markets, but for a majority it is a concern. Driven by a surge of financial inflows, copper prices jumped higher in early March, eclipsing US\$9,100/mt for the first time in a year, but have now retreated. In the same vein, oil prices have risen over recent weeks due to intensifying escalations between Russia and Ukraine. These price developments should be supportive for oil exporters such as Nigeria and copper producers such as Zambia. Similarly, the cocoa price could help Ghana and Ivory Coast<sup>’</sup>s current accounts as while production has declined, the magnitude of the price increase has been much larger. However, for a vast array of net petrol importers such as Egypt, Kenya and Pakistan, these developments are concerning. For now, our Commodities research team<sup>’</sup>s Brent oil forecast of US\$85/bbl by year-end should not adversely impact frontier oil importer current account balances.

## EM Hard Currency Strategy

## An even tighter market, no matter how you slice it: Stay MW the EMBIGD

The EMBIGD’s march tighter gained speed at the end of 1Q. The EMBIGD ex CCC (197bp) spread has kept grinding lower, reaching the tights of nearly the last 17 years. Meanwhile, the C-rated bucket of the index returned to “rip-mode<sup>”</sup> over the last two months, tightening by 620bp since the end of January. All told, this most distressed part of the EMBIGD has returned more than 22% ytd, representing 86% of the index<sup>’</sup>s 1.2% total return in 1Q and over half of the of the 3.2% spread return during the period.

Can we continue to grind? It’s hard to see the alignment of strong beta and good idiosyncratic stories fueling the rally in EMBIGD Bs/Cs at the same pace as Feb/March, but momentum still seems positive. And even where the EMBIGD ex CCC looks tight, its is arguably still attractive in relation to US high grade credit. Like the EMBIGD ex CCC, the JULI index of US IG corporates is also back to pre-GFC tights at 103bp. If we go back in time to 2007 when these previous tight levels were achieved following a period of persistent credit tightening in 2H06 and 1H07, the EMBIGD ex CCC traded much narrower to the JULI than it does at present—below 40bp by mid 2007 compared to around 95bp nowadays. So while there would appear to be little juice left in higher-rated EM sovereigns, history doesn<sup>’</sup>t rule out there being more to go here either.

Indeed, the 33bp rise in UST yields during 1Q 2024 was more than offset by the -51bp tightening in CEMBI BD, with a fairly strong relationship between rising yields and tightening spreads. We think this is due to the yield level being seen as the main source of attraction for credit asset classes, with stronger demand compressing spreads when rates go up whereas spreads compensate wider during rate declines.

The quarterly return profile over recent years shows CEMBI managing to deliver positive returns the majority of the time, but with notable exceptions when rates move more drastically. Looking at the quarterly return against spread and rate moves since 2010, returns were positive in 40 out of 57 observations. Out of the 40 quarters with positive returns, spreads were tighter in 31 instances and wider in 9, but the rate moves were mixed with lower rates in 21 and higher in 19 quarters. Among the 17 quarters with negative CEMBI returns, spreads were wider in 14 instances, which is split into 7 cases of declining rates and 7 rising. In particular, CEMBI returns were negative in all 8 quarters when rates moved more than 50bp, though spreads tightened in 3 of those instances to offset some of the negative impact.

Hence, we think the positive return for the CEMBI could be maintained over the coming quarters unless rate volatility rises significantly to over 50bp swings. Signs of weakening economic growth and rise in recession risk would pull rates down, but returns could be mixed depending on the extent of spread moves. An external shock, either from geopolitical developments or disruption in financial markets, would be a wildcard that could precipitate large cut in rates but also significant spread widening.
