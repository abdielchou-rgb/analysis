# US Economic Perspectives The US Fiscal Outlook

## Updating our estimates of the fiscal outlook

The Congressional Budget Office estimates that by the end of the 10-year budget window in 2034, US government debt held by the public will reach 116% of nominal GDP, and be almost \$50 trillion (\~\$48,300,000,000,000). At an event in Washington, D.C. this week, Phillip Swagel, the Director of the CBO, described the budget outlook as "daunting." University of California Professor Alan Auerbach followed, adding "daunting is probably an optimistic way of describing the situation."

That outlook assumes the individual income tax cuts enacted in 2017 expire at the end of next year. The CBO estimates extending the cuts would add \$4.3 trillion in new debt. The CBO also assumes discretionary spending declines as a share of nominal GDP, including defense spending falling from 3.0% to 2.5%, the lowest share since 1940. The CBO also assumes no recession, which is when deficits typically widen substantially, and in a large part from falling revenues. Net interest payments alone are expected to rise to 3.9% of GDP, and absorb over 20% of government revenue. In other words, even with the shrinking share of discretionary spending, a looming large tax hike and an uninterrupted 15 year economic expansion, gross US federal debt is projected to top \$54 trillion in the next ten years.

Oh, and we forgot, the social security trust fund is expected to run dry by then too. By 2034, the program is expected to no longer be able meet its commitments to retirees.

## Strong fiscal support for growth appears to be fading

Federal debt surged by 20 pp of GDP in FY2020, and government outlays in both 2020 and 2021 were roughly 10 pp of GDP higher than the years just preceding the pandemic. That scale of fiscal expansion had not been seen since World War II. Most of the expansion sought to address the impact of the pandemic, but that was followed by the passage of the CHIPS Act, the Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) and the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act (IIJA).

Since May 2023, the expansionary tide appears to have turned. As part of the debt ceiling agreement, federal government spending was capped for FY2024 and FY 2025. That was rewritten into appropriations bills that are now law. Congress has struggled to pass additional spending measures. The surge in manufacturing plant construction at the start of 2023 has slowed. Public sector construction spending has leveled off. State government revenues have come under pressure. In California, Governor Gavin Newsom has proposed budget cuts totaling \$33 billion over two years to try to close a potential \$45 billion budget deficit. Looking ahead, current law sunsets much of the 2017 tax cuts. In other words, the next Congress and administration would need to act and widen projected deficits just to avoid further fiscal drag. As it is, we estimate the economy has benefited from the deficit widening, but going forward, the deficits remain wide while the contribution to growth we estimate dissipates.

In this note we update our estimates of the deficit through 2026. We noted in our annual outlook that the November election could have a consequential impact on the outlook for fiscal policy because of potentially different ways of handling the expiration of the 2017 tax cuts. In addition, we took on board the information from tax season and account for bills we thought would pass this year but have not. We also looked at Q1 GDP details and the incoming activity data to update our estimates of fiscal stimulus and drag over the projection horizon.

## Updated deficit projections: still wide

The incoming information since our last update of the fiscal outlook through 2026 has not implied much revision to our deficit projections. Overall, we continue to expect modest deficit narrowing in FY 2024 relative to FY 2023, but also continue to estimate that the most recent CBO estimates of the FY 2024 deficit are too low. Compared to our prior estimates, Congress has passed less legislation expanding the deficit than we assumed, in particular a tax bill with expensing provisions and potential modest expansion of the Child Tax Credit. The recent aid package for Ukraine and other overseas priorities was also smaller on a per year basis than the headlines suggested, and less than we had previously assumed.

The \$95 billion aid package for Ukraine, Israel and the Indo-Pacific is expected to add around \$14 billion to outlays in FY 2024. Of the \$175 billion total funding approved by Congress so far for Ukraine, the Council on Foreign Relations estimates that \$107 billion of this directly aids the government of Ukraine and the remainder funds activities associated with the war in Ukraine. For the bills prior to this the AEI estimated that 60% of the Ukraine assistance thus far had been spent in the United States – this includes spending to produce weapons which are either shipped to Ukraine or used to replenish US weapons stocks that have been drawn upon.

With tax season ending, CBO noted in its April budget review that individual income taxes thus far this FY were up \$162 billion compared to the same point the prior year, and corporate taxes up \$63 billion (29%). That includes a payment shift from taxes whose deadline was extended in 2023 due to the California wildfires. However, the increase in tax revenues has been less than expected, and roughly 1 pp below the revenues expected in the CBO's latest budget baseline published in February (a roughly \$100 billion tax shortfall). Tax refunds year-to-date fell by \$33 billion, partly due to the effect of the Employee Retention Tax Credit, which boosted refunds in 2023. We had expected that would wind down, and the IRS has stepped up oversight.

## Updated fiscal contributions to growth

Applying separate multipliers to the 2023 deficit widening, the Biden administration spending programs, the contribution of state and local government expenditures and investment, among other factors, we expect fiscal policy was a substantial support to economic growth in 2023, and an important factor helping to insulate US growth from the historically rapid rate hiking cycle by the Federal Reserve.

In 2023, government spending and investment (the component of real GDP) expanded a rapid 4.6% Q4/Q4. The private sector response to the Biden administration programs, the Chips Act and the IRA, was extremely strong. As shown in the chart below, the construction of manufacturing plants surged (look at the right hand side axis) at the end of 2022 and start of 2023, alone contributing almost half a percentage point to 2023 Q4/Q4 real GDP growth. Adding up the components, we estimate fiscal policy, the fiscal impetus, added \~1.1 pp to real GDP growth in 2023 Q4/Q4.

As can be seen in the chart above, that thrust appears to be falling. The growth in the construction of manufacturing plants has slowed notably. Private structures investment contracted 0.1% (saar) in Q1. The nominal public sector spending on construction (including infrastructure) has declined slightly in the first three months of 2024. In the Q1 real GDP data, government spending and investment expanded 1.2% (saar), a substantial slowing from the 4.6% 2023 pace.

In May 2023, as part of the legislation to lift the debt ceiling, federal spending caps were agreed to that cut FY 2024 spending. Under the caps, defense spending would rise 3% in FY 2024 and non-defense, nonemergency (and non-VA) discretionary spending would be held essentially flat, and both grow at 1% in FY 2025. In addition, state government budgets have been under increasing pressure. "During the combined first three quarters of calendar year 2023, state government tax revenues from major sources declined 9.6 percent in nominal terms and 13.0 percent in real terms compared with the same period in 2022,"reported the Urban Institute in February. The nation's largest state, California is facing a deficit of \$45 billion in FY 2024-25, and projecting another \$28 billion shortfall in FY 2025-26. That has prompted Governor Gavin Newsom to propose budget cuts totaling nearly \$34 billion over the two years.

The incoming data on investment and government spending among local, state and federal have slowed considerably to start 2024 compared with the strength in 2023. With the state budgets under pressure, inflation having eroded some of the strength in rainy day funds built up during the pandemic, and federal spending capped through FY 2025 we expect that our prior assessment that the strong support to growth in 2023 would dissipate in 2024 is unfolding broadly as expected.

Updated with the details noted above, we expect fiscal policy's 1.1 pp contribution to the 3.1% real GDP growth in 2023 (Q4/Q4) flips to mild drag in 2024. We estimate a contribution of -0.3 pp to Q4/Q4 2024 real GDP growth. That turns mildly positive in 2025, before turning to slight drag again in 2026, as we expect partial expiration of the 2017 tax cuts to lead to some fiscal consolidation, continuing a trend toward drag that was kicked off with the debt ceiling agreement in May 2023. Note that, as we wrote in our essay on the President election, the potential outcomes for fiscal policy could be quite different depending on the outcome of this fall's election.

## 2017 tax cuts to expire end 2025

Many of the individual and estate tax provisions in the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA) are set to expire at the end of 2025 unless extended by legislative action (action that also needs to be signed into law by the President). The budget implications are large. In updated estimates (and with a new 10 year budget window), the CBO estimates the cost of fully extending the expiring 2017 TCJA provisions would cost \$4.3 trillion over the 2025-34 period. Adding debt servicing costs from the associated widening in the deficit, \$605 billion, brings the estimated added total cost of fully extending the tax cuts to \$4.9 trillion. That would be added debt on top of a CBO baseline that already estimates debt held by the public to rise to nearly \$50 trillion in 2034, or 116% of GDP.

The extension of individual income tax cuts are the bulk of that cost, widening the deficit relative to the CBO's baseline by \$3.256 trillion and an associated debt servicing cost of \$467 billion over the 2025-34 period. The extension of TCJA business provisions would add \$886 billion to the deficit with an associated debt servicing cost of \$116 billion over the 2025-2034 period. Recall that unlike the many of the individual tax cuts, many of the changes for businesses were made permanent in the 2017 law, such as the flat 21% federal income tax rate on corporations (previously 35%) as well as the elimination of the corporate alternative minimum tax (AMT) and among other provisions.

In our baseline forecasts we have assumed a partial extension of the tax cuts, similar to what unfolded when the Bush tax cuts expired at the start of 2013. As a result, we expect modest deficit narrowing in FY 2026, and the deficit narrowing from 6.1% to 5.4% of nominal GDP. Admittedly that is a few years and a Presidential election away. However the next Congress and White House decide, a substantial amount of tax policy and government revenue is at stake. In addition, the tax increase would affect a wide swath of the income distribution because of the doubling of the standard deduction. The tax increase from Congressional inaction would not just affect upper income tax payers but a potential majority of US households. That likely provides at least some incentive for action on Capitol Hill to avoid simply running off the so-called fiscal cliff.

As we noted in our November outlook, and as outlined in the President’s Budget for fiscal year 2025, released in March, a second Biden administration would perhaps allow some parts of the tax cuts to sunset. The President's Budget may not ultimately become law, but the budget proposal offers some insight into likely policy priorities or potential plans (the budget is a plan after all). Under a status quo assumption of divided government that we walked through in the November outlook, where the Republicans control one or both houses of Congress, a second Biden administration would soon face the debt ceiling binding early in 2025, and the looming tax hike. While we do not ultimately know how the negotiations between a Democratic administration and a Republican branch of Congress would proceed, the template of the Obama administration agreement with House Republicans in 2012 looks similar to what the Biden administration has publicly discussed. The tax cuts enacted under the Bush administration were made permanent for individuals earning less than \$400K per year and couples earning less than \$450K per year, but under the American Taxpayer Relief Act of 2012 the provisions that had lowered income taxes for higher-income households sunset, and those households' tax rates rose. At the time Republicans sought full extension of the Bush administration tax cuts, but because taxes would have risen automatically with inaction, a bargaining advantage for the then Obama administration, avoiding an even more widespread tax increase led to a negotiated compromise.

Looking at the administration's budget proposals, to address the looming tax increase, the administration appears to seek extension of the tax cuts for those earning less than \$400K per year, but not filers making over that threshold. In other words, that would restore the top marginal tax rate to 39.6% for single filers making more than \$400K per year and married couples making more than \$450K per year, which the CRFB estimates would save \$256 billion in revenues over the 2024-34 budget period. In addition, the President's Budget proposes setting the corporate tax rate at 28% versus the 21% established in the 2017 TCJA, but is still below the 35% rate that existed prior to the 2017 tax cuts, and would raise \$1425 billion according to the same CRFB estimates. The administration has also proposed raising the investment tax, and raising capital gains taxes for upper income households.

## There are a lot of moving parts, with a wide range of potential outcomes

As we noted in our November outlook Republicans could seek offsetting spending cuts including cutting IRS funding, those outlined in the plan released by Republicans last September, the further expansion of work requirements or the repeal of certain climate initiatives in the Inflation Reduction Act. The CBO and the Joint Committee on Taxation (JCT) put the original cost of the bill's energy and climate provisions which includes clean electricity tax credits and credits for electric vehicles at around \$400 billion through the 2022-31 budget period but updated estimates released in May 2023 put the cost of these provisions at a far higher \$652 billion through the 2023-33 budget period. In the November outlook should former President Trump win reelection, and potentially carry with that Republican control of both houses of Congress, which would allow tax and spending policy to be enacted with simple majorities through budget reconciliation procedures, the fiscal outlook could be quite different. We noted in the November outlook that Republicans could push to fully extend the tax cuts, and do so potentially with few material cost cuts accompanying that outcome.

## Wide deficits at full employment are unusual

Indeed, in US history they have tended to exceed 3 to 4% percent of GDP only in the wake of recessions or war. Even a 4% deficit with the economy close to full employment has been rare in peacetime. The chart below shows the CBO baseline budget projections, as well as the history of the primary deficit, the total deficit minus net interest payments. We do not show total debt service costs, under the assumption that if the US wants to, it could roll its debt, and thus the principal of any debt into a new bond.

Two things are worth noting in the chart below. First, the 2010s were the first decade during that time where the primary deficit did not eventually turn into surplus, and the first expansion to do so during that time, other than the Fed/Treasury turn credit off and on expansion at the start of the 1980s. That is projected to be the case for the next decade too. The second thing worth noting is that net interest outlays are not large just due to higher interest rates. The CBO assumes a funds rate around 3% over that projection horizon, and a roughly 4% 10-year yield. But the other moving part is the stock of outstanding debt. The higher it is, the higher are net interest payments too. However, if the US budget deficits are 6% of GDP with a low unemployment rate, then budget would look worse with a higher interest rate. Prior to the pandemic, the increases in the deficit that occur during downturns are historically due more to falling tax revenue than added government spending.

## Longer-term moving into budget window

"The debt-to-GDP ratio was approximately 97 percent at the end of FY 2022. Under current policy and based on this report’s assumptions, it is projected to reach 566 percent by 2097. The projected continuous rise of the debt-to-GDP ratio indicates that current policy is unsustainable." - US Treasury Department, Financial Report of the United States Government, February 16, 2023.

Congress tends to look at issues in terms of the budget window, a ten year window for planning and considering tax policy and spending. As we noted in the introduction, the Congressional Budget Office estimates that by the end of the 10-year budget window in 2034, US government debt held by the public will reach 116% of nominal GDP, and be almost \$50 trillion (\~\$48,300,000,000,000).

At an event in Washington, D.C. this week the Director of the CBO described the budget outlook as "daunting." University of California Professor Alan Auerbach followed, adding "daunting is probably an optimistic way of describing the situation." Dr. Auerbach's point was that debt tends to move with a ratchet since 2000. Debt to GDP rose in the 2000s recession, the recession following the GFC and the pandemic. The budget situation deteriorates in recessions more due to the loss of tax revenue than automatic stabilizers in the US. Deficits would expand in recessions even if there were no fiscal stimulus enacted. Then, after the widening, there is little effort to undo it. Thus, if the CBO assumes both current law (assumptions that may be optimistic) and uninterrupted economic expansion (which they assume) the actual outcomes could be much more debt-laden than the CBO baseline shown below.

Another assumption in the CBO baseline that may prove untenable is the shrinking of domestic discretionary spending. Consider defense spending. In the late 1960s defense spending reached 9% of nominal GDP, and in the 1980s was 6%. It has now declined to 3%. That implicitly has freed up 6 pp of GDP to fund other priorities. Now at 3% of nominal GDP, the math is pretty obvious: there is not another 6 pp of nominal GDP to go.

Indeed, it may be hard to justify going from 3% to the 2.5% over the budget window that CBO assumes. The agency offers numerous reports online of maintenance and other routine costs, let alone overseas obligations or additional investment. The CBO projected this fall that over the 10 years between 2028 and 2038, the cost of the defense budget could increase by 10%, reaching \$922 billion in 2038 in 2024 dollars.

Nearly 70 percent of that increase would be for military personnel or operations & maintenance — the salaries of civilians who work for the Department of Defense, as well as equipment upkeep, training, fuel, and so on. In particular, the CBO suggest in their report that the Department of Defense is prone to underestimating the cost of operations & maintenance.

We could also argue that current fiscal position is already influencing government spending decisions, and potentially restraining investing in priorities other than defense. The failure to pass the proposed tax bill at the end of this year, and which is still awaiting action on tax topics typically approved such as accelerated depreciation and research and development could be a sign of the fiscal position inhibited the willingness to occur other costs. The federal spending caps in place are another example.

At the moment, the structural deficit (or the primary balance) is expected to remain historically wide in the years ahead. CBO estimates it will average 2.2% of GDP over the 10-year budget window. This is around a 1pp higher deficit relative to the pre-pandemic average of -1.1% (1990-2019) which includes the large widening in the primary deficit following the financial crisis. Debt-to-GDP is on track to surpass rates last seen in 1946 (106.1%) by the year 2028.

## Social Security insolvency moving into view

The other fiscal issue coming into view is the looming insolvency of the Social Security system, the nation's primary anti-poverty insurance for retirees, and an important component of many Americans' retirement income. The latest estimates from the Social Security Administration (SSA) project the Old-Age and Survivors Insurance (OASI) Trust Fund will be exhausted by 2033. If combined with the healthier but smaller DI trust fund, total scheduled benefits could be covered until 2035. That is one year later than the SSA estimated last year and than the CBO estimated in their Long-Term Budget Outlook: 2024 to 2054 released in March, see the figure below. The CBO in their budget outlook also noted the improvement due to higher assumptions of net international migration (a point we researched as well).

## Do deficits matter?

In an interview in 2022, former Treasury Secretary Larry Summers said "it's clear that inflation is the dominant economic problem as seen by the American people." In the same interview, he linked that inflation to fiscal stimulus: "We had an economy where income was running short by \$50 billion a month because of the pandemic, and we injected \$150 billion to \$200 billion a month into that economy. It’s perhaps not surprising that that’s led to an overflow of demand, which has generated inflation that on the CPI [Consumer Price Index] measure has risen to 7 percent." Another Harvard Professor and former Biden administration economic advisor, Jason Furman argued fiscal stimulus played an important role in the inflation. We have generally seen the supply constraints as playing a more important role in the demand surge, but the link between fiscal expansion and the inflation could raise questions about future expansions or at least their size. We have discussed the decomposition of inflation elsewhere, but the point we made in our November outlook was that should fiscal policy be constrained, that returns the Federal Reserve to an era where for business cycle management, it may be "the only game in town."

In our November outlook we also touched on the debate over returns to government investment, interest rates, and debt sustainability. But last year provided another insight on why deficits may matter for financial markets. Term premium rose in line with concerns over the widening deficit and rising coupon auctions last fall. Our rates strategy team often notes that the cyclical dominates the structural in US government bond yields, which means that growth and inflation expectations, and the prospect of FOMC reaction, are the first order movements in the Treasury market. However, that does not mean supply concerns do not matter at all. Looking at last fall, how do we know supply concerns have started to matter?

Consider the regression discontinuity seen in term premia timed with the November refunding announcement suggesting that the increases in size of Treasury note and bond auctions would be coming to an end. As the chart below shows, that had followed a move higher in term premia as concerns rose about issuance of duration to the market. The sharp unwind following the Treasury plans to shift issuance back toward bills led to a pull back in term premium and decline in the 10-year bond yield that continued afterwards, and then resumed declining after the December FOMC meeting.

Thus, it appears the market's assessment of government borrowing needs pushed up yields on longer dated U.S. debt with the US 10 year rising 66 bp between the August and November refunding announcements and at least part of that rise was due to higher term premia, rather than inflation expectations or assumptions over the future path of the policy rate. It may be measured in basis points and not percentage points but in addition to the fiscal position potentially crowding out other investment priorities, the reaction in yields would appear to indicate markets care about the US government financing needs.
