# Private Credit Overview Ed Reardon, Managing Director

Worlds Collide – Private Credit and Securitized Markets

Private credit. We review the fast-growing private credit market, historically dominated by middle market loans. Today, “private credit” is expanding and includes other types of debt (consumer loans, CRE transition loans, Single Family Rental loans).

Alternative asset managers originate/purchase these loans, and then add leverage to deliver high returns to investors with long-term investment horizons (e.g., high net worth, pension funds, sovereign wealth funds).

Investment implications. Historically, the securitized bond market provides leverage for many “private credit” assets. More securitized issuance will shift toward private markets, especially during volatile capital markets. Buyers must balance spread pickup and customized loan terms available in private credit debt with the liquidity in securitized bonds. Private credit could also refinance some loans (middle market, commercial real estate) that public markets view today as highly likely to default.

## What is private credit?

▪ Caution: Private credit is a loosely defined term. Private credit often means different things to different market participants.

▪ Umbrella term. “Private credit” initially defined by direct lending to middle market companies; middle market loans are still the core of private credit. Today, private credit is expanding to include other types of debt/loans.

▪ Also known as: Direct lending, Middle-market lending, Asset-based lending.

## Key Aspects of Private Credit

1. Loans or debt are not publicly traded and are not marked-to-market.

2. Loans most commonly originated by non-banks; large private equity/alternative asset firms dominate the private credit market.

3. Loans are made directly or originated to sell/distribute.

▪ Private credit investors are buy-and-hold and have a long investment horizon.

Debt investing. Private ABS, 4a2 deals and bilateral loans provide term, non-recourse leverage and are the way to invest in debt form. Key buyers: insurance, money managers outside of traditional bond funds.

▪ Equity investing. Business development companies, mortgage REITs and credit funds. Key buyers: high net worth individuals, pensions, endowments, sovereign wealth funds.

## Middle market loans.

▪ High yield, floating rate loans to mid-sized companies that historically were not large enough to borrow in public markets (\$10-\$50mn EBITDA).

▪ When capital markets became choppy during COVID, middle market lenders grabbed market share and even extended into larger, broadly-syndicated loans.

Middle markets are the largest underlying “private credit” sector.

## Traditional securitized sectors

▪ Consumer loans. Consumer installment loans, Student loans, Loans backed by esoteric debt or real assets (green projects, aircraft leases, solar, music royalties).

▪ Residential real estate. Residential loans (Single Family Rental debt), “Fix and Flip” loans.

▪ Commercial-backed real estate. Transitional property loans, distressed/special situations debt. Core, stabilized properties less likely to be in private credit due to tighter spreads.

Alternative asset managers and money managers want to increase AUM in “private credit.” Specialization required means management fees are significantly higher than fees to manage traditional credit funds.

▪ Bank regulation. Increased regulation and higher capital mean banks less willing to originate assets and hold on balance sheet. “Basel III: Endgame” is expected to lead to higher bank capital requirements.

▪ Search for yield (investor driven). Private credit frequently offers investors incremental yield.

▪ Capital treatment. Insurers often buy private credit debt. Private ABS and 4a2 deals can be customized to achieve a specific rating and are not marked-tomarket. Private credit debt investments can potentially have lower regulatorycapital requirements compared to holding securitized bonds.

▪ Fees (manager driven). Private credit provides managers with an attractive fee stream and growth opportunity.

## ▪ Benchmarking.

Middle market lending. Investors use the Cliffwater Direct Lending index or the LSTA index (broadly syndicated loan index).

▪ Developing sectors lack benchmarks and face challenges.

▪ One-off nature of underlying deals/collateral.

▪ Lower issuance. Harder to compare if not much outstanding.

▪ We feel these challenges make reliable performance benchmarking unlikely over the next few years.

Private credit mostly uses long-term financing and is sold to investors with long-term horizons who (in theory) don’t need liquidity. However, we do have some cautionary comments.

Any loan is a package of features for borrowers and lenders.

▪ Features: Leverage (LTV limit), loan parameters (e.g., occupancy status, DSCR, etc.), loan covenants (debt/EBITDA, interest coverage, etc.), fixed/floating rate, loan term, borrower prepayment/extension options, substitution rights, reps and warrantees, ongoing reporting requirements.

What’s the right credit spread?

▪ Best execution. In public bond markets, a borrower seeks the best trade-off between the package of features above and the cost and amount of debt. Credit spread is a clearing price for a specific package of debt features.

In private credit, investors may price the package differently than public markets (e.g., value some package of features more cheaply or dearly).

No mark-to-market. There is no mark-to-market on the underlying credit to validate how risk in a portfolio might be changing.

Why might private credit liquidity cost rise even more during credit stress?

Credit stress often brings regime change in the C-suite; “Buy and hold” investments become "time to sell" investments.

▪ No CUSIP/public ratings/easily tracked performance. The public market is deep and large, and often requires these features to provide liquidity. Market will not have a natural buyer of highly customized package of debt options in private credit debt, if holders need liquidity.

Credit risk. Private credit has grown most rapidly during relatively benign credit environment; sector has not truly been tested by severe market downturn.

## Fed Financial Stability Report, May 2023

▪ Overall, the financial stability vulnerabilities posed by private credit funds appear limited.

▪ Buyer base. Private credit funds are the largest class of lenders and manage over five times more in assets than business development companies, the second-largest class of lenders.

▪ Redemption risk. Most private credit funds have a closed-end fund structure; locked up the capital for 5 to 10 years.

▪ Leverage. Risks to financial stability from leverage at private credit funds appear low. Most private credit funds are unlevered, with no borrowings or derivative exposures.

Transparency challenge. Sector remains opaque, and it is difficult to assess the default risk in private credit portfolios.

▪ Credit crunch risk. A deterioration in credit quality and investor risk appetite could limit the capacity of private credit funds to provide new financing to firms that rely on private credit.

Source: https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/files/financial-stability-report-20230508.pdf

▪ “Risk-Adjusting the Returns of Private Debt Funds” (Erel / Flanagan / Weisbach). Recent working paper National Bureau of Economic Research.

Key conclusion: Study finds that after adjusting for fees and appropriate benchmarks, private credit investments do not generate significant excess returns.

Study. The study evaluated the risk-adjusted returns of 532 private debt funds originated between 1992 and 2015. Risk-adjusted approach includes both debt and equity factors, because roughly 20% of portfolios include equity features such as warrants.

▪ Fees. Adjusting for fees and risks, investors in private debt funds receive returns that are commensurate with the risks they face.

▪ Higher gross returns earned by the private debt funds accrue to the general partners [through high fees]. The fees appear to reflect compensation for identifying, negotiating, and monitoring private loans to firms that could not otherwise raise financing from traditional sources.

▪ Study challenges the view of private credit as a source of alpha, or outperformance relative to traditional lending.

Source: https://www.nber.org/system/files/working\_papers/w32278/w32278.pdf <sup>▪</sup> Private credit borrowing may be ‘4a2’ offerings. 4a2 is often a natural fit for insurance portfolios.

Section 4(a)(2) of the Securities Act of 1933 is a statutory “private placement” exemption from SEC registration requirements for securities offerings; used to raise capital from a limited number of sophisticated investors (Qualified institutional buyers, Accredited institutions).

Flexibility: 4a2 provides flexibility in terms of the terms of the offering, the types of securities offered, and the use of proceeds.

Speed and cost: Given no specific requirements for disclosure or reporting, raising capital can be completed relatively quickly. 4a2 eliminates the need for the time-consuming and costly process of registering the offering with the SEC.

<sup>▪</sup> Less Restrictive: 4a2 does not have any specific requirements for the form of securities or the use of proceeds.

<sup>▪</sup> No Limitation on the number of investors: 4a2 does not have any specific limitations on the number of investors that can participate, unlike Reg D’s limitations on the number of nonaccredited investors.

## Weekly Commentary

After the prior week’s \$11.1B in net volume (new issue + refi / reset), last week’s \$5.7B looks anemic by comparison. But last week’s print by 2023 standards would have been more than 2x the average, and the highest weekly amount. 2024’s start has no comparison, and at \$51B YTD in NI is 45% ahead of last year’s pace, and 50% ahead of 2022’s.

BSL continues to be a key driver, the result of spreads which have returned to Q2 2022 levels. The current pace of BSL activity is nearly 50% ahead of last year’s deal activity, and 10% ahead of 2021’s level at this stage in the year.

Given BSL’s resurgence, it would be natural to expect the middle market / private credit (MM / PC) category to lose market share, due to investor and manager attention pivoting towards the public markets. However, this has not necessarily been the case – MM / PC CLOs account for roughly 20% of NI volume YTD, compared to 21% at this time last year. And at nearly \$10B for the year, it is roughly 35% ahead of last year’s figure.

The growth of the category seems like a durable, not a fleeting trend. At 12% of total CLO outstanding, and a private credit market that now rivals the public markets in size, the sector should continue to see heightened interest and deal activity, marking a growing mix-shift for CLOs in the years ahead.

▪ Volume aside, market focus has begun to turn towards credit over the last two weeks. Altice France SA’s downgrade from B- to CCC+ / Caa by both S&P and Moody’s, resulting from unsustainable debt levels and forecasted EBITDA shrinkage in 2024.

## Private / Direct lending – a catalyst for MM / PC CLOs

## Redistribution of risk

In the aftermath of the Global Financial crises, a key focus of policy reform was to address and mitigate the downside potential from bank lending to riskier segments of the economy, such as highly leveraged borrowers. Legislation like Dodd Frank helped to establish a modified lending paradigm that enabled investment banks to reposition as arrangers and underwriters for a deal, rather than buyers and holders of a deal.

The adjustment mitigated balance sheet risk, while enabling banks to maintain an integral role in the deal-making process by sustaining underwriting revenue streams. This pivot in operating and business model created an opportunity for large asset managers to buy the debt of riskier assets which banks were no longer incentivized to hold.

In conjunction, monetary policy that kept interest rates under 50 bps for the majority of 2009 to 2022, was conducive for borrowing and lending, particularly to larger, highly-leveraged companies in the High Yield and Leveraged Loan markets. But low interest rates were also a boon for small-to-mid sized companies with EBITDA between \$25- \$50M, which often did not fit the profile of larger BSL lending yet offered a significant yield pickup compared to the public market. The attractive returns, use of tighter covenants, and diversification from the public market positioned private debt as an attractive alternative for institutional money managers.

These factors led to an uptick in private debt fundraising, part of which was funneled into direct lending for MM / PC loans (top RHS). The amount of capital deployed through direct lending platforms hit an inflection point in the mid-2010s and has continued to scale. This growth has fed into CLO MM volume – the total outstanding surpassed \$100B in 2023 and is currently 12% of total market share.

## Aligned incentives

However, the growth of the middle market and private credit is also partially a demand-side story. A rise in volatility and interest rate hikes at the beginning of 2022 dampened the number of LBOs being executed via the public markets, restricting a channel of financing. This led to a spike in demand by PE firms for financing LBOs, enabling private lenders exposure to the sponsor-backed firms. Over the last five quarters, the private market has financed 17 LBO deals for every 1 financed by the public market (top).

Although private credit comes attached with additional costs for the borrower – a higher interest premium and stricter covenant structure are embedded into most deals – these are tradeoffs for the speed and certainty of execution that private lenders offer borrowers. During a rapid market downturn, as witnessed during the onset of COVID, access for a borrower to needed capital can be quicker through the private market due to the relative simplicity that involves only one, or a handful of lenders versus a large syndicate of 100 or more. Additionally, the direct access and close relationship to a borrower’s management, in conjunction with the information flow due to tighter covenants, can enable a needed pivot in deal terms to weather an economic downturn or a set of operational hurdles that may arise for a company and which could impact EBITDA margins.

The aligned incentives, speed of financing, use of covenants, and credit underwriting associated with private lending have been effective – when comparing the default rates for large corporate borrowers in the BSL market to the private market, the private market held up better in 2023 (chart, bottom RHS).

## Higher spread, lower dollar price

When viewing the spread and price pickup for MM loans that are publicly traded in the LLI – and thus, offering a comp for non-publicly traded MM / PC loans – the difference in yield, spread, and price between MM and larger BSL loans is apparent. Investors and CLO managers are compensated for the exposure to smaller companies, which is accretive to the underlying deal economics for a MM / PC CLO.

In 2023, MM loans, on average, provided a spread pickup of roughly 260 bps over larger BSL, and a +1.4% effective yield. Dollar prices between the two at the end of 2023 were at \$3.5, providing an attractive pull-to-par opportunity.

## Relationship capital

The main distinction between middle market (private lending) and broadly syndicated lending had traditionally been one of size – financing for later stage growth companies with EBITDA of \$50M or less seeking a check size of \$350M or less. This upper bound on check size made private (direct) lending a better fit for small-to-mid sized companies than did the public (broadly syndicated) lending market, traditionally reserved for larger companies and deal sizes.

However, as the category has evolved since the GFC, the size of the companies seeking direct (private) loans has expanded beyond the traditional \$50M threshold, and now ranges from approximately \$100M+ in EBITDA at the high end to \$5M at the low end. Additionally, check sizes have also tripled, with \$1B+ currently defining the upper bound.

To reflect the evolution of the category, the terms “direct lending” and “private lending” have become somewhat interchangeable with middle market lending, a shift in focus to emphasize the borrower-lender relationship rather than the check or company size.

1 Revenue: Privately held companies with EBITDA that ranges from \$5M to \$150M+

2 Loan Facility Size: Loans upwards of \$1B+; \$350M or less has traditionally been core MM deal size

3 ● Origination: Lenders have a direct and often pre-existing relationship with the borrowing company, as well as a deep understanding of the business and sector. A Business Development Corporation (BDC) often used as a financing vehicle

4 ● Lenders: Often a single lender or a micro syndication for larger deal sizes (up to 10 participants)

5 Liquidity: Unlike BSL loans, relatively illiquid due to limited secondary trading

A few characteristics define the middle / private lending market (below). These attributes are key points of differentiation with publicly financed corporate loans financed through syndication on the secondary markets.

## A point in time vs. a recurring assessment

A credit assessment for a MM / PC borrower, rather than a credit rating, is provided by a ratings agency. The key difference is that a credit assessment is a point-in-time estimate of an obligor’s credit worthiness rather than a recurring assessment. Additionally, the information is not publicly provided.

However, a credit assessment for the underlying collateral is a needed precursor for a MM / PC CLO. The rating agency model requires an estimate for the underlying collateral.

Most BSL CLOs use the credit ratings of two agencies, while a MM / PC CLO typically only has one rating agency assigning an assessment. S&P currently has significant market share based on an estimate of deals through February 2024.

▪ Since Q3 2023, spot rates for 3M SOFR have moderated following the FOMC’s last rate hike on July 26 (2023). With additional rate hikes off the table (for now), the market is focused on the timing of the first interest rate cut – there is currently a 51% likelihood of a 25-bps cut at the June 12 meeting, down from 67% two weeks ago. Three rate cuts on the year is the market’s current base case, although the probability of two has increased.

▪ 3M Term SOFR fell by 9 bps on the week, while the 1M rate fell by 19 bps and the 6M gained 5 bps. Last week’s stronger-than-expected non-farm payrolls print seemed to influence the 6M rate, and this week’s March CPI figure will also likely be a rate catalyst.

▪ Elevated base rates continue to serve as a compelling factor for investors in CLO and lev loan paper. At the same time, these elevated rates have made debt servicing costs more expensive for lev loan borrowers, a headwind that will likely extend well into 2024.

▪ With LIBOR’s cessation at the end of June, a synthetic LIBOR rate is being published until September 30, 2024, to provide additional runway for legacy deals to transfer to the new base rate. The difference between the two has remained locked to the ARRC recommended spread of 26.2 bps, eliminating any spread differentials that used to arise between the two due to the differences in pricing macro moves.

ABS update: Will private credit eclipse public ABS?

▪ With daily headlines in the business press, private credit is a frequent topic of conversation and instant clickbait.

▪ That said, defining private credit can be challenging as the term is almost impossibly broad. Even finding good data on private credit is difficult, although most seem to have settled on \$1.6 to \$1.7 trillion as the current market size.

▪ In the asset-backed world, this can take the form of non-bank direct lending, warehouse facilities, and private credit ABS.

▪ Private credit ABS includes 4a2 and some 144A transactions which are CUSIP’d but not readily tradeable. Can be bilateral or have small number of investors. In comparison, the “public” ABS market includes SECregistered deals and broadly-syndicated 144A transactions.

▪ In this report, we look at what growth in private credit ABS means for the broader ABS market, considerations for issuing and investing in private credit ABS, and how the two markets can coexist.

## KBRA-Rated ABS (by Deal Count)

![](images/1a9e2de33d939a9a20b4e38f58cbe6a56e7c9b6e92eb405385404c6794a6c254.jpg)

## KBRA-Rated ABS (by Dollar Volume)

![](images/c52304080f9a0a0c814ae2220ed7a2e9d632eedb872a2d0cf8925d46aefa8b39.jpg)

▪ How big is the private credit ABS market? Good data is hard to come by, but we’ll take a stab at it. Over the last five years, roughly 18% of KBRA’s ABS ratings (by \$ amount) have been on private credit ABS.

▪ Using our data for annual public ABS issuance rated by KBRA, we estimate issuance of KBRA-rated private credit ABS to have averaged roughly \$11 billion per year since 2019, with more than \$40 billion in total from 2021-2023.

▪ If the other rating agencies had a similar mix of public vs private credit ABS ratings, it would imply annual private credit ABS issuance of more than \$50 billion per year. In the absence of better data, we think private credit ABS outstandings are \$50-\$200 billion at present.

Realistically, any asset type that could be issued in the public ABS market could be issued in the private credit market.

▪ But why choose to issue in the private market? Newer, smaller issuers might opt for the private market if they don’t want their performance data to be made widely available, or if there is something novel about the assets being securitized.

▪ Private credit ABS offers issuers an opportunity to diversify their funding sources beyond the public ABS and unsecured markets. Some issuers have brought both private credit and public ABS deals to market.

▪ Similar rating process: According to Kroll, they use the same rating methodologies, processes and staff for private credit ABS as they do for public ABS.

▪ Issuers who have already lined up an investor or small group of investors might see going the private route as an opportunity to lower structuring fees vs. issuing a broadly syndicated public deal.

▪ It’s an alternative to fighting for allocation of new issue ABS in the public market. Additionally, it’s a good way to build relationships with nascent issuers who are new to securitization but could see growing origination levels in the future.

▪ There is a premium to compensate investors for the illiquid nature of these investments. For investors who don’t want to pay up for liquidity or don’t need it, this is a major selling point.

▪ Structures are often simpler, and investors can work closely with issuers to tailor structures and deal terms around specific opportunities. If changes need to be made to deal documents, having a smaller number of investors can be helpful for coordination purposes vs seeking bondholder approval in a public deal.

▪ There is potentially more transparency for investors as they can work directly with sponsors to obtain the data they need without the information being shared publicly.

▪ Availability of asset types that are not often found in the public ABS market, such as litigation finance or oil & gas. Some other asset types seen in the private credit ABS market include subprime auto, consumer loan, solar, venture debt, music royalties, and equipment.

▪ Disintermediation is occurring. Banks can be bypassed or see their role diminished in the issuance process for private credit ABS.

▪ There is a niche for private credit ABS. Smaller, newer issuers and certain asset types are better suited to the private credit ABS market. For investors who don’t need the liquidity that the public ABS market provides, but want similar types of credit risk, private credit ABS offers the opportunity to pick up yield and tailor deal structures to meet their specific needs.

▪ That said, liquidity is the big risk. Whether bug or feature, the illiquid nature of private credit ABS presents significant risk. It is up to investors to make sure they are appropriately compensated for this risk.

▪ Public ABS and private credit ABS can coexist. While issuance of private credit ABS has grown, the public ABS market is on pace for a new post-GFC record in new issue supply. Ultimately, when issuers can bring deals in the public ABS market at attractive levels, we expect they will do so vs going the private credit route.

▪ What about mark-to-market? A natural holder of private credit ABS, such as an insurance company, might hold this paper to maturity and not need to mark the position to market, absent an impairment. That said, we’ve heard some criticism of private credit as being primarily a way to avoid mark-to-market.

## Lessor issuance by type (2010 – 2023)

![](images/ccf8492fce253af54c4a08326ce90bb6adaa2ceffeb221a1d44569f44e6c2303.jpg)

Aircraft lessors have grown over the last 20 years in both number and aggregate size, with just over 50% of the global fleet of commercial aircraft owned and managed by an aircraft lessor. Aircraft lessors now own and operate more than 60% of the world’s fleet of 18,000 single-aisle aircraft, an aircraft type with numerous operators that is relatively easy to transition between airlines, making it an ideal aircraft type for lessors. Large lessors have migrated to investment grade ratings over the last five years, providing access to large pools of funding at spreads of T+125 area currently. As a result, the WACC of most lessors can be low relative to the return hurdles of private credit, we believe, providing a barrier for private credit that is seeking higher returns to enter the more “vanilla” areas of the market such as new and relatively new narrowbody passenger aircraft and/or those of highest quality. Aircraft lessors also have technical expertise in aircraft management that not all private credit providers may currently possess and may need to create.

## Western Mainline Lessor Owned and Managed Aircraft

![](images/973dd107808d2b2750354f98c743ba4d0f1a37b45b032713efa340c0eb3f5eff.jpg)

• The market for non-airline owned mainline aircraft has grown tremendously in both units in volume since the globa financial crisis. Lessors, as measured by Cirium fleet data, have grown from 5,400 narrowbody aircraft (estimated market value of approximately \$125 billion) and just over 1,000 widebody aircraft (estimated market value of \$52 billion) to over 10,000 narrowbody aircraft (estimated market value of over \$300 billion) and 2,200 widebody aircraft (estimated market value of over \$80 billion). The vast majority of these aircraft have been acquired in the primary market, particularly for the large lessors, through either speculative orders or the sale-lease back market (where lessors purchase the aircraft from the airline that placed the original order with the manufacturer and then lease it back to the same airline). Speculative orders are not a space for private credit, we believe, given that long-term financial commitments are required with aircraft only available for delivery in the current market after five or more years. The primary market for newly delivered aircraft may provide some opportunities for idiosyncratic aircraft types largely avoided by mainstream lessors (the largest widebodies, regional jets, turboprops), as well as for airlines in more challenging situations or where aircraft are not-taken-up (NTU) by an airline that may have ceased to exist or is otherwise unable to take delivery.

## Western Mainline Passenger Total Fleet vs. Storage Forecast

The onset of the pandemic caused a sharp decline in values for commercial aircraft, particularly for widebody passenger aircraft whose values were already soft before 2020 and associated with Covid-induced declines in global passenger traffic. Values for benchmark widebody mid-aged passenger aircraft (we use the mid-sized A330-300 as a relatively ubiquitous example) fell by as much as 60% during the depths of the pandemic before starting to improve, although values remain well below pre-pandemic values. Narrowbody values proved far more resilient, with values falling approximately 25% for mid-aged aircraft, with values now recovered to levels materially above 2020 levels.

We expect that the market for narrowbody passenger aircraft will tighten into 2028 if not beyond, increasing demand for middle-aged passenger aircraft that we believe may be best suited for private credit. Widebody passenger aircraft should see further improvements in values but remain a far more idiosyncratic sector of the market with greater volatility and loss-given-defaults, we believe, providing higher potential returns but not necessarily better risk-adjusted returns for private credit. We believe that the idiosyncratic nature of widebodies (particularly mature widebodies) may provide some of the more interesting prospects for private credit going forward.

## Private Credit in Aviation: Select Participants

## Private credit has long participated in aviation finance and is growing

• Private credit, defined as non-bank lending with non-publicly traded instruments (i.e., no CUSIPs) that are privately originated by non-traditional participants, has long played a role in aviation finance. The participation has been dwarfed by other, more traditional sources of funding for commercial aircraft for both new aircraft (i.e., the primary market) and used aircraft (i.e., the secondary market). Commercial aircraft finance has many attractive features that make it attractive for private credit, including: physical assets that, for many aircraft types, have a history of preserving value over depreciation curves that can be estimated with some confidence (barring occasional black-swan events like Covid), the ability to repossess and relocate physical assets in the event of repossession to mitigate loss-given-default, special treatment of claims in the event of obligor bankruptcy in many jurisdictions (e.g., Section 1110 of the bankruptcy code in the US, Cape Town Convention in more than 50 jurisdictions globally), a large and growing market for both primary aircraft finance and secondary market opportunities for refinancing, the preference of many incumbent aircraft financiers for new aircraft with more highly-rated airlines leaving opportunities for private credit, complex transactions with less well known and lower-rated airlines that require more diligence and risk tolerance and longer horizons, restructurings of airlines that may have time sensitive needs for secured financing (sometimes secured by unusual assets) that may not be appropriate for public credit markets and assets with long economic lives of 20 or more years for primary aircraft and 10 or more years for secondary aircraft that match the longer durations desired by many participants in private credit, we believe. In addition, we believe that private credit may also provide financing for smaller operating lessors without access to the investment-grade bond market or who may be less suitable for traditional bank financing.

• Among the longest participants in private credit (depending on how it is defined), we would include PK AirFinance, long a part of GE Capital Aviation Services (GECAS) before its eventual sale to Apollo and Athene in 2019 for a premium to its \$3.6 billion of financing receivables. PK AirFinance complemented the operating lease business of GECAS by providing long-term loans secured by aircraft assets and would, in the assessment of the purchaser, be similarly complementary to the aircraft leasing capabilities of Apollo. We believe that having an associated platform with expertise in remarketing aircraft, potentially necessary in the event of the bankruptcy of the airline obligor, is a distinct advantage for participants in aviation-backed private credit.

Source: Boeing, Deutsche Bank
