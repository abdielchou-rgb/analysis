## January Employment

Low jobless claims and warm weather in early January suggest strong payrolls. We expect private payrolls rose 165k and headline 215k ( Exhibit 2 ). Earnings forecasted to rise at their recent pace, +0.3%M (+4.1%Y, unchanged from prior month). We expect the unemployment rate remained unchanged at 3.7%, reflecting strong employment growth matched by some rebound in the labor force (+0.1pp).

The January report includes annual benchmark revisions to the payroll survey - probably about a 25k per month average cut to payrolls in the year through March 2023 (based on the BLS preliminary estimate). Some downward revision probably spills into later in the year, adding to the pattern of downward revisions (see US Economics: Answering Your Questions on Employment (19 Jan 2024)).

The household survey will include its annual update of population assumptions, which, as always, creates a break in the levels of employment, unemployment, and the labor force. Rates - unemployment rate & the like---are still "clean" data, more or less; but the levels series are not.

## Does last month's plunge in household-survey employment signal weakness?

While payrolls rose by 216k in December, the household-survey measure of employment plunged 683k. And for 4Q23 as a whole, payrolls rose 165k per month on average while the household-survey measure fell 122k on average ( Exhibit 3 ). The household survey weakness, along with recent declines in the labor force and a plunge in the services ISM employment index, raises the possibility that payrolls are overstating activity. Perhaps the payroll survey, failing to recognize faster business closures, is ignoring a new deterioration in labor market conditions. We don't think payrolls are overstated, rather, the household survey can be volatile and has likely exaggerated the decline ( Exhibit 4 ).

The drop in HH-survey employment has been matched by a decline in the labor force that bears watching. Prime-age participation has fallen for three months ( Exhibit 5 & Exhibit 6 ).

## Payrolls

Low jobless claims, warm weather, and favorable seasonal adjustment add strength.

For January, we expect widespread gains in payrolls, with particular strength in construction, because of warm weather, and a rebound in couriers after an outsized decline in December. There is risk in public education payrolls simply because December is a month of large layoffs NSA, and adjusting for seasonal layoffs is difficult. But we do not see a bias toward higher or lower education payrolls.

Weather was warm in December and remained warm into early January. Typically, January employment is more sensitive to warmth than earlier months – a later start to worsening winter weather encourages employment growth in weather sensitive industries. We estimate a 15k boost.

Seasonal factors also create upside risk. On an NSA basis, private payrolls tend to fall dramatically in January. Last year's seasonal adjustment for that decline added 2.584 million to change in NSA figures to arrive at the seasonally adjusted change in private payrolls [ Exhibit 10 ]. However, the addition was much smaller than in previous years: 2.737 million in 2022; 2.688 million in 2021; 2.773 million in pre-pandemic 2019. Any return toward earlier-year adjustment factors could add significantly to this January.

As noted, the upcoming employment report includes the annual benchmark revision that realigns the level of employment between April 2022 and March 2023 with comprehensive data from unemployment insurance records. The preliminary estimate, released in August 2023, was that the March 2023 level of payrolls will be lowered by 306k, or 0.2% - a bit larger than the average adjustment over the past 10 years of +/-0.1%. As published, payrolls rose an average of 337k per month in the 12 months through March 2023; the preliminary estimate suggests they will be cut by 26k per month to 311k per month on average. Some of the softer trend through last March could be extrapolated into more recent months.

Transport and warehousing payrolls get cut sharply, accounting for almost half of the overall downward revision. Professional and business services will also be trimmed, along with leisure and hospitality and other services. Retail and wholesale trade payrolls get a slight boost, as does government.

Our 0.3% forecast for average hourly earnings follows an above-trend 0.4% rise in December. It leaves the 12-month pace at 4.1%, in line with December and the 4Q average.

## Labor Force Participation & Unemployment Rate

The low unemployment rate and the net decline in the participation rate will be an important focus. We expect an unchanged unemployment rate at 3.7%, despite rapid payroll growth, as the labor force begins to recover. The labor force participation rate ticks up from 62.5% to 62.6%. The flows out of employment and out of the labor force will be an especial focus.

The fall in the unemployment rate in the past two months has coincided with stronger labor market assessments in the Conference Board survey of consumer confidence. Still, the Conference Board's tally of labor market assessments, even improvements in November and December, continues to suggest upward pressure on unemployment. Our forecasts for a rising unemployment rate this year rely on continued gains in labor supply.

## What's happening with labor supply?

Labor force flows provide some perspective on the December weakness in household employment. There are flows into and out of employment from the unemployed and also from "not in the labor force". Perhaps surprisingly, flows to employment from "not in the labor force" - meaning people who haven't searched for work - are about twice the size of flows from unemployment into employment. These flows are important for their size and, right now, for what they may indicate about continued labor force growth.

In December, almost all of the decline in household-survey employment reflected a sharp drop-off in flows from not in the labor force into employment - slower hiring [ Exhibit 16 ]. At the same time, there were not particularly faster layoffs (flows from employment to unemployment) - no sign there of slower labor demand [ Exhibit 17 ]. Still, the implication is that fewer people were being pulled into jobs by the tight labor market.

We are skeptical that that is what's happening simply because other indicators do not suggest such an interruption. On labor demand: layoffs haven't increased, jobless claims are low, and vacancies remain high [ Exhibit 18 ]. One possible explanation for the dropoff in flows to employment could be labor mismatch. However, small firms, are not reporting it any harder to find qualified workers [ Exhibit 19 ]. Another possible explanation for the dropoff in flows to employment would be labor supply, but other flows into the labor force do not suggest a recent shock [ Exhibit 20 ].

We do not completely dismiss the decline in flows from not in the labor force to employment, they have gone on for several months. But real wages are rising; we think prime-age participation has further to run; the combination should continue to support employment growth. And most other labor indicators remains strong.

New and continuing jobless claims are low. Some analysts have suggested that fewer of those who are out of work are making claims. However, new claims do not look low as a share of flows into unemployment; nor do continuing claims look low relative to total unemployment.

Job openings suggest continued strong labor demand. They remain high, including a slight rise in the latest month. The ratio of vacancies-to-unemployment had been declining, but the decline has stalled. Other Job Openings and Labor Turnover (JOLTs) data indicate somewhat depressed hiring and layoff rates- less churn -but there's been no particular further slowdown in recent months.
