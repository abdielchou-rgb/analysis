# Global Equity Strategy

## Stick to year-end target and an overweight of Europe. Stay overweight defensives, in general.

There seem to have been 4 potential catalysts for recent events which in order are: i) weak US data (initial jobless claims, UoM consumer confidence, NFP, ISM), ii) the general concern over tech (both valuation and the worry of too much capex going into AI), iii) the surprise BoJ move (and hence carry trade unwind)), iv) the lack of any aggressive stimulus in China post the Third Plenum. July/ August sell-offs of 10%+ are not that unusual can be quickly reversed (2007, 2010, 2011, 2013 and 2015) or, occasionally, the start of a bear market (2008).

We stick with our year-end target of MSCI AC World 830 (c5% upside). We have the following supports: i) Tacticals: Risk Appetite is now 0.4 std below average and consistent with the current weak ISM (which is pricing in c1% GDP), VIX has risen much more than implied by high credit spreads (and spreads tend to be the judge and jury of the bull and bear markets), Gerry Fowler highlights risk targetting allocation is back to 18 month low, markets are now discounting a rollover in earnings momentum (see Figure 4) ii) the ERP is now 4.7% without Gen AI (5.4%and back to its average if you believe, as we do, that Gen AI pushes up productivity growth by 1% from 28) - the warranted ERP (based on PMI/ISM and spreads) is 4.9% - this gives c9% upside; iv) credit spreads are 2x the default rate (and still more upgrades than downgrades in IG) so we don’t see a further sell off in spreads; v) the best lead indicators imply 3% US wage growth (i.e unit labour costs of 1.5% or lower) - So the Fed can respond aggressively if required (to support their dual mandate and recall 3 out of the last 4 rate cutting cycles started with 50bp off rates), it also alleviates the margin squeeze; vi) we see no reason why monetary policy should not work (there is no bank or private sector balance sheet problem or over-investment) - 1% off rates in Europe adds 1% to GDP growth; vi) if the Fed cut and there is no recession, then normally markets do well (as in1995, 1998).

The downside risk is that US profit margins are abnormally high (but if anything the margin squeeze should alleviate as US wage growth slows and our margin models suggest the time of peak squeeze is behind us) and China (the triple bubble has burst and the policy response is very lagging).

Regions: the US is normally the place to be if global GDP growth is sub 3.5% but this time around much of the economic weakness is US not Europe focussed and valuations in Europe are abnormally cheap (the sector adjusted P/E is on a 21% discount versus a norm of 7%). The UK remains a port in a storm (outperforms when markets fall or defensives outperform).

Sector: we continue to be overweight ‘growth’ defensives. Cyclicals are still pricing in higher PMIs and on no measures are cheap (indeed in the US the p/s and p/e relatives are expensive). Intra-index volatility is up but still at low level (often a bad sign for cyclicals). We do however, focus on banks (too cheap and as Sweden showed into a recession NPLs do not rise, see here) and the European consumer (the consumer is always the most defensive cyclical sector especially as oil and rates fall and as inflation falls much more quickly than wages). What has been notable into this sell-off is that growth stocks have underperformed as the TIPS yield fall and the dollar is not the safe haven. Style: we find that low PEG and low leverage have on average been the best performing styles in both up and down phases of the market.

## 3) US Wage data if anything coming in lower than expected.

Low wages is critical for profit margins and the Fed policy response (and thus the length of the cycle). If wage growth is too high, not only does it squeeze margins but the Fed have to tighten policy until wage growth falls to a level consistent with their inflation target.

Already we can see that most reliable measures of wage growth are hovering around 3% to 3.6% which even without Gen AI is consistent with unit labour costs of 1.5% to 2% and the best lead indicator is consistent with 3% wage growth. This means that the full employment rate is considerably lower than the current level (probably closer to 3% than 4.3%).

## The downside risk:

1) China.

We continue to worry very significantly about China. China has been responsible for 36% of the increase in GDP growth since 2010.

China nominal GDP growth in Q2 was just 4% yoy and the triple bubble (credit real estate and investment) has truly burst.

We saw the fourth largest credit bubble over a 10 year period of any region which resulted in a huge over-build in real estate (which peaked at 13% of GDP v 7% of GDP in the US pre-GFC with a vacancy rate of 18%) and huge overvaluation (even now the rental yield is half the mortgage rate, having been above it in 2008). Secondary real estate prices are falling at near record amounts.

We also have had an investment share of GDP higher than any country has ever had. Initially two thirds of investment went into real estate and infrastructure and now it is focussed on manufacturing capacity. The problem is that when producer prices fall (as they are) and export deflators fall, then there is clear cut excess capacity (as we witness bottom up in most industries). China increasingly is finding it difficult to export this excess capacity given increased trade barrier.

China’s policy response so far has been very limited in our opinion. Large scale fiscal easing without QE is hard given a primary budget deficit of c6% of GDP on IMF data and government debt to GDP of c85% of GDP in 2024. Meanwhile the PBOC have been committed to a strong RmB policy and hence its balance sheet (and especially the PSL) has not expanded. Something has to give we believe.

The good news is that there is still policy flexibility because China is a net creditor with a current account surplus (i.e they can print) and if necessary do fiscal QE. Also there is not quite true deflation because wages are not yet falling (thought actual wage data is much weaker than official wage data)

China credit spreads remain low and Chinese banks have not been underperformingboth of which would happen if we were into true deflation. Apart from China itself, we have been underweight most things related to China.

## On the cyclical side:

1) We would highlight our overweight of banks: We do believe that on the cyclical side that banks are clearly pricing in too much bad news. The fact was that Sweden had a recession and hardly saw a rise in NPLs. The bank team have an excellent note on this (see here)

2) We would think that the consumer should be defensive (historically it has almost no corelation with credit spreads or PMIs and is thus the most defensive of the cyclical sectors). The rationale is that inflation falls more quickly than wages into this environment, the fall in energy prices helps the consumer as does the fall in rates. Excess savings are around 16% of GDP in UK and 8% of GDP in Europe, helping provide a safety net. 1% off GDP growth adds about 70bp to consumption after a year (For more info see here).

Elsewhere we would highlight that into the sell-off, the dollar has weakened not strengthened as is normally the case. This again helps the consumer and domestic plays (such as banks and utilities)
