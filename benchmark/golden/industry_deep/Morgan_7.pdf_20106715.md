Further, AI/ML emerged as the top CIO IT priority in 4Q23 (see here). This development is further supported by recent AI-related data center expansions as our analysts now forecast US hyperscaler data center capex to grow \~40% Y/Y in 2024, up from \~30% before 4Q23 results.

• ...and the Broader Market Starting Next Year. We see AI-driven productivity adding an additional 30 bps to 2025 net margin for the S&P 500 (13.0% net margin in the base case) though we believe risk is skewed to the upside/our bull case in this respect—50 bps of added impact. ## Executive Summary

As the macro cycle ages, corporates have been increasingly focused on driving operational efficiency ( Exhibit 1 ). This dynamic is centered on both expense discipline and on select investments that have the potential to foster future productivity, including AI. On this front, there is a notable amount of overlap among the industries discussing operational efficiency most prevalently and those that have the potential to realize more significant AI-driven efficiency gains based on the new framework we introduce in this report ( Exhibit 2 ; blue borders surround such industries).

Our new industry group framework identifies areas of the market where the potential for AI-driven efficiency is more significant over a multi-year time horizon ( Exhibit 4 ). In constructing this framework, we assess the following for each industry group: (1) the Morgan Stanley AI Index Score—represents the percent of US occupations that could be augmented by AI; (2) Adopter Concentration—we map input from our analysts in terms of AI adoption at the company level onto the GICS industry groups; (3) Workforce Efficiency—groups with low revenue per employee CAGRs and high dispersion are seen as having more significant potential efficiency gains as these cohorts may have more upside opportunity from a productivity standpoint if they can successfully adopt and integrate AI into their operating models (i.e., the laggards may have an opportunity to partially close the gap on the leaders from an efficiency perspective, driving equal-weighted productivity across the industry group higher); and (4) Margin Expectations—we look at long term profit margin expectations (out to 2025) to assess where potential efficiency gains may be particularly underestimated. We find that Software & Services, Consumer Services, Health Care Equipment & Services, Financial Services, and Media & Entertainment possess a more significant potential opportunity with respect to AI-driven efficiency gains. Software's strong potential efficiency gains score supports our relative preference for the cohort within the broader quality growth universe, which we remain constructive on. Our analysts see three potential phases of generative AI-driven productivity: (1) generic language AI tools (i.e., information retrieval apps), (2) custom models with access to company data (i.e., proprietary Q&A and summarization apps), and (3) custom models that take action on command and become agents/assistants (i.e., process automation apps). The progression of these phases can be characterized by a shift from task augmentation, such as a basic writing assistant, to process automation, such as an agent that can perform work processes on behalf of employees. They see us as currently being in Phase 1 ( Exhibit 5-7 ) as companies adopt generic off-the-shelf AI tools such as ChatGPT, Gemini/ Bard, and Github Co-Pilot. Investment in more proprietary tools/models is expected to ramp throughout '24 and into '25 (i.e., Microsoft Co-Pilot, Customized Llama models, etc.), and they expect a gradual slope of adoption due to the inherent frictions (i.e., ROI, Data/Skill Sets, Legal/ Regulatory) and technology hurdles (i.e., hallucinations, lack of a standardized tooling layer, etc.). As a result, our analysts believe we're not likely to see broad based productivity gains until 2025 and beyond.

Importantly, the path forward is unlikely to be linear nor will the productivity gains be consistent across sectors/industries as evidenced by the dispersion across industries shown in Exhibit 4 . Our analysts already see anecdotal and empirical evidence that indicates varying degrees of productivity gains across use cases and industries during this Phase 1. There are strong fundamental reasons why coding productivity gains tend to be larger and more consistent today compared with more general knowledge based work flows (more on this below). As a result, our analysts believe there is a higher likelihood that we see broad based efficiency/productivity gains within Software/Internet first, before the broader economy and non-tech industries outlined in Exhibit 4 . See Phases and Examples of AI-Driven Productivity for detail on how AI has the potential to drive operational efficiency and productivity across segments of the market/economy.

On the macro front, our economists believe that the diffusion of AI may eventually sustain the recent increases in the level of productivity, and potentially the growth of productivity in the economy. Between the goods and services sectors, the growth in productivity in the services sector may sustain the growth in aggregate productivity. Traditionally, the services sector has been characterized by lower average productivity than the goods sector. However, in the recent data, the productivity in goods appears to already be flattening, while our economists think the productivity in services can sustain its upward trend. In fact, our work in today's note suggests that the more significant potential opportunities from AI are likely to be in the services sectors ( Exhibit 4 ). On the employment front, labor market disruptions will likely require reskilling among the workforce. In terms of central bank policy paths, AI-led increases in productivity could also lead to lower inflation in the near-term, thereby allowing the central bank to set a lower policy rate. In the long-run, however, increases in r\* would likely push interest rates higher.

While AI-driven efficiency gains are likely to evolve over a multiyear time horizon, we don't believe it's too early to start thinking about potential single stock beneficiaries from this theme. On this score, we asked our equity analysts to consider Overweight and Equal-weight companies under coverage that they see incorporating AI to drive operational efficiency and productivity gains over the next 2 years ( Exhibit 9 ). Where possible/applicable, these companies have pricing power and can maintain it on a relative basis even as marginal costs theoretically come down as AI is successfully implemented. ## Industry Group Takeaways

## Software & Services

Keith Weiss sees a \$150bn GenAI enterprise software revenue opportunity in three years (see here), and sees the potential for GenAI to improve Software industry margins as the innovations are utilized internally (likely earlier than most companies) to improve the effectiveness and efficiency of software development (the machine starts to build the machine), go-to-market initiatives, and customer service (Software: 2024 Outlook – In GenAI We Trust (21 Dec 2023)). Across the US software industry, R&D makes up roughly 25% of revenue on average, which underscores the opportunity to drive efficiency gains as AI-coding assistants are integrated into engineering workflows. As discussed in Phases and Examples of AI-Driven Productivity , productivity gains from the adoption of AI-assisted coding tools are in the 20-50% range, and see promising early gains in go-to-market and customer support efficiency. Taken together, Software (and Internet) companies are well positioned to be early beneficiaries of AI-driven productivity gains, in our view.

IT Services companies are also poised to benefit from GenAI productivity gains but likely in a less significant way than Software. James Faucette and Adam Wood expect these benefits to be relatively small and limited to early adopters as: (1) productivity savings are likely shared with customers, and (2) IT Services vendors strategically invest to build AI capability (see here). In a recent Harvard Business School study of \~750 BCG consultants, they found that consultants who used AI completed \~12% more tasks on average and at a rate that was 25% faster. The output quality was also 40% higher compared to the control group. Beyond the potential for general productivity gains in IT Services, there are opportunities to drive efficiency in back office functions and quality assurance (i.e., code evaluations/testing). Should clients demand strategic guidance around Gen AI deployment, the team believes IT Services providers could potentially charge a premium for these services given the bespoke nature of these solutions for each respective client.

## Consumer Services

In the restaurant sector broadly, Brian Harbour believes that AI could help drive efficiency in several ways: (1) automated order taking, reducing the need for a human taking orders; over time, this could be faster and more accurate; (2) better personalization of marketing and digital experiences—more of a sales driver than cost savings, but return on marketing dollars or customer data spend could be better; (3) improved demand planning, enabled by AI-driven analytics; (4) improved labor and inventory planning with AI-driven software, and pairing with demand planning, which could reduce waste (food and labor hours) and reduce operational outages or disruptions; and (5) enablement of automated equipment in stores—more restaurants are looking at automation, which does not require AI, but if paired with AI for demand/labor/inventory planning, it could similarly reduce waste, labor hours required, maintenance downtime, and could improve speed of service.

As Stephen Grambling notes, online sports betting and internet gambling are ripe for AI efficiency gains given over 50 billion odds are collected, analyzed, changed and transacted upon every year. Moreover, acquiring and engaging with millions of betting customers across the globe continues to lean on substantial marketing investments that could be streamlined with AI. He estimates long-term marketing and promotions will amount to \~30% of gross gaming revenue and \~\$9bn in the US alone, suggesting significant savings from even small improvements in operating / marketing efficiencies.

In eCommerce, Brian Nowak sees AI leading to improved conversion and personalization, improved inventory management, back-office/ customer support savings, and logistics improvements (shipping and in-warehouse efficiencies through robotics) (see here). In Shared Economy & Online Travel, he expects AI will lead to improved conversion and fleet utilization, and ultimately more durable multi-year growth and profitability. Within Online Travel, there is a risk that AI could lead to a higher share of traffic coming from GOOGL (as GOOGL improves the search experience and collapses the funnel) which could lead to lower overall profitability for the OTAs and ABNB even through faster growth and potential internal productivity driven by the adoption of AI tools.

## Industry Framework Methodology

## AI Index Score

Our colleagues in technology and quantitative equity research have developed a proprietary AI Impact Index, which represents the percent of US occupations that could be augmented by AI. In constructing this index, our colleagues leveraged internal Morgan Stanley research crowd sourcing to define levels to which discrete work activities could be augmented or automated by Generative AI functionality today and in 3 years. They then applied this work activity augmentation ranking to the US government's database of skill sets needed across jobs, resulting in a job impact rate. That rate is then aggregated up to the NAICS industry level. For the purposes of our framework above, we then map the NAICS industry rates onto the GICS level 2 industry groups for the S&P 1500. GICS industry groups are then ranked based on their estimated labor impact rate. Groups in the top tercile (i.e., relatively high percentage of labor that could be augmented by Generative AI) are denoted as having more significant potential efficiency gains in the heat map above. Groups in the bottom tercile are denoted as having less significant potential efficiency gains. Groups in the middle tercile have a neutral reading.

## Adopter Concentration

As a part of Mapping AI's Diffusion led by our thematic research team, Morgan Stanley's equity analysts assessed each of their stocks under coverage in following context: (1) the company's role in, or impact from, AI; and (2) the speed of expected diffusion of disruption from AI to the investment debate on the stock. For the purposes of this report, given the theme of operational efficiency gains, we focus on the list of US adopters (i.e., option B in Exhibit 14 ). Each stock is then given a scaled score based on the importance of AI to the stock's debate. We then aggregate those scores up to the GICS level 2 industry group level and normalize by the number of companies in each industry group. GICS industry groups are then ranked based on their adopter concentration. Groups in the top tercile (i.e., relatively high adopter concentration) are denoted as having more significant potential efficiency gains in the heat map above. Groups in the bottom tercile are denoted as having less significant potential efficiency gains. Groups in the middle tercile have a neutral reading.

## Workforce Efficiency

This criteria looks at (1) output per employee growth and (2) dispersion of output per employee growth among companies in a given industry group. For the former, we assess the compound annual growth rate of revenue per employee from 2010 to 2023 for the median company within each industry group. For the latter, we compute the standard deviation of revenue per employee CAGRs across companies in each industry group. Industry groups with low revenue per employee CAGRs and high dispersion are seen as having more significant potential efficiency gains. The rationale is that these cohorts may have more upside opportunity from a productivity standpoint if they can successfully adopt and integrate AI into their operating models. Further, this potential upside in productivity may be underappreciated given these cohorts' relatively low revenue per employee CAGR at the median company level from 2010-2023. Incorporating dispersion then allows us to assess where there is clear bifurcation between highly productive and less highly productive companies within industries. If an industry group has a low median revenue per employee CAGR, but high dispersion, there are companies within the industry that are executing more effectively on productivity and operational efficiency. AI adoption could then lead to a broader productivity catch up for the median company across the cohort where the industry laggards have the opportunity to partially catch up to the industry leaders.

## Margin Expectations

For this variable, we look at longer term net profit margin expectations out to 2025 (i.e., FY2) for companies in the S&P 1500. We then compare these expectations to the historical median FY2 expectation as of the same point in prior years. Finally, we aggregate this comparison up from the company level to the industry group level and compare industries on this basis. Groups in the top tercile (i.e., relatively high margin expectations) are denoted as having less significant potential efficiency gains in the heat map as estimates are already elevated. Groups in the bottom tercile are denoted as having more significant potential efficiency gains. Groups in the middle tercile have a neutral reading.

## Efficiency Gains for the Overall Market

We see AI-driven productivity adding an additional 30 bps to 2025 net margin for the S&P 500 (13.0% net margin in the base case). This is based on our analysis on historical periods where tech investment ramped and led to an increase in productivity which then flowed through to profit margins. Change in profit margins tends to lag the rate of change in tech driven productivity growth by one year. So if 2024 marks a broader AI investment ramp, the margin impact should be more visible in 2025 (and beyond that). This fits with our general view that 2025 will represent a cyclical inflection that is aided by secular tailwinds. With respect to our margin assumptions and the impact of tech-driven productivity growth, we believe risk is skewed to the upside/our bull case—50 bps of added impact; 13.6% net margin. In terms of industry drivers of productivity gains, our new framework highlights that services-oriented pockets of the market possess a more significant opportunity with respect to AI-driven efficiency gains; as mentioned previously, these groups include Software & Services, Consumer Services, Health Care Equipment & Services, Financial Services, and Media & Entertainment. Just these cohorts alone represent over 30% of expected 2025 net income for the S&P 500, which speaks to the potential margin opportunity.

# Phases and Examples of AI-Driven Productivity

Brian Nowak, Kieran Kenny

We See Three Potential Phases of Generative AI-Driven Productivity: (1) generic language AI tools (i.e., information retrieval apps), (2) custom models with access to company data (i.e., proprietary Q&A and summarization apps), and (3) custom models that take action on command and become agents/assistants (i.e., process automation apps). The progression of these phases can be characterized by a shift from task augmentation, such as a basic writing assistant, to process automation, such as an agent that can perform work processes on behalf of employees. We're currently in Phase 1 as companies adopt generic off-the-shelf AI tools such as ChatGPT, Gemini/ Bard, and Github Co-Pilot. Investment in more proprietary tools/ models is expected to ramp throughout '24 and into '25 (i.e. Microsoft Co-Pilot, customized Llama models, etc.), and we expect a gradual slope of adoption due to the inherent frictions (i.e., ROI, Data/Skill Sets, Legal/Regulatory) and technology hurdles (i.e., hallucinations, lack of a standardized tooling layer, etc.). As a result, we're not likely to see broad based productivity gains until 2025 at the earliest.

AI-Assisted Coding: Strong Fit for LLM-based Tools and 20-50% Productivity Gains... Coding assistant tools are showing the largest and most consistent productivity gains according to anecdotes from various leading tech companies. Multiple attributes of coding workstreams make it a strong fit for LLM-driven efficiency improvements. First, coding occurs in a text interface that's linear. Because the interface is linear by nature (code is written line by line), it's natural for LLMs to make next line predictions. Second, most of the knowledge is publicly available online and included in LLM training data. Third, coding languages are arguably more precise 'languages' than written/spoken languages… and thus LLMs for coding are more accurate (less hallucinations). Lastly, code can be tested immediately, making it easy for the programer to test the LLM's output. All of these attributes make LLMs a great fit for augmenting coding workflows. Conversely, general knowledge work is not linear, and typically requires interacting with various interfaces/apps when completing a task. For example, an LLM could help an employee write an email response, but the employee still needs to read the original email to understand the context, review the AI's response, and many times, add context from another interface/app such as a web browser or productivity app (excel, powerpoint, etc.). The complexity and non-linear nature of general purpose knowledge workstreams make the productivity gains more elusive relative to more linear programming workflows.

# AI's Potential Impact on Productivity Growth: An Economic Perspective

Seth Carpenter, Arunima Sinha

The diffusion of AI is expected to be faster than other tech cycles, and may sustain the recent increases in the level of productivity in the US. Industry structures have been changing in the US and market concentration across industries has increased, specifically in services. This has been found to be correlated with higher productivity levels in these industries. On the labor market front, AI-related market disruptions will likely require extensive re-skilling among the workforce. AI-led increases in productivity could also lead to lower inflation in the near-term, thereby allowing the central bank to set a lower policy rate. Though the effect on rates would be temporary because, in the long-run, structural shifts in productivity eventually lead to higher trend interest rates.

## AI’s Effect On The Macro Economy

The effect of AI on the macro economy is analyzed through its effects on productivity. Tracing these effects is complicated by the intricacies involved in the measurement of productivity. As productivity is a measure of output per worker-hour, in measuring productivity effects of ${ \mathsf { A l } } ,$ it will be difficult in real time to ascertain if gains are because of AI or because of a shortage of labor driving enhancements, or both. We believe that AI will support and enhance productivity, but the real time measurement will only be discerned ex-post.

Exhibit 26: The level of aggregate productivity has recently recovered
![](images/657020d43be1fcf03c6faae77d3af41de0d3f8383940a287aaab3a1af3251881.jpg)
Source: BEA, BLS, Morgan Stanley Research

Productivity in the United States grew rapidly in the mid-1990s, but slowed down dramatically between 2005-2020. The adoption of new information and communications technologies (ICT) in the 1990s led to the productivity boost. Research indicates that during the dot com era, ICT-based productivity growth revival was on average 3.3% (one-quarter annualized growth) between 1996-2004, but then productivity fell to 1.1% in 2010-2019.

Recently, productivity levels and growth rates have increased, and AI diffusion may sustain these effects. By the output per hour measure, productivity surged in the 2020Q2 to 2021Q4 as businesses cut back on their payrolls. As hiring picked up in 2022, measured productivity fell. In 2023, as the labor market remained tight, and labor costs increased, it created the incentives for businesses to protect their margins by economizing on labor. And so over the past year, we have seen productivity level rise, and it remains above the pre-covid trend. We have also seen an increase in the growth rate of productivity over the past two years, although the growth rate is still only approaching the pre-covid growth rate.

Between the goods and services sectors, the growth in productivity in the services sector may sustain the growth in aggregate productivity. Traditionally, the services sector has been characterized by lower productivity growth than the goods sector. However, in the recent data, while the productivity increases in goods appear to already be flattening, the productivity growth in services still appears to be on an upward trend. And in fact, as our analysts note above, the more significant potential opportunities from AI will likely initially be in the services sector.

Economic conditions and tight labor markets play an important role in determining productivity. We have previously noted that cyclicality in technology adoption can be driven by changes in aggregate demand, as well as the extent to which firms face labor shortages. Increases in labor costs can push firms to increase productivity and thereby protect their margins. The improvement on the services side of the economy follows our expectations as AI will support the services sector. Also, given the continuing tightness in the labor markets in the services sector, the firms would have greater impetus to adopt productivity-enhancing technologies.

The effects of AI on the labor market are multi-dimensional. Our previous research notes that the effects of AI on labor demand will likely work through three channels: the productivity effect, displacement effect, and the reinstatement effect. Among these, the displacement effect would lower labor demand while the other effects would increase labor demand. The displacement effect also interacts with the economic cycle: low unemployment means displacement will not be permanent as people will get re-hired. The same tight labor market and high wages mean that retraining will be more cost effective, all else equal, compared to other parts of the cycle as hiring people with the specific skills will be costly. Our analysts also think that the level of labor market disruptions related to AI would lead to demands for re-skilling among the labor force. They also see a shift in the labor market where there would be an increase in the demand for skills, rather than occupations/credentials.

From the macroeconomic perspective, investment spending on IT will be key, and the diffusion of GenAI may be faster than other technologies. IT spending will also drive the phases of technology adoption discussed above. Our recent CIO survey suggests that IT spending will increase over 2024, and that AI/ML remains a priority for CIOs this year. In outlining the diffusion of AI across tech and other sectors, our analysts have used adoption curves to estimate that AI is on track to reach the speed of diffusion in one year that it took the internet seven years to achieve.

The recent increase in inflation also makes it important to distinguish between nominal and real measures of productivity. While the measures of productivity in terms of output per hour are usually expressed in real terms, other measures of productivity, such as revenue per employee may be expressed in nominal values. As price increases dissipate in the current inflationary cycle, it will be important to distinguish whether the sources of the change in productivity measured using these nominal measures is due to price normalization or changes in the quantity of output. The current disinflationary path in the US makes it more challenging to parse out the cyclical components of real and nominal effects of the pricing changes. Structurally, increases in productivity would have a disinflationary effect in the medium term.

## Industry Structure and Productivity

The increase in market concentration has been associated with the rise of “superstar” firms in the United States. A growing line of research has highlighted the increase in the concentration of firms over the past 40 years. Market concentration, defined as the share of the top four firms within an industry, has increased over time: in the average industry, market share increased nearly 10 percentage points from 1977 to 2012, reaching nearly 40% by 2012<sup>1</sup> . Using the data on the largest 500 firms from S&P’s Compustat database, research finds that between 1985 and 2015, the combined sales of the top 25 firms almost doubled (increasing from \$1.88 trillion in sales in 1985 to \$3.74 trillion for a new set of top 25 firms)<sup>2</sup> . The average Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) has increased across industries over this period, with the largest increases in services, retail trade, utilities and transportation and finance.

The evolution of industry structure to oligopolies is determined by several factors. The increase in the superstar firms which dominate the market has been found to be a combination of factors: tough product market competition, strong network effects for companies in the internet space and declines in the (quality-adjusted) prices of information technology and intangible capital, such as software, which could give large firms an advantage if there are large fixed costs to adoption.

Increase of market concentration is found to be correlated with a rise in productivity, and the larger firms also have high markups. Academic research has found that increases in market share are correlated with increases in productivity and real output. Furthermore, industries with the most real productivity growth are those with the largest increases in industry concentration. It is worthwhile to note that these correlations do not imply causality: the relation between productivity and the rise of market concentration and market power is endogenous. The economic environment may be advantageous to the most productive firms in an industry, leading to an increase in their product market concentration. Alternatively, as the firms increase their market share, barriers to entry may be created for smaller, new firms due to high fixed costs. And so the incumbents continue to invest in more productive technologies.

Industries that are characterized by oligopolies have large pricing power, and this could be further amplified by productivity gains from AI. Markups are a common measure of market power, and measure whether firms can price their products above marginal costs.

Since 1980, not only have aggregate markups increased, the distribution has changed: a few large firms have high markups, while the majority of the firms have not observed an increase in markups and have lost market share. Further increases in productivity among the larger firms may lead to even higher markups for them by lowering marginal costs further. On the other hand, if markups remain stable, then declines in costs will also lead to lower prices, contributing to the disinflationary impulse of productivity increases.

## Productivity, Inflation And Monetary Policy

The effect of an increase in productivity on inflation must distinguish between whether the productivity change is a transitory or a permanent change. As our analysts note in this report, the different phases of technology adoption will only occur gradually. And so, while some sectors may see an immediate one-time jump in productivity, the gains will diffuse over time across the economy.

A one-time increase in the level of productivity that eventually dissipates would lead to lower inflation and larger output being produced relative to the potential level. The largest effects would be at beginning of the productivity increase. If we approximate the central bank’s reaction function by the Taylor-rule, the productivity gain, in the short term, would lead to expansionary monetary policy and a decrease in the policy rate. As the productivity jump dissipates, both the output gap and inflation would return to their potential and target values respectively, leading in the medium term to increases in the policy rate.

The change in rate of trend productivity growth is found to alter the inflation rate in the opposite direction. If productivity growth stabilizes at a new level, as it did in 1995-2005, the effect on inflation will also dissipate. A Brookings study suggests that the 1995-2005 revival of productivity growth held down inflation on average by 1.2 percentage points over this period. The effects of AI on productivity, the path of inflation, output and monetary policy will depend on whether these constitute a one-time, short-term changes in the productivity level that eventually dissipates, or whether the trend growth of productivity will be altered. The latter would entail a much longer runway for the disinflationary path over the medium term.

Over the longer run horizon, the effects of productivity on r\* must also be considered. As we have previously noted, an AI-led increased in productivity may eventually lead to increases in the neutral interest rate r\*. Then the central bank would eventually have to push the policy rate to this higher neutral rate, leading to an increase in interest rates across the economy. However, as the effects of productivity on r\* will occur over time, there may be an extended period where the central bank undershoots, and the policy rate is below the neutral rate, affecting investment decisions across the economy.
