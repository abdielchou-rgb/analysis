# 2024 US Election Watch

Too early to make assumptions on the outcome of the presidential elections

![](images/9b88415056df6c65904dcbc995378cf615f9a4ba5a6a1a3c4703a58153842b03.jpg)

We hosted the first webinar in our 2024 US Election Watch series on February 8, featuring two seasoned independent policy and political experts with deep experience analyzing Democratic and Republican priorities and agenda. Note that our meetings are conducted under Chatham House rules and that some of the views in this note represent the perspectives of the speakers and are not necessarily the opinions of J.P Morgan Research analysts.

Click here for a replay of the call and here for our accompanying PowerPoint presentation.

Despite betting markets and international views predicting a Trump victory (Figure 1), both of our guest speakers see the upcoming election as Biden’s to lose and share a base case of a narrow Biden presidential victory, although conviction is not that high nine months before an election. They also expect a split Congress with the Democrats taking the House and the Republicans taking the Senate as the most likely outcome. For additional background on the US elections and geopolitical implications, see our report on What to watch in the 2024 Election Superbowl, Joyce Chang et al., 10 Jan.

We highlight below the top 10 takeaways from the webinar, highlighting what to expect out of Congress this year along with likely drivers that will affect the outcome of the elections and potential market implications.

## 1. Low expectations for advancing partisan legislation in an election year.

Expectations are low for advancing partisan legislation due to the very narrow majorities in both the House of Representatives and Senate. 2023 was the least productive year for Congress in decades with only 34 bills passed despite Congress holding a total of 724 votes, as debt ceiling negotiations, removal of the previous Speaker of the House and FY24 appropriation bills dominated the agenda. Congress could accomplish even less this year as the current makeup of the House only allows Republicans to lose three votes and that is only if the entire caucus is present to vote. Speaker of the House Mike Johnson has been forced to work across the aisle to pass significant legislation. If a Democrat prevails in the special election to fill George Santos<sup>’</sup> NY House seat, it will further reduce the slim Republican House majority. The failure of two House votes to impeach Secretary of Homeland Security Alejandro Mayorkas and to pass an Israel aid package demonstrates how difficult it is to achieve consensus in Congress this year. And it likely will become even more difficult in the second half of the year as Congress returns to their home districts to campaign before elections. So far this year, Congress has been consumed with averting a government shutdown in which they introduced a third continuing resolution (CR) to extend government funding deadlines to March 1 and 8. Speakers expect Congress to approve full year funding for spending bills by the March deadline which will avoid an automatic 1% budget cut to government agencies. The Biden administration has turned its focus to regulatory actions that can be executed without Congressional approval. The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) is expected to advance antitrust litigation against Amazon, Google and Meta, but Congressional action is likely to fall short of votes for major action as FTC Chair Lina Khan remains a high profile target for Congressional Republicans.

## 2. Legal challenges unlikely to disqualify Trump and only an extraordinary health incident would derail a Biden/Trump rematch for the presidential race.

Barring the possibility of a health incident given the age of both candidates, the presidential race looks to be a Biden/Trump rematch. The many legal cases against Trump, which include 91 criminal counts and four upcoming trials, will likely prove to be non-disqualifying. Trump is currently working on delaying two of the four trials beyond the election date, but the Georgia election interference and Washington D.C. election obstruction cases will likely occur before the election. Even if Trump is convicted in either case, he still would be able to run for president but be unable to vote in his home state of Florida, which does not allow convicted felons to vote. Looking at precedents for Trump<sup>’</sup>s candidacy, Socialist Eugene Debs won 3% of the vote in the 1920 presidential election while serving a prison sentence for sedition. There has only been one instance where a president served four years, lost the next election, and then won four years later and that was Grover Cleveland in the late 1880s-90s.

Speakers dismissed the possibility of either Michelle Obama or Kamala Harris becoming the Democratic nominee. Primaries have already begun, and Michelle Obama has been clear that she has no interest in running for president. There is also little possibility of her being drafted at the Democratic convention as Kamala Harris would be favored if Biden were unable to run. Biden is committed to run in this presidential election because every poll over the last 12 months has indicated that he is the only Democrat who could defeat Trump. Polls show that if Kamala Harris were to run today, she is projected to lose by 12%-pts. Beyond the 2024 election, a speaker believes there is a 65% chance that Harris would finish Biden<sup>’</sup>s term if he were reelected.

## 3. High voter turnout anticipated with the undecided vote estimated at 10% and the anti-Trump wing of the Republican Party at 20%.

Speakers expect record turnout from women and Democrats and less cohesion from Republican voters. The last three elections have seen record voter turnout, but the 2020 presidential election saw the highest since the 1900

election with 155mn people turning out to vote, or 66.8% of citizens 18 years and older voting, according to data from the 2020 Current Population Survey Voting and Registration Supplement. Since both Biden and Trump are known candidates, speakers estimate that as little as 10% of voters remain undecided while there is an increasing share of Americans that hold negative opinions of both parties.

Speakers estimate that as much as 20% of the Republican party is anti-Trump and that this wing of the party may not vote at all. Trump<sup>’</sup>s candidacy could result in more Republicans staying home and more Democrats turning out to vote. Speakers suggested that the top contenders for Trump<sup>’</sup>s Vice Presidential candidate include Ben Carson (former Secretary of Housing and Urban Development), Kari Lake (Senate candidate for Arizona), Lee Zeldin (House of Representatives for NY) and Elise Stefanik (House of Representatives for NY). Speakers anticipate Trump is unlikely to pick a VP candidate who has ambitions to be the face of the Republican Party and is “looking for someone with COO potential rather than CEO potential.<sup>”</sup>

## 4. Third-party candidates seen as hurting Trump more than Biden.

Speakers argued that Robert F. Kennedy Jr. is the only impactful third-party candidate, and he is likely to draw votes away from Trump rather than Biden. Polls show that willingness to vote for an independent, thirdparty candidate is as high as 17%, coinciding with the low approval and favorability numbers for Biden and Trump. Gallup polling last month found that a record 41% of Americans see themselves as politically independent, outnumbering Republican and Democratic identifiers (28% apiece). The RealClearPolitics average of polls testing Biden and Trump against announced independent candidates Robert F. Kennedy Jr. and Cornel West and likely Green Party candidate Jill Stein shows 17.2% of voters willing to vote for third-party candidates (Figure 6).

5. Swing states currently favor Trump, but a felony conviction could cost him half of his support from swing state voters.

Speakers argued that five swing states will determine the election outcome—Arizona, Georgia, Michigan, Pennsylvania and Wisconsin. National-level polling is often misleading as the election outcome is determined by the Electoral College vote, not the popular vote. Trump won all five swing states to defeat Hillary Clinton in 2016, while Biden narrowly won all five in the 2020 election. Trump is currently leading in polls in all seven swing states. Trump won six of the seven in 2016 (lost in Nevada) while Biden won six of the seven in 2020 (lost in North Carolina).

## 6. The economy, immigration and abortion are the main issues that are dividing voters.

The number one issue driving voters across both parties remains the economy and partisanship is shaping economic perceptions. Figure 11 shows that the gap between partisan perceptions has never been wider. The disconnect between solid fundamentals vs. poor consumer sentiment has been a major focal point for the Biden administration as there is concern that the sentiment is reflecting what is heard from social media apps such as TikTok. The Biden administration is receiving political blame for the higher cost of living as households see elevated price levels as indicative of an inflation problem, even if there is substantial disinflation. Meanwhile, small businesses are concerned about margin compression due to still-elevated wage inflation. US real disposable income is now below the 2015-2019 trend, and housing affordability remains at its worst level in 40 years (see Washington Policy Perspectives: Don<sup>’</sup>t Fight the Fed, Joyce Chang et al., 16 Jan).

The second most important issue for Republican voters is the national security threat from the southern border and immigration while access to abortion ranks as the second most important issue for Democratic voters. Immigration to the United States is now back to pre-pandemic levels. In 2023, there was a record rise in people displaced globally while the number of migrants arriving at the US-Mexico border reached an unprecedented 2.5mn. Trump and Republican candidates have proposed expanding the immigration detention system, strengthening border patrol, greater use of local law enforcement and military at the border and large-scale deportation.

Abortion access has become an important election issue as nearly 22mn women of reproductive age, or almost 1 in 3 women, live in states where abortion is unavailable or severely restricted.<sup>2</sup> Women have registered and voted at higher rates than men in every presidential election since 1980. As of 2022, there were 7.4mn more women than the number of men registered to vote, according to the Center for American Women and Politics (see 2024 US election kickoff: 10 themes to watch while monitoring US elections, Joyce Chang and Amy Ho, 17 Jan). Since the Supreme Court decision to overturn the constitutional right to abortion in June 2022, states have become arbiters of abortion access. Democrats have won every special congressional election held to uphold reproductive freedom, even in Alaska, which was a rare Democratic win.

## 7. Trump 2.0 likely to be led by loyalists with greater consequences for US foreign policy.

Speakers argue that domestic aspects of Trump 1.0 policies would prevail in the event of a Trump 2.0 administration which entails advancing pro-market and pro-entrepreneurship initiatives, but that there likely would be greater consequences for foreign policy. Speakers characterized the Heritage Foundation<sup>’</sup>s Project 2025 as a “conservative wish list<sup>”</sup> rather than a template for Trump 2.0. However, Trump would likely turn to loyalists first for staffing, and he continues to blame pitfalls during his tenure and reelection campaign on the lack of loyalty. Among the names mentioned as possibilities for Treasury Secretary include Gary Cohn, Vivek Ramaswamy and Robert Lighthizer. Former White House strategist, Steve Bannon, could serve as a special advisor to Trump although his conviction on contempt charges would make security clearance difficult.

Trump’s rhetoric has raised alarm bells, but speakers felt that the most extreme proposals were unlikely to materialize given the high probability of a split Congress. The biggest changes under Trump 2.0 could be initiatives related to the energy sector and measures to increase US exports of oil and gas. Speakers argued that a roll back of the spending provided by the Inflation Reduction Act (IRA), CHIPS and Science Act and Bilateral Infrastructure Law (BIL) is unlikely as incoming administrations tend to retain the increased spending measures they inherit. Moreover, Republican states have attracted the largest investments for large solar, wind and storage projects as a result of the IRA, estimated by the White House at \$337bn. Trump is likely to extend his Tax Cuts and Jobs Act which is set to sunset at the end of 2025. If a Red sweep were to play out, which is viewed as unlikely, he could propose cutting the corporate tax rate to 15% from 21% through the budget reconciliation process as it only requires a simple majority. Trump would also likely push for further bank deregulation. Speakers do not think that he would change the mandate of the Fed although he would appoint a new Fed chair.

The foreign policy consequences could be much more consequential although Trump’s wish to implement 10% universal tariffs and 60% tariffs on China were seen as unlikely. Such measures would be instantly inflationary and could lead to around a 1%-pt loss to US GDP according to a speaker. However, a meaningful increase in trade tensions is likely with the EU and China. Trump likely would focus on negotiating new trade agreements under a threat of broadly imposed tariffs.

The single biggest sector that could benefit if Trump is elected is the defense sector as he has indicated he would take actions to compel NATO countries to increase their defense spending up to 2% of GDP. Trump is described as “unconditionally supportive<sup>”</sup> of Israel as well as supportive of reinstituting the Muslim travel ban to include Gazans. Trump has also come out and said he would not continue to fund aid to Ukraine, even if it means that Russia would take all of the Donbas region. Sanctions on Russia<sup>’</sup>s financial services and aviation sectors could potentially be removed.

## 8. If Trump loses, the moderate wing of the Republican Party could make a comeback.

If Trump loses, it is likely that the Republican party moves back towards the center, particularly if Republicans take the Senate and Mitch McConnell becomes the Majority leader. More moderate Republicans such as Senator John Cornyn would likely play a more influential role in reviewing the priorities of the Republican party, with the traditional wing taking back the party. The MAGA wing also could become its own party and it<sup>’</sup>s possible that Trump could walk away from politics as a defeat in 2024 would represent his third loss of the popular vote. The Republican party could look to purge Trump with younger Republicans such as Liz Cheney and Nikki Haley who are gaining prominence.

## 9. Biden 2.0 would see some personnel shifts, but no big policy changes.

A Biden 2.0 outcome would maintain status quo policies and likely include the passage of a CHIPS 2.0 Act given large bipartisan support. Personnel could shift starting with Treasury Secretary Janet Yellen, who could retire, with the frontrunners to replace her mentioned as including current Commerce Secretary Gina Raimondo or Director of the National Economic Council Lael Brainard. Certain figures such as Transportation Secretary Pete Buttigieg could also depart as some turnover in the Cabinet is seen as normal. Under either a Trump or Biden administration, US exceptionalism could prevail over the next three to five years, driven by an economic boom fueled by the massive fiscal spending approved over the last two years, which when added together with private spending, totals up to \$1.5trn of planned investment spending. With respect to foreign policy, greater engagement with the Indo-Pacific region likely would be prioritized while support for Ukraine would continue.

## 10. Split Congress seen as most likely outcome with both the House and Senate to flip.

The speakers were in agreement with a base case that Biden is reelected with a split Congress but with both chambers flipping (i.e., House flips to Democrats and Senate flips to Republicans). In the 2024 Congressional elections, all 435 House seats and 33 Senate seats are up for election. At present, 50 members of Congress are not seeking reelection, including 7 Senators and 43 members of Congress.<sup>3</sup> Partisan term limits and state-level redistricting have led to a surge in House retirements announced since November, which appear to favor House Democrats as the House Republican majority has narrowed. Special elections are being held in California, New York and Ohio. Speakers argued that a split Congress with a Biden victory would be the best outcome for financial markets as there would be continuity of policy and the continued implementation of the CHIPS and Science Act, IRA and BIL, but a divided government means Democrats would not be able to raise taxes.

## Market implications

Inflation and the US presidential election will have the biggest impact on markets this year, according to traders surveyed in J.P. Morgan<sup>’</sup>s annual E-Trading Edit. Now in its eighth year, the latest survey was the biggest yet, gathering the views of more than 4,000 institutional traders on everything from commodities and currencies to cash equities and derivatives. Those factors are likely to cause a spike in volatility in 2024, which traders said will be their biggest daily challenge.

Our FX strategists highlight the growing drumbeat on tariffs ahead of US elections in their previous research, and they see downside risk for EUR with rising probabilities of an imposition strengthening USD with a beta of 50-60% (so a 10% tariff imposition on imports from a country is likely to weaken FX by 5-6%). While China is widely considered to be the recipient of tariffs or any trade conflict, some news reports have suggested that Europe could come into scope as well. EUR/USD has been undershooting fair value by 2-3%, partly coinciding with this intensified focus on tariffs (Figure 15). Even if trade measures are limited to China, they argue that the Euro won<sup>’</sup>t be immune. As China tariffs were imposed in 2018-19, growth outlooks in both China and the Eurozone were downgraded and the weakness in European growth that started before tariffs intensified in the aftermath (Figure 16 Figure 15Figure 16). The euro weakened in sympathy with CNY over that period (Figure 16Figure 17).
