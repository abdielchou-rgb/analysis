## Opportunistic disinflation, redux?

Global growth and inflation far exceeded our expectations last quarter and shook both market and central bank confidence that a significant interest rate decline is in the offing. Global GDP is tracking a 3.3%ar gain in 1Q24, a full %-point above its potential pace and our forecast at the start of the year. Although US growth downshifted, it remained a powerful demand engine generating rapid import and job gains. With Western Europe rising from its slumber and strong increases posted across Asia, 1Q24 global GDP growth outside the US and China rose at a 2.6%ar, its fastest gain in eight quarters. Core CPI inflation, meanwhile, accelerated to a 3.5%ar last quarter (ex. China and Türkiye). In contrast to the growth surprises, this acceleration was concentrated in the US and Euro area, which posted annualized gains of 4.4% and 3.2%, respectively, last quarter.

These outcomes reinforce our view that central banks will have limited space for easing this year. But we see 1Q24 momentum fading and anticipate global GDP and core CPI gains will moderate to a 2.5%ar and 3%ar, respectively, this quarter. While this week<sup>’</sup>s initial April activity releases, the global manufacturing PMI and US jobs, point to slowing, it is too early to confidently gauge current-quarter growth momentum.

Along with this anticipated downshift, the outlook depends on how broader financial conditions respond to last quarter<sup>’</sup>s surprises and interest rate repricing. Financial markets have been impressively resilient so far (Figure 1). To a large degree, this resilience is being encouraged by central bank guidance. The ECB is firming a commitment to a June easing despite a 1Q24 upside inflation surprise.

This week<sup>’</sup>s FOMC communications were also supportive. While acknowledging that the recent disappointing progress in bringing inflation down will likely delay the start of easing beyond midyear, Chair Powell expressed patient optimism. He maintained his view that the Fed<sup>’</sup>s policy stance is restrictive and that labor markets are moving toward better balance. He envisioned three likely paths ahead: a path where inflation remains persistent, and they “hold off on rate cuts;<sup>”</sup> a path where they regain confidence that inflation is coming down and they cut, and a path of unexpected weakening in the labor market that prompts cuts.

Powell<sup>’</sup>s resistance to discuss rate hikes hints at the opportunistic disinflation strategy applied in the 1980s. Under this approach, the Fed does not act in the face of moderately elevated inflation. Instead, it waits for external circumstances (favorable supply shocks and unforeseen recessions) to return inflation to target while standing by to aggressively resist inflation turning too hot. Market reaction to this message was understandably favorable. But Powell is unlikely to be speaking for a committee that may not be as patient.

Maintaining a balance between growth, inflation, and financial conditions will be challenging and we do not have conviction in the medium-term sustainability of the expansion. This week<sup>’</sup>s news showed a 1Q24 rebound in US unit labor cost gains and a decline in Euro area GDP deflator growth last quarter, which is consistent with downward pressure on profit margins. This in turn could sour business sentiment and amplify any slowing in growth. At the same time, easing financial conditions could over-stimulate activity and raise inflation above the opportunistic comfort zone.

## Cautiously upbeat on global industry

Amidst noisy activity readings we see encouraging signs that global industry is gradually recovering as final demand remains resilient and inventory drags are fading. This enthusiasm is tempered only a bit by this week<sup>’</sup>s disappointing manufacturing PMI report for April. A 0.4pt dip in the output PMI comes after three straight strong gains, leaving the level consistent with still-okay 1.6%ar growth in global factory output—just a touch below our 1H24 forecast of 2%ar growth. The hard activity data are still tracking a notable improvement through the first three months of the year (Figure 2). After ending 4Q23 on a very soft note, global retail sales spending has been choppy but has accelerated on net. Business equipment spending also looks to have accelerated this year, continuing a trend up after stalling around mid-2023.

The firming in demand has outpaced production growth, and this has ended the inventory correction that weighed on manufacturing around the turn of the year. On balance, the mix of final demand and inventories looks encouraging for further

gains in output in 2Q24. However, the April PMIs came with a more cautionary note than just the small dip in the output component. A large 2.6pt tumble in the investment goods PMI for last month slashes our global capexnowcast by 3.4%-pts to a now-soft 1.6%ar for this quarter. It is still early in the quarter, but the momentum loss would be a concern if sustained.

## EM Asian exports leading global industry

In contrast to the modest dip in the April global reading, the April PMI data were on balance positive for Asia, with the April new export orders PMI rising back to 2021 levels. This bodes well for trade, with exports already accelerating materially in recent months (Figure 3). The region<sup>’</sup>s tech production took a step down in 1Q but this seems more a breather from the booming gains in 2H23 than a break in the trend. Indeed, underlying final demand has remained solid even as non-tech output remained soft. Within the region, Japan<sup>’</sup>s goods sector looks to be emerging from a long slumber following the shocks of earthquake-related disruptions and auto plant shutdowns. Chip production is at decade highs, reflecting tailwinds from a regional tech cycle upturn and new domestic production facilities coming online.

Much of Mainland China<sup>’</sup>s 30%ar surge in 1Q24 exports reflects a growing market share in non-G3 markets, notably other emerging markets. While some of the trade with LatAm (especially Mexico) and ASEAN likely reflects trans-shipment flows, the rise in trade with other regions including Brazil, Russia, and the Middle East likely reflects strong final demand for Chinese goods. Follow-through in the rest of the Asia region has been mixed, with the weakness in Korea<sup>’</sup>s March IP spread across both tech and non-tech production. At the same time, Taiwan<sup>’</sup>s capex has rebounded after contracting in recent quarters, reflecting an improving export outlook.

## Shallow easing starts in Western Europe

Next week, the Riksbank should follow the SNB as the second DM central bank to enter easing mode. While other Western European central banks are on track to ease, the risks have shifted in the direction of a shallow adjustment through the end of this year.

Currency weakness is the primary constraint in Scandinavia. For Sweden, sluggish growth, a weak labor market, and a sharp fall in pricing indicators justify the 100bp easing we forecast this year. However, the slide in the SEK could reduce the size of this move. A weaker NOK and a high wage settlement prompted Norges Bank to shift forward guidance at this week<sup>’</sup>s meeting, and we have pushed our forecast for a first cut from September to December.

The Euro area is on track to ease in June but we are lowering our sights for 2H24 as the Euro area delivered upside surprises on growth and core inflation. In light of improved growth prospects and upside inflation risks, we have slowed our expectations for ECB cuts to 75bp.

The BoE is on track to ease next quarter but will likely show little urgency to cut rates at next week<sup>’</sup>s meeting. Near-term services inflation and pay growth are thus tracking slightly above the Bank<sup>’</sup>s forecast, and revisions to growth and the labor market are likely to reflect more resiliency. We thus think the aim is to leave the impression that a first cut is likely around August, with only some probability of a cut in June.

## Testing time for the BoJ

Pressures on the yen reached a boiling point after the BoJ<sup>’</sup>s meager defense last Friday. As USD/JPY breached the optically-important 160 threshold going into the long Golden Week holiday, it looks likely that Japan<sup>’</sup>s Ministry of Finance intervened several times. Such large actions are not sustainable without more fundamental support on rates, a fact that markets are now likely to keep testing. However, given sticky inflation and rising wage growth, we think that markets are underestimating the BoJ<sup>’</sup>s path through year-end. In our baseline, we continue to project two more hikes by December, bringing the policy rate to 50bp, and we look for the tightening cycle to extend gradually through 2025. Nevertheless, sustained currency pressure could telescope this trajectory.

## EM still on cautious easing path

EM central banks have turned more cautious but outside of Asia, where wider rate spreads to the US have pressured currencies, most remain on course for further easing. The USD is stronger since the start of the year but has not broken out of its recent range and global financial stress remains minimal. Indeed, EM portfolio inflows look to have rebounded after contracting last month (Figure 4).

In EM Asia, growth remains subpar and inflation is running within CB target ranges as evidenced by this week<sup>’</sup>s soft

April CPI reports in Indonesia and Korea. However, currency concerns have led us to push rate cuts back in Indonesia (from 3Q24), with risks of another near-term rate hike. With more ample FX buffers, the BoK appears less constrained, and we still see a cut in 4Q24. In CEE, central banks are still easing but some are losing conviction amid rebounding growth and sticky core inflation. The CNB this week cut another 50bp but with hawkish messaging, and we look for easing to slow to 25bp next month. Next week, we see Poland<sup>’</sup>s NBP on hold; our call for 50bp of cuts later this year crucially hinges on Fed and ECB easing. In Romania, domestic inflation pressures point to risks of delayed easing.

In LatAm, high real rates argue for further cuts, but FX volatility is keeping central banks cautious. Colombia<sup>’</sup>s BanRep stuck to a 50bp easing pace this week and we see it continuing at a steady clip. Brazil<sup>’</sup>s COPOM is likely to slow its easing pace to 25bp next week amid tighter global conditions, a 1Q growth recovery, and a lack of fiscal restraint. In Mexico, fiscal policy is loosening and the peso becoming more sensitive to external conditions. We thus expect a strategic pause next week that continues through the August meeting. Peru<sup>’</sup>s BCRP should stay put next week and should resume easing in June with another 25bp cut.

## Policy supports China as housing drags

A tightening in policy after China<sup>’</sup>s strong 1Q GDP bounce (to 7.4%ar) led us to mark down 2Q growth to a 4%ar, but weakness in March activity data kept risks skewed to the downside. After this week<sup>’</sup>s Politburo meeting, growth risks appear more balanced as policymakers look attentive to growth drags and show willingness to act if needed. On housing, the focus was on addressing the inventory overhang and boosting public rental housing investment. Despite the constructive steps, we maintain housing will be a drag for some time. We expect that, without a major change in housing policy, the housing market decline will continue in 2024, with key volume indicators falling another 10%, stabilizing only in 2025, with the macro drag persisting into 2026.
