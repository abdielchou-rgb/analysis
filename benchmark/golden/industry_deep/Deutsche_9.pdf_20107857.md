# Moving target

There is limited conviction over the rates outlook for Asia amid elevated uncertainty over US rates and oil price and fx volatility. If that wasn't enough, domestic politics (and fiscal stimulus push) added another layer of complexity in decision making.

After BI surprised the market with a rate hike, the CBC stood out as the leading candidate to do the same.

While both BSP and SBV remain reluctant, the latter had to tighten liquidity to support the dong. Even if current headwinds ease, we do not expect the SBV to cut, as we see inflation heading higher. Meanwhile, the timing of monetary easing by BI and BSP remain tied to that of the Fed. Although not as sensitive, we also see RBI waiting at least until October to ease.

Despite relative weakness in domestic demand, stronger headline growth renders the optics of monetary easing difficult to justify, without hard data confirming either a significant easing of growth momentum or disinflation, especially when arguing against a sizable fiscal stimulus. Given the timing of related data releases, the BoK may wait until August to ease. The BoT may also wait until August, due to the Senate election in June, while Singapore's general election also poses risks to our forecast of the MAS's easing in October.

Although the PBoC will resume its rate cuts earlier, the depth of its rate cuts will likely be rather shallow, as not to adversely affect the yuan.

## Growth beat expectation in Q1 but slowed in March

China's real GDP grew by 5.3% YoY in Q1 2024, beating our expectations of 5% and market consensus forecast of 4.8%, thanks to strong 6% growth in the industrial sector and a moderate 5% expansion in the services sector. Sequential growth improved to 1.6% QoQ in Q1, and Q4 2023 growth was also revised up to 1.2% QoQ from 1% previously.

However, the beat in headline GDP should be interpreted with caution. Deflation pressures remained: nominal GDP growth was only 4.2% YoY in Q1, implying a GDP deflator at -1.1% YoY, which has stayed negative for a fourth consecutive quarter and has dropped by another 0.1ppt from the previous quarter. March activity data also disappointed: industrial production and retail sales growth both missed market expectations by more than 1 percentage point. We estimate overall activity growth has decelerated in March, to 4.6% YoY from 5.4% YoY in the first 2 months.

The industrial sector underperformed in March. Industrial output grew by 4.5% yoy in March, moderating from 5.0% yoy ytd in Jan-Feb and declined by -0.1% mom on a seasonally adjusted basis. Property sector activity stayed at the bottom with little signs of a rebound. Real estate investment, property sales value and housing construction starts declined to 75%, 63% and 33% respectively compared to their levels in 2019.

Encouragingly, consumer spending recovery appears back on track. The labor market improved slightly in Q1, with the headline unemployment rate dropping by 0.1ppt to 5.2%. Migrant workers saw 7.7% yoy growth in wages, marking the highest improvement since 2022. Household spending rose by 8.3% yoy in Q1, continuing to outperform income growth for the fifth consecutive quarter. Accordingly, the household savings rate on a seasonally adjusted basis decreased to 31.1% from 31.7% in 2023, not far from the 30% rate in 2019.

Looking forward, we think sequential growth momentum will likely moderate in the next few quarters. QoQ growth will likely slow from 1.6% in Q1 to slightly above 1% in the next few quarters. A slowdown in industrial output growth is expected, from a 6% to 5-5.5% range, owing to less favorable base effects and downward pressures from prices and demand. Meanwhile, services sector growth will likely be sustained at around 5% or slightly above that. Under these assumptions, we raise our 2024 full year GDP growth forecast by half a percentage point to 5.2%, and see balanced risk around our updated forecast. Upward surprises may come from exports (if the global economy strengthens) and domestic demand (if government stimulus measures are more effective than expected), while downside risks could arise from a further deterioration in property investment, trade frictions, and geopolitical risk events.

## PBOC's trade-off

The PBOC has a tough decision to make on its policy rate. On the one hand, rate cuts remains justified by economic fundamentals and credit demand. The March readings suggest China's recovery is not yet on a strong footing. Credit growth has slowed further, which in turn has pushed down market rates across the board. At 2.5%, PBOC's 1y MLF policy rate is much higher than today's interbank borrowing rates (1y Shibor at 2.1%) and government bond yields (10y CGB at 2.25%). This interest rate overhang could negatively affect the effectiveness of PBOC's liquidity operations. To boost credit demand, PBOC needs to bring down real interest rate further, which is still too high today owing to dropping prices. On the other hand, PBOC is facing two key constraints that prevented a rate cut so far this year: the external constraint on preserving exchange rate stability, and the internal constraint on preserving bank profit margins. While the latter can be partially resolved by letting banks cut deposit rates further (which we think will likely happen in the coming months), the former may continue to bind, at least in the near term, owing to a strong US economy and the dollar.

We now expect a total of 20bps rate cut by the PBOC　 before end-2024, down from 45bps that we had previously expected. But the PBOC is tightly constrained in the near term by intensifying depreciation pressures on the CNY. We now expect 2 rate cuts of 10bps each in 2024; the first one will likely happen on or after June, depending on FX market developments.

## Hong Kong still struggles

Unlike PBOC, the HKMA has no choice but to maintain its base rate unchanged at 5.75% until the Fed easing cycle starts, which is now expected to happen only in December. That means HKD deposit and lending rates will likely stay high at restrictive levels throughout the year, adding to the headwinds facing Hong Kong's economy. Thus we have trimmed our 2024 Hong Kong GDP growth forecast by 0.5 ppt to 2.5%. A main drag to Hong Kong's economy lately came from its financial sector. The sector played a key countercyclical role during Hong Kong's economic downturn in 2019-20. But just as the broader Hong Kong economy started to recover, Hong Kong's financial sector activity shrank in the past 2 years, owing to a Fed rate hike cycle and a lost of confidence by investors on China's economy, both of which reduced the attractiveness of assets floated on Hong Kong's financial markets.

The financial sector downturn has broader negative spillover effects on the wider Hong Kong economy. For example, the number of visitors to Hong Kong has only recovered to 60% of 2018 levels in Q4 2023, and their spending in Hong Kong was only 50% of 2018 levels. In contrast, HK outbound spending has already fully recovered to pre-Covid levels. This asymmetric recovery is in part because Hong Kong is a hub for business travel; people may not visit and spend in Hong Kong as much as before until the number of IPOs and shareholder conferences in Hong Kong returns to previous levels.

The high interest rates and financial sector downturn is also bad news for Hong Kong's property sector. The government has decisively removed all the restrictions that previously prevented nonresidents from buying housing units in Hong Kong. However, the policy change has only resulted in a modest rebound in property transactions in the first month, while property prices have continued to fall.

Looking forward, a stronger Hong Kong economy will largely depend on two factors: the timing of the Fed's policy pivot, and investors regaining confidence on China. Neither factor is within the control of the HKSAR government. What the government can do today is to utilize its still-ample fiscal buffer to support incomes and living of Hong Kong residents, especially among the lower income groups. The government's 2024-25 budget now envisages a much more gradual reduction in deficits; it will maintain a 4.5% fiscal deficit in FY2024-25 and will not return to budget balance until FY2027-28.

## Headline GDP growth remains robust, but private consumption growth weak

While headline GDP growth remains robust, the internals show that private consumption momentum (close to 60% of GDP) remains weak. Private consumption grew 3.5%yoy and 2.4%yoy in Oct-Dec’23 and July-Sep’23 respectively, with CSO projecting the full year FY24 growth to be only 3.0%yoy for that segment. Private consumption had grown 7.1%yoy in FY23; so there has been a material slowdown in FY24, from FY23. One possible reason could be weakness in rural consumption, led by patchy monsoon last year. This year, with monsoon expected to be normal, rural consumption could improve, to some extent, but urban consumption momentum may ease, after doing the heavy lifting in the last two years. So, private consumption growth momentum will remain the key focus for monetary policy considerations in the quarters ahead.

Investment growth has been the key support for India’s overall GDP growth. As per CSO’s projection, investment growth is likely to improve to 10.2%yoy in FY24, from 8.1%yoy in FY23. While investment growth is mostly supported by government capex, private sector investment momentum has also picked up, and may become more broad-based post the general elections. If overall real GDP growth averages around 6.5%yoy in the next few years, supported primarily by investment, with consumption growth remaining below trend, this is likely to be non-inflationary from the demand side. India’s potential growth rate of 7.25% is unlikely to have been impacted by Covid-19, as we had feared initially, thanks to various supply-side reforms, and the actual growth outturn, adjusted for large base effects and discrepancies, seems to be tracking below the potential growth rate, which to a large extent explains the steady decline in core inflation during FY24, in our view.

Finally, we prefer to give more weight to real GVA trend rather than real GDP growth, due to statistical reasons and data collection issues. As per CSO’s projections, real GVA growth for FY24 is expected around 6.9%yoy, 50bps lower than the 7.6%yoy projection for real GDP growth. We think FY24 growth could get revised up further – both for GDP and GVA – which could impact the FY25 growth projections as well, but at this stage we are holding on to our FY25 real GDP growth projection of 6.5%yoy (vs. RBI’s projection of 7.0%yoy).

## March CPI inflation fell below 5%, aided by a fuel price cut, but monetary easing will have to wait till Oct-Dec'24

March CPI inflation came in at 4.85%yoy, broadly in line with our and Bloomberg consensus estimate of 4.90%. In Jan and Feb, CPI inflation was 5.1%yoy. The downtick in headline CPI inflation was on account of a cut in LPG (cooking gas) prices on 8 March (LPG prices were cut INR100/cylinder) and reduction in domestic pump prices of petrol and diesel on 15 March (petrol and diesel prices were cut by INR2/litre respectively) ahead of the general elections in April/May 2024. Indeed, fuel inflation and transport inflation (where the changes in petrol and diesel prices are recorded) eased -2.6%mom (-3.2%yoy) and -0.2%mom (1.5%yoy) respectively in March from February. Consistent with our forecast, food & beverages prices momentum was up +0.16%mom (7.7%yoy) in March vs. +0.11%mom (7.8%yoy) in Feb, while core inflation (CPI ex food and fuel) rose +0.21%mom (3.31%yoy) in March vs. +0.26%mom (3.37%yoy) in Feb. Jan-March'24 CPI inflation averaged 5.0%, in line with the RBI's forecast.

In FY25, we are forecasting core inflation to average about 3.4%, 90bps lower than the FY24 outturn of 4.3%.

CPI inflation likely to moderate to 4.6% in FY25, from 5.4% in FY24

## Monetary policy outlook - 10 key points

Post the April monetary policy, we have changed our monetary policy call:

i) We now expect the first 25bps rate cut in October and a total of 50bps of rate cut in FY25. In FY26, we expect another 25bps of rate cut. In calender year terms, we now expect only one 25bps rate cut in 2024 and 50bps of rate cut in 2025.

ii) It appears to us that the Fed rate easing cycle stands the risk of getting delayed and that bulk of the easing is likely to be pushed forward to 2025. We adjust our RBI easing cycle accordingly, with a delayed rate cut cycle and more easing likely in 2025 compared to 2024.

iii) The MPC may consider changing the stance to neutral in August, if inflation trajectory plays out in line with the RBI's forecast, and if the central bank is prepared to cut rates in October.

iv) We expect the RBI to continue with fine-tuning the money market liquidity (through VRRR and VRR), so as to keep the short-term rates between 6.50-6.75%.

v) Using RBI's forecast of growth and inflation for FY25, Taylor Rule implied repo rate works out to 5.63%, but this assumes a neutral real rate of 100bps. Assuming slightly higher real rates, the RBI can deliver a cumulative 75bps rate cut in this cycle, but this is predicated on a Fed rate easing cycle of 150bps or higher.

vi) RBI's long-term forecasts show that CPI inflation could fall to 4% by Jan-March'26. Assuming this will be achieved, then the repo rate could then come down to 5.75% by the first half of next year, as monetary policy is forward looking and works with considerable lags. In that scenario, real rates will still be 175bps positive on a forward looking basis, which is more than what is warranted, in our view (we think 100bps positive real rates are sufficient ).

vii) Analyzing the various monetary policy cycles in the last two decades, we note that the maximum time period that the RBI has paused between the end of a rate hike cycle and start of a rate cut cycle is 11 months (in two episodes). Given that the last rate hike was delivered in Feb'23, a potential rate cut cycle commencing from October'24 will be a 20-month gap, which will be the highest gestation period between the end of a rate hike cycle and start of a rate cut cycle.

viii) If the Fed rate easing magnitude turns out to be significantly lower, then the RBI is unlikely to ease rates more than 50bps, in our view.

ix) Also, if the RBI decides to work with a higher real rate assumption, then easing of repo rate may remain contained to 50bps.

x) If the RBI decides to cut rates, it is unlikely to be one rate cut of 25bps. In such a scenario, it is better, in our view, that the central bank does not cut rates at all. In our view, for rate cuts to be meaningful, the central bank should ease 50bps at least, if not 75bps in this cycle. While we are forecasting 75bps easing in our base case scenario, a conservative RBI may decide to limit the rate easing cycle to just 50bps.

## Fiscal consolidation likely to continue; global bond index inclusion structurally positive for fixed income markets

The central government set a fiscal deficit target of 5.1% of GDP for FY25, while revising down the FY24 estimate to 5.8% of GDP vs. budget estimate of 5.9% of GDP. This indicated a strong commitment on the government's part to stick with the medium-term goal of bringing centre’s fiscal deficit down to 4.5% of GDP by FY26. The revenue and expenditure assumptions are broadly realistic and we think the FY25 fiscal targets are achievable. JP Morgan Global Bond Index team including India in the GBI-EM Global Series starting June 2024 is indeed positive, which should bring about USD25bn inflows in a staggered manner through FY25. This should lead to a fundamental change in India’s Fixed Income market, with demandsupply dynamics of bonds improving in FY25. India's inclusion in global bond index will help reduce the cost of capital in the economy with a lag.

## Current account deficit to remain below 1.5% of GDP, but rupee to depreciate by the end of the year

In FY25, we expect the current account deficit to rise to USD35bn (0.9% of GDP), from 0.6% of GDP (USD23bn), which is likely to result in a BOP surplus of about USD55bn. We expect rupee to trade in the broad range of 83-85 in 2024, with RBI continuing with its proactive FX intervention strategy, though we don't rule out intermittent overshoot and undershoot from time to time. We expect INR/USD to be around 85.0 levels by end-Dec’24.

## S Korea and Taiwan: Strong exports put their CBs on edge

Korea's detailed data not as strong, but still has policy implications.

Juliana Lee, Chief Economist, APAC

1 The CBC could hike again.

## Why such a big gap in the Q1 GDP outcome and expectation?

South Korea's headline GDP growth accelerated to 3.4%yoy in Q1, from 2.2% in Q4, surprising sharply to the upside against the consensus and our forecasts at around mid 2%. In contrast to the headline growth, however, detailed demand data did not contain such big surprises. The sum of their contribution to growth stood 1ppts lower at 2.4% instead – a drawback associated with using the chain-weight measure in GDP. Discounting these factors, however, the Q1 GDP outcome is qualitatively consistent with our long-held view that the semi recovery would see South Korea's growth accelerate significantly this year, despite below trend growth in domestic consumption. Although improving (from -0,2ppts in Q4), private domestic demand's contribution to headline GPP growth stood at +0.4ppts in Q1 (vs. a 5-yr avg of +1ppts and a 10-yr avg of +1.6ppts ), while net trade's contribution remained very high at +3.5ppts (vs. +3.2ppts in Q4 and a 5-yr avg of 0.6ppts and a 10-yr avg of 0.1ppts). We expect a notable payback in headline growth in Q2, although the Q1 upside calls for a 0.2ppts upward revision to our 2024 growth forecast to 2.5%. We expect sustained relative weakness in private demand amid elevated borrowing rates – note the notable increases in delinquency ratios for SMEs and households to levels not seen since 2017 and 2015, respectively, although still well below 1%.

## Does the Q1 GDP result change macro policy outlook?

Despite some weakness in the details of the Q1 GDP, the optics of strong headline growth render it difficult for the BoK to be more proactive with its easing. It may require a notable pullback in growth in Q2, data for which would not be available until 25 July. As such, 22 August may be the earliest possible date for the BoK's first rate cut, followed a second one on 28 November, with the BoK policy rate bottoming at 3%. An alternative view is that the BoK waits until it sees a sharp drop in CPI inflation. below mid 2%, which we do not expect until August, with its data confirmed on 3 September. In a world of high uncertainty and lacking strong conviction, the BoK may opt to react to the data instead, rather than make a decision based on forecasts. If so, then it would delay its first rate cut until 11 October (or even 28 November, post the US election). This would still be before the Fed's rate cut – our US team has pushed back the timing of the Fed's first 25bps rate cut to December from June. Our prevailing view assumes that brent prices average USD85/bbl this year, as per our commodity analyst's forecast, and that the local fx stabilize. As elaborated in our earlier note, sustained upward pressure on oil prices would render rate cuts rather difficult this year. Meanwhile, a large cash handout program proposed by the main opposition party may not be justified, unless limited to the poor.

## Taiwan: CBC maintains its tightening bias

The CBC struck a relatively positive tone on growth, as it delivered a 12.5bp rate hike on 21 March, noting that " exports recorded continuous growth" while "private consumption gained momentum from stronger consumer confidence amid an economic recovery" as "the number of employed persons continued increasing in recent months, the unemployment rate dropped further, and the number of furloughed workers also decreased". . The difference between South Korea and Taiwan is the latter's strength in consumer demand. High-frequency data suggest that Taiwan's GDP likely stabilized at high levels in Q1, after expanding 2.1%QoQ sa in Q4, leaving its yoy growth rate higher at 6%yoy in Q1, vs. 4.9% in Q4. While it is not our baseline view that the CBC hikes again, we would not be surprised either. Even with another measured (12.5bps) rate hike, the CBC policy rate would still be well below what is implied by our Taylor rule model, which suggests that 2.5% as the appropriate level. At the current level of 2%, the CBC rate is still in negative territory, even after the fall in Taiwan's CPI inflation to 2.1%yoy in March. We expect inflation to average 2.3% in 2024, as the economy expands 3.7%, vs. the CBC's new inflation and growth forecasts of 2.2% and 3.2%, respectively. Adding to the pressure on the CBC to hike is a broad-based expectation of elevated US rates for longer, accompanied by fx volatility, with the latter worsened by rising risks to oil prices amid the Middle East tensions. Moreover, the CBC remains concerned over "growth in housing loans as well as loans to the real estate sector picking up, as housing market transactions surge again, and the ratio of real estate lending to the total lending of all banks remains elevated". There is also the matter of a "structural change of inflation" that the CBC has warned us of, not relating to electricity price hikes, per se. The latter was implemented from 1 April, with households facing an average price hike of slightly over 5%, albeit below the 7-14% in price hikes that general industry faces, for example.

## ASEAN: Multiple risks and limited conviction

BI delivers a surprise hike, but BSP hints at no change, although the timing of first rate cut by both remains tied to the Fed's. The BoT's rate cut decision is complicated by the digital wallet programme, with the timing pushed back to August, given the Senate election in June. While our baseline view has the MAS easing in October, this is difficult to pin down timing-wise, given the potential general election ahead. While our forecast of steady BNM and SBV rates remains unchanged, risks remain tilted to the upside, amid sustained pressure on their fx, with the latter opting for a stealth tightening.

Juliana Lee, Chief Economist, APAC

Bank Indonesia (BI) surprised and hiked its policy rates by 25bps at the April meeting, to "strengthen Rupiah stability against the impact of deteriorating global risks". BI described the rate hike decision as "a preemptive and forward-looking measure" to achieve its inflation target, "in line with the pro-stability monetary policy stance". More importantly, BI added that "global investors are rebalancing their portfolios towards safer assets, specifically US dollars and gold, thus triggering larger capital outflows and currency depreciation in emerging market economies (EMEs). Moving forward, the risks associated with the direction of FFR reductions and the dynamics of global geopolitical tensions will continue to demand vigilance due to their potential for exacerbating global financial market uncertainty, intensifying inflationary pressures and undermining the world economic growth outlook. Such conditions require a strong policy response to mitigate the adverse impact of global uncertainty spillovers on emerging market economies, including Indonesia." This in turn suggest to us that BI left the option of further rate hikes on the table to respond to further significant pressure on the rupiah, as "Indonesia's economy remains resilient despite the build-up of global uncertainty". To the latter's point, BI expects growth in the first two quarters of 2024 "to exceed growth recorded in the fourth quarter of 2023", on the back of persistently solid household consumption during the Ramadan and Eid-ul-Fitr festive period, while goods exports remain "unfazed" by declining commodity exports. Meanwhile, it saw construction outperformance supported by national strategic projects and private property development supported by government incentives. Despite the recent rate hike, risks to BI rates remain tilted to the upside, although we have already pushed back the timing of BI's first rate cut to December with the depth of its (total) cuts to match that of the Fed's. The latter's uncertainty points to also limited conviction on BI rates.

We do not expect Bangko Sentral Ng Pilipinas (BSP) to follow BI and hike its policy rates in the near term to support the peso. BSP estimates that a one peso drop against the US dollar leads to a 0.076ppt rise in inflation in the short run. The latter, however, suggests that risks remain tilted to the upside, with its latest policy statement proving to be more hawkish. In particular, it raised its CPI headline inflation forecast by 0.1ppts to 4% for 2024, partly reversing its downward revision back in February. While upside risks to inflation, from higher transport charges and electricity rates, elevated food prices, and global oil prices have already been well documented by the central bank, BSP noted its concerns over the second round effects of potential minimum wage hikes in the April statement. According to our estimate, BSP stands out as one of the most sensitive central banks in Asia to the Fed when it comes to its policy rate decisions. The Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) kept its policy stance unchanged, as widely expected. More importantly, its monetary policy statement reflected the MAS's increased confidence over Singapore's growth and disinflation prospects. While we have penciled in easing by the MAS in October, uncertainty over the timing of Singapore's general election poses risks to this view. If the latter is held later this year, we see the MAS waiting until after the election. If it is held by September, then it could go ahead in October, as per our outlook.

Although GDP growth disappointed to the downside in Q1, at 0.1%QoQ sa vs. the market expectation of 0.5%, the MAS struck a constructive view, noting that "the Singapore economy is expected to strengthen over 2024, with growth becoming more broad based. The slightly negative output gap is projected to narrow further in H2 2024", with its annual growth coming in between 1–3% (unchanged from its earlier projection). In particular, it added that "the recovery in the manufacturing and financial sectors should resume, supported by the upturn in the electronics cycle and anticipated easing in global interest rates" – with growth in domesticoriented sectors normalizing, to pre-pandemic levels. Although "core inflation is forecast to stay elevated in the immediate quarters ahead", following the GST rate and carbon tax hikes and increases in essential services fees, the MAS expects it to moderate ahead, with a "more discernible" fall in Q4 2024 and into 2025. Singapore report a weaker than expected core inflation of 3.1% in March, down from 3.6% in February. For the year, it sees MAS core inflation averaging 2.5–3.5%, and 1.5–2.5% excluding the GST impact (unchanged from its earlier assessment). The MAS noted that "although crude oil prices rose over the last three months, global prices of most food commodities as well as intermediate and final goods remain subdued". More importantly, it went on to add: "Wage growth has eased and should moderate this year as labor market tightness dissipates. Productivity is also expected to pick up. Consequently, unit labor costs should increase at a significantly slower pace compared to the preceding two years."

The Bank of Thailand (BoT) also left its policy rate steady in April, but its revised economic outlook provides further support for easing. In particular, the BoT revised its growth forecast to 2.6%, at the lower end of the range of 2.5-3% that it expected earlier, amid continued weakness in public spending and investment in Q1, which would result in rather weak GDP growth of 1% yoy during the quarter. It expects only a "gradual" recovery in goods exports, at 2%, revising it down further from its already weak growth forecast of 2.6%, restrained by "structural headwinds". In contrast, however, it revised up its tourist estimate to 35.5mn from 34.5mn, the continued improvement of which would support Thailand's growth acceleration in the remainder of the year, also pushed higher by a recovery in public expenditure and sustained expansion in private consumption. The BoT sees its growth rising to 3% in 2025, as tourist arrivals rise to 39.5mn and both goods export growth and public expenditure improve further. Our forecasts for this year and the next remain at 2.5% and 3.2%, respectively.

The BoT has also revised down its inflation forecast, to an annual average of 0.6% in 2024, vs. its earlier expectation of its stabilisation "at levels close to 1%", attributing its downward revision to "the decline in prices of certain raw food items due to excess supply and the decrease in energy prices due to an extension of government subsidies, while the recent negative inflation readings excluding subsidies remained positive". That said, it expects inflation "to return to target by the end of the year" and sees 2025 inflation at 1.3% in 2025, within its inflation target range of 1% to 3%.

Assuming the BoT's growth and inflation forecasts, our Taylor rule model suggests that its policy rate should be at least one 25bp cut lower. For its part, the BoT noted that its estimated neutral rate stands near but slightly below the current policy rate of 2.5%. Two members continued to vote for a cut "to reflect Thailand’s lower potential growth as a result of structural challenges and to partly alleviate the debt-servicing burden of borrowers". According to the BoT, "overall loan growth moderated due to debt repayments, while some small businesses may face relatively tighter financial conditions". And, "despite high levels of household debt, the deleveraging process continues gradually". That said, it continued to reiterate its concerns that "a low interest rate environment could lead to a build-up of financial fragilities".

That said, the government's digital wallet (DW) programme (THB500bn), aimed at boosting growth by 1.2%-1.6%, continue to pose risks to Thailand's outlook, including the policy rate, with the BoT reiterating its apprehensionover it – including "whether the government can achieve that funding" although its source is "clearer". The government is seeking to fund it through the budget (THB175bn in FY24 and THB152bn in FY25) and the state's Bank for Agriculture and Agricultural Cooperatives (THB173bn, with the latter remaining more uncertain. Beyond the market repricing of US rates and accompanying pressure on the baht, there is now also the matter of the senate election, which is now scheduled to be held in June – with three rounds of voting planned for 9 June, 16 June and 26 June – which suggests no rate change by the BoT on 12 June. 21 August may be a more appropriate timing in this regard, as it will also have at hand the Q2 GDP report (released on 19 August). That said, continued volatility in oil prices and fx markets would further delay or preclude easing this year. To this effect, the BoT noted that the baht "depreciated more, relative to other regional currencies" and that "the Committee will continue to closely monitor the volatilities in the foreign exchange market".

We continue to expect Bank Negara Malaysia (BNM) and State Bank of Vietnam (SBV) to stay on the sidelines in 2024, although risks to their rates have tilted to the upside, amid rising oil price and fx volatility. That said, the hurdle for their rate hikes remains high – to be considered in the event of an idiosyncratic surge in the local currency. Vietnam recently reported GDP growth weaker than expected, at 5.7%yoy in Q1 vs. 6.7% in Q4, amid sustained weakness in domestic demand, despite the strong rebound in export growth to +18% in Q1, from -2.5%in Q4, amid a housing market downturn. Despite very favourable base effects, consumption grew at only 4.9%. That is, if not for its concerns over the dong's stability, Vietnam's domestic fundamentals would have prompted the SBV to lower rates further. In fact, the SBV has been encouraging banks to lower their lending rates for priority sectors. That said, however, while ruling out rate hikes, the central bank has tightened liquidity to support the dong, with OMO rates higher and gold auctions. Such ad hoc measures may prove unsustainable, however, in the event of further rise in US rates and surge in global oil price, despite the sharp rebound in exports, especially amid rising domestic CPI inflation.

Macroeconomic fluctuations often account for most of the risks associated with exposures to instruments that promise to pay fixed or variable interest rates. For an investor who is long fixed-rate instruments (thus receiving these cash flows), increases in interest rates naturally lift the discount factors applied to the expected cash flows and thus cause a loss. The longer the maturity of a certain cash flow and the higher the move in the discount factor, the higher will be the loss. Upside surprises in inflation, fiscal funding needs, and FX depreciation rates are among the most common adverse macroeconomic shocks to receivers. But counterparty exposure, issuer creditworthiness, client segmentation, regulation (including changes in assets holding limits for different types of investors), changes in tax policies, currency convertibility (which may constrain currency conversion, repatriation of profits and/or liquidation of positions), and settlement issues related to local clearing houses are also important risk factors. The sensitivity of fixed-income instruments to macroeconomic shocks may be mitigated by indexing the contracted cash flows to inflation, to FX depreciation, or to specified interest rates - these are common in emerging markets. The index fixings may - by construction - lag or mis-measure the actual move in the underlying variables they are intended to track. The choice of the proper fixing (or metric) is particularly important in swaps markets, where floating coupon rates (i.e., coupons indexed to a typically short-dated interest rate reference index) are exchanged for fixed coupons. Funding in a currency that differs from the currency in which coupons are denominated carries FX risk.
