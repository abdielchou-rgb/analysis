## China: The future of the housing market

• China<sup>’</sup>s housing market correction is not over yet...

• …based on both an international comparison and the current market situation

• The solution lies in public and rental housing

• Key measures: institutionalized funding support in the near term, rural land reform in the long run

Since 2021, China<sup>’</sup> s housing market has experienced the most severe downturn since the reforms allowed the development of the private housing market (known as commodity housing) in the late 1990s (Figure 1). As of 1Q24, new home sales fell by 46% (in value terms) or 48% (in floor space) from the peak in 2021. New home starts (in floor space) fell by 60%, and the share of real estate investment as % of GDP fell from 14.0% in 2020 to 8.4% in 1Q24. The collapse in housing market activity not only drags on economic growth, but also increases financial vulnerabilities related to local government hidden debt, shadow credit and regional banks.

## A cross-country comparison: China vs. international experiences

How severe is China<sup>’</sup>s housing market correction? Here we assess price and volume indicators in an international comparison.

In term of house price decline, China<sup>’</sup>s new home price declined by 5-6% and secondary home price fell slightly over 10% from the peak in 2021, with aggregate home price down by about 10% (Figure 2). Compared to a few well-known house price correction episodes that have been popularly cited in the comparison, China<sup>’</sup>s house price decline is modest. It is similar to Korea<sup>’</sup>s episode in 2Q91-3Q95, but much less severe than HK (3Q97-3Q03), Japan (1Q91-2Q09), the US (1Q06-1Q11) and Spain (3Q07-1Q04) (Figure 3 and Table 1).

Two things are worth noting. First, the benchmark is important. The chosen episodes are among the most severe housing market corrections in modern history. Table 1 summarizes 90 house price decline episodes in 57 economies since 1970 (based on BIS data). The average duration of the housing downturns is 10 quarters and the average magnitude of house price decline is 17%. China<sup>’</sup>s house price has declined for 10 quarters (sample average) with a cumulative decline of 10% (around 40 percentile in the global sample). The international experience has been quite divergent, both in duration (minimum 1 quarter in Russia starting in 4Q10 and maximum 73 quarters in Japan starting in 1Q91) and the magnitude (minimum -3% in Luxembourg in 2Q08-3Q09 and maximum -65% in HK in 3Q97-3Q03). Among the above chosen episodes, except for Korea, the other four episodes rank among the top-10 percentile in terms of duration, and the top 20-percentile in terms of the magnitude of house price decline.

In addition, the relatively modest decline in house prices in China reflects stronger price control by the government, e.g., a ceiling control on home price inflation during upturns and a floor control during downturns (especially new home prices). The control on new home prices runs at the cost of a larger volume correction, perhaps an intentionally chosen strategy to mitigate financial spillover risk (link). As a result, in volume terms, China<sup>’</sup>s housing market correction has been remarkable. New home starts in China have declined by 60% from the peak, more severe than Japan and the US but less than

Spain (Figure 4).

The asymmetric adjustment in home prices and housing activity volumes partly explains why the housing market correction has become a significant macro drag, but financial spillover risk has been contained in China (as house price decline is relatively modest and the implicit government protection on LGFV debt is strong). Real estate investment declined by 10% in both 2022 and 2023, dragging GDP growth down by roughly 1.5%pts in each year based on our estimates. The share of real estate and construction in GDP fell from 14.4% in 2020 to 12.6% in 2023 (Figure 5). What is somewhat unexpected is that, from an international comparison perspective, the share of real estate and construction in China is smaller than Japan and Spain even during China<sup>’</sup>s peak years, though it is higher than Korea and HK. In addition, the decline in the share (1.7%-pts in China) is larger than the Korea episode (-0.7%-pt) but more modest than Japan (-3.2%-pts), Spain (-3.4%-pts) and the US (-2.2%-pts).

Overall, international comparisons suggests that China<sup>’</sup>s housing market correction is still more modest than some more severe episodes. If one thinks China<sup>’</sup>s housing market problem (e.g., over-heating and over-valuation) was similar to these episodes, the correction process will likely continue. In particular, if the administrative control on new home prices were to be removed, house prices may decline further to clear the market.

## Market over-shooting will not end soon

Is China<sup>’</sup>s housing market correction approaching its end? Our property team estimates that the equilibrium demand growth in China<sup>’</sup>s housing market (based on estimates of home demand from urbanization, new marriages, re-settlements, home upgrade and investment demand) is currently about 1 billion square meters per annum. This implies that volume indicators (new home sales and new home starts) have entered an overshooting stage (see Figure 1). However, this does not necessarily mean that housing market activity will soon bottom out.

First, overshooting is a typical phenomenon in house market correction episodes. Housing market corrections took place because of excessive growth in earlier years. In China<sup>’</sup>s case, new home sales and new home starts were significantly above the equilibrium levels in 2018-22, leading to over-capacity in the housing market that will take time to absorb. For instance, new homes under construction are still above 5 billion sqm, much higher than the supply in a normal housing cycle (home construction equivalent to three years of demand). In addition, potential supply in the secondary home market will add further pressure. Despite the lack of official data on vacancy rates, some estimates suggest that the vacancy rate (owned but not occupied) may be around 20%. A reduction of 5-10% in vacancy rates would imply an additional 1.4-2.8 billion sqm supply from secondary home market.

Looking at near-term demand and supply conditions, both demand and supply likely will continue to be lower than the equilibrium value in the near term. On the demand side, weak income and house price expectations have added to weak

home demand. Meanwhile, concerns about home delivery have led to divergent performance in sales between SOE developers and private developers (Figure 6), and also generated divergence between pre-sales versus sales of completed projects or secondary home sales. On the supply side, weak home sales and liquidity pressure have caused developers to be more cautious in buying new land or starting new projects.

![](images/9d6a0694dd5880eaf05e94027625d90c68d94460a1dd5523c88a20461dc1d548.jpg)

Our forecast expects that, without a major change in housing policy, the housing market decline will continue in 2024, with key volume indicators (new home sales, new home starts, new home completion) falling another 10%. These volume activities will likely stabilize or bottom up in 2025, but the macro drag (decline in real estate investment and drag on growth) may persist for another 1-2 years.

We closely watch two indicators to assess the housing market outlook. One is house price dynamics. A narrowing in home price declines or price stabilization is positive for the recovery in home demand; by contrast, further (or faster) home price declines will lead to further downside risk to our baseline forecasts. The other is new home sales, especially for SOE developers. If home sales for SOE developers also deteriorate (Figure 6), they may face similar liquidity pressure to private developers (as home sales represent the most important funding source for real estate developers). Spillover from private to SOE developers would lead to further downside risk to our baseline forecasts.

## From housing.com to housing.org

With housing becoming the weakest link for the Chinese economy, policy has aimed to stabilize the housing market. So far, housing policy relaxation has mainly been city-specific and gradually phased in, hence not sufficient to achieve that aim.

The Politburo meeting on April 30 discussed the housing policy from the perspective of risk prevention. It reiterated the importance of ensuring home delivery and the continuation of city-specific policy adjustment, alongside the need for a new development model of the housing market and policy measures to absorb inventory stocks and improve new home supply. This may have two implications.

First, housing policy relaxation will likely continue, but a dramatic adjustment from current city-specific to nationwide policy adjustment measures appears unlikely. Further housing policy relaxation may include interest rate cuts (lower mortgage rates), reduction in housing transaction costs (e.g., reduction in stamp duty tax and relaxation in value-added tax for secondary home sales), and further relaxation in home purchase restrictions (HPRs). As of now, the remaining key regions which still have not fully lifted HPRs are: all four tier-1 cities (Guangzhou relaxed the most, with HPR only for small units in core areas), Hainan Province, Tianjin, Hangzhou, Xi<sup>’</sup>an and Zhuhai. A complete removal of HPR would be a positive surprise, but even in that case, it may only lead to a temporary recovery in home transactions. We maintain our cautious view on the commodity housing market, as the market correction is not only cyclical but also structural.

Second, the solution to the housing market (or the new development model) lies in a structural transformation. Equilibrium demand in the commodity housing market may have trended downwards in recent years, and will continue to trend downward due to slowing urbanization, the lower birth rate, lower marriages, population ageing and weaker demand for housing investment. But the potential demand for public housing (including rental housing) is huge. The discrepancy between China<sup>’</sup>s urbanization ratio by citizenship (i.e. hukou) and by residence is about 250 million, or 18% of total population. The new urbanization process (or hukou reform) will create huge potential demand for home replacement i.e. from rural area homes to urban area apartments for millions of beneficiary families.

However, most of the new urbanization process will not generate demand in the commodity housing market, as most of these households are low and middle-income families and not able to afford commodity housing. Hence, public housing (incl. rental housing) will be the solution. This is not new. As shown in Figure 7, public housing has played a more important role in China<sup>’</sup>s urban housing supply since 2010. Prior to 2010 (between 1997 and 2010), new commodity housing accounted for 45% of new urban home supply, while public housing only accounted for 4% but other channels (e.g., employer-subsided housing or when an area is re-classified from rural to urban) accounted for \~50% of new urban housing. In 2010-2022, the share of commodity housing in total new urban housing supply rose to 64%, and the share of public housing rose to 33% (incl. 36 million units of public housing during the 12<sup>th</sup> Five year Plan in 2011-15, 18 million units of shanty-town renovations in 2015-17 and an additional 15 million in 2018-20). However, the pace of public housing increase has slowed since 2020.

In a 2022 note, we pointed out that China<sup>’</sup>s housing market will observe a shift from housing.com to housing.org, or a trend of de-marketization with three features. First, the housing market used to be dominated by commodity housing; going forward we think it will evolve into a multi-pillar structure where the share of public and rental housing will increase. Second, within the commodity housing market, the share of SOE developers will increase at the cost of private developers, in our view. Third, home prices will not be fully market-determined. We believe our views have been verified by the housing market development in the past two years.

In our view, growth in China<sup>’</sup>s public and rental housing market can address the housing market problems. In particular, if public housing or rental housing is supported by the acquisition of unfinished projects or existing inventory in the secondary home market, it can serve multiple policy objectives.

First, the increase in public housing investment can partly offset the contraction in commodity housing investment. An important assumption in our macro forecast this year is a 1 trillion yuan package funded by the central bank (via PSL) to support public housing, urban village renovation and other public infrastructure, which will reduce the decline in real estate investment from 8-10% (without this package) to 2-4% (with this package).

Second, it can accelerate the inventory reduction if public housing is created by the government purchase of unfinished projects in the commodity housing market. Earlier this year, the PBOC approved a 100 billion yuan special facility to support the purchase of commodity housing and its conversion into long-term rental housing in eight trial cities.

Since mid-2023, more than 30 cities have called for “trade in old to buy new<sup>”</sup> in the housing market, which in practice means cities have adopted different approaches: (1) trade-in transaction to sell old secondary housing in core city areas in exchange for new commodity housing, e.g., in Nanjing (Jiangsu); (2) government<sup>’</sup>s subsidies (cash subsidies or rebate of deed tax) to homebuyers who manage to sell their existing residence within a year, e.g., in Suzhou (Jiangsu); (3) SOE acquisition of secondary units and conversion into public or rental housing, e.g., in Zhengzhou (Henan); and (4) allowing homebuyers to cancel a transaction without penalty if their existing residence cannot be sold, e.g., in Shenzhen.

Third, the development in public housing is an indirect form of government transfer to the household sector, an effective way to encourage consumption by beneficiary households and boost domestic demand, so as to mitigate the demandsupply imbalance and deflation pressure in the economy.

## What measures are needed?

The key to the development of public or rental housing, including the above-mentioned “sell old, buy new<sup>”</sup> strategy, is the magnitude of the program and the source of funding. If the magnitude exceeds our assumption of a 1-trillion yuan package, or it evolves into a multi-year project, it could lead to earlier bottoming and recovery in the housing market.

In the near term, given the fiscal difficulties faced by local governments (weak land sales and tightening in LGFV borrowing), the funding may need to come from the central government (e.g., via ultra-long central government bonds) or the central bank (e.g., utilization of PSL, a special fund for home delivery or a special facility to support long-term rental housing). Importantly, if the policymakers can better utilize these resources, or further expand these facilities, it should help offset the weakness in the commodity housing market. Along this line, there is also a policy proposal to establish a specific real estate stabilization fund or a specific institute, to institutionalize the policy support for the housing market.

In the longer run, the source of funding can be rural land reform, involving the transferability of rural homes. The implicit rental increase from such reform may offer a stable funding source for public housing, such as the land-ticket experiment in Chongqing a decade ago that allowed farmers to trade in their rural homes for a land ticket, and the local gov<sup>’</sup>t used the revenue from rural land sales (converting it into higher value usage in industrial or residential land) to provide public housing. Another interesting case, very recently in Lishui City (Zhejiang), the local gov<sup>’</sup>t allowed individuals to bid for residential land (rather than it being restricted to developers). Possible innovations in the urban and rural land systems are important to watch in order to assess the future of China<sup>’</sup>s housing market. While this is not in our baseline assumption, the 3<sup>rd</sup> Plenum of the 20<sup>th</sup> Party Congress to be held in July may deliver positive surprises in land reforms.
