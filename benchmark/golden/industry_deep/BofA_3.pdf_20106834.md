## SOEs outperformed, with increased investor interests

With SOEs’ outperformance in the past 2-3 years, global investors’ interests are gradually expanding from SOE insurers to oil & gas, and more recently, to SOE banks, coal, and utilities stocks. Our tripod strategy for 2024 continues to like high yield SOEs in the volatile market. We believe SOEs’ outperformance could continue, with their enhanced focus on shareholder return and financial discipline.

## Unlocking SOEs’ value: more focus on shareholder return

Since 2020, the SASAC introduced key financial metrics as SOEs’ KPIs, including “one profit and five ratios” in Jan-2023. In the past 2-3 years, A-share listed SOEs indeed improved on revenue growth, debt/equity leverage, and ROE, relative to the POEs. In Jan-2024, SASAC said market value management would become a KPI for listed central SOEs, indicating that SOE re-rating has become a policy priority. Besides fundamental improvement, SOEs also increased shareholder return recently. Central SOEs raised payout ratio from <45% on average in 2018-20, to 50-55% in 2021-22. Payout ratio for local and other SOEs rose to >45% in 2022, and was stable at \~30% for financial institutions. Stock buyback has been rare among SOEs, and the largest buybacks in HK/China so far were mainly by internet/consumer POEs and HK financials. Past 5 year (2018-22) average buyback to earnings ratio was \~60% for the S&P500, \~30% for the FTSE100, and \~15% for the Nikkei225, but only 1% for the CSI300. Increasing payout and buyback could be a “quick-win” for SOEs to drive multiple re-rating.

## Learning from Japan’s playbook

Japan has been focusing on enhancing corporate governance and shareholder return since 2009. In 2023, the Tokyo Stock Exchange (TSE) introduced a “name-and-shame” regime and required listed companies to “comply or explain” if they trade below 1x P/B. Many Japanese firms took initiatives to analyze profitability and cost of capital, increase ROE and dividend payout, implement management incentive systems, and improve disclosure and investor communications. Korea also recently announced a “Corporate Value-up Program”. China’s SOE re-rating agenda is similar to its neighbors’ efforts.

## Fantastic four (“YYDS”); the yield and buyback screens

Four SOE-dominated sectors have the highest dividend yield at 5-7% in the MSCI China universe. They are: financials (banks + insurance), telcos, utilities, and energy (oil & gas + coal). Some Chinese investors labeled them as the “Fantastic Four” or “YYDS”. These sectors generated positive return YTD, led by oil (+20%), coal (+20%) and banks (+9%), and also outperformed MSCI China over the past 3year and 10year horizon. At stock level, we screened large-cap HK/China stocks for: 1) high 2023E dividend yield (36 SOEs and 11 POEs), and 2) companies with strong cash position and low debt leverage, and could potentially increase buyback and/or payout (26 SOEs and 24 POEs).

## SOEs continued to outperform

We highlighted the value of defensive SOEs in a barbell strategy in our 2023 China strategy year ahead, and elaborated the SOE reform and re-rating theme in May-2023 (link to SOE report). Our 2024 China year ahead continued to prefer high-yield SOEs in a tripod strategy.

## SOE reform 2.0: unlocking the value

China’s SOE reform in the past decade focused mainly on mixed ownership reform, corporate governance, and supply side reform. Unlocking the value of SOEs has become a policy priority for the Chinese regulators since 4Q22. Chairman of the CSRC (China Securities Regulatory Commission) in Nov-2022 called for “exploring a valuation system with Chinese characteristics” and highlighted that SOEs’ valuation could be better appreciated by the market. In Dec-2022, SHEX announced a three-year action plan, and “promoting the valuation of central SOEs to return to appropriate levels” was a key priority. The efforts continued in 2024. In late Jan, SASAC suggested that market value management would become a KPI for listed central SOEs’ managers. On Jan 29<sup>th</sup>, the SASAC hosted a meeting with central SOEs and provincial-level SASACs, to roll out the evaluation of SOEs’ market value management.

In the short term, SOE re-rating can help stabilize the A-share market confidence, especially following the dismal China market performance in Jan-2024, and in the past three years. Over the long term, the reform/re-rating of SOEs could help drive a structural/steady appreciation of the A-share indices, attract more individual and institutional investors to participate in the A-share market, and support continued fund raising for Chinese companies. It could also have a positive wealth effect on households and offset the negative impact from real estate price decline.

Within the regional context, the TSE in 2023 required listed companies to “comply or explain” if they trade below 1x P/B, which effectively enhanced Japanese firms’ corporate governance and market valuation. Korea also recently announced a “Corporate Value-up Program”. SASAC’s agenda is consistent with the efforts of its neighbors.

We believe continued SOEs re-rating will be driven by: 1) ROE enhancement, 2) attractive shareholder return (eg dividend and buyback), and 3) improved disclosure and investor communications. While enhancing operating efficiency and ROE is a long-term endeavor, cash-rich SOEs can also achieve “quick-win” by raising payout or buyback.

## 1. Fundamental improvement in ROE

In 2020, the SASAC launched a 3-year plan, and introduced financial metrics (eg profit, asset/liability ratio, and R&D intensity) as SOEs’ KPIs. In Jan-2023, the SASAC adjusted the KPIs to “one profit and five ratios”, emphasizing on quality & profitable growth, and investing in innovation. Central SOEs are required to grow profit faster than GDP growth, maintain stable asset/liability leverage, and improve ROE, productivity, R&D intensity, and operating cash flow margin.

## 2. Dividend: central SOEs increased payout

We compiled the financial data of over 5,100 A-share listed, over 2,600 HK-listed (including HSBC/AIA, etc) companies and over 300 Chinese ADRs. HK-listed companies in aggregate paid slightly more dividends than A-share in 2018-19, although A-share companies significantly increased dividends in 2021-22. A-share companies in total paid USD312bn of dividends in 2022, up nearly 50% from USD208bn in 2018. Chinese ADRs paid little dividends in the past, but their dividends nearly quadrupled from 2018 and reached USD3.0bn by 2022.

Within the A-share universe, financial institutions (eg large banks) have been consistently paying dividends, and their total dividends rose at a more steady pace of \~8% p.a. in the past few years. Financial institutions made up 38-41% of total A-share dividends paid in 2018-19, but the ratio declined to 34% by 2022, as other SOEs raised payout. Central SOEs accounted for nearly 1/4 of the dividends paid in 2022, up from \~20% in 2018-19. Central SOEs significantly increased total dividend amount and payout ratio in 2021-22, thanks partly to the SOE reform.

## 3. Buyback: mostly by financials and internet POEs

Stock buyback hasn’t been a common practice for the Chinese companies. Buyback yield for the CSI300 has been <0.5% for the past 5-6 years, compared to 1.0-1.5% for the Nikkei225, 1.0-2.5% for the FTSE100, and 1.5-3.5% for the S&P500. S&P500 companies used nearly 60% of net profit to buy back stocks during 2018-22. Average buyback to earnings ratio for the past five years was around 30% for the FTSE100, and 15% for the Nikkei225, but only 1% for the CSI300 and HSCEI firms.

## Learning from Japan’s playbook

Japan’s corporate governance reform started in 2009. The Tokyo Stock Exchange (TSE) in 2009 required listed companies to appoint at least one independent director or one independent statutory auditor. In 2010, the government proposed a series of changes on non-financial disclosure, covering the counting of Annual General Meeting votes, executive remuneration, and large cross-shareholdings. After Mr. Shinzo Abe became Japan’s Prime Minister in 2012, he launched “three arrows” to bolster the economy, including: (1) aggressive monetary policy, (2) fiscal consolidation, and (3) growth strategy (with corporate governance being a key component). The reform boosted board independence (senior management teams should have at least one-third outside directors for companies listed on the Prime market, according to the 2021 Corporate Governance Code), improved gender diversity, and curbed cross-shareholdings to enhance management discipline and capital efficiency.

## “Name and shame” regime by the Tokyo Stock Exchange

In Jan-2023, the TSE required listed companies to “comply or explain” if they trade below 1x P/B. Companies must show how they plan to improve capital efficiency, demonstrate how they have engaged investors, and begin publishing public disclosures in English. Companies that do not meet the enhanced listing criteria could face delisting as soon as 2026. In Mar-23, the TSE demanded companies to increase corporate value over the mid-to-long-term by focusing on the cost of capital and profitability based on the balance sheet, rather than just sales and profit. The TSE requested listed companies to implement the following three key points: 1) analysis and evaluation of the company’s current situation (incl. the cost of capital, profitability, and valuation); 2) consideration and disclosure of initiatives; and 3) dialogue with shareholders and investors.

Companies need to conduct progress analysis and update disclosures at least once a year. The TSE has published a monthly list of companies that have disclosed their plans for optimizing capital management since Jan-2024, and it will compile corporate disclosures and feedback from investors once every six months.

## Summary of actions taken by Japanese firms

Japanese companies’ initiatives so far have been focusing on enhancing their profitability (eg reducing high cost debts, enhancing ROE), as well as valuation multiples (eg improving shareholder return and investor communications), including:

1) Analyzing company profitability (ROIC, ROE etc.) and market valuation (stock price, market cap, PB, PE etc.). Considering the cost of capital (WACC, etc.) from the investors’ perspective and being aware of the need to reduce cost of capital.

2) Fundamental initiatives to improve shareholder returns, dividend payout, capital allocation, profitability (ROIC, ROE, etc) and valuation targets (PE, PB, etc).

3) Designing a management compensation system that provides an incentive to increase corporate value over the medium to long term.

4) Constructive dialogue with shareholders and investors, and proactive involvement of management and board (e.g. disclosure of the status of dialogue and feedback to the board, and subsequent actions taken as a result).

Our Japan Strategist, Masashi Akutsu, noted that many companies announced the sales of strategic shareholdings, shareholder payouts, acquisitions, and management buyout (MBO) when they reported 3Q results. (The world after finally posting new record highs)

## Case study

## The “Fantastic Four” and stock screens

## 1. China’s “Fantastic Four” SOE sectors: “YYDS”

Within our 40-sector framework, six sectors (or four broad sectors) have the highest dividend yield at 5-7%. They are: banks, insurance, oil & gas, coal, telcos, and utilities. Some Chinese investors label these SOE-dominated, high yield sector as the “Fantastic Four” or “YYDS” – Yinhang Baoxian (financials: banks + insurance), Yunyingshang (telcos), Dianli (utilities), and Shiyou Meitan (energy: oil + coal).

## 4. Companies with buyback potentials

We used a quantitative model to screen for companies with strong cash position, low balance sheet leverage, and undemanding P/B valuation. Among the nearly 8,000 HK/China/ADR listed companies, only 50 companies meet the following criteria: 1) market cap >USD5bn, 2) ADTV >USD5mn, 3) <1.5x P/B, 4) positive Operating Cash Flow in 2022 and 1H23; 5) positive EBIT in 1H23, 6) Asset/equity leverage <2.5x, and 7) cash/market cap >10%.

26 of these 50 companies are SOEs, mainly in energy, telcos, utilities and industrials sectors. We believe they should have better potential to conduct share buyback or increase dividend payout, under SASAC’s push for SOE market value management.
