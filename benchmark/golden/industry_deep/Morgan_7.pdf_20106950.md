## Spreads keep squeezing, looking for a bit more juice

The EMBIGD spread returned to a tightening trend in February, after starting the year with a modest correction wider in the wake of the strong 4Q23 rally. The riskon dynamic at the end of last year reflected both a rally in yields and compression in spreads—as markets were crowded into embracing disinflation and the Fed<sup>’</sup>s dovish pivot—delivering strong overall returns in the process. At 367bp, the current EMBIGD spread is near the bottom of a rather wide 47bp range since the end of 2023 and grinding to lows overall not seen since January 2022. But the rather pronounced spread swings to start the year are largely a function of UST volatility, with the 10-year having moved in a similarly wide 44bp range so far in 2023. If we look at February<sup>’</sup>s EMBIGD spread tightening alone, the 33bp move lower was mirrored by a 33bp move wider in the UST 10yr. EMBIGD prices (yields) have therefore been quite sticky over the last month. Treasuries have dragged the overall EMBIGD total return to 0.6% YTD, all told, with a negative 1% fall in January followed by a +1.6% recovery in the 5 weeks since. The latter has been underpinned by a 2.6% spread return since the end of January, powering through the UST volatility.

As markets have settled into a top-down narrative, where higher for longer is prevailing and fewer Fed cuts will be forthcoming, the impact on EMBIGD spreads has been somewhat asymmetric. EM bonds have been more resilient to a UST sell-off, driven at its core by US economic resilience (alongside some related stickiness of inflation), while they have tended to follow a bit more any UST rallies (Figure 2)

But as we move into spring, it seems increasingly apparent that the days are getting longer, and maybe it<sup>’</sup>s not actually as late as we perceived it to be. ## ‘B’ack to the Future: Lower rated sovereigns probe the 10% threshold

After a record start to 2024 for gross issuance, much has been made of a return to the market of lower rated sovereigns. SSA issuers have returned to primary markets after a nearly two-year hiatus, with recent deals from Cote d<sup>’</sup>Ivoire (BB-), Benin (B+), and an issuance/tender offer from Kenya (B). We also got new issuance from B+rated Bahrain.

These green shoots of issuance at the low end of the HY spectrum foster an optimistic narrative that a host of distressed issuers that have effectively been priced out of the market could find their back. While we have been of the opinion that no new defaults are pending in 2024, even in a scenario of no market access for these names (see our Sept 15 report), hope for market refinancing could considerably improve the prospects of a host of mostly B-rated sovereigns that otherwise could eventually struggle to get through maturity walls.

Although forecasts of Fed cuts in the US are also improving the prospects of lower-rated sovereigns, all-in yields remain very high for many—above the psychologically relevent 10% threshold. Until the recent Kenya deal, the “greenshoot<sup>”</sup> issuance observed to date had come from credits that have long traded well below the average yields of the B-rated bucket of the EMBIGD (9.38% on average YTD). But the majority of lower-rated sovereigns, many of which are in SSA, continue to trade with yields around or north of 10%—a level not unheard of historically for EM issuance, but not really regularly seen in terms of primary issuance coupons in more than 20 years (urvey ofsign hadc b t10% p l3 Figure 7Survey of10%+cpn EMsig had  tl).

Is issuing above 10% the red flag it seems to be? Over the last twenty years, issuing above 10% has certainly been rare: we count only 14 bonds by 8 distinct issuers. Of these, 6 bonds from 3 distinct issuers (Venezuela, Ghana and Lebanon) have defaulted, a startlingly high percentage. However, in the prior decade of 1995- 2004, when the UST 10-year averaged over 5%, we count 120 bonds from 18 distinct sovereigns issued at over 10% coupons. Of these bonds, 20% defaulted, all of which came from a familiar set of names: Argentina, Ecuador and Venezuela. Hence, 15 of the 18 sovereigns that issued above 10% between 1995 and 2004 avoided default, managing to either refinance or pay off these bonds at maturity.

The current credit ratings of those sovereigns that issued above 10% in the decade up to 2004 is a mixed bag, but by no means does issuing ‘expensive’ in a high rate environment condemn a sovereign to distress in the end. In the absence of strong growth and a stable balance of payments, issuing in hard currency at above 10% in nominal terms will tend to weigh negatively on debt dynamics. However, if we look back 30 years, when issuing at such high rates was more <sup>‘</sup>normal<sup>’</sup>, we can observe that many of those countries have subsequently been able to implement reforms that have allowed themselves to grow, consolidate, refinance themselves into a much better credit standing. To be sure, half of the 18 countries that issued above 10% during 1995-04 are now rated BB or above, with five of them rated investment grade. Thus, historical evidence suggests that issuing above a 10% yield, while not sustainable indefinitely, does not guarantee a sovereign will inevitably end up in payment stress as long as it sticks to doing necessary reforms over the medium term.

## Panama: It’s not good, but it’s in the price – Move OW (from MW)

Since the onset of the First Quantum saga, Panama’s underperfomance has been stark. Despite the recent recovery of spreads from multi-year highs, Panama has been one of the worst performers, not only within the EMBIGD IG space, but the overall EMBIGD universe. With spreads widening 93bp over the last six months, total returns plummeted to -2.3% during that span, significantly underperfoming the IG benchmark (12.3%) and the overall EMBIGD (8.3%). While the move became accentuated following the Supreme Court<sup>’</sup>s decision to reject the contract between the State and First Quantum on constitutional grounds, the credit has lagged the sustained rally on sovereigns over the last month, with Panama spreads holding firmly close to the 300bp handle with EMBIGD spreads tightening 33bps since the beginning of February (Figure 13Panm’s uderpfoc hbtk vl ix).

From a macro standpoint, we remain of the opinion that, given its overall effect, the decision to close the mine will cost Panama its IG status, a move we expect to happen over the next twelve months. As we highlighted in the aftermath of the ruling (see Panama: Loss of IG expected - Enter 10s30s steepener , S. Palacio & G. Lalaguna, November 25 2023), the implications were two-fold: with the mine on its own representing close to 2% of GDP, we expect the closure of the mine to deteriorate Panama<sup>’</sup>s growth prospects to 0.5% during 2024, accompanied by a fiscal slippage that will push the debt to GDP ratio marginally above the 60% threshold. For the medium term, the expectation is for Panama<sup>’</sup>s medium-term average growth to settle at 3.5% given the erosion of the view around Panama<sup>’</sup>s investment friendly destination (for further details of our economists forecasts see Latin America Economic Reference Presentation - Panama S. Palacio, 05 March 2024). The impact of a drought on the Canal as well as increased focus on looming pressure from the pension system have added to overall sense of a downward spiral for fundamentals, with election year uncertainty not helping. The market has effectively already priced Panama to lose the IG rating, and at the end of November we entered a 10s30s steepener trade, which we hold.

Given the magnitude of the widening, more than pricing in the fallen angel narrative, and the recent lag in valuations, we think the bar for the credit to keep underperforming is high; we move OW Panama in our EMBIGD model portfolio. To be sure, our OW call is about overall valuations, and what we perceive to be in the price, and not expectations for near-term positive catalysts heading into May elections or great expectations that the new administration can pull a rabbit out of its hat. As mentioned above, we are of the opinion that the medium-term outlook remains challenging as the recent dynamic pushes Panama into a more complex and volatile equilibrium defined, largely, by deteriorating growth (Figure 16). For a credit in which growth was a central element of the puzzle, a sharp slowdown in economic activity compounded by higher fiscal needs (and, consequently, a larger risk premium) will naturally weigh on its debt dynamics over the long run. Moreover, the potential liability that Panama confronts with First Quantum is material. Nevertheless, we believe the recent move in valuations fully encapsulates this dynamic and the fallen angel narrative appears to be fully priced: Panama now trades in line with BB-/B+ names like Guatemala, Jordan, Morocco, among others. Furthermore a completed issuance schedule for the year and positive headlines around upcoming investments on the magnitude of \$8.5bn on the Canal between 2024-2030 should be constructive for valuations in the immediate term.

The biggest headwinds for the recommendation lie around the potential selling pressures in the aftermath of losing the investment grade and the electoral uncertainty. On the first point, we expect limited forced selling pressures. We find that Panama holders tend to be dedicated investors, and our client survey indicates the credit rising as a firm UW overall—despite the recent risk addition after the issuance (Figure 15Despit hrcnv oua, m flyUW P).

We estimate around \$3.4bn in market value of holdings at risk to an eventual downgrade. Potential short-term catalysts are May 5 elections. We expect the uncertainty around the outcome to add noise in the short term. As per their respective platforms, the pool of candidates continue leaning to the right and without major challenges around pursuing a business-friendly environment; nevertheless, the fact that the race remains open amid a highly fragmented field adds an extra layer of uncertainty, and we have seen few concrete proposals on how to address the myriad challenges. Aside from trying deal with the First Quantum situation, as well as pressure from the pension system, perhaps the over-arching hurdle for the next administration is to navigate increasing social tensions that will be exacerbated in a context of lower and stagnated growth. In that sense, we expect protests to remain a part of the picture going forward.

## Uruguay: Move UW against the backdrop of tight valuations and potential election noise

We move UW URUGUA in order to balance our overall EMBIGD MW stance given the overall tight spreads heading into elections. . From a fundamental standpoint, we remain of the opinion that the country remains on a constructive path: growth is expected to recover amid improved agricultural conditions; the fiscal landscape appears manageable (and improving), and the sovereign balance sheet is healthy (see Latin America Economic Reference Presentation - Uruguay, L. Barbeito, 05 March 2024). Nevertheless, the credit screens rich, especially under the lens of core rates current levels. Despite valuations remaining fairly in line vis a vis its BBB peers (Figure 17), the credit has outperformed the overall IG bucket of the benchmark on the margin over the last year with spreads close to multi-year tights (Figure 18). ## Nigeria: Work in Progress

The structural reform momentum justified the strong market performance last year, but the runway seems limited this year. Nigeria bonds saw a strong performance, with markets appreciating the reform focus of the new government. Over the past year, Nigeria bond spreads have moved from screening wider to similarly rated SSA peers (see EM Spread vs Ratings, 23 May 23) to now screening tighter to most of them (see EM Spread vs Ratings, 26 Feb 24). Moreover, EMBIGD Nigeria has also seen one of the largest tightenings over the past year when compared to its similarly rated EMBIGD peers (Figure 32). The shift in policies toward structural reforms justified the move tighter, in our view. While the reforms are expected to continue, the pace would likely be slower and navigating the path relatively more tricky given the execution risks. Furthermore, Nigeria bonds are already closer to 2-year tights, with EMBIGD NGERIA now screening closer to recent historical tights versus EMBIGD HY (Figure 33).

While valuations look tight, the continued focus on reforms and the current risk environment provide a balancing effect; we remain MW. Although valuations look tight compared to recent history, we believe markets would continue to focus on the reforms story. The current global risk backdrop, too, seems supportive with expectations that the Fed cuts rates later this year amidst a soft landing. Furthermore, investor positioning as per the J.P. Morgan EM Client Survey continues to be closer to neutral. As such, we do not expect repetition of last year<sup>’</sup>s outperformance, but material widening, too, seems unlikely. Given this view, over the near term we expect Nigeria to trade in line with the overall index and remain MW NGERIA in the EMBIGD.

## Pakistan: Politics is everything

Dust has settled on the post-election government formation but political risk has increased. The recent parliamentary election results didn<sup>’</sup>t turn out to be as expected, with none of the major parties attaining a majority, with independent candidates aligned to Imran Khan<sup>’</sup>s Pakistan Tehreek-e-Insaf (PTI) outperforming expectations and securing a plural majority. While the coalition of the incumbent parties, Pakistan Muslim League-Nawaz (PML-N) and Pakistan People<sup>’</sup>s Party (PPP) settles the dust on the government formation, political risks remain. The likelihood of securing a new/ extended IMF program seems reasonable, but the risks of an extended or stalled negotiation process are tangible and a larger program seems unlikely.

We remain sidelined given the current price action, with the risk/reward profile looking more skewed to the downside. The price action has been relatively stronger over the past week, with bond yields now between 13.5-18% range across the curve versus 19-40% range 6m ago (and 15-23% range around the end of 2023). A successful negotiation for an extended program could lead to a further rally in bond prices, with the quantum of the move depending on the size of the program. In such a scenario, we expect the curve to steepen and move closer to Egypt, which recently saw a strong rally on the back of \$35bn deal with the UAE (Figure 4). That said, even in this scenario, we expect some additional premium given lower reserve base position and domestic political concerns. On the flip side, significant delays or failure to secure an extended IMF deal or external support could lead to material decline in bond prices and further inversion of the curve towards a level seen 6 months ago (Figure 4). According to our estimates, at current levels, markets are pricing in a nearly 70% chance of an extended IMF support (Figure 5). While we think the likelihood of securing a program looks decent, the risks of protracted or stalled negotiations are tangible. Furthemore, the strong price action so far limits the runway for further upside, in our view, with the risk/ reward profile looking less attractive. We remain cautious on the near-term uncertainty, acknowledging the recent price action and maintain neutral stance on Pakistan sovereign credit.
