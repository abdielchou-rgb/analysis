In one sense, having a longshot framework is counter-intuitive; game-changing technology frequently comes from unexpected companies and as a result of entirely unpredictable catalysts. Nonetheless, we think it is a helpful guide in estimating where certain technologies are on their own respective adoption/innovation S-Curves.

There is no catch-all metric that can spot emerging longshot potential – if there were then the entire Venture industry would not be predicated on such high capital burn rates and the need for individual winners to "Return The Fund".

That said, there are a number of leading indicators:

• Academic publications: The first-movers in the longshot development curve tend to be found in academia. This takes the form of not only original thought-pieces but in subsequent citations of research which often reinforces the value in the original piece.

• Patent quantity & quality: Patents have long acted as a helpful proxy for corporate and start-up innovation spending. ## Moore's Law vs Eroom's Law

The healthcare industry sits at the intersection of two competing structural forces:

• The first – Moore's Law – is better known. Made famous in the semiconductor industry by doubling the number of transistors on a microchip every two years. However it can also be applied to the doubling speed of cost reduction in sequencing a human genome. This cost >\$1m two decades ago and was a timeconsuming process. It can now be done for c\$600 and can be stored in 3GB of data. Genome sequencing is critical in understanding gene function, expression and ultimately bespoke medication.

• The second – Eroom's Law – is less well known but is simply the inverse of Moore's Law. However it applies not to the cost decline of certain technologies, but rather value achieved per unit of input. Simply put, the amount of new drugs coming to market per \$1bn spent has been falling logarithmically - and in real terms.

The first needs little explanation, the latter though has been argued since the 1960s to be a function of (1) the complexity of biological systems; (2) rising regulatory burdens over time; (3) Diminishing returns after lower-hanging fruit therapies have been developed. The question we have is whether machine learning can alter the path of Eroom's Law for pharmaceuticals, but also lead to longevity breakthroughs in the rest of the healthcare space.

## Longevity: 80% Lifestyle and 20% Genetics

Longevity, loosely, can be considered to be the question of whether someone's phenotype (lifestyle and environment) can be supplemented to the point where it trumps their genotype

Whether the problem is to understand the process of aging, or to extend the natural limits of human life, the trouble is that we know too little about what is really going on. However, what we do know is that – despite the name and content of this report – a remarkable amount of longevity can be attributed to lifestyle rather than genetics. Consequently, aggregate longevity is likely to be impacted by a greater degree if we focus on cheaper, quicker, preventative changes to our lifestyles rather than attempting to find silver-bullet treatments to ailments.

Modern medicine, by focusing almost exclusively on treating disease rather than preventing it, has succeeded in keeping us alive, while sick, for longer and longer. We know the consequences in terms of the cost of healthcare, but treating disease does not increase our healthspan; in most cases it only increases the period of morbidity.

Preventing disease achieves two key goals: i) increasing healthspan and ii) reducing morbidity. Most centenarians do not develop any chronic disease until they are over 80 years old and some never even get one. Can we replicate the successful aging story of several centenarians for a larger slice of the population? There is not a single magic fix that will deliver on all these promises, but instead multiple, and we can select the best combination of them for each of us. And contrary to general belief, finding the cure for cancer is not one of these magic fixes. A University of Chicago study found that eliminating cancer as a disease would give us just 3 more years of healthspan and 2 fewer years of morbidity. What we should aim for instead is to delay the onset of all chronic diseases by 10-30 years.

## Genetics explains c20% of longevity

Much of the funding for old age care has and will continue to come from governments around the world. As healthcare improves, the UN projects the number of centenarians to surpass a quarter of a million in the UK alone by 2050. Simultaneously, the OECD predicts that across Europe population ageing will cause age-related health expenditures to rise from around 19% of GDP in 2000 to around 26% by 2050 – amounting to c£250bn by 2030 in the UK alone.

The longest documented living person, Jeanne Calment, a French woman, died at the incredible age of 122. She was born under the Ulysses Grant presidency and died under the Clinton presidency. Yet today, globally, there are only 663 supercentenarians aged 110 and older, or less than 0.00004% of all the people born in 1910. Among the 10 oldest super-centenarians globally today, there is not a single male.

The main reason centenarians have a much longer than average healthspan and a much shorter period of morbidity is that most avoid one of the four main chronic diseases and causes of death: cancer, cardiovascular disease, neurodegenerative disease and diabetes. One group analysed the disease history of 424 U.S. and Canadian centenarians: 82% of the male and 68% of the female centenarians had not experienced any of the following before age 80: cardiovascular disease, stroke, diabetes, Parkinson’s or cancer. For most centenarians, the leading cause of death is either a sudden heart attack or an infectious disease, neither of which lasts long

Thus, this is how healthy centenarians are able to become healthy centenarians: they avoid getting any chronic disease until at least 80 years of age. And how do they do that? We know that genetics help. Several studies conclude that genetics explain 15-30% of people’s longevity. A previous study based on the Scandinavian twins database published in 1996, covering 368 identical twin pairs, suggested that genetics account for 22-28% of longevity.

We also know that some diseases have a hereditary component. The APO-E gene, for example, has been shown to have a direct influence on longevity and healthspan: if you have the allele 4 (around 30% of the population) you have an increased risk of developing cardiovascular issues and Alzheimer’s compared to the more common allele 33 (57% of the population). The lucky ones with the allele 2 (\~13% of the population) have a slightly lower risk for both diseases relative to those without.

The human gut microbiota also plays important roles in the aging process as well as the cause and development of aging-related diseases (for example, diabetes mellitus and cancers). Centenarians, who usually exhibit a reduced susceptibility to chronic diseases, are an exemplary model for studying how the gut microbiota contributes to healthy aging. An enrichment of certain gut bacteria that are associated with aging (for example,

Akkermansia spp. and the Christensenellaceae family) has been identified in centenarians.

## Evolving Mortality & Morbidity

The rise in chronic diseases is beginning to dominate in middle income countries and overwhelm in high income countries – taking the UK as a proxy:

• Infections, responsible for over one third of all deaths between 1900 and 1950, have been all but wiped out as leading causes of death in any cohort or sex, predominantly as a function of improving antibiotics.

• Heart conditions then took over as major killers of the elderly, and particularly men. This was reduced when smoking was pronounced by the Surgeon General in 1964 to be a probable cause of not only lung cancer but also heart disease.

• Overwhelmingly, however, the primary cause of death in the UK, particularly in women is various forms of cancer alongside ischaemic heart diseases (12.4%).

• In the last decade though, there is a new leading cause of death and one that is emblematic of rapid population ageing. Dementia is now the leading cause of death in women over 80. With women statistically living longer than men in all countries, it is likely that this will be a gender-biased cause of death for some time to come. However, its emergence presents food for thought for investors. When diseases become leading causes of mortality, history shows convincingly that these can become multi-decadal trends that require a high R&D intensity and capital flows to make these conditions more manageable.

More than 50 million people worldwide currently live with dementia. This number is projected by the WHO to reach 152 million by 2050, presenting society with a considerable healthcare challenge. Alzheimer's disease is the most common form of dementia, accounting for 60-70% of all cases. No new treatment for Alzheimer's has been approved since 2003. There is significant impetus, therefore, to find solutions using regenerative medicine to not only extend lives but also to improve them whilst bringing down the overall public sector burden of healthcare provision.

There are broadly considered to be 6 forms of ageing.

• Genomic instability - accumulation of DNA damage or insufficient DNA repair throughout life. This can often by a precursor to the development of cancer.

• Telemere attrition - a breakdown in the internal protective or repair mechanism of DNA resulting in a decline in regenerative tissue capacity.

• Epigenome alterations - which controls which genes are turned off and on. Epigenetic alterations are theoretically reversible and as such could be the target of anti-aging treatments.

• Loss of proteostasis - Loss of healthy protein maintenance. Chronic protein dysfunction contributes to the development of Alzheimer’s & Parkinson’s disease.

• Deregulated nutrient sensing - Impairment in the sensing pathways associated with food processing. These pathways can be de-regulated in metabolic diseases e.g., diabetes.

• Mitochondrial dysfunction - Mitochondria lose their ability to provide energy and instead release harmful reactive oxygen species. This in turn can lead to: cellular senescence (lack of cell division); stem cell exhaustion (reduced response to injury); and altered intercellular communication.

## How Can Medicine & Technology Tip The Balance?

Delaying the onset of chronic diseases by 10-30 years is not an outlandish goal. It would most likely be a lot cheaper than treating cancer. And clearly it is much better to avoid cancer altogether than to be cured from cancer. To achieve this there is a growing consensus that we need to change the approach taken by most medical research.

By delaying aging, we should be able to delay the onset of all chronic diseases by 10-30 years and be “biologically” younger at an older age. This is the secret of healthy centenarians. Achieving this requires a combination of factors:

• Adopting consistently good behaviours (healthy nutrition, exercise, mental engagement, sun exposure, etc)

• Avoiding bad behaviours (smoking, most drugs, pollution, stress, overmedication, etc)

• Selectively leveraging the new array of “longevity” therapies that have been launched, albeit most still experimentally (metformin, hormones, rapamycin, spermidine, senolytics, etc.) - see Appendix for a full list of these experimental treatments and trials.

The combination of clinical trials on animals and epidemiological research on humans suggests that we are close to having drugs and therapies that can extend our healthspan by 10-15%. Below are some examples of the most recent discoveries in the area of medical and pharmacological treatments for aging - each have an array of ongoing trials in humans with results due over the coming 2-10 years:

1. Caloric Restriction - In all animal models, caloric restriction (CR), at the 20-30% level, extends lifespan. In humans, it has been difficult to keep people at a 20-30% caloric restriction for a long time.

2. Fasting - is a much lighter version than CR and easier to implement. Fasting has shown significant positive effects in multiple areas: cardiovascular, diabetes and cancer.

3. Metformin - is emerging as the most popular drug treatment on this list. Originally launched as a drug to treat diabetes, Metformin has shown protective effects against several age-related diseases in humans.

4. Rapamycin - is an mTOR inhibitor and one of the most promising “longevity” drugs. In animal models, rapamycin has been shown to extend the longevity of rats by 9-14% on average.

5. Hormone Therapy - has been used successfully to stimulate and compensate the effect of aging, and there are a few inexpensive and effective therapies depending on the hormonal makeup of each individual.

6. Glycolytic Inhibitors - have shown encouraging results in improving insulin sensitivity in dogs and rats and have extended the lifespan of rats.

7. NAD+ or Sirtuin Pathways Activators - Some of the most studied sirtuin activators are resveratrol and NAD+ There are more than 100 research studies in process in this area but so far not one single study has been able to support the “longevity” claim.

8. Senescent cells - accumulate during aging and secrete compounds that promote inflammation and cancer. Genetic and pharmacological strategies to target and kill senescent cells both enhance lifespan and improve health markers in mice with high levels of senescent cells.

9. Spermidine - is a key booster of autophagy and core substrate of any cell in our body. Spermidine in animal models is able to extend lifespan across flies, worms and mice. In humans, spermidine supplementation has also been correlated with lower blood pressure, improved immune response, improved cognitive parameters and lower inflammation.

10. SLGT2 Inhibitors and Acarbose - lower glucose and insulin peaks mimicking some of the positive effects of caloric restriction. Mice studies strongly support this conclusion with lifespan expansions of 10%-plus for males.

11. Trehalose - is a disaccharide that comprises two glucose molecules linked by a glycosidic bond. It is a nonreducing sugar that is not hydrolysed and does not interact with proteins or other molecules. Trehalose has attracted a lot of attention for its beneficial effects on animal models of neurodegenerative diseases.

12. Stem Cells - Most of the research has been focused on addressing diseases or structural (muscle, cartilage, bone) problems, in other words, repairing. However, as aging is characterised by a gradual decline in the ability of stems cells to regenerate and repair tissue and blood cells, the hypothesis is that injections of “induced pluripotent” cells or other type of “original” cells could counter the natural cellular decline and keep our body “young”. Their preventive use is still in its infancy but could be promising.

13. Reverse Thymic Involution - The thymus is the gland responsible for the production and maturation of T lymphocytes or T-cells, white blood cells. Marrow transplant or infusion of hematopoietic stem cells from a younger donor that can be used to regenerate T-cells and B-cells to a much “younger” stage of their “life.”

14. Parabiosis - consists in allowing older people to receive transfusions from younger and healthier individuals. While it works well for mice, the moral and ethical issues of applying the same treatment to humans are hard to overstate.

15. Cellular Reprogramming - Yamanaka factors can reverse age-related changes in vitro and in vivo. Median overall survival for wild mice treated with these 3 factors via injection was 109% longer. Human trials are probably five years away.

For more detail on genetic medicine, please see Genetic Medicine: The Path Toward Cures Vol. 5.

The Problem: Morgan Stanley estimates that the average novel therapy requires \$1B+ of funding and 10+ years of development to achieve approval. Despite the significant resources required to sustain drug development pipelines, probability-of-success (POS) remains low, with \~10% of programs entering clinical studies eventually obtaining FDA approval.

The Tufts Center for the Study of Drug Development (CSDD) estimates this figure to be even higher, at \~\$2.5B per marketed therapy when considering the additional costs of abandoned trials and clinical failures. Despite these significant costs, clinical development success rates for novel therapies are low. While estimates of these rates vary, they are consistently <10%. Additionally, it takes a novel drug an estimated 10.5 years on average to demonstrate the safety and efficacy necessary to support the submission of regulatory filings.

The Longshot: A key benefit of AI is to increase the potential viability of early-stage drug development; Our biotech team estimate that every \~1% improvement in preclinical development success could lead to an incremental \$15B+ in value generated over a 10- year period. Early data from AI-enabled drug development platforms is encouraging, with promising data points such as the ability to shorten the timeline to drug candidate identification by \~75% vs. the industry average. Additionally, initial data on the sector's ability to identify relevant targets and de-prioritize low viability projects early is suggestive of improvements in preclinical development that could be highly meaningful at scale. AI-enabled drug development is at an inflection point: our teams estimate a \$50B+ potential TAM. AI-enabled drug development platforms could be particularly useful for improving probability-of-success (POS) in early-stage development. They expect that a \~2% improvement in current preclinical and PhI development rates could result in the generation of an additional \~50 novel therapies over the next 10 years, which would translate to a \$50B+ NPV opportunity for the biopharma industry. Importantly, many of these companies also maintain economics on drugs in development, which allows them to share in the long-term success the technology could bring.

The Problem: The healthcare industry is only at the beginning of an S-Curve of technological revolution. AI in drug discovery and full, affordable genome sequencing were considered Longshots only a decade ago but have since come of age - particularly for the investment community. In spite of these technological advances, healthcare remains a relatively inefficient, costly (on a per capita basis) and wasteful industry by comparison to others which have embraced technology to modernise.

• To achieve healthy longevity – alongside or in the absence of advances in pluripotent stem cell therapies (discussed above) – it is likely that 3D bioprinting of skin grafts, implants or eventually organs will be needed for wholesale replacement of damaged tissues.

• To avoid the use of animals and rodents in testing - The technology allowing for bioprinted structures will enable researchers to study functions of the human body in vitro. 3D bioprinted structures are more biologically relevant compared to in vitro studies performed in 2D.

There are currently over 300,000 people in the US awaiting organ transplants, of which over 200,000 are awaiting a kidney. A lack of supply, lack of compatibility and silent rejection post-transplant are all major hurdles that bioprinting could help overcome.

The Longshot: 3D bioprinting is an additive manufacturing process that uses bioinks to print living cells developing structures layer-by-layer which imitate the behavior and structures of natural tissues. Bioinks, that are used as a material in bioprinting, are made of natural or synthetic biomaterials that can be mixed with living cells and then printed in layers or applied to biological scaffolding to speed up the process. Clinical settings such as 3D printed skin and bone grafts, implants and even full 3D printed organs are currently at the center of bioprinting research.

The latest academia on the subject suggests difficulties in achieving "biofunctional" material - in effect cells can be printed but they can't be used or integrated into living organisms without achieving biofunctional responses. If this can be overcome however, and comply with as yet unknown regulatory frameworks, the effects could be transformational for human health, those waiting for organ transplants and overall patient and government medical spending.

The Debate: The first steps in 3D printing were taken in the 1980s, when in 1984 Charles Hull filed a patent for the first commercial 3D printing technology. This has been a symbol of the birth of 3D printing, and it created the base for 3D bioprinting as well. Bioprinting came into picture in 1988, when Robert J. Klebe used an inkjet printer for printing cells. After these first steps, the field has constantly evolved, and new methods and techniques have been discovered.

Modern day 3D bioprinting is an additive manufacturing process that uses bioinks to print living cells developing structures layer-by-layer which imitate the behaviour and structures of natural tissues. Bioinks, that are used as a material in bioprinting, are made of natural or synthetic biomaterials that can be mixed with living cells.

Tissue/organ shortage is a major medical challenge due to donor scarcity and patient immune rejections. Furthermore, it is difficult to predict or mimic the human disease condition in animal models during preclinical studies because disease phenotype differs between humans and animals. 3D bioprinting is evolving into an unparalleled multidisciplinary technology for engineering 3D biological tissue with complex architecture and composition.

## Latest in Academia:

• Limited production of biofunctional tissue: The encapsulation of cells within gelphase materials to form bioinks offers distinct advantages for next-generation 3D bioprinting. 3D bioprinting has emerged as a promising tool for patterning cells, but the technology remains limited in its ability to produce biofunctional, tissuelike constructs due to a dearth of materials suitable for bioinks.

• Use cases emerging in skeletal muscle reconstruction: Skeletal muscles play important roles in critical body functions and their injury or disease can lead to limitation of mobility and loss of independence. Current treatments result in variable functional recovery, while reconstructive surgery is limited due to donor shortage, donor-site morbidity, and limited functional recovery. Skeletal muscle tissue engineering has recently emerged as an alternative solution for treatment of injured tissue and serves as a functional disease model. Bioprinting has emerged as a promising tool for recapitulating the complex and highly organized architecture of skeletal muscles at clinically relevant sizes.

A host of other studies on the space are ongoing. These include: stimuli-responsive biomaterials, structural formability, cell viability, bioink formulation strategies, bioprinting in a supporting bath, 4D bioprinting, and bioprinting new controlled drug delivery systems.

## What Hurdles Remain?:

## Advantages of 3D Bioprinting

• Allows mimicking of the real structure of desired tissue/ organ.

• Possibility to revolutionise future medical treatment capabilities.

• Possible creation of patient-specific and organ-specific treatments, Biocompatibility with human cells and tissues.

• Effects of drugs can be examined more accurately, which in turn would decrease the need for animal testing.

• Automating complex processes leading to consistency and fewer human errors.

## Disadvantages of 3D Bioprinting

• Pricing is prohibitive in many traditional 3D printing applications - which becomes more prohibitive still in the field of bioprinting where more variables must be taken into account.

• It is a substantially more complex process than printing plastic, metal or other more frequently used additive manufacturing techniques.

• Maintaining cell environment can be difficult.

• Ethical concerns which are mitigated by using fewer animals in the testing stage but the impact on human health and longevity remain concerns.

• Energy consumption in early stage prototypes is high.

The Regulation: It is still extremely early days in the development of this technology. In academic and policy discussion the main debating topic at this point is that technically bioprinting is a subcategory of 3D printing. However, clearly bioprinting will need substantially different policy considerations from a health and safety perspective than more generic 3D printing tools.

It is noteworthy that if the software and hardware set-ups for bioprinting are regarded as “medical devices” that fall within the auspices of new EU Medical Device Regulations (MDR), they will then need certification before being released on the market. Furthermore, the use of software in the process raises data protection issues.

Finally, since organs and tissues are not “original” body parts, are they to be regulated as “products”, medical devices, drugs, ATMP, or a new category? All of these considerations even before any regulation or public debate emerging over the ethical considerations associated with organ-printing-on-demand as it is colloquially becoming synonymous with.

## Current Real-World Development:

• Pharma: There have been proof of concepts which show the potential - such as this bioprinted organ which is shown to be breathing - but which remain many years away from achieving the complexity of full human anatomy. Bladder and Kidney scaffolds have also been built onto which bioink has been attached; less of a printed organ layer-by-layer but progress nonetheless.

While stem cell or organ transplants save countless lives, most recipients need to take immunosuppressive drugs for the rest of their lives, increasing their risk of infection and cancer. To solve this problem, researchers genetically modified donor cells, “cloaking” them to the recipient immune system so they aren’t rejected. They succeeded with mice, transplanting the modified donor cells and finding that they successfully integrated into the recipients’ cells without the need of immunosuppressive medication. “Our work paves the way for an ‘off-the-shelf’ supply of cells for therapies that could be safely given to many patients,” said the senior investigator. The research is still early-stage, but the applications are numerous and could have very positive impacts on patients if the technology is successfully matured.

And Long-term the panacea would be to find non-medical applications and create machine interfaces that are safe and powerful for the general population, so as to expand how we interact with each other, with the world, and with ourselves. This appears to be still some way from reality.

## Innovation & Innovators: Invasive vs Non-Invasive

## Invasive techniques

Neuralink (\$687.14m of funding): Micron-scale threads are inserted into areas of the brain that control movement. Each thread contains many electrodes and connects them to an implant - what Neuralink calls - the "Link". The company's aim is for at least two orders of magnitude more electrodes (i.e. communication channels) than current clinically-approved comparable devices. Ultimately, with a bluetooth connection, the wearer would theoretically be able to control any compatible wireless mouse or keyboard.

Synchron (\$139.87m of funding): The company's device - the "Stentrode" - is implanted within the motor cortex of the brain via the jugular vein in a minimally-invasive endovascular procedure. Once implanted, it detects and wirelessly transmits motor intent using a proprietary digital language to allow severely paralyzed patients to control personal devices with hands-free point-andclick.

Neurotech (\$49.74m of funding): The Neurotech devices measure just a few millimeters and are implanted into people’s brains in the area where doctors want to record activity. For a patient who struggles with hand movement, the BCI would be implanted in the pre-motor cortex to record activity there. Despite being less well known than Neuralink, Neurotech claims its technology is more advanced, having been implanted in 28 patients in the US, China and Europe as part of medical trials. Its tech has also been tested on primates and rodents.

## Non-invasive techniques

Battelle: (a not-for-profit) aims to develop a minutely invasive interface system that pairs an external transceiver with electromagnetic nanotransducers that are nonsurgically delivered to neurons of interest. The nanotransducers would convert electrical signals from the neurons into magnetic signals that can be recorded and processed by the external transceiver, and vice versa, to enable bidirectional communication.

Carnegie Mellon University: aims to develop a completely noninvasive device that uses an acousto-optical approach. The team will use ultrasound waves to guide light into and out of the brain to detect neural activity.

Johns Hopkins University: the approach is to develop a completely noninvasive, coherent optical system for recording from the brain. The system will directly measure optical pathlength changes in neural tissue that correlate with neural activity.

Rice University: is building a minutely invasive, bidirectional system for recording from and writing to the brain. For the recording function, the interface will use diffuse optical tomography to infer neural activity by measuring light scattering in neural tissue. To enable the write function, the team will use a magneto-genetic approach to make neurons sensitive to magnetic fields.

Teledyne Scientific & Imaging (Teledyne is covered by Kristine Liwag): aims to develop a completely noninvasive, integrated device that uses micro optically pumped magnetometers to detect small, localized magnetic fields that correlate with neural activity. The device will use focused ultrasound for writing to neurons.

Kernel (\$60.82m of funding): is building the next generation of brain measurement systems by leveraging the strengths of TDfNIRS into products that offer the following benefits: high-quality neural signal, full-head coverage, scalability, relatively low cost, natural environments, stimuli, and interactions, freedom of user motion.

Latest in Academia: As we have shown, academia typically leads patenting and VC activity by c3-5 years and public market implications by as much as a decade. The period between 2010-15 was dominated by academic research on the potential for restoring mobility and engagement with computing and mobile devices; patenting activity - and VC investment - is still very much focussed on this point. In the past 12-18 months however - and largely testament to an agreement in the scientific community that the technology works - academia has subsequently shifted towards ethical considerations, privacy concerns and even a wholesale re-thinking of what wearables, implantables, injectables and ingestables mean for the UN Declaration of Human Rights, which has barely changed since WW2.

What Hurdles Remain? (i) Scale of electrodes - need to be on the same scale as neurons in the brain, requiring major miniaturisation breakthroughs. (ii) Chips/Edge Compute - For latency and signal amplification reasons chips require an overhaul to deal with the microvolt input signals from the brain. (iii) Hermetic delivery - is required to avoid fluid and salt contamination from surrounding tissue. (iv) Scaling neurosurgery - the electrode threads are too fine to be inserted by hand. Neuralink is designing a surgical robot to achieve speed and precision of device insertion. (v) Neural decoding - algorithms are improving rapidly to decode brain signals and have bi-directional capabilities, ultimately the challenge is to develop constantly evolving algorithms as the user changes their brain usage over time. (vi) Ethics - this may be the largest headwind of all. In spite of (and because of) certain FDA rules which only allow human use for 30 day periods, the longterm effects are not known and there may be major ethical implications from inserting bidirectional technology inside patients' brains. The Problem: We do not have an ageing problem - by 2050, the UK alone will have >250,000 people aged 100+. However, a great challenge of the next 50 years will be the social-economic effects of ageing. As we age, our bodies become less efficient at repairing themselves. Through slowing or reversing ageing we can reduce other diseases to live longer, stay younger and healthier.

• From an outright cost perspective: The OECD predicts that across Europe population ageing will create a rise in age related social expenditures from \~19% of GDP in 2000 to \~26% by 2050. Ageing can be viewed as a disease, and there is growing recognition that ageing can be cured, treated, and prevented.

• From a social perspective: The most deprived in society spend >40% more time in poor elderly health than those from less deprived backgrounds. The implication is that dependency ratios (non-working population (0-14; 65+) / working population (15-64)) are not only worsening - but worsening at their fastest pace on record. In the absence of birth rates picking up materially, which seems unlikely, the race is on to find a Longshot for ageing.

The Longshot: Regenerative medicine involves extension therapies to halt or reverse the ageing process. Specialised cell therapies based on induced pluripotent stem cells are considered a front-runner Longshot technique that has shown promise in yeast and mice to-date - without adverse side effects such as cancers. This is different to CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing techniques but not mutually exclusive from them. The problem with the Longshot for ageing is the principal-agent issue.

Nonetheless, regenerative medicine has the potential to save hundreds of billions of dollars pa in direct healthcare spending. However, we note potential for unintended consequences from prolonging life. Innovations such as antibiotics that increased life expectancy also led to a rise in prevalence of other diseases such as cancer and immunology diseases. So while some longevity Longshots could seem game-changing, there are significant risks of medical, pension and insurance impacts.

CRISPR Therapeutics covered by Terence Flynn is a leading company in this field. For more detail on genetic medicine, please see Genetic Medicine: The Path Toward Cures Vol. 5.

The Debate: People living 105+ years appear to have a genetic makeup that allows them to repair their DNA better than most people, according to a new study. The researchers compared the genomes of 81 people aged 105 or older with those of 36 healthy people with an average age of 68. The researchers found five common genetic alterations to the genes COA1 and STK17A that were prevalent in the 105+ group. STK17A is involved in cells destroying themselves in response to DNA damage or high reactive oxygen species levels. COA1 mediates communication between the nucleus and the mitochondria. The researchers also found that the 105+ people had fewer mutations in six of the seven genes they examined. The researchers therefore concluded that efficient DNA repair and low mutational burden are key contributors to these individuals' longevity.

## Latest in Academia:

The Innovations: Regenerative Medicine focusses on the generation and use of therapeutic stem cells, tissue engineering and the production of artificial organs. While these clearly apply to cohorts of all ages, there is a growing trend to applications in the sub-theme of age-retardation. This has bioethical considerations for regulators and investors. However, a growing number of public and private companies are exploring the extent to which this can reach scale at palatable cost for patients, corporates and investors. This is not a new discipline and has seen competition from companies including Stryker, Medtronic, Organogenesis as well as AstraZeneca in Cell Therapies in Europe. This field received recent plaudits after a blind patient regained partial sight through a breakthrough light-sensing algal protein gene therapy. The array of possibilities is growing rapidly. It is possible, for example, to take skin cells and use the resulting stem cells to rebuild parts of the body. Parts of the eye, heart and leg muscle have been built in this way. Indeed, these cells can even be inserted into a pig to grow a human heart and be transferred to a human. These treatments have been shown to carry heightened cancer risk, for example, as well as regulatory complexities but they hold the potential for a number of ageing population applications.

• Cell and gene therapies: entail the use of genes and cells to treat certain illnesses. In gene therapy, genetic material is used to treat or replace defective genes in order to correct for genetic disorders. Cell therapy uses stem cells removed from a patient or a donor as a treatment for that patient. Cell and gene therapies can be a direct solution to aging as the therapies can directly target the hallmarks of aging such as the accumulation of ‘zombie’ cells and the prevention of telomere damage. Intranasal/injectable gene therapies targeting telomere damage in mice studies have shown a 41% median lifespan increase implying positive applications to humans in the longevity space.

• Senotherapeutics: vaccines have been developed which eliminate senescent ‘zombie’ cells – one of the hallmarks of ageing. Researchers at Juntendo University in 2021 identified the protein Gpnmb found in senescent cells and created a peptide vaccine against the protein to allow the immune system to remove the dysfunctional cells safely and selectively. The vaccine removed senescent cells and prolonged the lifespan of the mice.

• Cellular reprogramming: In 2016 the Salk Institute increased the lifespan of mice by 40% using a mixture of four reprogramming molecules or “Yamanaka factors” which reversed epigenetic changes that occur in cells during aging. Critically, they were able to make aged human skin cells and brain cells younger in the lab. The challenge with this technique is achieving these results without accompanying rises in cancer prevalence.

• Plasma-based therapeutics: Chronokines that rejuvenate can be found in blood plasma. Plasma-based therapeutics have the potential to directly address several ageing mechanisms including a range of acute and chronic pathologies

• CRISPR: Neuro-degenerative disorders, cancers, metabolic and inflammatory diseases are some of the most prevalent age-related pathologies affecting the growing population. Although still in its infancy, the CRISPR-Cas9 system has become a potent gene-editing tool capable of correcting gene-mediated agerelated pathology, and therefore ameliorating or eliminating disease symptoms. Deleting target genes using the CRISPR-Cas9 system or correcting for gene mutations may mitigate against many different neuro-degenerative disorders detected in the aging population.

• 3D printing: 3D printing can be used to make implants and replacements for all sorts of body parts, such as the jaw, pelvis, trachea, spine, hip, vertebrae, and skull. 3D printed healthcare applications are not meant to be age-specific, but are frequently used by older adults. Another healthcare industry currently affected by 3D printing is the field of dentistry. Many elderly people use dentures, and using 3D printing to fabricate them can help bring materials costs down.

## The Innovators:

As shown below, the field of anti-ageing is not new, simple, or cheap. Ageing treatments begin with dieting as a DIY tool for reversing ageing effects and extending healthy life expectancy. Medical intervention then becomes significantly more complex thereafter. Drug development both with and without the use of AI in the drug discovery process is already well established in the large-cap pharma space.

The real Longshot here is regenerative medicines and cellular therapies. The longer-term hope is that these techniques can not only extend life and slow the debilitating effects of ageing, but reverse the process of ageing itself.

The Regulation: Testing will be rigorous as this technology progresses beyond mice and towards human trials. In the UK alone, the relevant authorities which will be scrutinising the process and any adverse side effects (particularly cancers caused by gene editing) are as follows:

• Health Research Authority (HRA) have a remit to provide an ethics opinion on clinical trials. Those involving gene therapy regenerative medicines are reviewed through the Gene Therapy Advisory Committee (GTAC).

• Human Fertilisation and Embryology Authority (HFEA) regulates the use of human embryos or human admixed (human-animal) embryos to derive stem cells for use in the treatment of patients.

• Human Tissue Authority (HTA) has a remit to regulate organisations that remove, store and use human tissue or cells; this includes where they are used as starting materials for Advanced Therapy Medicinal Products (ATMPs). Under the European Union Tissues and Cells Directives (EUTCD), it licenses establishments that remove, test, process, store, and distribute tissues or cells that will (or may) be used to treat patients.

• Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) are responsible for granting the appropriate authorisation for the manufacturing site of Advanced Therapy Medicinal Products (ATMPs). In the area of clinical trials, the MHRA’s remit includes assessment of applications for clinical trial authorisation and the associated manufacturer’s licence for investigational ATMPs.

## Real World Developments

• In 2010, Harvard University managed to increase blood flow in the brain of old mice, effectively rejuvenating neurovascular pathways.

• In 2019, signs of ageing were delayed in elderly mice by using a single protein (eNAMPT) found in abundant supply in the blood of younger mice but which tends to be depleted with age. The same process happens with people which led the authors of a Washington University study (in Cell Metabolism) to determine the same could theoretically be achieved in humans. All mice receiving saline solution died within 881 days. For those receiving eNAMPT, a handful of mice surpassed the-1000 day mark.

• In 2022, researchers at the Salk Institute claimed similarly to have reversed the signs of ageing by using reprogramming molecules - known as Yamanaka factors - in the cells of mice. Importantly though, and in contrast to studies which pre-date this one, the incidence of cancer or abnormal side effects did not increase beyond control group levels. The treated animals resembled younger versions of themselves with improved kidney function and with the ability of skin cells to proliferate more easily and thus heal scarring and other age-related tissue problems.

The Mass Market Time Horizon: Given the extremely high cost of these technologies at the moment, mass market adoption remains a distant prospect. However, it is this mass addressable market which we expect governments to prioritise in time. One of the biggest challenges of ageing for governments is the healthcare costs associated with low quality or disabled life extension. People living in the most deprived areas of even developed countries like the UK achieve only 50 years of healthy living and 20 years of poor health quality life. Those in the least deprived areas achieve c10 years longer life anyway, with 70 lived healthily on average and only c10 lived in poor health. Regenerative medicine has the potential to save governments hundreds of billions of dollars per year in spending. Moreover it presents a prospect of social "levelling up" in developed and developing nations alike. This is under the major assumptions that (1) treatments are successful and approved and (2) these treatments trickle down to mass market.

The Problem: Globally, there are >650 million people who suffer from obesity. There are >100 million obese adults in the US and \~80 million overweight adults. The Longshot: GLP-1 agonists are medicines used to treat type 2 diabetes. They mimic (copy) the action of a hormone (chemical substance) called GLP-1. Your stomach naturally releases this hormone when you eat food. They (1) help your body to make more insulin (the hormone that controls the amount of sugar in your blood) when needed; (2) they reduce the amount of sugar (glucose) that your liver makes; (3) they slow down the digestion of food, so that it takes longer for your body to absorb (take in) the sugar from meals; (4) they can reduce your appetite. These drugs have been on the market since 2010, but it has taken until 2023 for the theme to come to life. A number of recent trials have found significant improvements in co-morbidities from taking these drugs (including stroke, cardiovascular and kidney health). The uptake of obesity medicines in the US over the past 12 months has been stronger than we had anticipated, whilst indirectly supporting a profound acceleration in the use of GLP-1 medicines in diabetes. Our supplydemand modelling now points to a \$105bn global market for obesity medication by 2030 and over \$140bn in a bull case. Supply is the gating factor, while evidence of benefits in associated health conditions could turbocharge demand, with implications for longevity and healthcare spend.

The Problem: Robotics has proven to be a fantastically powerful driver of productivity, both hardware and software robotics. When talking about physical robotics though, the major issue is that traditional robotics, when taken outside their programmed environment, cannot function. There are two causes for this: (1) They cannot dynamically learn new surroundings and skills to operate in those surroundings in real time – yet; (2) they are too rigid with arms, pivots, axes and actuators determining their movement which are incompatible in most places outside static and predictable (predominantly automotive and manufacturing) facilities. Essentially, what makes robots extraordinarily capable and effective in mass manufacturing are the same properties which undermine their usefulness and are in fact often dangerous in the real world.

Telerobotic surgery is already proving to be a game-changer by bringing precision to operating theatres, and even guided from remote locations. The next era of robotics could theoretically involve robots so small that they can be injected through even the smallest hypodermic syringes - something that has already been achieved - and then be instructed to perform tasks inside human bodies via remote instruction or which they are pre-programmed to undertake.

The Longshot: Cornell and Pennsylvania universities and MIT – amongst others – have announced plans to research remote-controlled micro-robots for use in surgical procedures. Still very much in academic exploration phase, these micro- and soft-robotics breakthroughs are typically based on insect evolution to achieve the ideal balance of robustness and agility. Advances in semiconductor technology mean that miniaturisation is not the bottleneck, nor is movement which can be achieved with air or voltage applied to micro-actuators. Rather, the bottleneck is in remote control and function. Time will tell if science can achieve use cases for these micro-smartphones with legs.

More recently still, scientists have created synthetic lifeforms designed by computers known as Xenobots. A ‘two-legged xenobot’ was able to show non-random, directional movement over a surface in 2020. Scientists subsequently, and unexpectedly, witnessed spontaneous reproduction of these nano-robots

Of any of the Longshots discussed in this report, fully functioning micro and/or soft robotics may be the furthest out from becoming a reality. However, from a market-fit perspective, innovation in this field could have myriad positive effects on personal health and society at large. From digesting water pollution to real-time monitoring of bodily functions and disaster recovery to remote inspections, the potential is theoretically enormous.

The Debate: Most of the technology needed to create miniature and soft robotics already exist. The semiconductor industry has achieved miniaturisation at a scale and rate once thought unimaginable. It is now possible to put 1 million transistors into the space occupied by a single celled paramecium. Sensors and LEDs can now also be built on a scale that cannot be seen by the human eye.

The major problem is that traditional robotics, when taken outside their programmed environment, cannot function. There are two causes of this: (1) They cannot dynamically learn new surroundings and skills to operate in those surroundings quickly; (2) they are too rigid with arms, pivots, axes and actuators determining their movement which are incompatible in most places outside automotive manufacturing facilities. Essentially, what makes robots extraordinarily capable and effective in mass manufacturing are the same properties which make them useless and dangerous in the real world.

## Latest in Academia:

• Micro-robotics research led by Cornell and Pennsylvania universities: Optical Wireless Integrated Circuits (OWICs) are about 100nm in width, 10,000 times smaller than a smartphones but aspiring to be able to perform some of the same functions on a cellular level. The devices contain solar cells for power and transistors and LEDs. About 1 million OWICs can be made in mass parallel on a single 4 inch wafer. And in the same way smartphones have apps, these OWICs can be used to measure voltage or temperature or simply have lighting for communications purposes. Each OWIC costs less than one penny to make. They can be used for secure smart tagging, as invisible IoT sensors or even for neural interfacing; involving placing these devices inside the brain to listen to individual neurons, further driving forward innovation in Brain-Computer Interfaces discussed elsewhere in this report. Actuators are similarly being built in parallel to create movement for the brains of the micro-robot when a voltage is applied to the device. These robots are so small they can be injected through the thinnest hypodermic needle, opening up the potential to be injected into humans or animals.

• UC Berkeley cockroach replication – Dynamic Autonomous Sprawled Hexapod (DASH), which can be assembled in 15 minutes and emulate the movement and robustness of cockroaches. ## What Hurdles Remain?:

• Movement: If we look at the hurdles precluding micro-robotics from being useful in the real world, it is generally mobility rather than micro-compute (which as already indicated has reached a scale that could be used in micro-robots imperceptible to the human eye). The best way to achieve this mobility appears to be through copying the form of insects moving over rough terrain. Their bodies

have evolved to use a combination of hard and soft mechanisms, which in microrobotics can be broadly replicated through the use of silicon and rubber. This technology has been shown to propel a 4mm robot as high as 40cm; 100x its own height. The potential, therefore, for this technology inside and outside the body is theoretically immense given that almost nothing else on earth – and certainly nothing man-made – is able to achieve such power-to-weight propulsion.

• Motors and magnetics: 10 body lengths per second can be achieved and 3D printed versions have been made which can climb over uneven surfaces. Within those 3D printed robots it is possible to integrate computing power which makes movement controllable from outside power sources.

• Soft Robotics: The aim is not to make robots with zero error rates - we already have hardware and software robotics which can achieve this. The newest field of robotic study aims to build robotics which are able to face unpredictable scenarios in the real world.

The first step towards this is to create robots with compliant bodies – structures which can undergo major deformations without damage.

The second step is to integrate in the body structure distributed actuation which replaces stiff structures and actuators.

Current Real-World Development: Micro-robotics for human health or humanitarian purposes is the real Longshot. Consequently, it is too early for such micro-robotics to have made meaningful real-world headway in some of the theoretical examples above. However, the field of soft robotics - in our view a precursor technology to many of the breakthroughs needed to make useful micro-robotics - is already showing real-world promise.

• Soft camera robots have already been used in surgery. These differ from the rigidity of an endoscope and can move thanks to the flexibility of the module using air pressure and small voltages to create mailable robotic movement in confined spaces controlled by doctors. It allows surgeons to see what other instruments are doing within a human body from a number of different points of view.

• In Stanford University, a team are pioneering a study in soft robotic needles. The flexibility of a needle within a body is allowing doctors to reach tumours in challenging areas of the body and thus be much more precise in their treatment of ailments in solid organs using only one insertion point. Furthermore, it allows doctors to bend around structures which sit in the way of the target, materially reducing complications of surgeries.

The Problem: Despite its origins in the 1960s and many shortfalls, traditional chemotherapy still accounts for over 37% of cancer prescriptions for solid tumours. The Longshot: Replacing traditional chemotherapy with smart chemotherapy. In the early 1900s, German chemist Paul Ehrlich pioneered the use of chemicals to treat disease, known today as chemotherapy. His ultimate goal was to discover chemicals that would kill cancer cells whilst sparing healthy tissues, so-called "magic bullets". However, it has taken >90 years and the marriage of biology and chemistry to realise this early vision. "Smart chemotherapy" is the idea of using an antibody to act as a guidance system to find a cancer, and attaching chemotherapy molecules to the antibody to destroy the cancer - a concept called an Antibody Drug Conjugate (ADC). The first ADC drug, called Mylotarg, was approved for a form of leukemia in 2000. However, it has taken 10-20 years to perfect the construction of the "biological missile" to better enable it to target common solid tumours which are more complex and difficult to infiltrate into.

AstraZeneca, covered by Mark Purcell, is a leading company in this field

Latest in Academia: Whilst the concept of ADC drugs acting like "guided biological missiles" to specifically target a tumour is relatively simple to understand, these medicines are extremely complex to construct. The process by which the cytotoxic payload is connected to the antibody via the linker is important for the successful construction of ADCs – this connection process is called conjugation and is critical to achieving better stability and less off-target toxicity. The complexity of ADC medicines provides protection against copycat generics, with the advent of new branded competition the more relevant long-term risk to revenues in our view. In this report, we explore advances in conjugation, the challenges of toxicity and ADC drug resistance, and the importance of patient selection, which we believe will become increasingly important going forward as the ADC field becomes increasingly crowded.

What hurdles remain: Beyond regulations and trials which make this a slower developing theme, there are a number of challenges with ADCs from achieving the optimal therapeutic window to their manufacturing:

• Whilst the concept of ADC drugs acting like "guided biological missiles" to specifically target a tumour is relatively simple to understand, the potential for substantial differences in each of the component parts – (1) the targeting antibody; (2) the cytotoxic payload; (3) the stability of the linker; (4) the overall pharmacokinetic/dynamic properties of the construct – means that the clinical performance of each ADC drug can be very different. This is further complicated by the multiple mechanisms of action seen with ADC drugs and the potential for unexpected toxicities, unrelated to the intended mechanism of action, to impact tolerability.
