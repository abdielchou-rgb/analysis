## APAC Economic Perspectives Asia by the Numbers (April 2024)

## Economics

## Asia

## China growth upgrade on better Q1 data

GDP growth beat expectations in Q1 at 5.3% y/y, which, together with expectations of stronger external demand, prompted us to revise up our GDP growth forecast for 2024E to 4.9% (Prev: 4.6%). However, we downgraded China's nominal GDP growth forecast from 5.1% to 4.9%, as inflation is likely to be weaker than previously envisaged. Stronger-than-expected Q1 growth may reduce the likelihood of policy rate cuts, even as we think LPR cut is still possible and implementation of government bond issuance is likely to accelerate.

## A rather uneven export trend across Asia

Evident from Korea's and Taiwan's export trends, demand for AI-related products remains strong. Korea's first 20 working-day average export growth in April accelerated to 11.1% y/y, with semiconductor exports up 43% y/y. Taiwan's exports were up 19.3% y/y in March, with shipments of data-processing machines (AI servers, mainly) more than quadrupling on a y/y basis. Trends were mixed in ASEAN. Whilst Malaysia's E&E exports rose more than 24% m/m in March (-1.5% y/y), electronics NODX and IP trends remain volatile in Singapore (declining in March after a February surge). Vietnam's April exports were up 11% y/y (15% y/y ytd), albeit slowing slightly from March. Exports of non-tech goods (e.g., commodities in Indonesia/Malaysia) were broadly resilient.

## Weaker currencies amidst higher oil and sticky US inflation

Amid a resilient US economy and sticky inflation, markets priced out Fed rate cuts further. Geopolitical tensions worsened, raising upside risk to oil prices. As most of Asia are net oil importers, higher oil prices worsens the region's terms of trade (Figure 1). The inflation impact is lagged—faster for Philippines and Singapore (small fuel CPI weight), but tempered by fiscal interventions in Malaysia, Indonesia, and Thailand and India to some extent (more here). India's buoyant services exports and resilient remittance inflow have built a buffer against global oil price volatility (more here). Alongside these increased headwinds to currency appreciation, we adjusted Asia FX forecasts this month (Figure 2). BSP, BoT, MAS and RBI left policy rates unchanged this month, while BI implemented a hawkish hike to defend the rupiah. Should the Fed not ease this year, we think the scope for rate cuts in the region would be limited.

## Gross Domestic Product (GDP)

GDP is the broadest measure of economic activity. It is strongly correlated with the profit cycle and provides important clues about inflation and the direction of policy. Strong GDP often suggests policy will tighten; weak GDP implies a policy bias for pro-growth. Shifts in macro policies affect markets.

## Investment

High savings and investment ratios have long been the key ingredient to high growth in Asia. The investment to GDP ratio averages around 37% for the region including China, and 27% of GDP for the region ex-China. Investment is a key driver of employment and income growth. It can have a larger impact on the economic cycle because it is volatile.

## BOP, FDI & Foreign Reserves

Foreign reserves are an important macro prudential indicator and in Asia a key source of base money. All Asian economies have large foreign reserves relative to short-term foreign liabilities. This provides a cushion against external financial shocks. Regarding money supply, the change in foreign reserves equals the change in central bank net foreign assets, which makes up the largest portion of base money or primary source of liquidity. Thus rising foreign reserves necessarily means upward pressure on domestic liquidity conditions. This typically results in foreign exchange rate intervention, which must then be sterilized. The pressures on domestic liquidity conditions tighten when foreign reserves fall. Big changes in foreign reserves also demonstrate the degree of pressure on currencies to adjust.

## Exchange Rate

Exchange rates affect inflation and trade. However, one of the reasons to look at exchange rates in Asia is because most central banks like to "lean against the wind" and in doing so affect domestic liquidity conditions. Many Asian central banks buy US dollars (increasing foreign reserves) when their currencies are under pressure to appreciate and this causes the domestic money supply to expand, which must then be sterilized, usually with central bank debt or by adjusting reserve requirement ratios on deposits as in China. The process flows in the other direction when currencies are under pressure to depreciate.
