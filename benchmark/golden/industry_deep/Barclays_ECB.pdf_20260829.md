# Grinding into the ECB

A quiet macro tape and high IG supply has left Main tight again to €IG cash. The ECB could serve as a catalyst for further IG tightening, while Main, amid extended long-risk investor positioning, could be sensitive to rate volatility from the US: we stay long IG cash, and see value in hedging with Main.

Away from headlines of an earlier-than-expected UK election, there was little macro news to drive price action. Fittingly, credit spreads were lacking direction, with €IG spreads efectively unchanged over the week.

IG supply remained fairly high, at €17.5bn week to Wednesday, vs €27.1bn the prior week. The fact that supply can remain fairly high without causing any weakness in IG is once against a testament to the demand strength in IG (and, for the cynics, a reflection of a very welltelegraphed pipeline).

HY supply, in contrast, remains in the doldrums: after a busy first week of May, the past week saw only €1.3bn of supply, lower than even the slow prior two weeks. The fact that HY has not outperformed IG, in spite of this supply diferential, also speaks to the relative strength of demand in IG over HY.

IG supply was tilted strongly to non-financials, with 71% of total supply. Fittingly, we have seen €IG financials make tighter tights ( Figure 1), while non-financials remain of the tights. On a 3yr horizon, €IG financials are now efectively in line with non-financials, but we remain OW financials regardless. However, as we discuss in the IG section, we see much of the upside in financials as a "bank premium" rather than a curve or rating premium: we have a strong up-inquality preference in banks.

The IG supply, and the quiet macro tape, was felt in IG CDS vs cash, with Main back to being notably tight to €IG cash ( Figure 2). On a 3yr horizon, Main is 2.5bp of the tights, while €IG cash is 27bp of the tights over the same horizon.

In our view, many factors support a preference for owning IG cash hedged with Main: Main is tight to cash, demand technicals remain strong in cash, there has been a meaningful reloading of long-risk positions in Main, and the proximate driver of volatility remains US rates, which bleeds into Europe strongly in derivatives (see also Dissecting realized vol in Main and CDX IG).

The UK election, at a macro level, could pose upside risk to growth, on the back of a deeper relationship with the EU. In turn, this could also help unwind some of the "Brexit premium" in the currency. On the credit side, we see few immediate catalysts to direct investor perspectives on UK credits, beyond a handful of individual issuers. As such, we see little premium in £-IG versus €-IG. A general uncertainty premium may build, but we would not expect underperformance greater than single-digit-basis-points unless the Labour manifesto produces bigger surprises.

With four weeks until the June ECB meeting, where the Governing Council is expect to cut its main policy rates, €-IG continues to trade in a tight range - aligned with our thoughts for Q2 ( Figure 1). Our thesis remains that the start of the ECB cutting cycle will unlock the final leg of spread tightening, via two channels. First, we expect some near-term FOMO as investors seek to "lock in" current yield levels, driving up demand for fixed-income duration versus cash and bank savings products. At the same time, a steeper corporate yield curve should, finally, allow 2s5s spread curves to flatten without inverting IG yields. Corporate yield curves remain remarkably flat and hence there is little room for credit curves to tighten until rates curves steepen ( Figure 2).

One risk to our view is that, as confidence around a June cut builds, the market could begin to price in some of the rally even before the ECB meeting. Indeed, while the overall level of index spreads has not tightened excessively, remaining above 100bp for now, under the hood we see that curve flattening has already begun.

In particular, we note that flattening momentum in 2s5s has emerged across the various segments of €-IG ( Figure 3). Since November, these curves are -10bp/-12bp/-10bp/-17bp across Non-Cyclical/Cyclical/Fin Snr/Bank T3, respectively. This is a significant retracement from the wides of last year, but for the non-financials there is still a further 10-12bp to go versus their 2021 averages. Financial curves, on the other hand, are already at their 2021 averages implying that they will struggle to flatten further in aggregate - keeping with our prior view that much of the spread upside in financials remains a "bank premium" rather than a curve or rating premium.

Looking at curves more holistically, we note that 2s5s10s have also come down over the past six months - particularly for Bank T3. However, in contrast to 2s5s, both Financial and Non-Financial butterflies remain well above their 2021 averages and the 5-7y part of the curve remains the sweet spot for spreads. Combined with the observation that Financials 2s5s are not particularly steep, a further implication is that Fins 5s10s remain too flat - indeed our model curves remain flat to near-inverted for this part of the index.

# Digging into €-\$ cross-currency

There is currently little to separate yields in €HY and \$HY after taking account of hedging costs. The convergence is partly a hedging cost function, but also due to rate dynamics in the US and Europe. Nevertheless, we still see opportunities for switches at a single-name level and highlight interesting trades.

There has been little to distinguish the performances of €HY and \$HY going back to the start of 2023 – €HY has delivered a cumulative total return of +14.8% while \$HY has returned +15.3%. However, €HY has gradually lost its valuation advantage relative to \$HY ( Figure 1).

Converting \$HY yields into €s using three-month FX forwards, the pickup in favour of €Bs vs \$Bs is just 55bp, compared to a hedged yield advantage of c.200bp at the start of 2023 and 100bp 12 months ago. For BBs, \$ bonds currently hold the better value (out-yielding €s by 16bp), but also with a large decline in pickup vs €BBs from c.150bp at the start of 2023 and slightly less than 100bp 12 months ago.

FIGURE 1. There is very little to separate €HY and \$HY after taking into accounting hedging costs

![](images/7c142f40f90313c30c54c061e512619a40efad55084a88f85913973c3141f981.jpg)
\$HY hedged to €HY using three-month FX forwards Source: Barclays Research, Bloomberg

The trend has been consistent for much of 2024 with a lot of the return to parity coming in the second half of last year. When we dissect the components, the trend is not explained by spreads: as Figure 2 shows, the spread diferential – albeit nearer the upper end of the range since 2023 today – has oscillated in a +/-40bp range for all of this period.

Hedging costs, however, are part of the explanation. The cost to hedge \$s to €s has not changed much this year, but it has reduced meaningfully since late-2022, from as high as 300bp to close to 160bp today ( Figure 3). In other words, the cost to hedge \$s has gotten increasingly less punitive.

Given this isn't a spread story, it can be largely explained by what is happening in rates, principally by them having been a lot sticker in the US than in Europe. Indeed, there are less than two cuts priced in for the Fed by December from as many as seven at the peaks in the first quarter ( Figure 5). While we've seen a similar trend for the ECB, a policy gap has developed with close to three cuts currently priced in by December (indeed Barclays economists expect four from the Fed versus only one from the Fed).

Then there is the growth dynamic ( Figure 6). There is little doubt that growth of the US economy has been impressive in recent quarters, at a run rate of 3.0% y/y for each of the last two quarters, while the euro area economy has barely grown at all during that period. However, the consensus based on economists' forecasts is for US and euro area growth to begin converging in the final quarter of this year and for the two economies to efectively grow at similar rates through 2025. Net-net, we think the macro environment looks relatively more attractive from a starting point today in Europe, and arguably the sources of macro volatility are more likely to be US-centric as the market continues to grapple with the magnitude of Fed easing.

## European CLOs A long wait

Call activity in 2024 has been higher than any other year in the CLO 2.0 era. We estimate 18 deals have been called YTD (totalling c.€4.9bn). But that represents just 7.2% of the volume out of reinvestment at the start of the year – far lower than in 2021. For non-static deals, the wait for a call is 2.8 years post RP.

30% (or €69bn) of the CLO 2.0 universe is currently out of their reinvestment periods (RP), with about 20% (or €47bn) of the market having been out of RP for longer than 12 months. Hence, we expect there to be an increase in calls, and a step up in amortisations. We now estimate that €85.6bn of CLO 2.0 will be out of RP by the end of 2024, down from the €98.5bn we estimated in September 2023 ( European CLOs: An amortising market, 29 September 2023). This suggests that c.€13bn has been paid down, either through amortisation or through calls (or even a rare reset).

We estimate that 18 deals have been called so far this year – the highest number recorded in the CLO 2.0 era (deals issued after the Global Financial Crisis). The previous record was in 2021 with 17 deals called in the full year. Defining deals that have been called is rather nuanced. We look at the CLO 2.0 universe and assume a deal whose balance fell to below €50mn in any given year was called in that year and deferred to the call date, as that specified in Kanerai/Intex data. Where the call date was unavailable, we use the payment date of the paydown. We have also included deals that were called according to Kanerai but were yet to materialise (except where they were replaced with resets).

We further estimate call volume based on the balance of the called deals at the previous yearend. For the full year 2021, €5.8bn was called which represented 3.2% of the then outstanding market. In 2024, we estimate €4.7bn has already been called year-to-date, but given the growth of the CLO market since 2021, this represents 2% of the market. Even so, this suggests that call activity in 2024 looks set to be considerably higher than in 2021, both in absolute terms and as a share of the market.

Another way to look at call volumes is vs the amount of deals out of RP, which should theoretically be more inclined to be called. With €65bn out of RP at the start of 2024, YTD call volumes represent 7.2% of that figure. If we also include the deals that have exited RP so far this year, the percentage falls further to 6.3%. However, in 2021, deals out of RP at the start of the year totalled €12bn, making call volumes nearly half of that figure, albeit this falls to c.22% if we include deals out of RP by the end of 2021. We caution that these numbers are estimations since we are looking back in history and actual call dates may difer, but it is clear that although call volumes in 2024 are up, they are actually down in terms of share of the deals out of RP.

When we adjust the deals for those that had been paying down in previous years (and hence, considered amortising rather than called) as well as those that are static, the number of calls falls to 14 YTD, totalling €3.6bn.

Amortisation, however, has picked up pace ( CLOs: Improving amortisation, 26 January 2024). The CLO market has grown by just c.€4bn since the end of last year, even though €13bn <sup>1</sup> of new issue has closed, suggesting at least €9bn of paydown (including calls). Of the 48 deals that have been called in the past five years (including six static deals), only nine deals had a AAA factor of 1.0 before being called. On the other hand, 14 deals had either paid down AAAs or factored down to below 20%.

As we have flagged in previous reports, the deals coming out of their reinvestment period are largely those that were issued or reset in 2018/19 given the RPs are typical four to five years. Some of these deals further refied select tranches in in 2021. During these periods, AAA spreads were under 100bp, at very tight levels compared to the current primary spreads of 145-150bp, in spite of the strong rally this year. Tighter spreads over those two periods largely reflect the strong credit market tightening at the time, but the spread levels were also supported by the embedded value in the Euribor floor at 0% in the then prevailing negative rates environment.

Therefore, those deals coming out of RP are not incentivised to reset in the current environment. Hence, those deals are only called once they have suficiently paid down.

As a case in point, CAIRNC4 is among the deals called in 2024. The deal was originally priced in November 2014 at AAA spread of 3mE+125bp, with an RP until January 2019. It was refinanced in April 2017 with AAA spread of 3mE+90bp. It was further reset in April 2019 with a AAA spread of 3mE+93bp and extended RP until April 2021. It was yet again refinanced in February 2021 achieving a AAA spread of 3mE+60bp near the end of its RP owing to its shorter life.  It was eventually called in May 2024, three years post reinvestment period, having been outstanding for nearly a decade. Excluding static deals, the median time to call for deals called in 2024 has been 2.8 years post reinvestment, while the average was 2.7 years post RP. There were two deals issued in 2022 with AAA spreads of 200bp over 3mE which were called one year from issue, due to the market rally. In contrast, the deals called in 2021 (excluding static deals), were called at median 1.3 years post RP and average of 0.7 year post RP.

We conclude that the increase in call activity this year is a reflection of the volume of deals out of RP and their amortisation, and not a direct response to the strong market rally. We continue to expect more deals to be called this year as they reach the three-year point from the end of RP. However, we do not expect the time to call to reduce significantly as a result of the market rally (that is to say we do not think deals are incentivised to call earlier). We acknowledge that the rally has led to unprecedented new CLO creation which in turn has supported loan pricing and is allowing call activity on the back of strong portfolio valuations – but we do not see this as the main reason behind increased call activity.

## Top cheap and rich names

We screen for cheap and rich names based on an algorithmic approach. For details on the methodological framework, please refer to Systematic single-name screens – review, 8 February 2019.

Our screen for cheap names consists of a ranking based on 50% of the "spread momentum" metric (using a 375-day look-back period). It also uses 50% of the "stock price moving average" metric (using 20- and 100-day look-back periods). The first metric picks names where the current spread is above the average spread when normalising for realised volatility, while the second metric picks names that have seen improvement in their equity price.

Our screen for rich names consists of a ranking based on 20% of the "spread momentum" metric (using a 375-day look-back period). It also uses 80% of the "stock price moving average" metric (using 20- and 100-day look-back periods). The first metric picks names where the current spread is below the average spread when normalising for realised volatility, while the second metric picks names that have not seen improvement in their equity price.
