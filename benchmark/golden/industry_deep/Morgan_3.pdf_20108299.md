# Morgan StanleyRESEArCH May 19 European Economics Mid-Year Outlook | Europe Reaching (Low) Potential

We expect a recovery of growth in 2024 and 2025 on the back of stronger consumption and exports. Inflation is on its way to target, even though services inflation should remain sticky. The ECB should start cutting rates in June (25bp) for a total of 75bp cuts in 2024 and 100bp in 2025.

Growth | Recovering: Euro area growth has bottomed last year and should reach 0.7%Y in 2024 and 1.2%Y in 2025. Private consumption is strengthening on the back of real income gains. Exports should pick up as global trade rebounds. Fiscal consolidation should be a mild drag on growth in 2024-25. Potential growth remains low, held down by structural challenges (demographics, productivity, energy transition).

Inflation | Target in sight: Inflation has declined notably from its peak of 10.6%Y in October 2022, reaching 2.4%Y in April 2024. Disinflation should continue in 2024 (2.4%Y) and 2025 (2.1%Y), albeit at a slower speed. The key factor behind the sluggish decline of core inflation is robust momentum in labour-intensive services. Wage growth is likely to have peaked in 2023 and services inflation should continue to recede. The path for headline should be affected by the bumpy profile for energy inflation, but it should be around the ECB’s target from 1Q25 onwards.

Monetary policy | First cut in June: With clear hints of an imminent cut, we expect the ECB to lower rates by 25bp in June. The ECB has not committed to a particular rate path afterwards, and we anticipate a gradual approach as it will have to observe whether developments in wages, profits and productivity support the inflation outlook and hence further cuts. This leads us to expect cuts only at projection meetings, and we forecast three cuts in 2024 (total 75bp) and four cuts in 2025 (total 100bp), bringing the DFR to 2.25% at the end of next year.

Risks | US, China and domestic inflation: We see risks of a slower cutting cycle stemming from stickier inflation in the US, stronger world demand or higher oil prices. On the other hand, downside surprises on the inflation front and significant weakening of wages could lead to a faster cutting cycle.

UK and Scandi | First steps: We see UK growth on a more sustainable footing over our forecast horizon as monetary policy restrictiveness is removed. As the energy shock dissipates, core inflation follows headline towards 2%Y. We see the BoE cutting rates three times this year (June, August and November), and see Bank Rate at 3.25% by the end of 2025. In Sweden, inflation is approaching target but a weak SEK poses a challenge. We see the Riksbank continuing its cutting cycle in September and December, with the policy rate at 2.25% by the end of 2025. In Norway, persistent core inflation and high wage growth this year should allow Norges Bank to begin its cutting cycle only in September, with risk tilted to a later start.

## Unusual Times: A Rebound Not by the Book

Our last two outlooks presented a downbeat assessment of European growth. It was the role of this section to highlight the positive aspects. This time around our outlook is more optimistic: the anticipated growth rebound has arrived and Europe is on its way to reaching its (low) potential growth rate. Yet the set-up of the growth rebound is highly unusual. This warrants highlighting.

Let's start with inflation. In a normal environment, higher growth leads to higher inflation and vice versa. This time is different. The euro area needs to grow faster to get inflation down. The reason is that faster growth should lead to better resource utilisation in sectors characterised by labour hoarding. This should keep unit labour costs in check: further wage increases are in the pipeline, mostly driven by the catch-up with past inflation, and higher productivity is one important channel to cushion the pass-through to prices (declining profit margins is the other).

If growth fails to rebound, and as a consequence productivity fails to improve as much as needed, inflation may well come in above the ECB’s target in 2025. Inevitably, this would slow down the rate-cutting cycle. So, we need robust growth – but not too much. If growth were to surprise significantly to the upside, the timely convergence to the ECB's 2%Y target may be put into question too, as inflationary forces coming from strong demand would then be in the driver's seat. Again, the rate-cutting cycle would be slowed down. As an orientation, we think that as long as growth in 2025 lands in a range between 0.9-1.6%Y, our forecast for euro area disinflation should be broadly on track.

The monetary and fiscal policy mix is another area where this cycle stands out. Normally, monetary policy would tighten into an upswing and ease into a downturn. Equally, fiscal policy would be expansionary in a downturn and contractionary in an upswing. There can be no doubt that euro area monetary policy is restrictive currently. Its peak impact likely occurred in the first quarter. It is set to get less restrictive over time (market pricing has already front-loaded a significant part of that future easing), all the while euro area growth is set to rebound. Meanwhile, fiscal policy is still expansionary – although with a negative impulse (we think that the ongoing consolidation of euro area fiscal deficit subtracts 15-20bp from growth in 2024). In effect, the euro area is set to get a more accommodative monetary policy and continues to run expansionary fiscal policy in the middle of an economic upswing. While this is hardly bad news for growth, it increases the stakes for monetary policymakers. The landing strip for our disinflation path is not too wide. And this provides yet another argument for a rather gradual monetary policy easing cycle.

…and testing the limit in 2025: This should pave the way for a further uptick in annual growth in 2025, to 1.2%Y. 2025 will likely be about whether the euro area sees enough positive impulses to grow above potential, which we estimate to be low, around 1%Y (see here). This will probably not come from domestic demand because of lagged effects of restrictive monetary policy and fiscal consolidation in some countries. So, the positive impulse taking euro area growth above potential might have to come from the external side. With a backdrop of elevated global uncertainties and heightened geopolitical tensions, we remain cautious that this will effectively be the case and we are below consensus (1.4%Y) for 2025.

What are the risks? In our downside and upside scenarios (see Risks: The US and China ), we explore some risks coming from the external side, envisaging more sticky inflation in the US or a stronger Chinese rebound. Idiosyncratic risks for the euro area abound and domestic demand could surprise in all directions, as we discuss in the following sections. European households have a high saving rate, which could provide some support if it declines or be a drag if it continues to increase. Investment has been relatively resilient until now, but the negative impacts from restrictive monetary policy could be more protracted. We think that fiscal policies will consolidate at a moderate pace, but there is a distinct risk that 2025 sees more consolidation than we currently project.

## Country outlook: Less divergence in 2025?

Our updated outlook confirms that the euro area continues to display considerable heterogeneity in 2024 ( Exhibit 11 ) – GDP is projected to increase by 2.0Y in Spain but only 0.2%Y in Germany. All economies share the two main narratives of our euro area forecast: private consumption will gain momentum on the back of real income gains while exports of goods will start to benefit from a rebound in global trade. But they are not equal in this environment.

For instance, Germany accumulates handicaps: it is more exposed to global trade, its business model is more affected by high energy prices and its labour force is expected to shrink. And it runs a lower fiscal deficit than other countries. The result is a larger drag on domestic demand and a bigger supply-side constraint than we see in most other countries.

On the other hand, Spain has surprised on the upside and this is our largest upwards revision for 2024. It benefits from a combination of strong demand – such as buoyant exports of services including tourism, and robust investment spending supported by EU funds – and strong supply, with very positive net migration flows.

Heterogeneity should continue to prevail in 2025, with Germany still below its long-term average growth, France close to it, and Spain and Italy significantly above theirs ( Exhibit 12 ). But this is likely to be less prominent than in 2024 as an improving outlook for global trade should provide some relief to the exportoriented German economy. This is the main factor behind our upwards revisions for Germany and France in 2025 since November.

With monetary policy becoming less of a drag, attention will also shift to fiscal policy. Most likely, France and Italy, among others, will face an Excessive Deficit Procedure (EDP) after this summer. We took down our forecast for Italy in 2025, because the boost provided by the Superbonus should start to fade, more than we previously thought. More fiscal consolidation in these two countries next year is a key risk for growth.

## Consumption

Real income growth = higher consumption: Private consumption is on its way up. In our previous outlook we expected consumption to be a key driver of growth for both 2024 and 2025, and we stick to that view. As we show in Exhibit 13 , we see household consumption growth moving from an average of around 0.15%Q in 2023 to 0.3%Q from 2Q24, as real income growth stabilises in positive territory. This wage catch-up process, coupled with falling inflation, should improve the outlook for consumer spending.

Migration flows have helped the outlook for consumption: The picture for consumers varies significantly between countries. Based on latest available data, Germany is once again the underperformer, while France emerges as the key driver of the euro area consumption recovery. Spain and Italy sit somewhere in between. Both countries consumption is almost flat versus the 2019 level. This picture changes quite dramatically once we look at consumption per capita rather than in aggregate terms. In particular, in the case of Germany, consumption per capita is meaningfully lower in 2023 versus 2019. Similarly, in Spain, while aggregate consumption saw some modest growth over 2019-23, consumption per capita in real terms fell by more than 2% over the same period. The moral of this story is that large migration flows (to Spain from South America and to Germany from Ukraine) have supported both economies, and without such flows both Spain and Germany would have likely seen a large(r) decline in private consumption than what actually materialised. Italy is the only country in which unfavourable population dynamics have been more than compensated by a sharp fall in the saving rate, leading to a sharp rise in per capita consumption between 2019 to 2023 (1.7%), and private consumption growth.

## Saving rate expected to stabilise: Our forecast for private

consumption is based on the assumption that the saving rate will stabilise at a relatively high level in the euro area, at least higher than where it was just before the pandemic ( Exhibit 16 ). After a phase of post-pandemic normalisation, the saving rate in the euro area has been again on the rise throughout 2023. In 2024, we think that household consumption will be supported by rising incomes rather than a further adjustment in the propensity to consume. The EC consumer survey shows that an increasing number of households expect to save more in the coming 12 months ( Exhibit 15 ). At the same time, as we discuss later in this outlook (see Labour Market: Tight and Not Very Productive ), a slowdown in employment growth should also be supportive of a stabilisation, rather than a fall, in the saving rate, for precautionary motives. Excess savings are a story of the past at this point: households in the euro area have not used any of their excess savings to boost consumption, and while all the excess cash and deposits accumulated throughout the pandemic have been

exhausted, this has not gone to support spending, but rather been reallocated to other forms of financial assets (households have seen a material increase in their exposure to bonds since the start of the ECB's hiking cycle).

## Tracking private consumption in real time

How to track private consumption? One key factor in the strengthening recovery in 2024 and 2025 is private consumption – not only because it accounts for around 50% of GDP but also because it is predicted to see a boost from stronger real income gains on the back of falling inflation and resilient labour markets. The problem with consumption is that the data are scarce: for the euro area, it is

released at a quarterly frequency within the national accounts, around two months after the end of a given quarter. In this box, we investigate how higher-frequency indicators might help us to track private consumption in real time.

Hard and soft monthly data… We use a combination of hard and soft indicators, available on a monthly basis and timelier than the national accounts. We cover three dimensions of household spending: 1) Retail sales, which include the food and goods part of the consumption basket; 2) Business confidence from the European Commission monthly surveys in consumer-related services (we obtain the best results with sentiment in the sector of 'travel agency, tour operator reservation service and related activities'); and 3) Consumer confidence.

…with different timing: Those monthly indicators are not available with the same timing. While confidence indicators (both for consumers and businesses) are published at the end of the month, retail sales data are issued with a lag of 35-40 days. Therefore, we build four different models depending on the information set that we have at hand.

A suite of four models: In the first model, we take the first months of the travel business survey and the consumer confidence indicator, which can already be used at the start of the second month of the quarter. In the second model, we include the first month of retail sales, along with the first and second months of the business survey and consumer confidence indicator. This can be used at the beginning of the third month of the quarter. In the third model, we add the second month of a quarter of retail sales and the third month of the soft indicators, which can be adopted shortly after the quarter has ended. Finally, in the last model, we work with all the information with three months of retail sales, business surveys and confidence indicators, allowing us to employ this model around 40 days after the end of the quarter, still ahead of the national accounts. All four models are estimated on the sample ranging from 1Q05 to 4Q19, due to data availability and to exclude the highly volatile pandemic period.

A forecast… Based on the information we have so far for 1Q24, with two prints of retail sales and soft indicators for the full quarter, we estimate that private consumption has picked up slightly (0.1%Q in 1Q24). Our first estimate for 2Q24 with this framework will be available at the beginning of June.

…and some interesting takeaways: Our suite of models also give interesting insights into the monitoring of private consumption. First, our models have a much better fit to the data today than in the post-Covid recovery phase. We are back in a more usual environment where the signal from early indicators is better. Second, we do find that the root mean square error declines, from 0.24 in the first model, to 0.22 in the second model, to 0.21 in the third model and finally to 0.20 in the last model. This implies that with each additional variable, we explain a greater share of the volatility in private consumption. Third, we also observe that the contribution of retail sales increases over time, whereas the weight of consumer confidence diminishes as more hard data become available. ## The euro area and the US economic relationship

Building on our recent work on implications of US elections for Europe, in this box we review the Europe-US economic relationship.

Goods, but not only: It goes without saying that Europe and the US have deep economic ties. It is well known, for instance, that the US is the largest export market for European firms (outside Europe), well ahead of the UK and China (see below). But, this relationship also goes beyond trading goods, as illustrated by the bilateral current account between the two regions ( Exhibit 38 ). Goods actually represent only 50% of the revenues ('credits') the euro area gets out of its economic exchanges with the US and only 30% of the revenues the US gets from it. The rest is made up of services and (mostly investment) income flows. This configuration is very similar to the ties Europe has with countries much closer geographically – the UK and Switzerland. And it is very different from its relationship with China, still highly dominated by exchanges of goods.

# Labour Market: Tight and Not Very Productive

Labour market | No question, this is tight: We recently took a deep dive into the euro area labour market. Our first finding is that it is tight, with labour shortages still close to all-time highs despite weak GDP growth ( Exhibit 44 ). From a long-term perspective, this is simply the result of an exceptional performance that brought down the unemployment rate from 12% in 2013 to 6.5% at the beginning of 2024. Unsurprisingly, it is harder to find workers now than a decade ago. This tightness is broad-based and not specific to some activities, as there is clear evidence that labour shortages are affecting all sectors ( Exhibit 45 ).

Exhibit 44: The euro area economy is still suffering from labour shortages

It could be more efficient: To put this tightness into perspective, it is key to understand the efficiency of the allocation of resources. We have two topics in mind – the number of hours worked per person employed and the number of persons employed in relation to activity, i.e., productivity. Our conclusion is that the strength of the labour market in Europe is also driven by changes that have likely led to losses in efficiency with: 1) Fewer hours worked, prolonging a secular trend; 2) (Limited) persistent loss of productivity post-Covid pushing employment permanently (slightly) higher; and 3) More recently a delayed adjustment of employment to weak growth in 2023.

Shorter working hours, but in line with past trends: Hours worked per employed person saw a dramatic shift lower in 2020-21 during the pandemic shock ( Exhibit 46 ). A large part of the fall has been recovered, but hours worked have now settled around 1.3% lower than pre-pandemic (4Q19), equivalent to an average annual fall of 0.35%Y. The longerterm perspective shows that hours worked per person are broadly on their secular declining trend ( Exhibit 46 ). And Exhibit 47 confirms that, from a sector perspective, the losses in average hours worked since 2019 are in line with the previous trends (a notable exception is the public sector, where hours worked have been falling more rapidly). What does it mean for the labour market? It is obviously bad news that hours worked are falling, but this is probably not something that will be reversed anytime soon (see also a recent IMF paper reaching a similar conclusion).

...a tale of two-post Covid phases: Exhibit 49 shows two phases since 2020. With generous furlough schemes, labour hoarding was a key feature in 2020-21 as employment was significantly above what would have been a priori needed. But in 2023, the amount of 'excess' labour started to increase (and productivity fell) again significantly, after the euro area economy was hit by the energy shock. So, from a macro perspective, the surprisingly strong level of employment today does not seem to be the legacy of Covid but rather a consequence of the economic downturn since mid-2022. ## Can immigration rescue Europe from demographic decline?

As we discussed above, growth in euro area labour force over 2022 and 2023 has been in part driven by higher migration flows than normal, as we show in Exhibit 54 . Keeping the flow of immigration at these levels would benefit the euro area labour market and growth outlook, in particular in the context of a falling population.

## Asymmetric wage pass-through

On the pass-through from wages to inflation (again)… The interplay between strong wages and core inflation remains a key focus of our inflation outlook. In November, we showed that the pass-through of wages to core inflation was on average rather limited, with the cumulative reaction of prices to a shock on wages well below 1 (see here). So, profit margins absorb a significant part of robust wage pressures, a mechanism which seems to be effectively at play in recent quarters.

…are they catching up or overheating? We want to dig a little bit deeper into that argument, asking ourselves if the pass-through might actually be dependent on the state of the economy. The issue we have in mind is that the current strong euro area wage inflation mostly comes from real wages catching up after considerable real losses and not because the economy is overheating. Our prior is that such a catch-up is less inflationary.

A tale of two states: We define two states, depending on whether real wages are below or above their long-term trend ( Exhibit 74 ), and estimate a threshold VAR model to evaluate the pass-through of wages to inflation in the euro area in each state. The VAR has two variables: the GDP deflator as a measure of core inflation and nominal wages adjusted for trend productivity. The model is estimated in quarterly frequency and the variables are expressed as growth rates between 2Q96 and 4Q19. Importantly, real wages remain in each state for a significant period of time (at least 10 consecutive quarters here), a key condition for the estimated responses to be consistent (see Ramey and Zubairy 2018 here). We order wages above the price deflator, as we assume that wages are not affected by inflation contemporaneously, but that inflation is affected by wages in the same period. Finally, we compute a Cholesky decomposition, which gives us the impulse response functions shown in Exhibit 75 .

Softer pass-through when catching up: We find evidence that when real wages come from below their trend (i.e., catching up), the pass-through to inflation is lower than in the 'average' estimate, as we can see from the impulse response function having a negative sign from the second quarter after the shock onwards. Meanwhile, the pass-through is assessed to be more persistent when real wages are already above trend. This confirms our view that, in the current circumstances, strong wage growth might not be too inflationary.

## Monetary Policy: Starting a New Season

We forecast three rate cuts in 2024 and four cuts in 2025, bringing the DFR to 2.25% at the end of 2025. All rate cuts are likely to take place at projection meetings.

A certainty and two new questions: Around a year ago we predicted that the ECB would start its cutting cycle in June 2024. One year later this forecast has turned into as close a certainty as things can get: the ECB is set to deliver an initial 25bp rate cut in June. As so often in life, the answer to one question just leads to the next. Since the April meeting, the market has moved on from when rate cuts start to how the ECB is going to cut rates. A related question concerns the endpoint of the cutting cycle. But for now the ECB's (and market) focus is firmly on the first question. President Lagarde offered a stylised characterisation of the time ahead in the March press conference. The "holding season" will give way to the "restrictiveness season" in June. At some point thereafter the "normalisation season" will follow. We interpret this last season as the period during which the Council determines and reaches the endpoint of the rate-cutting cycle. Chief Economist Lane followed up in a recent interview, making clear that the discussion about r\* (i.e., the endpoint of the cutting cycle) can wait a bit longer. The "normalisation season" will likely hit the screens only in 2025.

## r\* and the policy stance: A central bank's guide to the galaxy

The comparison of r\* and real rates indicates the stance of monetary policy: r\*, or the neutral real rate, is the (real) interest rate at which the economy neither expands nor contracts. It can therefore serve as a guidepost to the monetary policy stance: If real rates are above r\* they constrain growth and inflation, and vice versa for rates below r\*. And the higher the gap between the two, the higher the impact. To complicate matters, r\* is not observable and we need to rely on model estimates.<sup>1</sup> These estimates differ across models, and even within models there is high statistical uncertainty on individual estimates (see for example the discussion in the dedicated ECB Occasional Paper and BIS).

Recent estimates see r\* close to 0%... To account for uncertainty around measures of r\*, it can be insightful to consider estimates from different models. A range of estimates from ECB and BIS models suggest that the natural rate has fallen over the past decades and is currently close to 0% ( Exhibit 81 ). Generally speaking, r\* will go down if savings increase or investments decrease. There are three main reasons behind the decline, which has also been noted in other advanced economies like the US and the UK. First, a fall in potential growth has lowered investment as the rate of return declined. Second, population aging has led to structurally higher savings.<sup>2</sup> And third, a decline in risk aversion following the financial crisis has increased savings further as the demand for safe assets went up. Overall, this has recently suppressed r\* to levels close to 0%.

…and different forces will determine the path forward: Since potential growth is likely to fall further over the coming decade and the demographic shift is ongoing, these two forces are likely to continue to weigh on r\*. Higher real rates over recent years have recently sparked a debate about a potential increase in r\*, and the median estimate has moved up a touch relative to pre-pandemic levels ( Exhibit 81 ). While it seems too early to tell whether this shift persists, there are three factors that could lead to a higher r\*: 1) Large investments related to the energy transition; 2) Higher public deficits due to increased spending on defence and aging-related costs; and 3) Productivity gains from AI, which also require large investments and should lift productivity.

Monetary policy will likely remain restrictive, despite rate cuts: If we take the most recent r\* estimate as a guide, we can see that monetary policy will remain restrictive for some time, despite the looming cutting cycle ( Exhibit 82 ). Based on our forecast for the policy rate path and euro area inflation, we see real rates falling only slowly during 2024. The restrictiveness should then fade out in 2024 and real rates should approach the current r\* level toward the end of 2025.

In a high-level statement, the ECB decided that it will keep operating the floor system for steering short-term money market rates under the new 'normal' (see our take here). Focusing on the decisions with a closer implementation date, the ECB decided to narrow the corridor between the DFR and MRO to 15bp (from September 18, 2024). The aim is to limit the

volatility of short-term money market rates, while allowing some space for money market activity. In the same announcement, the minimum reserve requirement was confirmed to stay at 1%. As anticipated, a decision on the remuneration on government deposits followed closely after and the ceiling was kept at ESTR-20bp. The national central banks therefore have the freedom themselves to decide whether they want to keep remunerating government deposits or move to 0% following the move of Bundesbank.

...but with implementation earliest in 2026: The implementation date of the new structural bond portfolio is linked to the moment the ECB needs to inject more liquidity in the system to maintain the floor system in place. Based on our excess liquidity forecasts ( Exhibit 84 ), this time will likely come only in 2026, when excess liquidity starts to approach €2.0 trillion, which we see as the level from which short-term rates may start to increase.<sup>3</sup> The role of the new longer-term refinancing operations could be to specifically cater for banks' reserve demand induced by bank regulation (LCR, NFSR). The bond portfolio should provide stable liquidity demand (e.g., banknotes). We think that the portfolio could be a fully risk-shared, capital key-guided portfolio, similar to APP and likely tilted to shorter maturities.

## Fiscal Policy: Progressing While Consolidating

Euro area balances are consolidating gradually... The aggregated euro area deficit figure came in higher than we forecast in 2023 (3.6% of GDP versus 3.3% in our 2024 Outlook). Even though fiscal balances did consolidate, the improvement from 2022 was rather marginal (only 10bp). However, we have good reasons to expect more consolidation for this year and next. We envision a 60bp deficit reduction in 2024, followed by a 30bp decline in 2025, allowing the euro area to reach 2.7% ( Exhibit 85 ).

…even though they vary across countries: The slower consolidation in 2023 was mainly due to France and Italy printing deficit numbers significantly above expectations (more on this below). From here, Italy should compensate for the high deficits it had in 2023. We expect the country to have one of the largest deficit reductions among the countries in our coverage, with a 2.4pp deficit decline expected in 2024. France also surprised in 2023, and we expect its budget tightening to remain relatively modest. Spain should benefit from higher growth to improve its government balances, while Germany's deficits should continue to be contained, due to its constitutional debt brake.

The debt reduction should slow down in the euro area... The euro area debt/GDP ratio has declined over recent years. Despite debt ratios rising by 13pp in the first year of the pandemic ( Exhibit 86 ), high nominal growth has allowed for a clear consolidation of the debt. Debt ratios in 2023 were only 4.5pp above their 2019 levels. With inflation receding, the times of fast progress are ending and our forecast suggests that future debt reduction will become increasingly more difficult.

…but some will be more affected than others: Germany and Spain should continue to make progress on debt reduction, despite doing it at a significantly slower pace. In Italy, the reduction of the deficit in 2024 shouldn't translate into lower debt ratios. A large share of its deficit consolidation will be due to the accounting of the Superbonus. While Italy is very likely to see lower deficits because of it, it should also get higher stock-flow adjustments, accounting for a higher debt/GDP ratio. Similarly, France should see a higher debt ratio overall due to its slower deficit consolidation.

## The euro area fiscal impulse

Defining the fiscal impulse: What does more consolidation mean for euro area growth? To answer this question we look at the so-called 'fiscal impulse'. It is usually defined as the change in the structural primary deficit from one year to the next – that is, the change in the deficit after cyclical factors and interest expenses

Source: Eurostat, Morgan Stanley Research forecasts

are excluded. As can be seen in Exhibit 89 , if the impulse is negative the structural primary deficit is reduced. This should correlate with lower stimulus.

A 100bp increase in the impulse could lead to 77bp of growth: A larger impulse implies more stimulus and, a priori, a positive impact on growth. We can gauge the impact by looking at correlations between the fiscal impulse and the euro area growth rate. We conclude that the impulse can be channeled through the economy in two different ways: 1) A direct impact, which is relatively mild. For a 100bp increase in the impulse we can expect 77bp of growth in the current year ( Exhibit 90 ); and 2) A lagged effect, meaning that for every 100bp increase in the impulse, we could expect around 128bp of growth in the following period ( Exhibit 90 ). These estimates do not control for several important factors (business cycle, monetary policy, and so on), but overall they show that the impulse clearly matters for growth. The regressions in Exhibit 90 imply two potential slopes driving the relationship between the fiscal impulse and growth. One slope is flatter (in blue) and suggests a more muted relationship (77bp of growth for 100bp in impulse), while the other (in yellow) is steeper, supporting a stronger correlation (128bp of growth for 100bp in impulse). While it is difficult to say which slope is correct, we can confidently support the idea that a minimum impact would be compatible with the blue line. In turn, one can realistically expect at least 77bp of growth for 100bp of fiscal impulse.

The impulse should be contractionary in the near term: Our forecast expects a fiscal impulse of -90bp, looking highly contractionary on paper. And this would also be an issue in 2025, with a fiscal impulse estimated at around -30bp. But this conclusion would be rather misleading in current circumstances. There are three reasons why we think that this time is different: 1) The Superbonus in Italy; 2) The amount of fiscal consolidation coming from the unwinding of energy-related measures; and 3) The role of spending financed through the EU Recovery and Resilience Facility (RRF).

The Superbonus needs to be discounted... One key factor behind the large deficit posted by Italy in 2023 (7.2% of GDP) is the Superbonus, a tax credit scheme subsidising house renovations which has boosted construction activity tremendously. On our calculations, the Superbonus contributed to the Italian deficit by more than 3.5% of GDP in 2023 – 0.5% of GDP for the euro area. The measure was ended last year and so there will be a lot less Superbonus-related spending in 2024. And this is a key factor behind the significant reduction of the euro area deficit in 2024. However, we think that simply looking at the delta in Italy’s fiscal deficit numbers in 2023 versus 2024 might be overstating the extent of fiscal consolidation. The take-up of the Superbonus of 2H23 is likely also going to boost economic activity in 2024, just because renovating a house takes time. So, the true negative fiscal impulse coming in 2024 from the Superbonus should be much more muted than the reduction implied by the deficit figures. This is essentially an accounting argument – the deficit is booked in one year, (part of) the spending occurs in the next.

…as does the unwinding of energy measures... We need to think carefully about the unwinding of energy measures. In our forecasts, the unwinding contributes to around 90bp of the reduction of the euro area deficit in 2024. Yet, these measures were put in place to protect households and companies’ income and spending in an environment where prices were surging. As energy prices go down, fewer energy measures do not necessarily imply a drag on growth. So, there is probably only a fraction of the reduction of energy-related measures that should be considered as a negative fiscal impulse in 2024. There is no easy way to know the correct number, but for households the gain provided by falling electricity and gas prices since their peak is around 0.1pp of GDP in 2024 ( Exhibit 92 ), and this is what we would trim off the apparent consolidation coming from energy-related measures.

…and the recovery fund: We also need to consider the impact of EU grants and loans from the Recovery and Resilience Facility (RRF) on euro area deficit numbers (or better the absence thereof). EU funds are channelled into the national economy via the budget, but they will in general not impact the national deficit (and the euro area deficit is calculated as the sum of countries’ deficits), because they increase both resources and uses. So, the RRF provides a stimulus that is not captured by deficit statistics. This was meaningful in 2023, around 0.6% of GDP. On our estimates, the figure will be even slightly larger in 2024, by around 0.1pp of GDP ( Exhibit 92 ), so EU funds should continue to support growth.

Not that restrictive after all: At face value, the expected deficit reduction for the euro area in 2024 compared to 2023 means a negative fiscal impulse of around 90bp, i.e., rather contractionary fiscal policy ( Exhibit 91 ). However, when we zoom in on the details behind the number, we see strong reasons to think that the actual impact is much lower: we need to deduct 10bp from energy measures, 10bp from RRF and around 30bp from the Superbonus (this last number is no more than an educated guess). And with these numbers, we have all we need to write our conclusion. Euro area fiscal policy in 2024 is contractionary, but 'only' by around 40bp ( Exhibit 92 ), which won’t be a large drag on growth in 2024 or 2025.

## Risks: The US and China

What is the next shock? Geopolitical risks will likely remain at the centre of attention in the coming months, as conflicts in Ukraine and the Middle East are not showing signs of de-escalation. The euro area economy remains exposed to trade tensions, uncertainty shocks or surges in commodity prices. From the global perspective, we explore the impact for Europe of two key macroeconomic risks. The downside scenario envisions more persistent core inflation in the US. The upside scenario comes from a fundamentally stronger Chinese economy.

Downside scenario | Persistent US inflation forces the ECB to pause at end-2024... In the downside scenario, persistent services inflation in the US – an idiosyncratic, domesticdriven phenomenon – leads the Fed to stay higher for longer, lowering US growth. The ECB would cut as planned in June and September 2024, to 3.50%. But as it turns out that the Fed will cut less than expected, exchange rate pressures would start pushing euro area inflation up, above the base scenario. The ECB would then pause at 3.50%.

…before resuming its cutting cycle in mid-2025: More restrictive monetary policy and weaker US demand weigh on euro area GDP. Combined with lower oil prices, this pushes euro area inflation below the base scenario by mid-2025 with clear risks of undershooting the target. The ECB resumes its easing cycle, with 100bp of cuts in 2H25.

Upside scenario | Stronger Chinese economy pushes euro area growth and inflation up: The upside scenario sees a more resilient Chinese economy, where the debt-deflation loop is attenuated by more efficient policies and stronger domestic demand. More robust external demand would boost euro area growth thanks to higher exports and investment. Stronger activity and higher oil prices would progressively push euro area inflation further away from target in 2H24. After cutting as planned in June and September, the ECB would stop at 3.50% as inflation turns out to be persistently higher than in the baseline.

## Potential implications of further escalation in Ukraine

The situation in Ukraine seems to be locked in stalemate. However, the summer months might offer a window of opportunity for one of the belligerent countries to gain some decisive advantage. We explore potential economic implications for Europe of escalation in Ukraine that would increase geopolitical tensions around the conflict. We see four main channels potentially at play – energy supply, trade, defence spending and confidence.

Energy: For energy, the key difference with February 2022 is the dramatic reduction of the share of Russia as a provider of oil and natural gas for Europe. In 2021, Europe was sourcing 25% of its petroleum oil and around 40% of its natural gas from Russia. At the end of 2023, those shares are down to 4% and 14%, respectively ( Exhibit 101 ). Europe has diversified its providers (and also reduced its consumption of gas), which means that any escalation would very likely not trigger price surges of the magnitude experienced in 2022-23.

Trade: Since 2022, European trade with Russia has been reduced by 60% for exports and 80% for imports, so this is probably not where to look at for a trade shock ( Exhibit 102 ). The key question will be global, around trade tensions and possibly increasing trade barriers. As things stands, Europe remains very dependent on the global supply side and any shock would be very harmful. A recent study from a German think tank shows that a sudden, full decoupling from China could cost Germany around 4% of GDP in the first year (see here).

We expect GDP growth to be stable in 2024 and pick up a touch in 2025. France faces two main challenges: low productivity gains and a high deficit. Risks to growth are probably tilted to the downside in case of a larger fiscal consolidation in 2025.

Low volatility, in both directions: France, as usual, is proving to be a less volatile economy than the rest of the euro area. It experienced significantly higher growth in 2023 when Europe stalled. But, as the euro area gains momentum, this gap is likely to narrow. So, we think France will be experiencing growth just below the euro area average in 2025 and see significant hurdles for a more favourable outlook.

Not many tailwinds: With again positive real income gains, consumption should remain robust. But the elevated saving rate is unlikely to drop, as we think that it reflects a shift in the composition of aggregate income. As already observed since 2019, France's exports are also likely to underperform other countries and thus benefit less from the expected global recovery. And, of course, fiscal policy should start to be a drag.

Two key challenges... France faces two structural challenges, which are likely to shape the outlook. The first relates to productivity, which has fallen by 4pp since 2019 ( Exhibit 108 ). The second is fiscal, with France printing a deficit of 5.5% of GDP in 2023 ( Exhibit 109 ).

...falling productivity... On the surface, the French labour market looks like a success story: the unemployment rate is at 7.5% in 4Q23, 1pp lower than in 2019, while the participation rate is at the highest since this has been measured by Insee (1975). Almost 1.3 million jobs have been added since 2019, of which very few are in the public sector. But the flip side has been the observed drop in productivity. It can be only very partially explained by labour market policies (subsidised apprenticeships) and labour hoarding (Banque de France explains 60% of the drop with those two factors). If persistent, this is a clear risk to French productivity gains and potential growth in the long run.

...and a high fiscal deficit: In 2023, the French fiscal deficit reached 5.5% of GDP, increasing meaningfully from 4.8% in 2022. This is the consequence of significant tax cuts implemented since 2021 while spending (as a percentage of GDP) remains higher than pre-Covid. From there, the direction of travel is clear: France is very likely to face an Excessive Deficit Procedure (EDP) this summer and will be required to reduce its deficit towards 3%. The French government actually intends to bring it back to 5.1% of GDP in 2024 and 4.1% in 2025, before getting below 3% in 2027. We think that the target for 2024 is plausible, given announced spending cuts. But, as it stands, 2025 will require additional spending cuts or tax increases which are not yet documented. More fundamentally, to get its debt path on a sustainable track, France needs to stop posting primary deficits like it has done since 2008. This is likely to be the main risk to growth.

A correction in construction activity is likely, with the end of the Superbonus. Fiscal deficits remain too high, and debt/GDP is on a rising path to 2025.

Supergrowth thanks to the Superbonus? The Italian economy has outperformed the euro area in the past two years. While post-pandemic tailwinds supporting the services sector can explain part of it, one of the main reasons behind the relative strength of the Italian economy has been the Superbonus. From the implementation of this tax credit scheme in 2020 until now, the construction portion of industrial production is up 60%; other major euro area economies are flat. Construction activity has had material spillovers onto the industrial sector too (the only category within Italian industrial production to have seen positive growth versus the end of 2019 is investment goods). The government has ended the Superbonus with one last decree in March 2024. However, the take-up of the scheme extended to the first three months of 2024, and that will likely continue to support construction activity throughout the year, together with the Superbonus works that have started in late 2023 but are not yet completed. From the end of 2024 onwards, we expect a normalisation in the flow of construction activity in Italy, only in part balanced out by the positive effects of the recovery fund. We see negative growth (- 0.1%Y) in construction in 2025. On the back of this dynamic, we revise our 2025 GDP forecast down by 30bp to 0.7%Y (consensus: 1.1%Y). We see risks skewed towards a faster correction in construction activity, in particular if the RRF implementation delays continue.

Good news from the labour market: Participation and the employment rate are at record levels as of the start of 2024. Employment growth has been strong (3.2% since end-2019), but driven by only a few selected pockets. Almost half of that growth is concentrated in professional, science, tech and administration, a third in construction (Superbonus again), and a fifth comes from the public sector. With the envisaged slowdown, employment growth should decline significantly. Wage growth has lagged in Italy, both in nominal and real terms. In this context, Italian households have reduced their saving rate well below pre-pandemic levels, to consume a similar basket in real terms as before the inflation shock. We think that this is only temporary, and as real wage growth picks up throughout 2024, the saving rate should also return to more normal levels.

Fiscal | It's not what it looks like: The impact of the Superbonus on public finances is as yet to show up in full. The measure is accounted in the deficit of the year in which the tax credit is approved, but the impact on debt and the borrowing requirement takes place when the lack of revenues from the tax credits materialises for the government. As a result, we see debt/GDP on a rising path, mostly caused by an adverse stock-flow adjustment on the back of the Superbonus. We expect a primary deficit in both 2024 and 2025. While Italy will likely end up in an EDP, we think that the actual implications are limited: the government's fiscal plan should meet EC requirements under the EDP, and in case of compliance with SGP rules, Italy would remain eligible under the ECB's TPI.

…and public consumption remains strong… Government consumption has been expanding steadily in recent years and supported growth strongly last year. Such a government arrangement would be based on a less specific coalition agreement and would likely shift more decision-making power on individual policies to the parliament. However, dramatic changes to the economy are unlikely, as the Netherlands is characterised by an overall stable and prosperous environment and is firmly embedded in EU and euro area legislation.

The major question for Belgium comes from the June federal elections. As polls stand, it could take some time before a coalition emerges. This might not prevent Belgium from enjoying robust growth, but risks delaying the required adjustments of its public finances.

Strong growth thank to consumption: The Belgian economy continued to post robust growth numbers. GDP was up 1.3%Y in 4Q23 when the euro area was down 0.1%Y. But private (1.6%Y in 4Q23) and public (1.5%Y) consumption were strong enough for GDP to expand.

High deficit and sticky inflation: Indeed, domestic activity continued to be supported by a high public deficit, 4.4% of GDP in 2023, the third largest in the euro area, after Italy and France. And household income also benefited from the national system of wage indexation, totally unique among the largest countries of the euro area. Wage indexation comes of course at a cost – more persistent inflation. Core inflation (HICP) in Belgium was 3.5%Y in April, above the euro area figure (2.7%Y).

And no party is projected to have much more than 25 seats in parliament. A majority needs 76.

...but one clear priority for the next government: In any case, the future Belgian government will have to cope with deteriorated public finances. Before 2019, Belgium used to post primary surpluses ( Exhibit 120 ), a key factor for keeping its debt on track. But the primary deficit now stands at 2.4% of GDP and Belgium faces pressures on social spending because of its ageing population: according to Bank of Belgium, the costs of health care and pensions will increase by 2pp of GDP in the next 10 years. A change of course is needed, which is a challenge for the future coalition, and a key risk for the growth outlook in 2025 and after.

Growth | The most unusual recession: Even by Irish standards, the country's economic accounts are getting challenging to interpret. In 2023, for example, GDP collapsed by 3.3%Y. However, private consumption was actually up by 3.2%Y. Net trade was the main driver of the sharp headline GDP decline – in particular the decline in pharmaceutical sector exports. As this is a volatile category, we expect it to reverse its sharp drop in 2024. More broadly, underlying exports should firm as UK and euro area growth pick up.

Resilient consumer: Most importantly, we think there are still good reasons to be constructive on the consumer – despite the quarterly slowdown seen in 4Q23. For one, real incomes have picked up as headline inflation collapsed. The Central Bank of Ireland assesses the mortgage drag as mild, and the stock of excess savings outstanding is still sizeable. The labour market is rebalancing, but employment growth should remain solid. Consumer sentiment data look upbeat and supportive of our view.

Labour market | Tight but rebalancing: As noted, the labour market remains tight, but has started to show signs of rebalancing. The unemployment rate has picked up and vacancies have fallen by 20% from their post-Covid peaks. While employment growth should slow over our forecast horizon, note that we see a slowdown in labour supply growth too – largely as immigration flows moderate a touch.

Inflation | De-energised, but sticky: Energy and core goods prices have pushed headline inflation off its peaks. Core goods prices are in steep deflation as of April, with energy deflation troughing at near -9%Y in March. As some of these favourable base effects peter out – and go into reverse – keeping headline inflation at target will require further disinflation in services prices. Good progress has already been made – restaurant & hotels prices, for example, have seen price inflation fall from a peak of 8.6%Y in 2023 to 4.9%Y in April – but slowing wage growth should lead towards further easing. Overall, we see HICP inflation slowing to 2.1%Y in 2024, and broadly towards the target in 2025.

Policy | Enjoying the windfall: The public finances – helped by very strong (MNE-related) corporation tax revenues – remain incredibly robust, with solid fiscal surpluses projected over the forecast horizon. The autumn budget has brought about a number of one-off measures aimed at supporting households, including a one-year mortgage interest tax relief for some homeowners, as well as energy credits. The government still aims to grow a €14 billion public investment fund over the next seven years with the tax receipts windfall. Ministers will be able to partly draw down funds for climate-focused projects.

Politics | What next? However, the trajectory of public finances will depend on the February 2025 election – with Sinn Fein, for example, planning a large-scale house building programme should it enter government. Support for Sinn Fein has fallen fairly sharply early this year, although it continues to top the polls. Meanwhile, independent antiimmigration politicians have seen a surge in the polls. A general election has to legally be held by March 22, 2025.

We maintain our high-level view that the UK's recent stagflationary dynamic was largely a product of the energy shock. The hypothesis will be tested in the coming months. We see core inflation following recent moves lower in headline. Growth picks up, but potential is capped at under 1.5%Y. The BoE cuts three times this year.

Growth | Accelerating: After a sharp drop in 4Q23, GDP bounced back in 1Q24. As some of the 1Q24 one-offs deflate, we see a modest payback in 2Q24, before the quarterly growth pace re-accelerates towards 0.35%Q (1.4%Y), our estimate of the UK's potential growth. (Average) real wage growth was likely at its peak, in %Y terms, in 1Q24, with (aggregate) real disposable income growth slowing from last year's 2.2%Y. Overall, consumption growth should improve this year – but the more notable boost to growth should come in 2025. Private sector capex should pick up on stronger aggregate demand and lower borrowing costs. Net trade remains a mild boost to growth this year on ongoing solid demand growth among key trading partners.

Labour market | Catch-up time: Various labour market surveys suggest that the labour market is now broadly balanced. After a drop in hiring in 1Q24, we see moderate quarterly employment growth and an ongoing improvement in labour supply – helped by another 350-400k of net migrant arrivals this year. Inflation | Suddenly, then gradually: Momentum in inflation has looked very soft since last August. Softer pay growth next year, anchored inflation expectations, waning rental pressures and less of an uplift from indexation in administrative prices should all allow for services inflation to average 3%Y next year – leaving core at target over 2025. We see risks as broadly balanced.

We see two rate cuts in the coming months – in June and August – with the BoE then pausing to assess the impact of its actions on the economy. We see a November cut thereafter. In 2025, as the BoE gains confidence that 2%Y core can be attained sustainably, we see it cutting towards 3.25% at successive meetings, from February next year. Active sales stop from next September, with passive QT and TFSME repayments driving balance sheet normalisation.

Risks | Remixing policy? The UK's overall fiscal drag this year is 80bp, narrowing, on our estimates, to 40bp next year. We see a bigger fiscal drag next year as the largest domestic downside risk to our growth and our BoE forecasts. The global upside scenario sees stronger China-led growth, with the BoE stopping its cuts after the first 50bp. The downside sees US stagflation capping the initial BoE cuts at 50bp too. Yet, higher rates over 2024 lead to a further deceleration in growth and continuation of cuts next year.

Growth finally looking up: Forward-looking indicators in Sweden finally show a slightly better picture. Even though the Swedish consumer remains weak due to a challenging labour market and low wage growth in 2024, positive sentiment is finally returning. The Riksbank introduced a first cut, and lower mortgage rates are gradually easing the pain inflicted by high interest rate expenses. Yet, private consumption is likely to pick up only in 2H24 and mainly in 2025. On the other hand, government spending should be the backbone of Swedish growth throughout 2024 and 2025. The Spring Budget Bill introduced SEK 16.8 billion of additional spending. The Bill also signalled larger investments in infrastructure for 2025, together with various tax cuts. In a small, open economy, significant weight is put on FX considerations, interest rate expectations abroad and geopolitical developments.

Staying strong: The Norwegian economy proved resilient this cycle, and should continue to do so. Norway's households will be in an even better position this year due to higher real wage gains than initially expected. Private consumption should recover further in 2025 as the anticipated rate cuts free up more disposable income on the back of lower interest rate expenses. Strong oil and gas investments from last year should fuel offshore support sectors further this year, and are likely to increase even more. Looking ahead, while we do not expect a boost from construction for investment this year, higher house prices should start to fuel improved sentiment in the construction sector going into 2025. Mainland exports are likely to be supported by the weak currency and improved global trade.

Fiscal policy to remain expansionary: The revised budget this spring brought an additional NOK 36.2 billion of spending. The focus is primarily on defence, security and policing. NATO's target will already be met this year. Taking into account the increased value of the fund, the withdrawal from the pension fund remained 2.7%.

Sticky inflation: We expect core inflation to be sticky in 1H24. Core goods have driven the disinflation process, but services remain strong and should stay elevated. The list of inflationary pressures in the pipeline remains long: wage negotiations, an increase in business costs and pass-through of a weaker krone. Strong wage growth: Wage growth this year should be higher than expected. On the back of a relatively resilient growth outlook and labour market, it can maintain restrictive monetary policy for a bit longer than its DM peers. We see Norges Bank cutting for the first time by 25bp in September 2024. We think that the cutting cycle will be gradual, and hence only see Norges Bank cutting at projection meetings. On balance we see cuts of 50bp in 2024 and 100bp in 2025. The balance of risks is tilted towards a later start on the back of stronger inflationary pressures arising from higher-than-expected wage growth in 2024 and a weaker krone.

Jens Eisenschmidt, Jean-Francois Ouvrard, Chiara Zangarelli, Skander Garchi Casal, Gabriela Silova, Claire A Thuerwaechter, Eric S Oynoyan, Lorenzo Testa et al.

## UK BoE Reaction: Lacking Commitment (May 10, 2024)

Bruna Skarica, Wanting Low, Fabio Bassanin CFA As we had expected, forecasts were dovish and Ramsden voted for a cut. Governor Bailey
actually explicitly said more cuts than market priced were likely to be delivered. And yet...the BoE could not quite commit to commencing the cutting cycle in June. As the meeting was
broadly in line with our view, we stick with our base case – 75bp of cuts this year, June start – but the ride will be bumpy, and the near-term risks hawkish.

## Nordic Macro Watch | Riksbank: So Long, Terminal (May 8, 2024)

In a close call today, the Riksbank cut rates by 25bp to 3.75% on the back of inflation approaching the target and weak economic activity. In line with our expectations, two more cuts for 2H24 were signalled, taking the rate to 3.25% by the end of 2024.

## Germany: A Structurally Challenged Growth Model (March 13, 2024)

Jens Eisenschmidt, Claire A Thuerwaechter, Jean-Francois Ouvrard, Marina Zavolock et al. Headwinds from elevated energy prices, stagnant world trade and labor supply weigh on German GDP and growth. We expect growth below consensus out to 2025 and see these structural factors posing risks of lower growth for longer.

In Part 4 of a series of notes explaining crucial parts of the ECB's balance sheet, we focus on the profit and loss account of the Eurosystem. We expect that the Eurosystem will report losses across 2023-2025.
