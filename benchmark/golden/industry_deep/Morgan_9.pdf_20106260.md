# Correction: FOMC Reaction: March Is Out, B/S Discussions to Begin

According to Chair Powell, it isn't likely the Committee would be confident to cut by March. The Fed will be cutting rates this year, but is in no hurry. We continue to call for a June start. Our strategists stay long duration via 5y UST, and neutral agency MBS.

## Key Takeaways

• Rates decision: The FOMC held the policy rate in a range of 5.25- 5.50%, where it has remained since July 2023. Year-over-year growth in core PCE inflation was 2.9% in December, and so the Committee has seen "good progress" toward its goals, but inflation is still "elevated".

• Outlook: "Almost everyone" favors cutting rates this year, suggesting there has been no change in views since the December SEP. But the Committee is in no hurry to get started and Chair Powell was confident enough to rule out March. We continue to expect the first rate cut in June. Powell noted the Fed will begin in-depth discussions on the balance sheet at the March meeting. We expect a slow taper of QT to begin in June, end in 1Q25.

• Rates: Our rates strategists think headline around the March cut, or timing of the first cut in general, has limited consequences for duration overall, and the totality of the Fed's communication is conducive to being long duration. They maintain long 5y USTs.

• FX: The USD shows a close relationship to the pace of cuts priced by the Fed and by central banks abroad. A large rise or decline in the implied pace of rate cuts in the US and abroad (either above six or below two in six months) in the US and abroad would likely boost USD. A continued modest pace of cuts implied for the Fed and other central banks (roughly three to five in six months) will likely weigh on the USD relative to yield differentials.

• MBS: Our MBS strategists maintain their neutral index positioning, suggesting that the 2019 cutting cycle is the right framework, and they think the index should trade between 40bp and 55bp.

## Batting Cleanup

Edits to the statement provide more clarity on the Committee's degree of patience while it debates the timing of the next rate adjustment. We are left with a cleaner readout that allows for a smoother transition when the Committee does decide to deliver the first cut, but it is in no hurry – according to Powell, cuts will happen "at some point". The Committee does not have enough "good" inflation data to cut in March. We continue to expect sticky services inflation will keep the Fed on hold until June – delivering 100bp in cuts in 2024 (Jun, Sep, Nov, Dec), and an additional 200bp in 2025.

The statement confirmed the Fed's focus remains largely on inflation, where there has been progress, but it is still elevated. More data are needed and therefore the Committee is not in a hurry to cut soon. "The Committee judges that the risks to achieving its employment and inflation goals are moving into better balance," but it maintains the Committee is "highly attentive to inflation risks" primarily because inflation is still "elevated". The statement refers to year-over-year inflation, where in December core PCE inflation was still 0.9pp above goal.

Forward guidance is now neutral, which helps policy shift more cleanly when the Fed is ready to begin cutting. In December, the Committee looked to incoming information to help it determine "the extent of any additional policy firming". However, now it will "carefully assess incoming data, the evolving outlook, and the balance of risks" in considering any adjustment to the target range for the federal funds rate. To balance the removal of a tightening bias, the statement added that the Committee "does not expect it will be appropriate to reduce the target range until it has gained greater confidence that inflation is moving sustainably toward 2%."

Finally, the paragraph around risks to the banking system – which had been inserted after a string of bank failures in March 2023 – and tighter financial conditions had become stale and was stricken from the statement.

In the press conference, Chair Powell commented he does "not think it is likely that the Committee will reach a level of confidence by the time of the March meeting," emphasizing he needs to see more "good data" on inflation to feel confident we are on the path toward sustainable 2% inflation. The last couple months of low inflation are not good enough. In particular, he is focused on the path forward for services inflation –

"goods inflation has been running significantly negative...this will flatten out...that would mean the services sectors have to contribute more" to overall disinflation. His commentary on inflation was slightly more hawkish, now focusing on the 12m measure for core inflation, which has been running higher than the previously cited 3m and 6m pace.

While Powell highlighted risks that inflation could accelerate, he sees a greater risk that inflation "would stabilize at a level meaningfully above 2%...if inflation were to surprise by moving back up, we would have to respond...this is why we are not rushing" to cut.

At this point, "almost everyone on the Committee is in favor of moving rates down this year" – in line with the December Summary of Economic Projections (SEP). What would get the Fed to cut earlier or later than its baseline? Chair Powell highlighted that "If we saw an unexpected weakening in...the labor market that would certainly...weigh on cutting sooner. Absolutely." On the other side, "If we saw inflation being stickier, or higher, or those sorts of things, we would argue for moving later."

On the balance sheet, the FOMC had "some discussion" at this meeting, but plans to begin an "in-depth discussion at the next meeting in March." The Fed sees lowering interest rates and the balance sheet runoff as independent tools – "For example, if you are normalizing policy, you might be reducing rates, but continuing to run off the balance sheet. In both cases that is normalization". We expect the Fed to initiate taper in June, cutting the pace of QT in half for Treasuries to \$30bn/month, but leaving MBS to run off with prepayments.

Our focus remains on a June start to cuts. As Powell reiterated, the FOMC does not yet have enough confidence that inflation is moving sustainably toward 2% and by taking March off the table (unless there is significant weakness in the labor market and inflation), the bar is now higher to cut rates sooner than later. Our forecast for inflation shows a 12m pace of inflation that continues to inch lower, but will be paired with upward pressure in the 3m and 6m annualized growth rates due to core services prices. All three measures will show core inflation well above 2% in the first quarter ( Exhibit 1 ).

## Justifying a June Start

By the June meeting, we forecast job gains will be around replacement rates and core inflation will have shown broad slowing that convinces FOMC members progress is sustainable. As inflation falls, real rates become more restrictive, and we think gaining consensus to cut will be easier. This suggests the Fed follows a Taylor rule construct, which Governor Waller has championed but which Chair Powell pushed back against in the Q&A – policymaking should not be rules based. Still, the disinflation will encourage those policymakers who are ready to lower rates.

Prior to this meeting, we noted the balance of risks to our June call were tilted earlier, but pushed back strongly against calls for a March cut. Even though the FOMC pushed back on near-term cuts more firmly than even we expected, risk still leans slightly toward an earlier cut, at the May meeting. New information on a leading indicator of rent CPI led us to revise lower our forecast for 2024 core PCE inflation, bringing it down to 2.2% 4Q/4Q vs. 2.3% previously. Chair Powell was asked about the data, which showed new tenant rents (NTR) plummeted in 4Q23 – "The question is when will these lower market rents find their way into measured rents, as measured in PCE inflation. We think that is coming. We know it is coming. It is just a question of when and how big it will be. So, that is in everyone's forecast, I would say."

Indeed, we have noted that we expect the Fed to show a similar revision to its inflation forecasts in the March SEP, which was confirmed by the Chair. "We will update our inflation forecast at the next meeting...it may be lower now given the data we have gotten." We think this comment refers to both the incoming inflation data and the NTR data, which participants are likely to include in forecast adjustments. A downward adjustment in inflation could bring consensus on cutting sooner, where the March SEP changes provide the justification to set up a May cut. However, it is just as likely participants use the downward revision to justify pulling another rate cut into the path, moving from 75bp to 100bp in cuts this year (in line with our view).

Exhibit 2 provides an update of our illustrative roadmap setting up conditions that need to be met for a cut before June. Based on Chair Powell's explicit commentary, we have raised the bar for a March cut. We now think that the two conditions that would trigger a cut next meeting are: (i) an average core CPI print below 0.2%M for January and February (vs. 0.25% prior), and (ii) NFP prints below 50k on average the first two months of the year (vs. 85k prior). However, note that to trigger and early cut, lower-than-expected inflation prints have to show material progress on the services side of the basket. As Chair Powell noted, “services sectors have to contribute more” to ensure a sustainable path down to 2% inflation.

## The Balance Sheet

Chair Powell acknowledged that the Committee will begin in-depth discussions of its balance sheet at the March meeting. As stated in its normalization plans, "the Committee intends to slow and then stop the decline in the size of the balance sheet when reserve balances are somewhat above the level it judges to be consistent with ample reserves."

We expect the announcement of a June start to tapering at the May meeting.

## The Fed's Balance Sheet in the Spotlight – Our Key Expectations

The Fed will start discussing tapering QT soon but will not end QT soon. Recent Fed communication has stirred market discussion about the end of QT. That narrative is overdone in our view. We see the Fed announcing the parameters of a taper to QT in May and initiating the taper in June. QT ends around February 2025, but could go on for longer. Key expectations:

• The Fed will cut the pace of QT in half for Treasuries, to \$30bn/month, but leave MBS to run off with prepayments.

• The SOMA portfolio falls about \$750bn from the current level. We see the RRP facility falling to roughly \$0, and QT ending in early 2025 when reserves are about \$3.2tn.

• Important parameters – If the SOFR – IORB spread goes to zero or turns positive, it will be important. If the standing repo facility (SRF) is used in size, it will also be a meaningful signal.

• The shift in supply of Treasuries relative to expectations will likely not be substantial, and the effects will be most noticeable in the front end and swap spreads, rather than longer-dated yields.

## "Increased confidence" on duration longs

March cut debate – a red herring for duration investors: The big headline from the January FOMC meeting is that, while the FOMC removed their tightening bias and opened the door to "adjustments", Powell does not think a March cut is "likely". Ultimately, the timing of the rate cut comes down to having "greater confidence" on inflation, and Powell noted that it was not likely that he would have confidence by the March meeting. Our economists do not see a rate cut until June this year.

We think headlines around the March cut, or timing of the first cut in general, has limited consequences beyond the Fed funds contract pricing for the March meeting. We do not think the totality of the Fed's communication today was a detriment to being long duration. We maintain our long duration – long 5y USTs.

For a start, the pricing of a March cut met with an equal and opposite reaction on the June rate-cut pricing (see Exhibit 6 ). For most duration investors, the debate is less about whether the Fed starts cutting in March and more about a general cutting trajectory in the coming months, and Powell did not suggest anything that would dissuade the market from pricing a series of cuts in 2024.

Powell on base case for labor markets: In a base case, the economy is performing well and the labor market remains strong. If we saw an unexpected weakening in, certainly in the labor market, that would certainly weigh on cutting sooner. Absolutely.

Powell on the link between growth and supply: A lot of the growth we are seeing, it isn't just a tug-of-war between interest rates and demand. You are getting, you know, more activity because of labor market healing, and supply chains healing. So, I think the question is, when that peters out, I think the restriction will show up more sharply.

Markets, which operate in probabilities, and not binary calls, rightly decreased the probability of a March cut, but the chances still hover around 40%. Between now and March, there are 2 CPI reports and one CPI revisions plus 2 labor market prints and wage prints – all of which could decide whether the Fed can get "increased confidence" on inflation. Our economists do not expect much change from the CPI revisions. We expect markets to price a healthy probability of a March cut at least for the next few weeks.

It is especially notable that shelter inflation could cool in the coming months based on leading indicators like the "New Tenant Rent" index, which dropped sharply in its latest reading (see Exhibit 7 ). Rent inflation downshifts could go a long way in helping with a sense of "increased confidence". That's because shelter inflation prints tend to be sticky and one low print can convince investors that the median CPI path has been reset lower (see Exhibit 8 ).

## USD outlook

Over the past year, the US dollar has traded broadly in line with the market-implied pace of Fed cuts. The yellow line in Exhibit 9 shows the number of cuts markets have priced during the six after the Fed is priced to reach its terminal rate.

Exhibit 10 shows the same chart, but with the right axis reversed. The chart illustrates that the USD has risen as fewer (or a slower pace of) cuts are priced, and fallen as more (or a faster pace of) cuts are priced.

![](images/2a1ecd86d597ab61ae90e696344e145194ecad702ac45a93153b41de0076968e.jpg)
Source: Bloomberg, Morgan Stanley Research

As a result, we expect that the timing of the first Fed (whether in March, May, or June) will have less impact on the USD than whether the Fed is expected to cut quickly or slowly once it starts cutting.

However, this relationship is likely conditional on the reason why the Fed is cutting.

A slow pace of cuts is likely USD positive because it boosts US yields, but also because it is associated with US economic outperformance.

A swift but moderate pace of cuts (say, 4-5 cuts in six months) is likely USD negative because it is emblematic of decelerating inflation and roughly one 25bp Fed cut per meeting. That outcome is consistent with relatively resilient (but not outperforming) US growth. In other words, a USD-negative goldilocks environment (not too cold, not too hot).

An precipitous pace of cuts (for example, MSP1UT above six implying six or more cuts priced in six months) might no longer be USD negative, because multiple 50bp cuts in short succession might be associated with sharply decelerating US and global growth. In such a scenario, other central banks are likely cutting quickly and investors are seeking safe haven assets likely denominated in USD.

Exhibit 11: Central banks abroad are priced to cut faster than they were six months ago

As a result, USD has shown little relationship to the differential between the pace of cuts priced in the US and the pace of cuts priced for other central banks.

As inflation has declined outside the US over the past six months, swap pricing has implied a faster pace of cuts from central banks abroad ( Exhibit 11 ).

Exhibit 12 shows the spread between these lines, illustrating that the Fed is no longer alone in being expected to cut swiftly. The Fed is priced to cut only modestly faster than other central banks – in contrast to pricing roughly one year ago.

This faster (but still modest) pace of cutting outside the US (i.e., 3-4 25bp cuts in six months) reflects dynamics similar to what we observe in the US: decelerating inflation paired with a slowing-but-not-collapsing pace of growth.

Yield differentials have moved in the US's favor over the past six months, in part due to this rise in the market-implied pace of cuts abroad (see Exhibit 13 and Exhibit 14 ).

The dynamic described above may partially explain why the USD has underperformed what this move in yield differentials would imply.

The pace of cutting currently priced both in the US and abroad signals not-too-hot, nottoo-slow growth outside the US and the drop in global inflation pressures. That is an environment in which investors may feel comfortable adding to carry trades funded in USD – offsetting any USD advantage from yield differential moves in the US's favor.

Starting at the June meeting, our economists expect the Fed to cut three times in six months – somewhat less than implied by current market pricing. Any resulting decline in the market-implied pace of Fed cuts would likely boost USD.

However, the recent move to price fast cuts abroad is consistent with a benign, falling inflation global economic environment, and will likely limit near-term USD gains. As a result, we remain neutral on the broad USD.

A meaningful decline in the implied pace of rate cuts in the US and abroad (if US and global inflation unexpectedly re-accelerates, for example) would likely boost USD. Similarly, a rise in P1UT above six in the US and abroad would also probably lead to USD gains.

Inversely, a continued modest pace (3-5) of cuts implied for the Fed and other central banks will likely continue to weigh on the USD relative to yield differentials.

Mortgage Backed Securities (MBS) and Collateralized Mortgage Obligations (CMO)

Principal is returned on a monthly basis over the life of the security. Principal prepayment can significantly affect the monthly income stream and the maturity of any type of MBS, including standard MBS, CMOs and Lottery Bonds. Yields and average lives are estimated based on prepayment assumptions and are subject to change based on actual prepayment of the mortgages in the underlying pools. The level of predictability of an MBS/CMO's average life, and its market price, depends on the type of MBS/CMO class purchased and interest rate movements. In general, as interest rates fall, prepayment speeds are likely to increase, thus shortening the MBS/CMO's average life and likely causing its market price to rise. Conversely, as interest rates rise, prepayment speeds are likely to decrease, thus lengthening average life and likely causing the MBS/CMO's market price to fall. Some MBS/CMOs may have "original issue discount" (OID). OID occurs if the MBS/CMO’s original issue price is below its stated redemption price at maturity, and results in "imputed interest" that must be reported annually for tax purposes, resulting in a tax liability even though interest was not received.
