## Economics

Encouraging signs that Western Europe consumption is lifting, amid evidence of Germany’s persistent industry woes. We believe that global growth is poised to moderate to a trend pace during 1H24, as US consumer and global tech spending gains cool. This moderation is not, however, likely to be a signal of weakness if we are right that a modest lifting materializes in last year<sup>’</sup>s weak links—Western Europe and global manufacturing. A firming in US energy and core inflation looks likely to promote a downshift in US consumption. As the US consumer cools, we look for an offset from Western Europe where falling inflation and elevated wage gains (tracking >4.5%ar) are boosting purchasing power. Consistent with this picture, Euro area consumer confidence continued to recover in Feb, and we saw increases in the flash all-industry PMIs for both the Euro area and UK. The details of the Euro area PMI were less favorable as the manufacturing PMI slipped and remains in contractionary territory; relative weakness in German manufacturing suggest structural impediments in German industry will likely temper the regional lift.

Brazil and Mexico growth looks set to re-accelerate. In Brazil, we anticipate above-trend growth in 2024 on the back of strength in private sector balance sheets and recent productivity gains, particularly in the oil sector. Renewed signs of strong growth, as well as a temporary overshoot of 1Q core CPI and the tightening of global rates, have reduced downside risk to SELIC rates. Mexico<sup>’</sup>s GDP also should snap back on strong domestic demand and fiscal spending head of elections. But this outcome also should keep services inflation high. Thus, while we expect Banxico to cut as soon as March, rates should stay elevated and restrictive (9.75%) through the end of this year (GDW, Feb 23<sup>rd</sup>).

## Equities

1970s and Risk of a Second Wave, Elections and Markets, Positioning. We believe that there is a risk of the narrative turning back from goldilocks towards something like 1970s stagflation, with significant implications for asset allocation. In the 1970s, high inflation came in 3 separate waves, all in some ways related to geopolitical developments, equity markets were essentially flat in nominal terms, and bonds outperformed significantly. We see upside risks to inflation given loose financial conditions, tight labor markets, high government spending, and geopolitics (as the 1980s-2000s <sup>‘</sup>peace dividend<sup>’</sup> unwinds). Another macro risk, related to risks for global trade, geopolitical developments and inflation, is the US elections this year. Our view is that there is likely no market upside related to the US election, as the outcome is either status quo or increased uncertainty related to global trade and geopolitical/domestic tensions. Investor positioning has

increased significantly and presents an increasing headwind for the market (Market and Volatility Commentary, Feb 21<sup>st</sup>).

Three key drivers of to-date resilient corporate profitability to turn weaker. Bulls are to a good extent basing their constructive market call on the premise that corporate profits are set to accelerate, but the earnings reality might turn out to be the opposite as we move through the year. Corporate profit margins are elevated in a historical context, and appear to be peaking out. Historically, profit margins always start to move lower ahead of the next economic downturn. We see 3 sources of downside to profit margins and earnings: 1) net interest expense increasing as time passes; 2) weakening pricing power; and 3) a pickup in unit labor costs. With the above 3 points one might see a disappointing profits outcome even without a recession, and we note that 2024 EPS projections keep coming down in key regions (Equity Strategy, Feb 25<sup>th</sup>).

4Q23 Earnings Update. With 88% of S&P 500 companies having reported, 71% are beating 4Q earnings by an average of 5%, and 63% are beating revenue estimates by 1% on average. Since the beginning of the earnings season,1Q24 and 2Q24 have experienced downward revisions of -1.6% and -1.0%, respectively, while 2024 EPS estimates have been revised down -0.1% (US Equity Strategy, Feb 26<sup>th</sup>).

Incrementally more positive on China equities on emerging upside catalysts since LNY. ## Bonds

Bond yields rose over the past two weeks, led by the frontend, amid stronger US inflation and activity data, as well as central bank rhetoric that has pushed against near-term easing expectations. The extent of the re-pricing of Fed expectations, and a historically high share of neutrals in our Treasury Client Survey, means there is scope for yields to decline over the medium term as the uncertainty introduced by recent inflation and labor market data fades. We stay long 5Y USTs, and keep 5s/30s steepeners and 2s/5s flatteners.

In the Euro area, stronger-than-expected Euro area flash PMIs and push-back from ECB speakers on near-term easing expectations contributed to the bear-flattening. Markets now price an ECB terminal rate at 2.4% vs. our expectation of 2%, and we hold longs in 5Y Germany and keep bearish hedges via option structures. In the UK, we see scope for the curve to price in further BoE easing and add Aug24/Nov24 MPC OIS flatteners to Mar24/Sep24 SONIA futures curve flatteners.

In EM, the global environment is a good one for EM fixed income from a cyclical standpoint, though risks around the Fed outlook remain a headwind. We stay OW local bond duration, with OWs in Latam (Brazil, Colombia, Uruguay), EMEA EM (Czechia, Poland, South Africa), and EM Asia (Indonesia), partially offset by a short duration overlay and an UW in Romania (EM Fixed Income Focus, Feb 15<sup>th</sup>).

## Credit

US HG bond spreads have been in a narrow 5bp range for the past 2.5 weeks. This is an impressive outcome with the record pace of supply YTD and spreads close to post-GFC tights. A strong risk-on environment has been supportive for spreads, and the demand for HG credit is being driven by attractive yields and the widely held expectation that yields will be lower in the future. This has proven incorrect YTD, but so far higher yields seem to be increasing demand for the asset class rather than causing investors to reconsider (Credit Market Outlook & Strategy, Feb 23<sup>rd</sup>).

Asia investors continue to be somewhat reluctant buyers of US IG. Overseas investors constitute 28% of the total US corporate bond ownership, the largest slice of the pie and the second-fastest growing segment. Overnight trading volumes have risen 24% so far this year. Asian investors<sup>’</sup> reluctance to buy US IG has increased with increasingly expensive valuations but, for now, there are few attractive alternatives to put money to work as the Asia credit universe is shrinking and JGB yields are still low (Credit Watch, Feb 23<sup>rd</sup>).

Inflows continue to drive European credit spreads tighter despite disappointing total returns YTD. We don<sup>’</sup>t yet see evidence of extreme crowding in credit markets, judging by the performance of primary, and metrics such as HY fund betas. Investors have taken a slower pace of rate cuts in their stride, but with spreads at tight levels there is diminishing room for high beta credit instruments to absorb incremental rises in government yields should the disinflation story be challenged further. As a result, we recommend shortening duration in subordinated paper and prefer to source duration in senior debt instead (European Credit Weekly, Feb 23<sup>rd</sup>).

CEMBI BD STW dropped below 250bp, still 40-50bp above post GFC tights. The arguments for low spreads include higher quality composition, reasonable pick-up over DM credit, attractive yields, stable fundamentals, and normalizing default rate. We also believe new issuance picked up just enough to encourage interest in the asset class but not lead to a supply overhang. This suggests CEMBI spread can compress further if the macro environment remains supportive (CEMBI Segment Recommendations, Feb 23<sup>rd</sup>).

## Uptick in yield offers entry point for duration, although less rate cuts ahead

Fed minutes reinforced a theme of patient approach to cutting rates, with more participants worried about the risk of cutting too early rather than the potential for waiting too long. Regarding financial stability, the staff highlighted asset class valuations as a source of vulnerability. Several participants expressed a concern that financial conditions could become too easy while others noted that conditions are tighter than before the hiking cycle (Figure 8). All in all, there was nothing to change our economists<sup>’</sup> view on the timing of the first cut, which they continue to expect in June. Perhaps more interesting is the reduction in the amount of cumulative cutting expected this year from 125bps to \~80bps, which arguably limits the upside in bonds, although we are pricing in 4 cuts which is more than the current market expectation. With equities and credit pricing little to no risk of recession, underappreciated growth risk can provide a rationale for bonds to utilize the full range of their diversification benefit.

ECB minutes similarly echoed a cautious tone, more on the hawkish side, sticking to data dependence rather than early cuts. Europe<sup>’</sup>s data this week was encouraging on growth while showing stickiness on core inflation. Euro area composite PMI rose, while the manufacturing PMI fell only slightly, masking a sharper decline in Germany. As in the US, the combination of better-than-expected growth, stickier inflation, and Central Banks pushing back against premature cuts, has shifted the pricing from 2Q to 3Q24 for the start of cutting for most DM rates markets, although the market still prices June <sup>’</sup>24 as the start for the Euro area. Like the US, any front-loading of DM cuts in 1H24 has been pushed into 2H24 and 1H25, with the bulk of the cutting in 2H24. Over the past 25 years, rate cutting has been fairly in sync and we expect it to be even more synchronized in this cycle. In additional to favoring intermediate long positions like 5y Germany, we also expect the pattern of broad steepening of the yield curves will re-emerge: beginning 2-3 months before the first cut and continuing 6 months after in the Euro Area and US, although tapering off earlier in UK (Figure 9).

As for the ytd moves in US bond yields, we view the uptick in yields will provide a good entry point for getting long duration, as we continue to be OW bonds in our asset allocation. The uptick in yields is logical given the revision of our 1Q24 US growth from 1.25% to 1.75%, and inflation expectations rising back to the middle of their range (Figure 10). The strength suggested by the January data is likely not sustainable and given moderating growth and inflation, we see a downward bias to rates for the rest of 2024. Positioning was fairly long in January, and it has since neutralized, with macro hedge funds and bond funds lightening up on exposure. Within our Client Survey, this put the percent neutral at 85%, which is the highest level since 2011, and a level we have seen only a handful of times in the past 20 years. Given the heightened uncertainty, we feel there is a need for greater risk premium than initially expected, so we have raised our yearend targets for both the long and the short end, revising our US 10y year-end target 15bps higher at 3.8%.

As for the earnings season, we highlight the growing divergence between the US and Europe (Figure 11). With 80% having reported in the S&P 500 vs 55% for Stoxx600, EPS for the quarter is +7% yoy in US vs -11% in Europe. US companies beat expectations with an EPS surprise of +8% vs. expectations, while Europe disappointed by 2%. The results support the outperformance of US vs European equities and are in line with our regional allocation favoring the US vs Europe. Apart from Tesla, the Magnificent 7 have had a strong quarter, with Communication services, Tech and Discretionary being the biggest contributors to Q4 EPS growth, although the Mag7 result may worsen the market concentration. In Japan, 94% have reported, with EPS growth at 10%. Sales beats are in-line with historical averages while earnings beats are slightly below. The situation in Japan is atypical, with stocks at highs in the midst of a technical recession, although we continue to be OW given the structural changes with TSE reform.

In China, we are viewing the market moves through the lens of five domestic cycles in our analytical framework for China equities: the business cycle, earnings cycle, property cycle, credit cycle, and the domestic rebalancing cycle. There are reasons for both optimism and caution at different time frames. As for the recent dramatic rally we<sup>’</sup>ve seen in February, the question is whether the Lunar New Year uplift and short-covering can provide a durable uplift, with Chinese indices looking stretched in the near-term with an RSI near 70. For the domestic rebalancing cycle, the appointment of the new CRSC chairman points to better protection for investors, such as accountability for all involved in IPOs, e.g. listing firms, brokers, accounting firms, etc. As for the business and earnings cycle update, 2024 LNY consumption was better than expected, with volume indicators exceeding comparable periods in 2019. According to PBoC, online transaction value during the LNY period for entertainment (catering, lodging, retail, etc.) rose by >20% yoy, and data showed hotel bookings for major counties and T3-4 cities surged 120%/\~200% y-y. On the property cycle, while housing activity has remained soft, with home sales in terms of area and value both down \~25% yoy, total financing for 65 leading developers rose +25.5% m/m from Dec to Jan with bond issuance rising by +27.4% m/m. As for the credit cycle, the large rise in the medium- to long-term residential loans in the January credit data likely reflected a decline in mortgage prepayment with the rate differential having compressed between existing and new mortgages. On policy measures, the 5y LPR, the benchmark rate for mortgage loan pricing, was lowered by 25bp (vs consensus 10bp) to 3.95%, while the 1y LPR remains unchanged, with the asymmetric LPR adjustment a response to weak housing market activity.

Despite reasons for optimism, worries about geopolitics continue to weigh on China. Currently, there are 3 active geopolitical conflict zones – eastern Europe, Middle East, and South China Sea. Related to these conflicts we already saw one wave of an energy crisis, and current shipping disruptions in the Red Sea. By far the largest risk is tensions or a trade war with China that would have a much bigger impact on the global economy and would lead to a significant second wave of inflation and market selloff. So, in a reversal of the 1980s to 2000s when the West was enjoying positive feedback loops from the <sup>‘</sup>peace dividend<sup>’</sup>, expanding trade and importing disinflation from Asia, we have the risk of this being replaced by a <sup>‘</sup>conflict tax<sup>’</sup>, de-globalization, and friend-shoring inflation.

Taking a longer-term perspective, a pattern of rising stock markets against a backdrop of falling leading economic indicators (LEI) is reminiscent of other late-cycle melt-ups. Even in the case where the LEIs don<sup>’</sup>t signal imminent recession but rather economic headwinds, the current contrast within the components is worth noting, with buoyant stocks themselves being one of the LEIs along with tight credit spreads, new orders prone to rise given low inventories, consumer expectations still supported by a strong jobs market, and at the other extreme the bearish message sent by the yield curve steepness. In general, having a big disconnect between stocks and the LEIs has historically ended badly, with a late-cycle melt-up eventually succumbing to a growth slowdown (Figure 12).

## The Long-term Strategist: Will Americans continue to love equities?, Jan Loeys

This move is not imminent, but should be an important factor in deciding whether to stay fully allocated to US equities vs the rest of the world.

## Global house prices: Turning a corner after the correction, Joseph Lupton

Despite moderating significantly from the boomy gains of 2022, appreciation in house prices in many countries remains solid, including the US. With financial conditions now easing, including a fading in the pace of tightening credit standards and broader financial stress, we think the threat of a housing shock looks limited. While the turn back up in house prices is encouraging, the elevated levels remain a concern for housing affordability and sustainability. Furthermore, healthy fundamentals temper exuberance concerns, but loss of labor income could spark sharp correction.
