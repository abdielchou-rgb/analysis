Building on our November chartbook, this updated analysis examines some of the profound geopolitical and defence shifts that are reshaping modern defence priorities.

The escalation in the Middle East has highlighted 3 trends:

1. Renewed focus on conventional defence capabilities to address state-based threats even as many G7 militaries lag strategic competitors.

2. Geopolitical realignments, with the US potentially recalibrating its strategic focus amid regional tensions. The 2024 US election will likely continue to impact this trajectory.

3. Evolving nature of conflict, driven by factors like hightech innovation, geoeconomic fractures, and non-state actors, has been fuelling more complex and frequent crises worldwide.

In this updated chartbook, we analyse (i) how these dynamics are transforming national security in the 21st century; (ii) the latest events including the Israel-Iran-Palestine conflict; (iii) evolving US and European priorities and spending; and we provide (iv) an updated 2024 outlook for defence equities.

The recent conflicts in Ukraine and the Israel/Gaza region demonstrated that conventional warfare continues to play a key role in defence. These conflicts have revealed vulnerabilities stemming from reductions in conventional capabilities.

Prior to these conflicts, investment in conventional defence had waned.

The lack of investment in Main Battle Tanks (MBTs) in the past two decades left Western economies, except the US (6% of total MBTs), with minimal MBT strength.

Of the G7 and NATO, only the US ranks among the top ten countries in terms of defence personnel, as other G7 countries prioritise leaner, higher-skilled personnel.

## US geopolitical priorities are shifting

The US is undergoing a rebalancing of geopolitical priorities with (i) greater emphasis on domestic affairs; (ii) attention on tensions in the Middle East; (iii) intensifying high-tech competition with China.

In the 2025 FY DoD budget, \$4.2bn, \$17.1bn, and \$9.9bn are allocated to Ukraine, the Middle East, and the Indo-Pacific respectively. This excludes supplemental funding requests.

The US looks to support Israel’s defence through security assistance and to deter attacks through funding defence systems such as the Iron Dome.

In the Asia Pacific, the US is focused on strengthening deterrence against China and building a resilient security architecture.

## Middle East

If further escalation occurs, Israel is likely to respond (given history), potentially with support from allies, including the US.

After the April 13 missile strike, President Biden stated Washington would not be involved in retaliation against Iran.

## Asia-Pacific

On April 8, the AUKUS group of Australia, the UK and the US said it was considering cooperation with Japan, which has defence partnerships with all three countries, on advanced capabilities.

In Dec 2023, Japan approved its largest-ever defence budget of \$55.9bn for 2024FY.

The status quo seems unlikely to change anytime soon in Taiwan. The US is expected to increase shipbuilding efforts, with defence through marine systems.

## High-tech innovation

States, firms, and non-state actors investing in technological superiority, e.g. recent US Israel aid allocates an additional \$5.2bn to the Iron Dome, David’s Sling and Iron Beam defence.

Includes big data, AI, autonomous weaponry, and hypersonic weapons e.g. Ukraine employing AI in conflict with Russia, use of SpaceX’s Starlink in Ukraine and Middle East.

‒ High-tech defence capabilities can act as a deterrent to escalation.

## Deglobalisation

Alliances and economic relationships could see conflict become multidomain e.g. Houthi attacks in Red Sea.

Economic policies increasingly used defensively, to build self-sufficiency (US CHIPS Act), deployed to prevent the rise of others.

Fewer neutral countries e.g. Switzerland draws closer to NATO, while Argentina, Ethiopia, Egypt, Iran, UAE, Saudi Arabia join BRICs.

## Non-state actors

Terrorist organisations (Houthis, Hezbollah, Hamas), and private armies (Wagner Group), challenging statecentric models of governance, with power over geopolitical chokepoints.

Increased risk of a proliferation of new technologies to non-state actors e.g. drone use by Houthis in the Red Sea.

‒ Non-state actors not subject to same degree of training and codes of conduct.

## US Presidential Election

On April 20, the House of Representatives passed a \$95bn foreign aid supplemental package for Ukraine, Israel and the Indo-Pacific, after months of delay. It includes:

\$61bn to Ukraine, including \$13.8bn for security assistance, \$13.4bn for the replenishment of US stocks, and \$9.5bn in forgivable economic loans.

\$26bn to Israel, including \$13bn for defence activities and \$3.6bn for security assistance.

\$8bn to Indo-Pacific, including \$5.6bn for deterrence.

Previously, the Republican-controlled House had not passed any requests by President Biden for emergency aid for Ukraine since December 2022.

Candidate Trump has signalled openness to approving additional aid to Ukraine, but only in the form of a loan, not unconditional aid.

## US budget outlook: Balancing priorities amid rising costs

DB equity analysts expect a 2-3% annual budget increase over the next five years, save for new conflict or escalation. However, sustaining longer-term funding for Taiwan, Ukraine and Israel would be difficult.

A key challenge: interest-servicing costs have surged 1.7 times since 2020, with 2023 interest payments (\$890bn) exceeding of Department of Defense budget (\$852bn).

As Ukraine-Russia conflict becomes entrenched, and US priorities shift away from the region, the EU‘s defence spending increased 4.5% to reach a record €270bn (\$295bn) in 2023. Acquisitions from outside the EU accounted for 78% of EU countries’ 2022-2023 procurement commitments, with 63% sourced from the US.

In March 2024, the European Commission presented the first EU level European Defence Industrial Strategy, with key goals:

(i) Procure at least 40% of defence equipment in collaborative manner by 2030;

(ii) Value of intra-EU defence trade to reach at least 35% of the value of EU defence market;

(iii) Procure at least 50% of the defence procurement budget within the EU by 2030.

## Financing the future: supporting SMEs and start-ups

In January 2024, the European Investment Fund launched a €175mn Defence Equity Facility to support SMEs and start-ups.

Financing agreements signed under the Strategic European Security Initiative exceed €2bn.

In April 2024, European Investment Bank (EIB) stated its intention to update its definition of dual-use projects and extend credit to SMEs and startups in the security and defence sectors.

‒ The EIB has earmarked €6bn for the security and defence sector.

## EU EQUITIES

Increasing conflicts support EU defence budgets – Recent Iran attack underlines growing global conflicts, supporting pressure for Europe to sustainably boost defence spending. EU forced to find more ways of funding defence spending quickly. Proposed EU-wide defence bond issue likely to be a good sentiment boost for EU defence stocks.

Growth visibility until 2030. Order intake (ex-ammunitions) is still slow to materialise in the EU, implying higher defence spending should continue to support higher order intake (book to bill >1x) at least until 2027/28.

Solid equity story. EU companies could see multiple avenues of growth: prospects of annual sales increases of >6%, higher margins and greater FCF generation driven by financials and prepayments on orders.

Targeted M&A - Capital allocation to support targeted M&A in def. to gain expertise/capacity even as focus remains on dividends and share buybacks.

EU companies valuation getting richer, but not overly so – EU companies' growth fundamentals significantly re-rated post Ukraine. Historical valuation discounts of most EU defence companies vs US peers has narrowed sharply. But geopolitical catalysts and growth outperformance should support valuation.

European firms to capitalise on greater international presence. Greater geographical diversification than US peers puts them in a more favourable position for international tenders. EU firms should gain more from expanded defence spending in Asia, Africa and Middle East, driving up order intake and sales growth.

![](images/303bd1920edbfa20c7f452c0649a0f951f3b5ee784c45904fbdb9354e0710100.jpg)

Competitive pressure: Hanwha and Elbit made commercial forays in Europe (Poland) on aggressive pricing and strong execution.

Managing growth. Unlike US firms, EU firms need to transition from a slow-growth to a high-growth environment. Failure to adapt corporate structures to the new environment could lead to execution issues.

## US EQUITIES

Strong growth visibility: Given recent backlog expansion and our outlook for continued budget growth, we expect US contractors to sustain 5%+ sales growth over the coming years. Potential upside stems from the possibility of Congress passing supplemental defence funding, supporting HSD/LDD revenue growth through CY26. Companies with heavy exposure to tactical munitions, air and missile defence, combat vehicles, and high-priority strategic platforms (e.g. bombers, ICBMs, ballistic missile submarines) all appear positioned to outgrow the market.

IRR outlook favourable: We expect defence stocks to deliver strong returns over coming years, primarily driven by FCF/share growth as revenue growth is sustained in the mid-single digits, margins expand modestly, and share repurchases continue. Multiple expansion may be warranted if supplemental defence spend is passed by a divided Congress, signifying less-than-feared gridlock risk.

U.S Presidential election: We think the 2024 election is more likely to be a positive catalyst, with opportunities for defence to benefit both from a Republican or a Democrat power shift. The main negative scenario for defence stocks would be a status quo outcome, in which the House and Senate remain split.

International growth: Substantial opportunity for US firms to increase sales internationally in tactical aircraft, army munitions, and/or air and missile defence systems. Eastern Europe, APAC, and Middle East all areas with continued growth prospects.

Supplier costs: Supplier costs remain one of the largest risks for defence companies. Many defence contractors have long-term fixed price contracts in their books of business, and increased supplier costs can drive margin pressure. This has been a trend over the last several years and remains a risk for the future.
