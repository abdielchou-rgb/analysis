## Euro Area

• Euro area sentiment increased as Germany lags

• But, German services activity has improved and the labor market remains strong

• French deficit at 5.5% in 2023; slippage likely this year as well

This week was relatively light in terms of data. On the activity front, the EC sentiment data continued to increase, reinforcing the positive signal from the PMI survey last week. At this stage, the surveys are suggesting that growth has improved to a 0.75%ar and that therefore any further increase would create some near-term upside risk to our forecast. At the same time, the EC survey shows Germany lagging behind, especially due to its manufacturing sector. In the PMI, the latter has been holding the composite output index back in a modest contraction territory. Incoming official activity data are not challenging this message, although the labor market continues to be solid. This highlights the very mixed signals being sent about the German economy. We will take stock of this after seeing more of the February activity data, especially IP and any mid-month revisions to retail sales.

On the inflation side, the price expectations balances in the EC survey declined again in March after some recent gains (Figure 1). But, even through the recent changes, it remains at a more modest level, suggesting that the GDP deflator could slow to around a 3%oya pace in the coming quarters. The drivers of this are less clear. Unit labor cost growth is still firm reflecting solid wage growth and weakness in labor productivity. Similarly, it is possible that unit profits have softened, but this is uncertain going forward as the PMI has shown some improvement in growth, making it easier for firms to protect their margins. But, while the drivers of the moderation signaled by the EC price balances are unclear, the survey is suggestive of some moderation in domestic price setting. The next test of this will be the upcoming March inflation reports, which will likely include some upward distortions from the early Easter.

## Sentiment increased further

The EC sentiment survey increased 0.8pt to 96.3 in March, another positive signal for activity after last week<sup>’</sup>s PMI increase (Figure 2). Through the noise, the survey remained on the upward trend seen since the autumn and the signal of the survey is still a bit firmer than the signal of the PMI (which suggested growth at 0.75% ar at the end of 1Q).

Figure 2: Euro area EC economic sentiment and PMI Standard deviations from 1999-2019 avg
![](images/18a6f0398c4c16304df7febc035c850598a6ab37d952a07a962999263aaf7dbc.jpg)

Looking at the details, the March sentiment increase was broad based, but there is still a large level gap between the manufacturing sector (+0.6%-pt to -8.8%bal) and the services sector (+0.3%-pt to +6.3%bal). Consumer confidence meanwhile increased 0.6%-pt to -14.9%, an encouraging step linked to real income gains, although the survey remains below its long run average (we expect some further gains in the coming months).

At the country level, the economic sentiment survey stands at similar levels in France, Italy and Spain (100.7, 100.9, 102.0, respectively, and above their long run averages), while it remained depressed in Germany at just 89.8 (Figure 3). This echoes the PMI, in which Germany is also underperforming, largely due to its manufacturing sector.

## German data sending mixed signals

The German economy is currently underperforming not just in the EC survey but also in the PMI. This week<sup>’</sup>s official

data highlighted however the unusual crosscurrents in the broader data flow. This week showed a large decline in retail sales in February, with Jan/Feb now almost 7%ar below 4Q23 (Figure 3). However, it is well known that German retail sales are volatile and highly prone to revision, with late responses often leading to upward revisions. Nevertheless, it is hard to see the entire miss in February getting revised away, while a rebound in March is uncertain.

Figure 4: German retail sales volumes
![](images/2254988625c79619283dd538bbc54d27ce3bede3404a047dfe83ebf7d7d9e17f.jpg)

At the same time, the labor market continues to support German households. This week<sup>’</sup>s report showed employment rising further in February. The monthly gain was smaller than in January, but the trend has accelerated to almost a 1%ar (Figure 5). This has not been sufficient to prevent unemployment from still nudging higher, reflecting firmer gains in the labor force. But, there is still no sign of any job losses, which would likely be more damaging for the household sector. Even the usage of the short-time work subsidy scheme remains relatively low and has not picked up meaningfully, despite the ongoing weakness in GDP growth.

Figure 5: Germany employment growth
![](images/dbc13801002d2a732c8ba765f63e221e1459f8c9905cc2d8896aac73b0aa6250.jpg)

Similarly, vacancies continued to edge lower in March, but the official data only capture vacancies registered with the Job Agencies. The more comprehensive data from the IAB have also moved lower but are still far above the prepandemic peak (Figure 6). This suggests that labor shortages remain high, consistent with the EC survey.

## French budget: Large 2023 miss

The French statistics office released this week its first estimate of the 2023 general government deficit and debt. The numbers show a significantly larger deficit and debt level than the government expected. The general government deficit last year increased 0.7%-pt to 5.5% of GDP (€154bn), compared to the 4.9% government forecast (released in September 2023 as part of the 2024 budget bill). Meanwhile, the debt-to-GDP ratio only declined 1.3%-pts to 110.6%, instead of reaching the 108.1% government forecast (Table 1).

In the details, public revenues rose 2.0% in 2023, while expenditures rose 3.7%. Weak GDP growth weighed on revenues, but there were also some specific drags: taxes on real estate transactions were down €4.8bn (0.2% of GDP) due to the weaker real estate market, the scrapping of some business taxes was worth €3.7bn (0.1%) and corporate income taxes were down €10.7bn (0.4%) following strong 2022 incomes. On the expenditure side, energy support measures were worth €24.3bn last year (0.9% of GDP) and some stronger expenses were linked to the high level of inflation last year (wages and social benefits are partly indexed).

With the 2023 numbers in hand, the 4.4% deficit forecast is ambitious for this year. It is reasonable to assume that the 0.6%-pt deficit miss in 2023 carries over into 2024, taking 2024 closer to 5.0%. Earlier in the month, the government acknowledged this risk and announced extra spending cuts worth €10bn for 2024 (i.e. 0.3% of GDP). But this would only fill half of the 0.6%-pt gap and leave the deficit at 4.7%, which is what we expect for 2024.
