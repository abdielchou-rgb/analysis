The UK has endured a decade of uncertainty and change. Despite it all, the country is in better shape than many people realise. Although it has been shaken by the years of instability, its institutions remain intact, business confidence is returning, and many corporates have realised that, after several years of experimentation, the UK’s advantages are very difficult to replicate in other countries.

Yet, with polls indicating a change in government for the first time since 2010, the UK is finely poised. It has the foundations for medium-term growth, however, the response to its economic and societal problems will influence the speed and direction of that growth. In turn, corporates will need to adapt.

In this presentation, we highlight why the UK can navigate its near-term challenges if it embraces its longer-term strengths. We point out factors including: relatively favourable demographics, the depth of financial markets (including private capital) and the potential within the large pension industry.

We expect the rest of the decade to be a good one for UK companies and equities. That is despite the concern about the state of the UK’s capital markets, such as the slowing rate of IPOs, challenging credit conditions for smaller companies, and the low rate of corporate net capital issuance. Our calculations show that UK equities are generally resilient as economic uncertainty increases – a phenomenon that lends weight to some of the UK’s structural advantages.

Geopolitics will be an ever-present feature of the rest of the 2020s. This year’s US presidential election could be highly consequential for the UK and its desire for a trade deal. Relations with China may evolve differently under a new UK government. Meanwhile, the impact of Brexit will continue to be felt in many ways. Already, many UK companies are accelerating reshoring efforts, something that is becoming a greater feature of a deglobalising world.

Looking ahead, while the UK has substantial challenges, it also has the tools to be a corporate and financial powerhouse. For corporates, the way they adapt to the changes of a potential new UK government will be key. Just as important will be how they respond to the global economic and geopolitical winds that are likely to grow stronger as the decade progresses.
