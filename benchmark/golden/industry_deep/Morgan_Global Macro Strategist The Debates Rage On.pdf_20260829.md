# Global Macro Strategist

# The Debates Rage On

To ease, or not to ease, that is the question. While several central banks have answered the question already, even those that have answered "yes" struggle to answer the follow ups: how much to ease and how quickly? The debates will not conclude next week, but the first US presidential debate will.

## Global Macro Strategy

We discuss the circularity between central bank and government policies, economic activity, and markets. We show how small businesses may play an interesting role in the current cycle. We also discuss one aspect of our midyear outlook: our forecast for a rising USD and lower UST yields.

## Interest Rate Strategy

In the US, we stay neutral on duration, and maintain receive July FOMC OIS swap. In the euro area, we maintain our structural receive EUR 1y1y, and maintain our long ERZ4 97.125 call. In cash, we maintain our 2s10s BTPs steepeners vs. Bunds. In the UK, we closed our SFIU4/U5 flatteners, and we enter into a 10s30s gilt flattener, while we keep our pay 5s10s30s SONIA fly and short 15y ASW positions. In Japan, we maintain our long 5s10s ASW box flattener, and keep our long 20y JGBs on 5s20s30s fly position. We also maintain pay 10y on 2s10s20s fly.

## Currency & Foreign Exchange

We argue that recent strength in European inflation does not pose a material risk to our short EUR view. Markets are underestimating risks of BoC dovishness, and CAD could emerge as a major funding currency. We add GBP/CAD longs, which should emerge as a key carry trade. We look to the Riksbank meeting and expect to see a cautious status quo. We stay long NOK/SEK. We also discuss risks to our base case that USD/CAD rises.

## Inflation-Linked Bonds

We suggest going long 2y TIPS breakevens. We like the trade as a way to position for possible inflationary policies after the US elections, positive voladjusted carry, cheap levels vs. our fair value model, and a bullish view on oil.

## Short-Duration Strategy

We discuss how the CBO's latest update to its Budget and Economic Outlook turned up the heat on the fiscal picture. With coupons likely to be unchanged, bills will meet larger-than-expected financing needs.

## Interest Rate Derivatives

Curve gamma is priced with attractive implied metrics versus history, yet implied/realized vol ratios have led to non-stop selling YTD. We run a volatility cone analysis to put current valuations into perspective, and find that present levels could provide an attractive entry point to expiry switches.

## Economic and Market Cycles Meet Small Businesses

Analyzing the economic cycle represents an important step in properly assessing the market cycle. To properly assess the economic cycle, investors need to first assess the policy cycles of the relevant central bank and fiscal authority. Investors also need to appreciate the circularity of the cyclical analysis.

Exhibit 1 displays our stylized view of how central bank and government policies interact with the economy and markets, i.e., economic and financial conditions, and how these conditions feed back into central bank and government policies.

• The setting of central bank policy affects economic activity with "long and variable lags," as does the setting of fiscal policy (which includes multiplier effects as well).

• In the meantime, changing investor expectations about these forthcoming economic effects lead to changes in investment behavior, and that moves markets, i.e., changes financial conditions.

• As financial conditions and economic activity change, businesses adjust investment behavior and consumers adjust spending behavior, which then ultimately feed back into the setting of central bank and central government policies.

Exhibit 2 suggests that the broader equity market performance accurately and generally reflects changes in economic activity, with a natural tendency to over/undershoot.

## Staying Long USD Even as UST Yields Drop

One of the more eyebrow-raising combination of views in our Mid-Year Outlook was our forecast for a rising USD and lower UST yields. Most investors quite reasonably believe that the USD would go down as UST yields fall.

Our view was based more on how we saw yield differentials playing out, at least vs the EUR, which is the major driver of how the DXY behaves, of course. So far we have seen yield differentials help to push EUR/USD down, with a broad-based drop in core government bond yields ( Exhibit 18 and Exhibit 19 ).

Exhibit 18: UST yields have been falling since mid-April but now bunds are catching up
![](images/baa026c808408bc58428d07dd5a852cb19038d5f211daee24110f9a82cf6dc49.jpg)
Source: Bloomberg, Morgan Stanley Research

Exhibit 19: EUR/USD continues to move in line with yield differentials
![](images/0d8298071979d1d81355b2a3919f2bc27c3a18347e16ad0ac2a544c5a79e027c.jpg)
Source: Bloomberg, Morgan Stanley Research

Admittedly, the catalyst for the decline in Bund yields was not something we anticipated – i.e., a flight to quality on the back of concerns about the French fiscal outlook.

Nonetheless, this period aptly demonstrates that yes, indeed the USD can go up even as UST yields fall. Expecting USD weakness as the US economy slows is, of course, not controversial, but it is a narrow and fragile path that primarily depends on growth conditions in the rest of the world remaining robust, or at least improving.

If Europe and China/EM are struggling to deliver a growth rebound, then any US slowdown will raise tail risks around the global economy and safe-haven demand would likely increase – boosting USD.

If anything, the most recent round of manufacturing and services sector PMI data suggests the US economy continues to outperform vs Europe, delivering another boost to USD on top of the recent fiscal concerns in Europe. (See EUR | Will the rise in European inflation support EUR? )

This is not just a European story though. The USD has strengthened vs Asia even as 10y UST yields have fallen by more than 10y yields in Asia.

Exhibit 20: USD/Asia continues to rise despite the drop in 10y UST-Asia yield differentials

For some currencies, like IDR, this is easily explained. The government’s plans to raise debt/GDP has pushed INDO GB yields higher in the past month, and concerns about the implications of the plans for the credit have increased risk premia, which is reflected in both CDS and the currency.

For other currencies that typically have a higher correlation with how yield differentials evolve, the divergence needs further examination. The path of USD/JPY typically follows UST-JGB yields closely, for example, yet there has been a notable divergence recently that also has been reflected in the broader region since the beginning of June. There are at least a few potential explanations:

• The level of short-term US yields remains attractive: Even though UST yields have fallen, they remain at an elevated level vs Asia, particularly at the front end of the US curve. This is particularly important as corporates would leave their export earnings on deposit. As the Fed has not cut rates and USD deposit rates are still elevated, exporters in Asia still have incentives to leave cash in USD rather than convert to local currency.

• Investors are concerned about trade tariffs: We believe that tariffs on US imports will be positive for the dollar, particularly vs the countries against which the tariffs are applied. China remains a significant focus for the market, and a move up in USD/CNH/Y on the back of any tariffs will likely spillover to the region. Investors may increasingly be focused on this risk following recent elections in Europe and emerging markets, and the first US presidential election debate at the end of the month.

We continue to see USD upside and maintain our long positions vs EUR and Asian currencies in particular. The picture vs the remainder of EM is differentiated, but broadly we do see USD gains, as per our forecasts.

# Interest Rate Strategy

## United States

Another reason to stay neutral duration: Treasury yields moved in a narrow range, with 10y yields down slightly since last week. On net, yields have reacted meaningfully to the stream of overall negative data since early May (when we had suggested turning long). Currently, economic surprise indexes have reached very low levels vs. history, leaving it ripe for their typical mean reversion – the fifth reason to stay neutral on duration.

The problem with steepeners persists: The 2s10s curve has bull flattened as yields have fallen in recent weeks. We highlighted the under-appreciated risk of bull flattening in our note, The Problem with Steepeners. As we expected, the significant bear steepening in the curve in the last year was susceptible to mean reversion via bull flattening. This keeps steepeners unattractive for now. We think duration trades will be more attractive than steepeners (we are neutral duration).

Neutral duration; receive July FOMC OIS and add long 2y breakevens: We stay tactically neutral on duration – especially heading into the US presidential debate next week. We will re-assess whether to be long duration again after the debates, ahead of the next payroll and CPI data. For now, we continue to suggest attractive risk-reward trades like receiving July FOMC OIS, and we add long 2y breakevens.

## Euro area

We note how duration longs are still supported by seasonality and valuation considerations, and that in the absence of meaningful data in the next week, the price action will continue to be dominated by risk-on/risk-off sentiment.

We note the big U-turn in positioning since the beginning of the June, coincident with the announcement of the French snap elections, with the market having a long exposure predominantly on the Schatz and on the Bund, although we point out that part of these longs may be held in ASW, or used as a long leg of OAT or IK widener positions.

On OAT, after discussing last week the establishment of a new trading regime, that we estimated to range between 65bp and 80bp, this week we try to estimate an upper bound on the spread in the event of an adverse scenario. In doing so, we concurrently try to quantify the top on BTP/Bund, OAT invoice and Bund ASW.

Concerning the 10y OAT/Bund spread, we think that in any unforeseen adverse event the spread could spike to \~100/110bp.

## United Kingdom

Rate cuts should come if data allow them, and the June MPC meeting signaled that the start of the easing cycle might be as close as August. Meanwhile, the May Inflation Report was mixed: services inflation came in at \~5.7%Y, above consensus expectations at 5.5Y%, while headline and core CPI came in broadly in line. That said, the overshoot in services inflation was driven mostly by volatile components.

After the past week of data, easing expectations repriced higher and front-end rates rallied; this, in our view, makes risks more balanced and reduces the

attractiveness of being long front-end duration. Accordingly, we closed our SFIU4- SFIU5 flattener after the May Inflation Report as we reached our target and saw little risk-reward in keeping the trade open.

In addition, the back end of the curve has steepened significantly over the past weeks, and tactical flatteners look attractive in our view given both valuations and supply-demand considerations. We therefore enter tactically into a 10s30s gilt flattener, which can also be seen as a compelling alternative to outright long positions given its better carry and roll-down profile.

## Japan

The size of a “substantial” reduction in the BoJ’s JGB purchases is one of the big themes in Japan's rates market. To gauge the impact on JGB yields, we think that maturity composition matters as well as total reduction size. With interest rate risk-based BoJ JGB holding data and the BoJ’s estimated model for the 10-year yield, we quantify potential spillovers to Japan’s 10-year yield from the BoJ’s “substantial” reduction. We find that JPY 3 trillion of reduction per month centering on the sub-10-year tenor can translate into 12bp of upward pressure on 10-year JGB yields, which would not change the view from our mid-year outlook.

We also discuss the drivers of super-long JGBs. The long-end JGB has been trading volatile throughout this week, as media headlines suggest that the MoF is considering a reduction in long-end JGB issuance over the medium term. With supply/demand seemingly a key driver for long-end JGBs, we focus on recent supply/demand developments further out the curve along with key points to consider when looking ahead. We maintain pay 10y on 2s10s20s fly, 5s10s ASW flattener, and long 20y JGB on 5s20s30s fly.

One question on investors' minds is, what could be a ceiling on the 10y OAT/Bund spread in the event of an adverse scenario from here?

On the political backdrop, as mentioned in our previous note, the market could potentially be more keen at first to price an additional discount on French yields in the event that the left-wing coalition obtains a majority, given their more aggressive fiscal stance, which could give rise to concerns about France's public finances.

## Tenor should also matter when it comes to “substantial” reductions

Attention since the June 13-14 BoJ monetary policy meeting has been focusing on the extent to which the BoJ’s JGB purchases will be dialed back under the “detailed plan” that is now set to be announced at the July meeting.

Former board member Makoto Sakurai has spoken of a JPY 2 trillion reduction being a “good chance”, and other speculation about possible headline figures has been featuring quite prominently in the media. But the more important question for investors is the degree to which interest rates might end up being impacted, and we would argue that this is ultimately liable to depend not only on the total size of the cuts but also on how they break down across the curve.

Below we attempt to quantify potential spillovers to Japan's 10-year yield by using a longterm interest rate function that makes appropriate allowance for the maturity composition of the BoJ’s JGB holdings.

Our analysis references the estimation models for the 10-year JGB yield that were presented in the BoJ’s April 2024 Outlook for Economic Activity and Prices. The explanatory variables include “Expected BoJ holdings of JGBs in the future” (constructed with a view to capturing the “stock effect” of JGB purchases), the uncollateralized overnight call rate, the 10-year UST yield and the core-core CPI (all items less fresh food and energy), along with a more technical yardstick for the impact of the “yield curve control” (YCC) framework that is based on pricing in the options market.

The overall framework might be considered quite standard, but it is noteworthy that the “stock effect” variable is calculated on the basis of "interest rate risk" and denotes the envisaged two-year-ahead share of BoJ JGB holdings as a percentage of total outstanding.

Interest rate risk will of course vary depending on which particular maturities are held by the BoJ, with the risk premium naturally increasing with time to redemption. This approach effectively makes it possible to estimate the impact of BoJ purchases in different maturity sectors by using appropriate weights.

For the sake of simplicity we have opted to use the BoJ’s estimation results to analyze the impact of reductions in JGB purchases based on how they will affect the BoJ’s ownership share over time (when measured in terms of interest rate risk).

As should be evident from Exhibit 54 , the trajectory of the BoJ’s ownership share turns out to be a little different when measured in this fashion rather than being based simply on face value. The interest rate risk-based share increased until around March 2019 as a consequence of the BoJ making comparatively large purchases of super-long JGBs, but fell gradually thereafter due to shortening the average maturity of its holdings (as the BoJ mainly increased the purchase size for the short end in response to the MoF's additional issuance under Covid).

Average maturity has since stabilized at around 6.5 years (see Exhibit 55 ), with the BoJ having increased its purchases once again from 2022-23 in line with its defense against “attacks” on the YCC framework.

Our economists are forecasting that the BoJ will roughly halve its monthly purchases from the current pace of around JPY 6 trillion over the course of the next year or so. Suppose by way of an admittedly unrealistic thought experiment that the cuts are made solely from the short end of the curve or, alternatively, solely from the super-long sector.

The boost to the 10-year JGB yield – as estimated by applying the BoJ’s model to these two different scenarios for ownership share – could then be as little as 6bp or as large as 20bp, with the difference roughly corresponding to a single BoJ rate hike (25bp of rate hike \* estimated coefficient in the BoJ model: 0.583 = 15bp).

Furthermore, the maturity composition of the reduction in JGB purchases should become increasingly important over time as the BoJ’s ownership share shrinks, with a longer average maturity (greater reduction in terms of interest rate risk) meaning that the “stock effect” would diminish more quickly. This example serves to illustrate the importance of taking interest rate risk well and truly into account.

Taking the current balance of JGB purchases into account, our expectation is that the cuts will be focused mostly on the sub-10-year sector, where the BoJ’s ownership share is particularly high. The aforementioned model then implies a roughly 12bp boost for the 10- year JGB yield. Note that the model assumes a two-year ahead JGB holding ratio, and any gradual cut from JPY 6 trillion to JPY 3 trillion was not assumed in our analysis, so if it's gradual one, it could be a smaller impact until the pace of reduction reaches JPY 3 trillion per month.

We estimate the net boost at 23bp after also factoring in our economists' forecast that the BoJ will have hiked its policy rate to 0.5% by January 2025, and their forecast that inflation will slow gradually into next year, and our main scenario for declines in UST yields as the Fed shifts into rate cuts from 2H24 (see Exhibit 57 ).

A JPY 3 trillion reduction might very be viewed as “substantial”, but should not cause the 10-year JGB yield to deviate all that significantly from the forecast trajectory laid out in our mid-year outlook (1Q25: 1.15%, 2Q25: 1.10%).

## What will drive the long end of JGBs?

Long-end JGBs have traded volatile throughout this week, as media headlines suggests that the MoF is considering a reduction in long-end JGB issuance over the medium term. With supply/demand seemingly a key driver for the long-end JGBs, we focus on recent supply/demand developments further out the curve along with key points to consider when looking ahead.

JSDA trading flow data for May were released on June 20. Exhibit 58 shows net purchases of super-long JGBs by major domestic investor categories based on data for May 2021 through May 2024. The 30-year JGB yield climbed higher from around 1.9% to above 2.1% in May amid growing concerns about a possible deterioration in supply/ demand for the super-long sector.

We continuously saw the supply/demand concern during the month, as a whole, with the prospect of quantitative tightening (QT) also becoming a market theme following the BoJ’s surprising May 13 announcement of a JPY 50 billion reduction in its offer amount for that day’s bond-buying operation in the 5-10-year sector (to JPY 425 billion).

We have yet to see domestic investors increase their net purchases of super-long JGBs significantly in May despite a marked improvement in carry. Exhibit 59 shows lifers’ net purchases of JGBs. Lifers are ordinarily among the biggest buyers, but limited their net purchases in May despite the already attractive carry profile.

They tend to exert considerable influence in the super-long sector owing to their buy-andhold investment style, but have yet to ramp up their demand significantly to this point.

Which other factors have the potential to impact supply/demand in the super-long sector? There were also indications that pension funds (who are effectively covered by the “trust banks” category) may have increased their net purchases towards the end of the month for portfolio rebalancing purposes (following declines in value as yields rose).

To assess the potential demand for pension funds, we closely monitor the GPIF's upcoming basic portfolio revision, which will become effective from April 2025. Otherwise, Reuters reported on June 19 that the MoF will be advised to consider shortening the average maturity of its new issuance. This claim came amid concerns about a possible supply/demand deterioration that saw ASW cheapen as JGBs were sold off versus TONA-OIS positions (see Exhibit 28 ).

The article said that the MoF’s Study Group on Government Debt Management – which is comprised of market participants, academics, and other experts and tasked with finding ways to facilitate market absorption of JGBs over the longer term – would on June 21 recommend that the government look to reduce the supply of interest rate risk to the market by (say) shortening the maturity of its regular JGB issuance and/or resuming its offerings of floaters.

Super-long JGBs outperformed on the curve in the wake of this news (on the afternoon of June 20 Tokyo time), with ASW richening to some degree as a result. We do indeed see some potential for such policies to help to alleviate supply/demand concerns over the longer term.

However, there is as yet still much that needs to be clarified vis-à-vis

both the nature of any changes to the MoF’s JGB issuance plan and the timing with which they might be implemented. We would thus consider it premature to suppose that the study group’s advice might be sufficient to reverse the recent cheapening trend for super-long JGBs. But we need to monitor how this suggestion affects the supply/ demand concerns in the super long end of JGBs.

## Near-term view

We expect that JPY rates will continue to take their direction mostly from movements in overseas bond markets and the subsequent USD/JPY trajectory as we discussed in All Roads Lead to the Fed.

Given lower UST yield prospects in response to consecutive weak US economic data, we continue to see flattening of the curve, particularly in the 10-year+ OIS curve. Meanwhile, still-elevated USD/JPY should keep front-end pricing resilient. We continue to expect the belly of the OIS curve to underperform and maintain pay 10y on 2s10s20s fly.

We continue to prefer a 5s10s ASW box flattener. 10-year ASW remains cheap so far as to be on a par with the GC-TONA spread, suggesting that the impact of further BoJ QT has been adequately priced in. We find it difficult to justify further cheapening of the 10-year JGB yield to OIS given that the BoJ’s sizeable JGB holdings are set to continue to generate so-called “stock effects” for some time to come.

The prospect for an increase in issuance as well as greater reduction in BoJ purchases of less than 10-year tenor should not affect this box spread much, since higher net supply would be expected in both 5-year and 10-year JGBs.

We also stick to our long 5s20s30s fly position. We expect the belly of the JGB curve to underperform as investors now anticipate a relatively large increase in net supply in the belly of the curve.

## Dollar Bloc

## CAD | The Bay of Fundy-ing

Investors aren't as focused on Canada as they should be, in our view. The market has been focused on stronger growth and stickier inflation globally but Canada is a notable exception in both: Per capita GDP is effectively in recession; core inflation 3m SAAR is printing below 2%; and unemployment should rise to the secondhighest level in the G10.

The case for BoC dovishness is strong, in our view, but rates have so far failed to price this, limiting CAD's volatility. Investors appear reluctant to embrace Fed-BoC divergence, but we think they'll be forced to revisit the issue, weighing on CAD.

Carry has been a major 2024 FX theme, and investors have been seeking out voladjusted carry opportunities wherever they can. Even EUR/GBP shorts, which are not normally thought of as a carry trade, have found common support given the high carry-to-vol.

Our base case for BoC cuts – and particularly a bear case of cuts to 2% – suggests that CAD's attractiveness as a funding currency should improve meaningfully. GBP/CAD longs screen particularly well for risk-neutral vol-adjusted carry, and we add a long recommendation.

But as Fed-BoC policy continues to diverge, interest in CAD funding may evolve from risk-neutral to risk-negative given USD/CAD's sensitivity to equities. This means investors should be increasingly prepared to see increased convexity in USD/CAD's relationship to rate differentials and, in a risk-off event, a meaningful rise above 1.40.

## Is CAD the next funding currency for carry trades?

But what gets us excited about this narrative is that CAD's relationship could begin to change. After all, a rise to 1.40 from current levels is only about a 2% spot return.

Wait, isn't CAD a risk-sensitive currency? Normally, CAD isn't thought of as a funding currency as it typically displays a positive correlation to risk assets and energy prices (which themselves are often correlated to risk assets, especially when price action is driven by changes in demand; see Exhibit 85 ).

But CAD on crosses isn't nearly as sensitive to risk. Exhibit 86 and Exhibit 87 show the sensitivity of CAD/CCY to equities. A beta close to 0 implies that changes in equity prices have almost no impact on the currency.

We introduced our breakevens fair value model in our mid-year outlook. This model is helpful in identifying if breakevens are overbought or oversold in the short- to medium-term horizon. The model is based on rolling ridge regression and uses a mix of market and macroeconomic factors as input variables, namely: 1) Treasury yields; 2) crude oil futures; 3) S&P 500 volatility (VIX); 4) bond market volatility (MOVE); and 5) headline CPI.

The fair value model uses past six months of data at any point of time, and we can check the robustness of our model by looking at the

adjusted r-squared metric. A very low R-squared means that the independent variables are not able to capture the move in breakevens. The current adjusted r-squared is 0.50, which qualifies our baseline criteria of 0.2, implying that we can accept the model results ( Exhibit 101 ).

To determine whether breakevens are rich or cheap at any given point in time, we calculate model spread as a daily time series by subtracting the 3-month rolling sum of daily ∆BE\_model from the 3-month rolling sum of daily ∆BE\_market . A negative value denotes that breakevens are poised to widen according to the model, and vice versa. We observe that the model spread tends to revert from its current levels ( Exhibit 97 ).

## Fiscal Focus, Heating Up

In the Congressional Budget Office’s (CBO) June 2024 update to its Budget and Economic Outlook: 2024 to 2034, the budget deficit estimate for fiscal year 2024 was raised to \$1.9tr ( Exhibit 107 ). CBO projections assume laws governing taxes and spending generally remain unchanged.

The latest CBO projection is higher than where both the US federal government's cash flow deficit (i.e., the Treasury's financing need) and the actual budget deficit are annualizing, through the first eight months of this fiscal year. In the first eight months of fiscal year 2024, the federal budget deficit was \$1.2tr ( Exhibit 108 ). A key driver has been interest on Treasury debt securities.

Exhibit 107:Fiscal year 2024 budget deficit estimate comparison
![](images/c4fc637c26c761b7abbb7411cc314873cdb2992d832591e5a033694633182a6b.jpg)
Source: Congressional Budget Office, US Treasury, Morgan Stanley Research
Exhibit 108:Annualized budget deficit through first 8 months of fiscal year

The latest CBO projection is \$408bn (or 27%), larger than the \$1.5tr budget deficit estimate made in CBO's February 2024 baseline projections. In the June update, CBO increased its projected outlays in fiscal year 2024 by \$363bn (or 6%), and decreased its estimate of revenues by \$45bn (or 1%). The decrease in revenue projections was driven by weaker-than-expected tax collections, given current economic data.

Approximately 80% of the increase in the projected deficit for fiscal year 2024 is driven by the following four factors, which all increased projected outlays:

• Student loans (\$145bn)

• Deposit Insurance (\$72bn)

• Recently enacted legislation (\$60bn)

• Medicaid (\$47bn)

Upon assessment of these primary drivers behind the raised fiscal year 2024 budget deficit estimate, we find these revisions are largely attributable to technical changes. Indeed, the technical revisions made in between the February and June projections to student loans, Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) recovery payments, and Medicaid totaled \$264bn for fiscal year 2024.

Looking at the details of which the revisions are based, we conclude these revisions will not have an incremental impact on Treasury's actual cash needs:

• Student Loans: Revisions that the Department of Education (DoE) made to estimated costs of outstanding loans issued before 2024 added \$74bn. Half of the estimated cost of the Biden administration's proposed rule in April to reduce student loan balances for many borrowers added another \$66bn. If this proposed rule is not finalized before the end of fiscal year 2024, no costs related to the proposed rule will be recorded this year and CBO's deficit projection will have thus been too high.

• Deposit Insurance: Due to market conditions and decisions made by the FDIC, the agency has not recovered as much in payments related to bank failures as CBO had anticipated its baseline projections in February. This added \$72bn.

• Medicaid: Actual outlays for Medicaid have been higher than CBO expected in February. This added \$47bn.

The net impact of less deposit insurance collections and more Medicaid payments would be reflected in the larger realized fiscal deficit and the larger realized financing need in the current fiscal year to date. However, the revisions made to projected outlays for student loans is a non-cash charge, purely accounting in nature.

## Trading Strategy 1 – "Trade Longs/Fade Shorts"

When the 5-day moving average crosses above the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract (long duration) and hold for a 25-business-day period. When the 5-day moving average crosses below the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract and hold for a 25-business-day period. In short, this strategy buys futures when the Simple Moving Average Crossover (SMAX) generates both a long and a short signal, given the historical outperformance of long signals traded long and underperformance of short signals traded short. Given that the SMAX could generate both a long and a short signal within the predefined holding period, an investor may have a 200% long position since each of the two signals would be traded in separate portfolio sleeves.

## Definition of terms

Buy/Long: The analyst expects the total or excess return (depending on the nature of the recommendation) of the instrument or issuer that is the subject of the investment recommendation to be positive over the relevant time period.

Sell/Short: The analyst expects the total or excess return (depending on the nature of the recommendation) of the instrument or issuer that is the subject of the investment recommendation to be negative over the relevant time period.

Selling protection or Buying Risk: The analyst expects that the price of protection against the event occurring will decrease over the relevant time period.

Buying protection or Selling Risk: The analyst expects the price of protection against the event occurring will increase over the relevant time period.

Pay: The analyst expects that over the specified time period the variable rate underlying the swap agreement that is the subject of the investment recommendation will increase.

Receive: The analyst expects that over the specified time period the variable rate underlying the swap agreement that is the subject of the investment recommendation will decrease.

Unless otherwise specified, the time frame for recommendations included in the Morgan Stanley Fixed Income Research reports is 1 - 3 months and the price of financial instruments mentioned in the recommendation is as at the date and time of publication of the recommendation.

When more than one issuer or instrument is included in a recommendation, analyst expects one part of the trade to outperform the other trade or combination of other trades included in the recommendation on a relative basis.
