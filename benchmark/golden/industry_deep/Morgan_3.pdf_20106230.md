## The whitelist may not benefit distressed developers / Implications of an offshore winding-up

The five requirements to be included in a “project whitelist” (项目白名单), in our view, may not benefit many distressed developers. In reality, we believe most qualified projects may still be those developed by SOEs and high-quality POEs. Separately, investors are recently more concerned about the impact of a distressed developer’s offshore winding-up order. In general, we believe onshore operations may see limited impact as the Mainland China court will likely put homebuyers’ interests at a higher priority (as an onshore liquidation may worsen the progress of building completions). Kaisa & Logan are the next distressed developers to face a hearing on winding-up petition. For the sector, the share price impact of Guangzhou’s HPR easing (more) is expectedly limited. Accordingly, we believe the latest elimination of HPR in Suzhou and very mild easing of HPR in Shanghai will have limited impact on share prices.

The five requirements of a project whitelist: On 30<sup>th</sup> January, China Real Estate News reported that, at a meeting called by MOHURD, it was proposed that banks’ funding support should be given to projects (note: not developers) which can meet five requirements (see details in Table 1). Each local government will come up with its own project whitelist. Out of the five requirements, we think many distressed developers may not meet the one which requires collateral that can match the financing amount. Although authorities stressed that the target for funding support should be based on project, not developer, in reality we believe meeting the collateral requirement may be challenging for most distressed developers. On top of that, banks may remain cautious on any lending to distressed developers’ projects. That said, it is still possible that some unfinished buildings developed by distressed names may still be added to a project whitelist, but these will unlikely be the majority. We also reiterate our view that an official whitelist will likely only be at project level. For a developer-level whitelist, we believe banks will come up with one on their own, but they may not necessarily be shared with the public, and should only be interpreted as a reality check, not a survivor list (more in our earlier note).

Implications of an offshore winding-up order: In the event of a winding-up order by the Hong Kong court, despite the mutual recognition mechanism, we believe the Mainland Chinese court has the discretion to refuse recognition of a Hong Kong court ruling, especially when the rights of homebuyers (likely the first priority) and other onshore creditors are potentially violated. In the event of an onshore bankruptcy, the progress of building construction will likely be impacted, and this is against the policy priority of “ensuring home delivery”. In our view, with or without an offshore winding-up, both homebuyers’ and banks’ confidence in distressed developers will likely remain very low. Even for a developer who has its debt restructuring approved without any outstanding winding-up order, we believe additional funding support will likely be very limited, apart from some unfinished buildings. Logan & Kaisa are the next ones to face a hearing on a winding-up petition (Table 3), and if there are more winding-ups within the sector, this may accelerate the debt restructuring of other developers. So far, Sunac, R&F and Aoyuan are among those which have completed restructuring (Table 2). R&F took less than a year to complete the process, while others took 21-26 months since the date of default. For more views from a credit perspective, please refer to the note published by our credit analyst Frank Pan.

## Weekly update on Mainland China

Web traffic on property agencies trending down (more): Web traffic dropped another 8% W/W. The Y/Y change also worsened to -53% from -43% last week. The latest web traffic actually was the lowest since we started tracking the data.

60-city weekly sales update (more): Sales rose 23% W/W, but this may be mostly due to the backend-loading nature of sales recognitions towards month end. The Y/Y and 19-22 average comparison are disrupted by Chinese New Year (CNY) falling into the same week last year and 2020, during which data is typically lower than usual. If solely compared to the average in 2019, 2021 & 2022 (stripping out the impact of CNY), the latest weekly sales would be 51% below, which is still very weak.

Policy update: On 30<sup>th</sup> January, Shanghai eased home purchase restrictions (HPR) by allowing non-local singles with five-year tax proof to buy one home in non-core districts (vs. previously only non-local families were eligible, but not singles). This, in our view, is a very mild move which will unlikely move the needle. That said, over the next months, we still expect more aggressive easing of HPR in Shenzhen, Shanghai & Beijing. Separately, on the same day, Suzhou fully eliminated HPR across all apartment sizes in all districts.

## Weekly update on Hong Kong SAR

Hong Kong residential market update (more): Country Garden launched another batch of its Kowloon City project, Allegro, at a 30% discount compared to the previous batches. The latest batch achieved a sell-through rate of 67% over the weekend. As Hong Kong’s unsold primary residential inventory hit 23,000 units as of end-2023, which is a 20-year high, we expect continued price cuts by developers.
