Introduction

China is entering a new phase of outward investment amid supply chain de-risking and domestic structural challenges: Opening up and global integration have consistently played a crucial role in the Chinese economy since the opening-up in 1978. Since China adopted the "going global" strategy from 2000, it has been diversifying investment (away from FX reserves) to secure strategic resources and upgrade supply chains. Supply chain diversification has also been taking place, driven by localization demand, supply chain derisking, and domestic structural challenges (debt, demographics, deflation). In this context, Chinese companies have incentive to seek growth globally.

However, this macro backdrop has led to continuous corporate earnings pressure: Chinese companies' top line and earnings will remain challenged as China's nominal GDP growth stagnates at a low level (we forecast 4.8% in 2024 and 4.9% in 2025, compared to 11% CAGR during 2010-19). We expect earnings CAGRs of 0.4% for MSCI China and 2.8% for the CSI 300 during 2020-2025, compared to 7.2% and 10.9% during 2010-19.

Overseas expansion is emerging as a key theme, with a proven track record in Japan and China: In Japan, sectors with high foreign exposure (Auto, Healthcare, etc.) had outperformed others on margin and in the stock market. China's ROE also jumped significantly since accession to the WTO in 2001. Sectors skewed to exports and supply chain infrastructure (Telecom, Energy, Materials, etc.) did better than the rest.

Chinese companies with high foreign exposure have outperformed peers amid the recent macro slowdown, especially during 2023: The top 30 companies covered by Morgan Stanley and ranked by foreign revenue exposure collectively generated annualized alpha of 9.0% relative to MSCI China during May 2016-2024 YTD. In 2023, the top 30 list returned 22% vs. MSCI China's -11%.

Mitigating rising geopolitical complexity remains crucial: Trade tariff uncertainty is rising entering US election season – multiple outcomes on trade policy are possible, but we believe the overall policy is more likely to remain constant given implementation constraints. Meanwhile, we expect the relationship between the US and China to remain in the "Competitive Confrontation" state, with further supply chain diversification in the context of global geopolitical de-risking. China is also managing the risks proactively by developing more trade partners and diversifying supply chains.

As a solution, we launch the Morgan Stanley China Going Global stock list: These 20 stocks represent our highest-conviction ideas as beneficiaries of overseas expansion initiatives that also rank highly on outperforming factors against the current macro backdrop. Back-testing shows that an equally weighted portfolio based on the list would have generated 4,597bps of alpha vs. MSCI China since December 31, 2022. Discussion of stock-picking methodology, quantitative factor screening, and sector- and stock-level rationale can be found in China's 3D Journey: Going Global: 20 Stocks to Own & Industry Outlooks

## Executive Summary

## A new phase of "Going Global" as China faces supply chain de-risking and domestic challenges

Domestic macro slowdown and headwinds from global supply chain de-risking are motivating Chinese companies to pursue more overseas opportunities with Outward Direct Investment (ODI) on the rise: Exports have played a key role in China's economy since reforms to open it up were introduced in 1978. At the peak, exports accounted for a quarter of GDP in 2006 and stood at 14% as of 2022. Since the government's "going global" strategy was introduced in 2000, China's outward investment has gradually shifted from foreign reserves to ODI, a trend that may be intensified by domestic structural challenges and global supply chain de-risking.

On the domestic side,the environment is more challenging to operate in: This has arisen from China's "3D" challenges of debt, demographics, and deflation, and what we perceive as insufficient supply-side policies:

1. Deflation: As measured by GDP deflator, China is experiencing its longest deflationary episode since 1998;

2. Deteriorating ICOR: China’s incremental capital output ratio (ICOR) – which measures marginal investment needed to generate GDP growth at a macro level and is indicative of incremental returns on new investments at a micro level – has been rapidly deteriorating since the pandemic.

Against this backdrop, we have proposed a "5R" policy package: In our view, a combination of five Rs – Reflate, Rebalance, Restructure, Reform, and Rekindle – is necessary to engineer a decisive and sustainable reflation. However, we believe policy remains insufficient and supply-centric.

Externally, US-China trade tensions and global supply chain de-risking are likely to have a structural impact on China's ODI: This comes as Chinese firms more proactively mitigate risks related to tariffs and geopolitical tensions. We share some key macro observations amid global tensions:

1. Evolution of the mix of Chinese ODI reflects rising trade tensions: These are mitigated via rising greenfield investment in EMs as well as increasing focus on sectors like renewable energy.

2. Supply chains have been lengthening, not decoupling: This reflects Covid and geopolitical conflicts;

3. Trade policies are likely to continue to transform global supply chain networks: As US-China competition continues, we see a trend of friend-shoring, near-shoring, and reshoring continuing to deepen. These policies imply more cooperation with allies.

China had sub-optimal returns on outward investment in the past, which may improve as China is rebalancing its international asset position: China is allocating less investment to reserve assets and increasing high-yielding ODI. These may gradually

increase China's Gross National Income (GNI) to eventually exceed GDP, and to catch up with other countries that have positive net investments.

However, we note that ODI may entail various risks: These include political, social stability, and economic factors, as well as legal, regulatory, and cultural risks. Rising trade barriers and more intense competition between current major trading partners also pose additional risks.

## Corporate growth and earnings under persistent pressure; overseas expansion crucial, as learned from Japan's and China's experience

Foreign exposure has emerged as a key theme amid domestic structural challenges and pressure on corporate earnings: We continue to see significant pressure on listed companies' earnings growth in China as the country's nominal GDP growth stagnates in a new low range. Morgan Stanley's economics team for China forecasts below-consensus nominal GDP growth of 4.8% and 4.9%, respectively for 2024 and 2025, significantly lower than the nominal GDP CAGR of 11% in 2010-19. So far, we have seen the worst corporate earnings guidance season since 1Q20 with the latest 4Q23 pre-announcements, and will likely soon encounter the 10th consecutive quarter of earnings misses in Chinese equities. Near-term inflection of this downward trend appears very unlikely.

# "Going Global" amid Structural Challenges

Zhipeng Cai, Robin Xing

For the Chinese economy, since reform and opening up in 1978, periods of faster and deeper integration into the global economy have tended to be associated with growth outperformance. Faced with global supply chain de-risking and domestic structural challenges, China's factories are enhancing their global presence by setting up offshore operations and increasing the content of Chinese innovation and design in the final products. "Made in China" is transitioning towards "Made by China." As a by-product, China's outward investment is undergoing a rebalancing process, shifting away from foreign exchange reserves that are safe but also bear low returns. In turn, Gross National Income, a concept that emphasizes the ownership of onshore and offshore productive assets, may become a more appropriate measure of China's economic interests than GDP.

## #1 driver of "going global" – China's bumpy road to reflation

## China is facing renewed headwinds posed by the "3D" challenges of debt,

demographics, and deflation: The decade-long positive feedback loop between robust growth, the housing boom, and infrastructure investment has decisively ended, posing pervasive deflationary and deleveraging pressures. Measured by GDP deflator, China is experiencing its longest deflationary episode since 1998. Meanwhile, we think China has been running a higher-than-warranted ratio of investment to GDP for quite some time. The incremental capital output ratio (ICOR) – which measures marginal investment needed to generate GDP growth at a macro level and is indicative of incremental returns on new investments at a micro level – has been rapidly deteriorating since the pandemic. The sustainability of keeping investment ratios high at the expense of continued deterioration in returns is questionable.

Against this backdrop, we have proposed a 5R policy package, which in our view is necessary to engineer a decisive and sustainable reflation.

• Reflate: Keep monetary and fiscal policy stances accommodative.

• Rebalance: Focus on boosting consumption by increasing social welfare spending.

• Restructure: Take up restructuring of troubled sector balance sheets among property and local government financing vehicles (LGFV).

• Reform: Implement reforms to boost productivity and improve capital allocation.

• Rekindle: Ensure that the regulatory environment keeps animal spirits of the private sector alive.

In a nutshell, policy should fend off deflationary expectations, recognize and deal with losses from past misallocations, and free up resources (via reforms) in sectors with overcapacity for economic rebalancing towards consumption.

However, policy remains insufficient and supply-centric: The NPC budget report points to a very mild expansion in augmented fiscal deficit (12.1% vs. 11.6% last year), as a modestly higher central government deficit is largely offset by LGFV deleveraging and land sales decline. The government remains focused on upgrading supply chains, promoting “disruptive innovation” and self-sufficiency, as well as improving basic scientific education and research – these are its stated top key tasks for 2024. The central government allocated Rmb370bn in support for technology development (up 10% from 2023).

In comparison, economic rebalancing efforts have remained relatively slow. Central government subsidies for “urbanization of migrant workers” (i.e., improving social welfare coverage) are Rmb40bn, the same as in 2023. The budget report does not disclose detailed subsidy amounts for the consumer trade-in program for home appliances and cars.

Risk of a debt-deflation loop remains alive: Such modest and supply-centric fiscal stimulus, in light of the prevailing housing and LGFV deleveraging, poses downside risk to our already below-consensus nominal GDP growth forecast for 2024 (4.8%). We believe deflation/lowflation is likely to continue for the next two years.

This suggests that the domestic macro operating environment for Chinese companies, which have been more used to a fast-growing domestic market, will become incrementally more challenging in the next couple of years at least.

In this context, Chinese companies have incentive to seek growth opportunities globally, sustaining healthy returns on investment.

## #2 driver of "going global" – trade tensions and global supply chain derisking

US-China trade tensions and the global supply chain de-risking are likely to have a structural impact on China's ODI, as Chinese firms attempt to mitigate risks related to tariffs and geopolitical tensions in a more proactive way.

Evolution of mix of ODI reflects rising trade tensions: The share of M&A in ODI was on the rise prior to 2017 (it peaked at 45% in 2016) but declined to just 11% in 2021 ( Exhibit 12 ). Usually, M&A is aimed at acquiring core technologies and resources, while greenfield investment is about exploiting parent companies' firm-specific advantages. From an institutional perspective, M&A is comparatively easier in rule-based markets (usually DMs), while greenfield investment is easier in relationship-based markets (usually EMs).

Thus, the declining share of M&A in China's ODI (particularly after 2017) likely reflects (1) stricter restrictions on tech-related M&A by select DMs, and (2) rising investment by China's emerging MNCs in EMs to circumvent tariffs, by setting up offshore operations that utilize the companies' own expertise and cheaper local labor.

Supply chains are lengthening, not decoupling, amid Covid and geopolitical conflicts: One recent BIS study compares global supply chain networks between December 2021 and September 2023. It found that supply chains are lengthening, led by firms in APAC (excluding China) which account for a greater portion of suppliers to the US and of customers of Chinese suppliers.

Trade policies will continue to transform global supply chain networks: We think US-China competition will endure in the longer run; thus, trade and investment policies will continue to shape the realignment of global value chains. The US's prevailing tariffs, rising non-tariff barriers, increasing use of industrial policies, as well as the implied cooperation from allies that is required for some of these to work better, would mean that the ongoing trend of friend-shoring, near-shoring, and reshoring is likely to continue to deepen. Though China's reaction function might remain measured to retain supply chains, it is likely to double down on industrial policies and try to forge stronger economic relationships with the rest of the world.

## Macroeconomic implications

Sub-optimal returns on outward investment in the past… As a net asset holder, China incurs a net investment loss (receipt of international investment return minus payment of investment return to foreign investors). This is because China devotes an outsized share of its international investment to more liquid, but lower-return, reserve assets. Even though the share of reserve assets has fallen from a peak of 70% in 2009 to 36% currently, it is still much higher than levels in major economies ( Exhibit 15 ). As a result, the aggregate return of China's international investment has been relatively subdued compared with other countries ( Exhibit 16 ). This has contributed to a persistent negative gap between GNI and GDP ( Exhibit 13 ).

…may improve amid "going global" and rebalancing of international asset position: That said, China no longer allocates as much incremental investment to reserve assets as before (except for gold). Meanwhile, the share of higher-yielding (though riskier) assets will likely rise amid ODIs and portfolio investment, giving a structural tailwind to GNI relative to GDP. We see potential for China's GNI to catch up and eventually exceed GDP. Indeed, countries with a positive net international investment position tend to have higher GNI than GDP. The most successful story is Japan, whose GNI was 5.7% higher than its GDP in 2022.

## Risks to monitor

Common inherent risks related to outward direct investment: These include political risks (expropriation or unfavorable policies), social stability risks, and economic risks (economic downturn in host countries). There is often a need to overcome friction arising from differences in legal and regulatory frameworks, as well as culture. These risks may be more acute if US-China's "Competitive Confrontation" endures longer before "Constructive Competition" eventually settles.

Rising trade barriers: We see rising risks of additional tariff and non-tariff barriers in view of the sheer size of China's trade surplus, a supply-centric domestic economic policy that may exacerbate the imbalance, and US-China competition (bipartisan US support for a hawkish stance towards China). In fact, a number of US-based think tanks have found that recent policy efforts may not ultimately succeed in their objective to reduce US dependence on supply chains tied to China (for example, see here), which may trigger further investment/trade restrictions.

More intense competition: As China moves up the value chain, its export structure has been gravitating towards major trading partners with higher incomes (e.g., Germany, Korea). Therefore, many important trade relations are becoming more competitive and less complementary. It is plausible that China's trade partners and competitors could

## Integration with the global economy – key driver of China's macro growth

China’s integration with the global economy took off with its opening up policy in 1978 and this accelerated with the accession to WTO in 2001. The first stage of integration was marked by rising exports and inward FDI. In 2000, policymakers put forward the “going global” strategy, encouraging outward direct investment (ODI) to optimize supply chains and enhance competitiveness. Since then, as a net capital exporter, China's outward investment has gradually shifted from foreign reserves to ODI, a trend that may be intensified by domestic structural challenges and global supply chain de-risking.

The sharp rise and normalization of exports’ role in driving growth: In the first stage of China's opening-up, domestically made products "went global." To better gauge how economic growth depended on exports, we slightly modify the expenditure-based GDP components and broke it down between "true" domestic and external demand – "net exports" is not an appropriate measure because part of domestic demand (imported goods and services for consumption and investment) arises from export-driven income. We find that exports have played, and still play, a key role in driving China’s domestic production. Exports accounted for only \~3% of GDP before 1978. The ratio rose linearly to 13% in 2001, before reaching a peak level of 24% in 2006 ( Exhibit 17 ). It stood at 14% in 2022.

The lagged ascent of outward direct investment: China adopted a "going global" strategy in 2000 to diversify investment (away from FX reserves), secure strategic resources, and upgrade supply chains. ODI increased from just US\$2.7bn in 2002 to a peak of US\$181bn in 2016, before settling at US\$130-150bn in 2020-22. Investment in mining (to secure energy) was the dominant area of investment in the early ${ 2 0 0 0 5 } ,$ accounting for nearly half of ODI. Manufacturing ODI was another major area, contributing almost 20% of all ODI in 2020-22. To facilitate this ODI in secondary industries, almost half of China's ODI now goes to "leasing and commercial services" and "wholesale and retail", which provides related services, such as financing and distribution.

Finally, investment in three areas – property, entertainment, and hotels – once accounted for 11% of ODI in 2016, but was re-categorized as "irrational investment" and has been restricted ever since. These sectors contributed half of the decline in overall ODI between 2016 and 2022.

## Lessons from China (I) – MSCI China ROE jumped after WTO accession

We also traced China's recent history to see how global integration helped with ROE performance after it joined the WTO (World Trade Organization) in December 2001. This movement symbolizes China's effort to push forward its reform and modernization (see details in "Going Global" amid Structural Challenges ).

A year prior to this, the US granted China permanent normal trade relations (PNTR), which US President Clinton signed into law on October 10, 2000. These events had a positive impact, with MSCI China's overall ROE picking up from 6.8% as of year-end 2000, reaching a new high of 17.3% in 2009, before retreating as a result of the Global Financial Crisis's impact on global trade ( Exhibit 28 ).

The rapid growth of exports drove demand for infrastructure, which was a critical link to China's powerful supply chain network. Related sectors enjoyed high ROEs during those years – Telecom, Energy, Utilities, etc. – with the help of a policy-driven infrastructure investment strategy ( Exhibit 34 ). Meanwhile, Machinery, Instruments, and Materials topped export growth among all categories in 2000-05 with their respective export values more than tripling ( Exhibit 35 ).

Machine exports made up almost half of Chinese exports in the early 2000s, and were highly concentrated with export products including cell phones, LCDs, laptops, and integrated electronic circuits. The rapid increase stemmed from the ongoing industrial policy (China's focus in National University Science Parks development) and also a increase in global demand for these products. Instrument exports were second, with 225% export growth, with optical, photo, film equipment, and medical instruments in aggregate generating \~330% growth during the five-year span. Meanwhile, the Materials sector's export value went up more than 200% with significant improvement in ROE. One of the key reasons was growth in exports of metals, including aluminum, iron, and steel.

The top 30 companies in terms of foreign revenue exposure recorded an annualized alpha of 9% relative to MSCI China in May 2016-2024 YTD: In the full back-test period, a hypothetical portfolio composed of the top 30 Chinese-listed companies covered by Morgan Stanley, on an equally weighted basis, outperformed a hypothetical portfolio based on Morgan Stanley's overall Chinese coverage, equally weighted, by 109ppt, at 144% vs 35%. ## Mitigating Geopolitical Complexity

Laura Wang, Alex Straton, Ariana Salvatore, Catherine Chen, Raymond Liu

## Global geopolitical and Multipolar World complexity on the rise

As discussed previously, evidence from China in the past few years and from Japan during the deflationary era in the 1990s and early 2000s shows that gaining an overseas footprint and winning more market share can help industries and companies outperform, if the domestic market faces margin compression and more intense competition. That said, the current global geopolitical environment has become more complex against the "Multipolar World" backdrop compared to 30 years ago, which could make "going overseas" more challenging.

We see uncertainty rising in two areas which could complicate Chinese companies' overseas ventures.

## I. Direct trade policy – tariff uncertainty rises into and after US election season

Trade policy has become a key issue in the 2024 election, and we could see a wide range of outcomes if Donald Trump were to win a second presidential term. In 2018, during President Trump's previous term, his administration moved to impose a 25% tariff on imports from China, targeting 10 key sectors including robotics, aerospace, information and communication technology, and machinery. These had been identified by the "Made in China 2025" plan.

President Joseph Biden: The Biden administration policy, according to our US strategy team's report, has not increased tariffs from these levels – but notably, has also not rolled them back, instead opting to implement incremental non-tariff barriers through tools like export controls, licensing requirements, or limits on tech transfers. Since 2021, President Biden has largely continued former President Trump's strategy towards China with a more circumspect tone, primarily through the implementation of tools like tighter export controls (advanced computer chips and manufacturing equipment) and other non-tariffbarriers. Although not imposing additional tariffs, President Biden also left the prior administration's tariffs in place. He also enabled the bipartisan passage of and signed the CHIPS+ Act and Inflation Reduction Act on August 2022, aimed at enhancing US competitiveness with China. In the future, a Biden administration might adjust tariffs higher or facilitate passage of bipartisan legislation focused on greater restrictions on US-China trade.

For further potential changes to US-China trade policy, one can also refer to our US strategy report: US-China Trade Policy and the Energy Transition.

## II. Supply chain de-risking under Multipolar World

## We still see room for growth and investment opportunities – more selective positioning is key

We believe a scenario analysis of potential changes to the US tariff policy outlook indicates an outcome more manageable than apparent at first glance.

While the outlook on potential US trade tariffs is more uncertain, we believe any impact should be relatively more manageable than some of the proposed options.

There are various challenges associated with tariff implementation, as our US strategist pointed out:

1. Potentially revoking China’s MFN status and raising tariffs back to a range of 40- 60% seems unlikely, as does revoking PNTR (permanent normal trade relations): The US president cannot do this unilaterally and it would require annual Congressional authorization. Even during 2018-19 trade tensions, the highest level of tariffs imposed on goods from China into the US was 25%.

2. Possible universal baseline tariff – it is difficult to quantify any impact at this stage: That's because it’s unclear what would come under their scope (Mr. Trump has said "most" imports).

3. Exclusions might provide some tariff relief: It is possible to apply for exemptions, which are then reviewed by the US Trade Representative (USTR) on a case-by-case basis. Some products also tend to be better positioned than others for tariff exclusions.

4. Timeline is also uncertain: The process of tariff imposition from announcement to implementation can take time, because the USTR has to initiate an investigation and then decide that tariffs are an appropriate response measure – and this process can take months to complete ( Exhibit 45 ).

If President Biden wins a second term, we think the path is relatively well-understood, outside of unanticipated events or conflicts.

In the event of former President Trump winning, we see scope for tariff implementation but note that impacts are difficult to discern owing to potential cross-currents. Maintaining a hawkish tone on China is one of the few remaining areas of bipartisanship in Washington, DC. Multipolarity is likely to continue to develop irrespective of the US election outcome, though some volatility might emerge owing to nearer-term policy changes.

## China is spearheading diversification of product categories and geographic footprints to cushion against Multipolar World de-risking

As our Asian economics team pointed out in this report – Trade Tensions, Supply Chain Diversification, and China – the emergence of trade tensions leads to an acceleration in supply chain diversification. China has lost US import market share to new manufacturing hubs such as Mexico, Vietnam, and India, and in certain product categories, such as electrical machinery and machinery and mechanical appliances.

That said, China has not lost out. It has gained market share in global exports compared to before trade tensions began in 2018, aided by stronger exports in new segments and to new geographies. China’s market share in US imports has declined from 21.6% in December 2017 to 13.9% in December 2023, but its market share in global exports has risen from 12.9% to 14.4% in the same period. Data up to 2022 indicate some market share gains in vehicles and parts and chemicals, where China had relatively lower market shares previously. By geography, China has also sold more to Russia, Vietnam, Mexico, Saudi Arabia, Brazil, and the rest of Asia. There are some elements of rerouting in which China sells components to companies in other trade partner economies before they eventually sell to markets with direct restrictions on Chinese imports.

On a separate note, Chinese companies have been setting up operations in other trade partner economies and that has opened up avenues for Chinese companies to protect their revenues.
