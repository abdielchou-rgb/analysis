# How do bull markets end?

## Sometimes with a bang, sometimes with a whimper

The S&P 500 continues to breach prior highs, and the VIX grinds lower and credit spreads tighten, recalling the calm before the storm. Narrowing leadership, elevated valuations, and restrictive monetary policy have investors worried. We have seen a surge in requests over recent weeks for bull market signposts, the triggers that typically precede an S&P 500 peak. We revisit our data library and present some of the more robust signals. The good news? Today 40% have been triggered versus an average of 70% ahead of prior bull market peaks.

## Q: What should we be watching?

A: We highlight 10 indicators that have typically preceded market peaks and have had fewer false positives during bull markets. They span lofty sentiment (high sell side strategist equity allocations, high consensus analyst growth forecasts); late cycle hubris (elevated M&A activity, increasing consumer confidence, increasing investor confidence), valuation (elevated PE + inflation, high PE stock leadership), macro tightening (inverted yield curve) and tighter lending (SLOOS and credit markets via BofA Credit Stress Indicator). But most peaks also have unanticipated triggers that are then solved for over the next cycle.

## Q: What shouldn’t we worry about?

A: Many indicators sound worrisome but have little information content. A Fed funds rate above R-star, the neutral rate, reads restrictive but has persisted for decades including the entirety of bull markets throughout the 1970s, 1980s and 1990s. The VIX as a standalone is somewhat impotent: spikes are coincident with market troughs, and mean reversion from low levels requires patience– the VIX has declined twice as much as it has increased on a monthly basis. Narrow breadth, earnings revisions and initial jobless claims are other signals that are often invoked but have limited predictive power.

## Q: Is it better to sell early or late around market peaks?

A: Neither. Remaining invested is generally superior to emotional selling. With perfect foresight, selling 1 to 3 months early would have protected investors as gains sacrificed were lower than losses over the subsequent 1 to 3 months. But the tradeoff neutralized at 6-months and skewed positive for 12m+ holding periods. For the S&P 500, time is literally on your side: the probability of loss over a 1-day holding period is roughly equivalent to a coin flip, but drops precipitously as time horizons extend (Exhibit 1, cover margin).

## Long-term growth expectations

Similar to the SSI, sell side fundamental analysts’ long-term growth (LTG) expectations for S&P 500 companies tend to be inversely related to subsequent returns (see One More Bullish Indicator). When expectations are high, stocks are more likely to disappoint, and vice versa. In four of the six prior market peaks, the S&P 500’s LTG expectation was >1 standard deviation above its five-year average. Expectations have been rising for over one year but are still low (just 0.2 standard deviations above the 1yr average).

## Valuation

## High P/E + CPI

Valuation and CPI have had a mostly inverse relationship, where higher inflation tends to depress valuations mostly from the higher nominal earnings benefit. The S&P 500 has typically peaked after the sum of trailing PE and YoY CPI was at least one standard deviation above the 10yr average sum. Current trailing PE of 24 and CPI of 3.3% YoY (sum = 27) is 0.9 standard deviations above average, just below the trigger threshold.

## Expensive stocks vs. cheap stocks - performance

Low P/E stocks generally outperform expensive stocks, as theory would suggest, and since 1986 a Low PE factor has outperformed its High PE counterpart by 4.8ppt per year based on equal weighted baskets of top and bottom decile stocks in the S&P 500, rebalanced monthly. However, ahead of market peaks, the opposite tends to occur– inexpensive stocks lag and expensive stocks re-rate higher, where investors may become less price sensitive if they sense a scarcity in growth. Five of the last seven market peaks have seen Low Fwd PE underperform High Fwd PE by at least 2.5ppt in the prior 6 months. Despite what feels like an anti-value market environment, this indicator has not been triggered.

## Q: What shouldn’t we worry about?

Many indicators sound worrisome but have little information content.

## Implied equity volatility (VIX)

VIX spikes are generally negative for stocks, but on a coincident rather than leading basis (i.e., peak fear coincides with the market trough. Low VIX levels is not necessarily an indication of an overly complacent market ripe for a blowup. The VIX does mean revert, but there are long stretches of time during which the VIX remains well below average and grinds lower in the subsequent month. Expecting mean reversion from low levels can be frustrating– the VIX has declined twice as frequently as it has increased on a monthly basis.
