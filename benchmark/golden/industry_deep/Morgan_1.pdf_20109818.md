# The Viewpoint: How Asian Central Banks Will React

Domestic conditions will determine whether central banks will cut, hike or stay on hold. But in determining the timing and the pace of its next moves, Asian central banks will be considering the trio of US growth, Fed policy path and the USD.

## Key Takeaways

The key debate is still US recession risks. Our US economics team continues to expect a soft landing with the Fed only cutting rates to 3.625% by end-2025.

■ For Asian central banks, the US growth debate matters in the form of Fed policy and what it means for the outlook for the USD.

■ From both an interest rate differential and growth differential vs. rest of world perspective, our strategists believe the dollar could remain supported.

The backdrop of less-favourable rate differentials and relatively weak Asian currencies means that central banks will likely move only after the Fed.

Asia’s rate cut cycle will also be shallower as compared to the Fed, with the weighted average rate in Asia ex Japan falling by 30bp vs. 175bp in the US.

The US outlook, the Fed and Asian central banks: Given recent events, it is not a surprise that the US outlook continues to dominate investor conversations. As our global and US economics team have detailed in a series of reports, the base-case view remains that the US economy is on track for the soft landing that they have been predicting. See The Weekly Worldview: Rising Central Bank Action (12 Aug 2024), Friday Finish – US Economics: A slowing, not a slump: what we're watching (9 Aug 2024), US Economics: Some weak July data but we still expect a soft landing (5 Aug 2024). In conversations, this then shifts toward the outlook for the Fed policy path, what this means for Asia and how central banks in Asia will react.

What are Asian central banks focused on? At the outset, inflation for large parts of Asia have come within the central banks’ comfort zone and for some time now. While the backdrop for inflation is benign, central banks across Asia are having to contend with an external backdrop that has been less than conducive to reduce rates.

We would highlight the following:

## 1) Rate differentials – Policy rates are lower than the US

Policy rates in seven out of 10 Asian economies are lower than the US currently. Similarly, real rates are also lower in many Asian economies as compared to the US, which makes it tougher for the central banks in Asia to cut when the Fed has not yet embarked on rate cuts.

## 2) The USD to stay supported

The US dollar has been strong in the current cycle, having appreciated by 8% since 2021 and 5% since the Fed embarked on its rate hike cycle. Our FX strategy team also notes that there are a number of USD-supportive developments such as growth, inflation dynamics as well as the market pricing of shallower rate cuts elsewhere as compared to the US. Central banks in Asia have been cautious, highlighting that the strong dollar (and hence weak Asian currencies) could yet impose upside risks to the inflation outlook and therefore holding them back from cutting rates. Moreover, in the run up to US elections in November, our global FX strategy team has noted that policies proposed by the Republican Presidential campaign would likely provide a boost to the USD (see Global Macro Strategy: The White House and the Dollar, 6 August 2024).

US elections may delay the pace of cuts after November: But the US elections in November and the outcome may influence the timing and pace of subsequent rate cuts even as the cumulative rate cuts may be the same as we have expected in our base case. As our global FX strategy team notes, FX volatility may be heightened post the elections, independent of the outcome. For instance, if tariffs were to be imposed or if the outlook shifts in a way that makes the imposition of tariffs more likely, the US dollar may appreciate, which will make Asian central banks hold back as they would want to prioritise ensuring currency stability before contemplating their next move on rates.

But it may yet open the door for more rate cuts: However, in this scenario, it will also matter whether tariffs will be imposed on just China alone or if broad-based tariffs were to be imposed (in other words, 50% tariffs on imports from China and 10% tariffs on imports from the rest of the world). In the latter scenario, we expect corporate confidence and capex to be affected, resulting in a bigger damage to the growth cycle and hence potentially resulting in more rate cuts in this cycle than we expect in our base case.

Beyond the external backdrop, we also delve into specific debates for key Asian central banks.

# China – Will the Central Bank Take the Opportunity of Lower US Rates and Ease Aggressively?

Key debate: With growth momentum softening and markets now pricing in multiple Fed rate cuts in 2024, investors are asking if PBOC will move to ease aggressively to support growth.

Our view: We expect PBOC to cut policy rates by a cumulative 20bp between September and December once the Fed moves. That said, we do not expect aggressive rate cuts given still-wide interest rate differential vs. the US and concerns over FX weakness.

Reactive easing to continue: With markets also pricing in a more dovish Fed in recent weeks, investors have raised the question of whether PBOC could take up multiple rate cuts to support growth. Indeed, with China’s 2Q24 GDP growth having surprised to the downside and the Third Plenum having called for stronger policy implementation to achieve the full-year growth target of 5%, PBOC had already taken up a surprise 10bp cut in the seven-day OMO rate. On this, our Chief China Economist Robin Xing expects PBOC to take up a cumulative 20bp in policy rate cut from September to December to support growth. That said, he continues to expect policy to remain reactive in nature, and sees 10- 20bp downside to his growth forecasts of 4.8% YoY for 2024.

Rate differentials and FX weakness remain key constraints for PBOC: With successive shocks in the form of 2018-19 trade tensions, Covid shock and the ongoing debt-deflation challenge, China’s policy rates have been on a consistent downward trend since 2019. As such, policy rates in China are and will remain lower than the US even after taking into accounting the rate cuts by the Fed over 2024-25. Moreover, while the CNY has strengthened against the dollar in recent weeks, dollar strength in this cycle has meant USDCNY remains weak relative to historical levels.

Fiscal policy and boost to consumption is the key solution to China’s growth: Taking a step back, it is also the case that fiscal policy – which has been contractionary in 1H24 – remains the more effective policy tool at the current juncture. Given this backdrop, we are not penciling in an aggressive rate cut cycle in China at this stage. The risk is if US enters into a deeper slowdown, or if tariff risks lead to an even deeper slowdown in China.

# Japan – Is BOJ Done Hiking?

Key debate: The successive pivots in BOJ communication has led to significant repricing in BOJ rate path in recent weeks. With markets pricing in the next 25bp rate hike only in Dec-25, is the BOJ set for an extended pause for now?

Our view: We expect BOJ to deliver a 25bp hike in Jan-25, bringing policy rates to 0.50%. We expect solid wage growth trend to support inflation at near 2%, supporting BOJ to hike in January.

BOJ was more hawkish than necessary: The US growth scare alongside a hawkish BoJ contributed to high volatility in Japan's asset markets this past week. In particular, our Chief Japan Economist Takeshi Yamaguchi saw Governor Ueda’s comments at the press conference as more hawkish than necessary. Key comments included “current levels of interest rates are much lower than neutral rate levels” and that “gradual adjustments of interest rates from very low levels... would be positive overall in terms of reducing the risk of being forced to make very rapid adjustments later”. This marked a significant departure from his previous comments that “the cost of waiting was not high”. This suggested BOJ would take up early successive hikes that markets had not priced in.

But this hawkishness was subsequently toned down: Deputy Governor Uchida’s comments on August 7 were helpful in reversing the hawkish tilt from the BOJ. Specifically, the Deputy Governor highlighted that BOJ was not at risk of falling behind the curve on rate hikes. He also acknowledged that recent market volatility would cause the BOJ to reassess the rate path, by saying BOJ would not raise policy rates when financial and capital markets are unstable. He further mentioned that the upside risk to prices arising from higher import prices has decreased as a result of the correction in yen’s depreciation. Given his dovish tilt, some investors have asked whether Deputy Governor Uchida’s comments sufficiently reverse Governor Ueda’s previous hawkishness. To this end, our Japan economics team notes that the Deputy Governor wished to indicate that his stance was consistent with BOJ’s existing policy decision process (for more details, please see Japan Economics: Our Impression About BoJ Deputy Gov. Uchida’s Speech, 7 August 2024).

BOJ to hike at a gradual pace in our base case: In our view, beyond near-term market volatility, the BOJ policy path would depend on the inflation, wage as well as the Fed outlook. As it is, our Japan economics team’s current inflation outlook already incorporates our FX strategy team’s USDJPY forecasts of 146 by end-2024 and 140 by end-2Q25. As such, we expect US-style core CPI to sustain near 2% over our forecast horizon, reaching 1.8% YoY in 4Q24 and averaging 1.7% YoY in 2025 (vs. 1.9% YoY currently). We also do not think recent yen appreciation will affect the corporate sector’s ability to continue to sustain robust wage growth (for more details, please see Japan Economics and Strategy: Answering your questions on Japan macro developments, 6 August 2024). Against this backdrop, our base case remains for BOJ to take up one 25bp rate hike at the Jan-25 meeting, with BOJ to be data dependent on any rate decisions thereafter. Our Japan economics team thinks the risk of a rate hike within this year has emerged, though the Deputy Governor’s comments that "factors requiring more caution have emerged" diminishes probability of an early rate hike in September/October, in our view (see Japan Economics: Our Impression About BoJ Deputy Gov. Uchida’s Speech, 7 August 2024).

US deeper slowdown the key risk: Key risks to our BOJ outlook would be if the US faces a deeper slowdown or an outright recession. In this scenario, Japan’s export growth would face downward pressures while Japanese corporates also have significant direct foreign revenue exposure. Moreover, a sharper pace of Fed rate cuts will mean the yen will face more appreciation pressures than our FX team expects in their base case. This will in turn weigh on inflation, corporate profits and the growth outlook, keeping BOJ on hold. We will watch for more clarifying comments from the BOJ on its policy framework and that its rate path is not independent of yen appreciation moves.

## India – What Will Prompt RBI to Cut Rates?

Key debate: Markets have priced in a first rate cut from RBI in Feb-25, while RBI has remained on hold. Against this backdrop, investors are asking what it would take for RBI to begin its rate cut cycle.

Our view: Contrary to market pricing, we see RBI remaining on hold through 2025. A combination of robust growth, inflation continuing to track above 4%, and the need for RBI to lean against leverage trends are the key reasons. While not in our base case, durable disinflation to 4% or growth slowdown could enable rate cuts.

RBI to stay on hold in our base case: As we have discussed before, we think RBI stands out as one of the central banks in Asia that will not cut rates and see three reasons why (see The Viewpoint: India – Why We Don't See Rate Cuts, 17 June 2024). First, a robust growth cycle with a healthy productivity dynamic driven by capex is expected to remain underway, implying a higher neutral rate. Indeed, recent RBI research indicated that the neutral rate has shifted up to an estimated 1.4-1.9% as of end-1Q24 (vs. 0.8-1.0% in 4Q21). Second, RBI has continued to reiterate “its commitment to aligning inflation to the 4% target on a durable basis”, and that “resilient and steady growth in GDP enables monetary policy to focus unambiguously on inflation”. In this context, neither our Chief India Economist Upasana Chachra’s inflation forecasts nor RBI’s forecasts see inflation decelerating durably to 4%. Finally, the leverage cycle is strong and the banking system is stretched. In this environment, the RBI should stay put to lean against these leverage trends.

Durable inflation deceleration or growth moderation would open the door for rate cuts: We see two possible conditions that could prompt the start of rate cuts. First, headline inflation will have to moderate to 4% or below on a sustained basis. Importantly, Governor Das has stated that food inflation pressures cannot be ignored given food’s high CPI weighting. While not our base case, there is a chance that favourable weather conditions – as indicated by cumulative monsoon rainfall running above the long period average – help slow food inflation meaningfully over the next few months (see India Economics: July CPI Moderates, Driven by Base Effect, 12 Aug 2024). Relatedly, investors have also asked if recent recommendations from the government on lowering the food CPI weight from 46% currently to 38% could enable inflation decline more durably. On this, news flow suggests that any implementation could only come in Jan-26, which is beyond our current forecast horizon.

Second, policy space for rate cuts could open up if GDP growth slows unexpectedly in a significant way either due to domestic or global factors and brings with it a moderation in credit growth.

## BI – Could Fiscal Concerns Limit Rate Cuts?

Key debate: As one of the central banks that are more, if not most, sensitive to FX movements, how soon can BI cut interest rates?

Our view: We would need the Fed to be well underway in its rate cut cycle for political clarity to emerge post the US elections as well as an abating of fiscal concerns. The resolution of these factors will then allow for BI to commence cutting rates. Risks are skewed toward an earlier start of the rate cut cycle.

It is not about inflation: Inflation in Indonesia has been structurally trending lower. In this cycle, inflation in Indonesia has been hovering below global inflation. For the past 14 months, headline inflation has also already been well within BI’s target range, suggesting that inflationary pressures are not the constraint holding the central bank back from cutting rates.

It has always been about the currency: Instead, the external backdrop has meant that BI had to take up two rate hikes in Oct-23 and Apr-24 in response to trailing currency depreciation. Since then, concerns over the future conduct of fiscal policy have also emerged, which then added to currency depreciation pressures over the past six months. Policy makers have then stressed that they will keep to a prudent fiscal policy stance, keeping the fiscal deficit below the mandated 3% of GDP. Our base case is that BI will be able to embark on its rate cut cycle from early 2025 onward. By then, we expect that the Fed would have been well underway in its rate cut cycle and that with both US election uncertainty as well as greater clarity and continued commitment to a prudent fiscal policy stance will be able to anchor currency expectations, allowing the central bank to cut rates. The risks are skewed toward an earlier start of the rate cut cycle, possibly starting from 4Q24 onward.

# BOK – Could Upside Risks to Inflation and Renewed Concerns of Rise in Household Debt and Constrain BOK from Cutting?

Key debate: Investors are debating whether the renewed pickup in inflation and mortgage debt growth could keep BOK from cutting despite weakening domestic demand trend.

Our view: We expect further moderation in inflation, continued weakness in consumption and the Fed cut from September to be enough for BOK to cut in October. The implementation of a tighter stress debt servicing ratio (DSR) in September will also help allay BOK’s concerns around financial stability.

Inflation and housing market do not pose hurdles to rate cut, in our view: The pickup in July headline CPI and recent upturn in property market price and mortgage debt growth have raised questions on the timing of the first rate cut. Our chief Korea economist Kathleen Oh expects rate cuts to commence from October based on the following rationale.

1. On inflation, the 20bp pickup in July headline CPI to 2.6% snapped a three-month disinflation trend. However, this was largely driven by non-core components of food and oil prices. In particular, Kathleen estimates that a reduction in fuel tax cuts contributed to 13bp increase in headline inflation. Favourable base effects and the government’s food supply measures should lead to continued disinflation to low-2% range from August, giving BOK enough comfort on the inflation trend.

2. On the housing market, the recent pickup in housing prices and acceleration in mortgage debt growth did lead to renewed emphasis from the BOK on financial stability risks at the July meeting (see BoK Review: Not So Fast, 11 Jul 2024 and BoK MPC Minutes: Weighing Costs and Benefits of Rate Cuts, 30 Jul 2024). However, we do not believe it will be a barrier to a first rate cut. Indeed, Governor Rhee mentioned that it is the government’s role to address measures to cool the housing market, and the implementation of the second phase of stressed debt service ratio by the Financial Services Commission from September should help to address BOK’s concerns on this front.

Weaker domestic demand warrant rate cuts: Importantly, 2Q24 advance GDP confirmed that domestic demand was now softening in a broad-based manner. In particular, private consumption growth turned negative on a QoQ basis for the first time in four quarters. Private gross fixed capital formation growth also fell 4.3% QoQ SAAR, reversing the pickup in 1Q24. As it stands, all six speaking BOK members have already acknowledged weakening domestic growth in the July meeting minutes. In this context, 2Q GDP data would bolster conversations at the August meeting among policy makers about removing monetary tightness and lead to a first dissent vote, helping to open up the possibility of an October rate cut, in our view.

# Australia – What Explains Our More Hawkish RBA Rate Path?

Key debate: Markets are pricing in a more dovish RBA rate path than our forecasts, and expects a first rate cut from Feb-25.

Our view: We expect RBA to remain on an extended hold until May, given our above-consensus growth forecasts and the fact that policy rates are only modestly restrictive. We see greater fiscal stimulus spending as the key hawkish risk.

Hawkish RBA reaffirms our expectations for extended pause: At the August policy meeting, our Australia Economist Chris Read noted that RBA delivered more hawkish commentary across inflation, labour market and demand (for more details, please see RBA Review: Hawkish Hold, 6 Aug 2024). In particular, despite the weaker-than-marketexpected 2Q trimmed mean CPI print, policymakers nonetheless highlighted how long inflation has been above target and the lack of progress over the past year, while raising core inflation targets across the forecast horizon. The board also flagged that employment growth was stronger than expected and that there is less capacity in the labor market than previously thought, and upgraded its GDP forecasts. Importantly, the governor emphasized that market pricing for rate cuts this year “doesn’t align with the Board's thinking”. In our view, this reaffirms our views of an extended pause until May-24.

RBA focus to shift to demand and labor market outlook: With the implementation of various state and federal subsidies in 2H24, Chris expects incoming inflation prints to become much noisier. In this sense, he expects RBA’s data-dependence emphasis will likely shift away from inflation toward spending and labour market data. Against this backdrop, meaningful fiscal spending measures announced at the Budget including cost of living are supportive of a 2H24 sequential growth recovery and support our modestly more constructive growth forecasts for Australia vs. consensus (2.3% YoY for 2025 vs. consensus estimate of 2.1% YoY). Along with the fact that policy rates are only modestly restrictive, this helps to explain our more hawkish RBA path. The key swing factor would be a weaker household propensity to spend government stimulus over the next 12 months.

## BOT – Will the BOT Pivot?

Key debate: The economic backdrop of a slow recovery and weak inflation has led to calls for lower interest rates, which the BOT has pushed back against so far. Hence, investors are asking when and what will prompt the BOT to move away from its current hawkish stance.

Our view: We expect BOT to commence its rate cuts from October onward with a total quantum of 100bp of cuts, as we see the Fed cutting rates as removing a key hurdle for the BOT. The risks are skewed toward a later and an even shallower rate cut cycle.

Why the BOT has remained hawkish: While the BOT has stated that it would be open to recalibrating its neutral stance, it has continued to push back against a rate cut in the near term. Policy makers have expressed concern over the low starting point of nominal rates, which at 2.5% is well below that of the US at 5.375%. Meanwhile, it has also highlighted that rate cuts will not solve the structural issues that the BOT sees as the key drag to the economy, namely the loss of export competitiveness and a sluggish tourism revival. Meanwhile, rate cuts may help alleviate near-term pressures on household debt, but in the BOT’s view, cutting rates may lead to the potential emergence of issues from a mediumterm perspective, which may be detrimental to the objective of deleveraging the household balance sheet.

What will prompt a pivot: In our base case, we continue to see a relatively sluggish recovery, which are driven by a relatively narrow set of drivers – namely an improvement in goods exports and better tourism inflows and receipts. A high level of real rates relative to real GDP growth suggest that monetary policy at the starting point is restrictive, which given the lack of demand-led inflationary pressures, would argue for an adjustment to the level of policy rates. We expect that the start of the Fed rate cut cycle would alleviate the BOT’s concerns over policy rate differentials. While we see an October start of a 100bp rate cut cycle, the risks are skewed toward a later and shallower rate cut cycle.
