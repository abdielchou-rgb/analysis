## Structural change has been obfuscated

## Global Strategy

Figures 1 & 2 tell the story of how, in its single-minded pursuit of reflation, the BoJ bought vast quantities of JGBs. In the process it drove down funding costs for a heavily indebted government engaged in continuous deficit spending. Over the past decade they took down not just all new issuance but fifty percent more from the balance sheets of Japan's institutional investors. This cushioned the GPIF's transition to a more balanced portfolio. It left banks with large amounts of deposits locked up at the central bank. And it temporarily pushed life insurance companies into foreign assets when negative rates were implemented in 2016.

## It's foreigners, not domestics, who will now be key

Foreigners alone mirrored the BoJ and as a result have the capacity to sell a lot of bonds should they sense mounting vulnerability or become worried about debt sustainability. This puts Governor Ueda in an awkward position. He must counter yield spikes to smooth the Bank's exit from QE but cannot afford to have its more than 50% share of the market grow after declaring mission accomplished. It shifts the onus back onto Prime Minister Kishida to pursue fiscal reform. But that is obviously difficult given demographic pressures and his weak political position.

## We're near-term sanguine but wary further out

Many players appear primed to buy the dip, and express no view about how these dynamics ultimately work out. That's understandable as it hasn't paid to worry about such lofty matters for several decades. But now they will start crystallize and force everyone to grapple with them. We have every reason to believe that Japanese policymakers will handle things deftly. But that is not to diminish the scale of the challenge they face. This is about much more than Japanese retrenchment from global markets, which we show to be an exaggerated concern.

## 1. The BoJ faces a unique challenge

Monetary normalization by the Bank of Japan involves two aspects, raising rates and shrinking its balance sheet. The first can be smoothed by forward guidance and amended on the fly, but the second is destined to be a much more complex communication and calibration exercise.

Normal practice for a central bank exiting QE is simply to stabilize its balance sheet initially. That means committing to reinvest maturing paper while the market readjusts to digesting the entirety of net government bond supply. Only later is QT initiated, and even then just passively. Outright asset sales may follow and be scaled up beyond that point. By then the balance sheet is more or less on auto pilot towards some approximately envisaged and well telegraphed end destination. So fine tuning reverts to being conducted conventionally via the policy rate.

Pulling back from QE is a much more delicate operation than raising the policy rate because its market footprint has been so large and long lived, and the private sector's capacity to re-buy has diminished.

Japan's case is no different theoretically. Yet the unprecedented degree to which the Bank has bought government bonds and the length of time over which it has been doing so (Figures 3 & 4) means that nobody really knows whether just stopping QE would create a considerable shock. Hence, Governor Ueda has pledged to smooth any pull back to avoid any discontinuity in purchases.

That sounds straightforward but presents a further problem. If the BoJ ends up buying significantly more JGBs to fulfil its financial stability mandate after having acknowledged their inflation goal has been secured, it could alternatively be construed as printing money to fund the government at below-market rates. That flies in the face of orthodoxy and could quickly have a broader detrimental impact on confidence, fuelling further yen weakness.

Suppressing yields when the economy has exited deflation would look like fiscal dominance, which is especially dangerous with debt-to-GDP exceeding 250%.

## 2. New JGB supply of JPY25 trillion (4% of GDP)

The headline figure for net term Japanese government bond issuance has been about JPY45 trillion annually for more than a decade, when excluding the impact of extreme shocks such as the Tohoku earthquake, where special bonds were used to pay for reconstruction (and are excluded from national debt statistics) and COVID for which support measures were largely funded by T-bills (Figure 5). Actual new supply to market is considerably lower, by about JPY15 trillion nowadays, due to a legal requirement for all JGBs to be redeemed in 60 years (Figure 6).

For this study, we think the next three years is likely to be somewhat less than the JPY32 trillion historical average thanks to stronger nominal growth and tax revenues. But we are not quite as optimistic as the government, which always starts out with initial budgets that suggest a much lower number; in this case JPY21.5 trillion for FY24. Such figures are conventionally boosted by supplementary spending packages in the autumn/ winter, of which there has been at least one every year since 1990, totalling 54 in all. So we assume a little more to be conservative, plumping for a nice round JPY25 trillion.

New bond supply is unlikely to deviate materially from historical averages for the next few years even if nominal growth remains higher, due to big LDP spending pledges and deteriorating demographics.

True, the government has a fiscal consolidation plan and pledges a primary surplus from 2026 (just pushed back from FY25). Our economists also believe that the situation is improving. But one must balance this against the fact that such projections have been routine since 1976, when national debt-to-GDP was about 25% versus 10 times that today. The Kishida administration is also committed to several large new spending projects, funding for several of which has yet to be worked out (see this, this and this). More clarity should come this summer, and with that confidence in consolidation if the LDP's Tax Reform Commission convenes an extraordinary session to push forward things like corporate tax increases. Yet the while, demographic pressure is raising social security and healthcare outlays. And experts warn it will be impossible to remedy that if delayed beyond 2030, when the country's dependency ratio will surge. They agree that what's needed is root-and-branch entitlement reform and a considerably higher VAT rate. The only question is whether this is politically achievable.

## 3. Big banks to buy 5 tril/yr, extra 10 exceptionally

Appendix One provides a breakdown of balance sheets for the four largest local investor groups that accounted for the bulk of JGB holdings before the BoJ came to dominate the market. We begin with the largest, as indicated in Figure 7, which is domestically licenced deposit-taking institutions. This covers all banks with the exception of Japan Post Bank or yucho, which is grouped separately with those catering to small businesses (shinkin) and labour groups, as well as foreign banks and agricultural cooperatives.

Banks are the biggest potential buyer of fresh JGB supply on a stock and flow basis.

The assets of what we will refer to here as 'banks' have been growing strongly for years as the BoJ bought JGBs from them at high prices, encouraging them to leverage up by making more loans that then come back in the form of higher deposits (Figure 33 on page 16). Then under COVID, deposits from households jumped even more. Normalization now means reverting to a state somewhat akin to 2006/7, the point at which the BoJ ended five years of earlier QE and then raised rates in two 25bp increments. Trend asset growth then fell to zero, though loan growth, which had been much weaker than recently, barely budged (Figure 34).

Our assumption this time is that asset growth slows but remains mildly positive (Figure 43). JPY1 trillion or so per quarter would sum to JPY5 trillion annually, making way or being caused by a similar extension in loan growth, as the economy is in better shape. That suggests a net of zero for other investments barring asset sales. Here, we think running down other yen bonds is unlikely, as they trade largely with JGBs but offer a yield pickup and their holdings support client relationships. So scope for routine JGB purchases would have to come from selling foreign securities. Flow wise, a figure of JPY 5 trillion annually looks about right since more would be unprecedented (Figure 36).

One supposes they would break new ground if JPB yields rose substantially. Figure 8 shows just how small their current JGB holdings have become at JPY20 trillion or just 1.3% of total assets (T-bills are another JPY39 trillion). While they will be wary of capital losses, they still want to improve ROE. But scope will still be much more limited because nothing else on their balance sheets has changed much proportionately beyond BoJ deposits. Those funds will not be redeployable so long as the central bank renumerates them at the policy rate to maintain the size of its balance sheet. Only when QT takes place does the transfer of bonds back to the private sector take place, effecting the transfer at falling prices i.e., the opposite of how bank holdings were run down originally. We also downplay such aspects here because they don't inject new demand that would counter rising yields.

Another JPY10 trillion could credibly be freed up exceptionally by shedding foreign securities, pushing such holdings back at the lower end of their long-term trend channel (Figure 37). But other scope seems almost non-existent. Cross share-holding unwinds are advanced, which is why their listed equity holdings fluctuate only with market prices (Figure 37). Sure, they could lock in gains, but logically that money would go into buybacks and raising dividends to protect their own share prices. Investment trusts are clearly held passively, and currency / deposit holdings are essential to maintain as well as being too small to move the needle (Figure 38).

But they will face much slower asset growth outside of QE, can't run down their BoJ deposits at will without causing the latter to sell and have limited foreign assets to repatriate.

Domestic equity sales would probably only free up cash for their own buybacks or dividend increases to protect their own share prices.

## 5. In what world could banks buy more?

It should come as no surprise that the Ministry of Finance has been conducting periodic consultations with local players in preparation for the BoJ pulling back. Material posted on its website from last June's third meeting of its Study Group on National Debt Management from one large participant tries to estimate just how many more JGBs the banks could buy in the abstract. Given VaR constraints and Interest Rate Risk on the Banking Book (IRRBB) guidelines, the answer generated by one of the megabanks was about JPY165 trillion (Figure 11). That's a huge number, but it comes with caveats.

One, as Figure 12 from the same presentation shows, is that banks' actual appetite for taking interest rate risk is extremely uneven. And as we have shown above, that would imply assuming considerable opportunity costs by rejigging other parts of their portfolios accordingly. These things can't happen in a vacuum, and the BoJ would be shrinking its balance sheet dramatically, too, which might presume bigger problems (e.g., runaway inflation).

Some calculations assume banks max out their regulatory capacity to buy irrespective of opportunity cost or interest rate risks, effecting an immaculate BoJ asset transfer.

The other caveat is that, to be comfortable going much closer to their regulatory limits, the submission spells out the government would need to be making great progress with fiscal reform: securing stable sources of additional revenue through tax hikes, lifting the economy's growth potential and so forth. Reducing issuance volumes, shortening its duration and switching from fixed-rate to floating bonds specifically are also cited as preconditions, all of which makes sense. They don’t want to be breaching their limits or assuming large amounts of what they see still quite rationally as potentially underpriced long-term risk.

## 6. Lifers look like 10 normally, nothing more

New assets at life insurance companies, which include Postal Life or kampo, could grow JPY5 trillion per year on a mean reversion estimate (Figure 45 on page 18). That could prove an over-estimate, as Japan’s population is in decline and penetration of life products is already very high. We assume they will put the entirely into JGBs (Figure 46). They also are likely to continue selling down foreign securities at a similar pace to recently, encouraged by the implementation of economic value-based solvency ratio (ESR) accounting in FY25 (Figure 47). This has the effect of penalizing asset-liability duration mismatches more heavily. Still, we believe all firms are adequately prepared so envisage no additional impetus in response.

Buying more JGBs in response to surging yields, however, is much less likely. That's because lifers have already sold all of the foreign bonds that they bought since the BoJ's imposition of negative rates, which caused a collapse in 20-30Y interest rates and basically drove these firms offshore in 2016 (Figure 13). After 2022, however, FX hedging became prohibitive due to inverted curves abroad. Superlong-end domestic yields also started to recover and are now are back to where they stood in late 2012. Consequently, lifers sold back 80% of the JPY225 trillion foreign bonds they bought since March 2013, and their foreign equity positions have been flatlining lately (Figure 14 ).

The notion that foreign bond selling will slow from here is corroborated by their semiannual investment plans sending no consistent message of intent of late. As our Figure 15 shows, they retain a modest bias to cut FX-hedged foreign bonds, though the bulk of companies plan to hold or increase their unhedged offshore holdings. This also reflects the fact that repatriating from foreign markets now locks in large losses, as it does similarly for banks, and thereby is moderating this impulse.

Most of the foreign bonds that were accumulated post-Abenomics were already sold back in 2022 when the cost of foreign hedging rocketed and back-end JGB yields rebounded.

Investment plans imply a preference for JGBs but no strong consensus on repatriating more foreign assets.

Even so, seven out of ten lifers plan to add to investments at home on the basis that yields increase. Buying aggressively at home is also not realistic, however. Their JGB holdings as a portion of total assets stands at 40%, only five percentage points below historical peaks reached in late-2012 and mid-2016 (see Figure 8). Few significant changes are afoot with their other asset holdings, either. Loans have flattened out after being reduced substantially post-GFC (and to a lesser degree since 2016), other yen bonds were cut post-COVID and deposit holdings are in mid-range (Figures 49 & 50).

Lifers could cut back on listed domestic and foreign equities / investment trusts (Figure 51). But it's unlikely they would use such funds to buy JGBs, as the sector is pushing into private equity and private credit plus novel ventures like ESG lending. These offer diversification and higher returns as well as double-digit interest rates. Dai-ichi Life, for example, has more than 10% of of its portfolio in alternatives and is planning to add to that.

Further equity sales are more likely to fund new investment in alternatives.

## 7. Social security zero, +5 at the extreme

The Government Pension Investment Fund (GPIF) contributes nearly two-thirds of social security's JPY350 trillion in assets. Its portfolio management framework was also revamped afresh under Prime Minister Abe to raise returns, better conform to global standards (set by the likes of CalPers, Norway's GPFG and Korea's National Pension Service), helping weaken the yen and provide an early tailwind to the BoJ's reflation efforts in the process. But having had its JGB benchmark weights cut thrice in the past 11 years, it appears to have reached an equilibrium (see page 19 and Figure 16 below).

In fact, President Miyazono said lately he doesn't envisage further changes in its upcoming five-yearly review. This probably explains why they haven't used their deviation limits since their fourth medium-term plan went into effect as many years ago. As a passive investor, they are already supporting JGBs when prices weaken.

We calculate automatic rebalancing could be worth about JPY5 trillion in extra JGB purchases in a stress scenario, given the entire sector's holdings are only a bit over JPY50 trillion (they also hold roughly JPY10 trillion apiece in local government and corporate bonds) and bond market weakness would probably spill over to hit local stocks considerably, too. In ordinary circumstances, by contrast, they will not be investing any meaningful amount as new asset inflows are growing at less than JPY5 trillion per year. That presupposes only JPY 1 trillion going into JGBs, which here is a rounding error.

This sector has little in the way of fresh inflows, so can only buy more JGBs by selling other assets. This happens automatically when JGBs underderperform via passive rebalancing, but the potential numbers are small.

## 8. Foreigners might muster 5 or sell 15

Though Japan is 11% of the WGBI, foreign real money investors tend to be persistently underweight due to the repressed level of yields. However, they did accumulate a lot of paper in recent years and now can boast of being the market's third largest group of holders at 7% of outstanding paper ex-T-bills versus 14% for lifers and 54% for the BoJ. Furthermore, because international hedge funds are very active in trading this market, their combined turnover accounts for 45% of cash bond trading (ex-bllls) and 70% of futures volumes (Figure 18).

Over the last decade, foreign JGB accumulation averaged JPY2-3 trillion per year as they (unlike domestics) pretty much mirrored the actions of the BoJ until COVID, since when their investment has become more erratic (Figure 19). It is obviously reverting to following global yields a bit more closely. Looking at the magnitude of their flow data, however, we could surmise that they have the capacity to buy (or sell) about JPY5 trillion in normal times, and double that in any period of stress. Combined with the fact that the BoJ is finally pulling back and the outlook for yen rates is clearly asymmetric, we assume foreigners will buy JPY5 trillion in a good year and sell an unprecedented JPY15 trillion in any bad one. Their current stock is JPY72 trillion, meaning the latter could be sustained and need not depend on naked shorting.

MoF has gone to great lengths in recent years to build a rapport with foreign investors, recognizing their importance. Its annual Debt Management Report details the number of interactions staff have, and concludes 'Our biggest concern with JGB issuance is that there will be doubts about the stable absorption of JGBs and that JGBs will be downgraded (which includes being put on negative watch). ## 9. Households merely substitute

Households are the other group that has been courted by MoF to diversify and broaden their JGB investor base. MOF introduced 10Y floating-rate bonds for retail investors in 2003, and have added 3- and 5-year fixed-rate securities as well as a tailored OTC trading system since. These provide preferential terms such as yields floored near zero (5bp) and cover brokerage fees. Demand initially surged such that by late 2007, households held JPY35 trillion or about nearly 5% of the market, accounting for 2.3% of household assets (Figure 20). But the attraction then waned with the resumption of rate cuts in 2008 and subsequent further easing. Holdings hereby slumped to JPY13 trillion, now 0.6% of household assets, and have yet to perk up even with yields inching back up. The trigger, judging by 2005-7, will be 5-year JGBs trading above 50bps, i.e., 10bp higher and another year or two.

An intriguing possibility is that households shift heavily into JGBs in response to higher yields by moving out of cash and sight deposits. But this wouldn’t inject new demand, it would simply cannibalize the capacity of other parts of the system to hold bonds.

An enticing observation that relates to this is that Japanese households are sitting on more cash and sight deposits than ever before: JPY109 and 652 trillion, respectively (Figure 21). That's due to many term deposits maturing over the past decade but not having been worth rolling over at prevailing rates (less than 1bp annually for fixed 5Y and 10Y deposits at all three megabanks). This suggests we could see something akin to big hot-money inflows on this front if JGB yields rose rapidly. Yet that wouldn't add to net private sector demand as it would force banks, where most of this money is currently held, to hold back commensurately or withdraw their own BoJ deposits. Cash being run down would similarly just force the central bank to adjust the asset side of its balance sheet, as we established earlier.

This would, however, challenge the notion of what constitutes 'sticky deposits. Regulators define them primarily as funds that have not moved for an extended period, which may be misleading in this instance as well as under new competition from fintechs. As the BoJ recognizes in its semi-annnual Financial System Report, the reality is they are 'state dependent.' The only response so far has been a small ratcheting up of term deposits rates, to about 10bp for a 5Y and 20bp for a 10Y fixed contract. But that's still markedly below what is available to households direct and absent early withdrawal fees.

## 10. Aggregation and price implications

Plotting our issuance and JGB purchase estimates alongside the historical data split by QE regime since 2013 creates the table in Figure 22. For our projections, the BoJ— figures highlighted in pink—is assumed to be the residual. In the benign 'next 3 years case, everybody else buys and yields moderately while BoJ holdings flatline. The difference in the stress scenario is that foreigners sense weakness and sell, forcing up yields. That entices locals to liquidate other assets and buy more. But now debt service costs have also increased (they go up by JPY4 trillion as every 1% rise in 10Y yields, according to MoF). So the BoJ still ends with more bonds as a result of its periodic forays in to stabilize the market.

The difference between the BoJ buying zero and five trillion might not sound like much after they have been accumulating many multiples of that. But it makes a big difference when we graph it using textbook demand and supply curves (Figure 23). Until recently, under pre-existing demand curve D, large-scale BoJ purchases had been holding JGBs at an arbitrarily high price, P. This falls to P1 when the central bank pulls back and private sector demand takes up the slack smoothly, which is represented by D1. Suppose though that foreign demand inverts, which would likely make domestics warier and steepening their demand function as in D2. That's our second scenario, where P2 is also not a stable equilibrium because domestics don't have the wherewithal to keep buying at this elevated rate even as foreigners can continue conceivably to sell out of their existing stock of bonds.

Foreigners are pivotal in determining how far yields rise while BoJ buying changes little in either scenario.

Inherent fragility is exposed in the stress scenario, meaning it would be futile for the BoJ to try and backstop the market alone here. A government-wide response would be required to avert a fiscal crisis.

## 11. Duration will manifest vulnerability

Relative weakness in 10-30Y JGBs since US rates rallied in late 2023 speaks of waning support for this part of the curve. It became more pronounced after the BoJ scaled back its monthly purchase plans in November and December, and kept them at a lower level for January and February following the abandonment of its hard YCC ceiling in October (Figure 24). Life insurance companies had been holding off in anticipation of being able to buy at higher yields, but are compelled to invest ahead of fiscal year-end.

Recent curve steepening pressure is a sign of things to come, and there is no reason why it should flatten if the policy rate is not being hiked beyond neutral anytime soon.

Appendix Two on page 20 shows the degree to which the super-long end is the preferred habitat of Japanese lifers and pension funds due to their unique liability structure and ability to apply accrual accounting for bonds that are intended to be heldto-maturity, i.e., almost completely. In the 10Y space, what stands out most is the degree to which foreigners are active and have switched from consistent buying to selling, with major banks and trusts taking the other side to some degree (along with the BoJ). This makes sense because it's the best part of the curve to express directional views or hedge interest rate risk thanks to the futures contract and its sensitivity to both domestic conditions and global developments (see also below). Meantime, the shorter end has seen consistent buying from all these groups. It represents the 'preferred habitat' of the conventional banking sector and feels the brunt of monetary policy changes most directly.

BoJ purchases are not shown, as they are grouped with those of other players including Post Bank in this JSDA data set. But we know from its own disclosure that before April 2013 it had been buying JGBs with an average remaining duration of slightly less than three years. Mr Kuroda began by pledging to add 30- and 40-year paper, and targeted an average maturity of 6-8 years. This was extended to 7-10 years the following October, then bumped up further to 7-12 years in December 2015. He had to abandon prior restrictions on debt monetization in the process, including the bank note rule that limited the Bank's JGB holdings to the level of paper money in circulation, and a purchase rule that prohibited the central bank from buying bonds that had not been in the market for more than one year.

As QE subsides we can infer various changes from what we have established so far. Lifers and social security have limited capacity to buy on a normal or stress-scenario basis, i.e. JPY5 and 10 trillion, respectively. Banks defined broadly to include Post Bank can buy JPY15 ordinarily and 30 trillion at a push. Together, this means little support for the very long end, especially at the extreme, while the front end is much better anchored. Meanwhile, foreigners buying 5 or selling 10 should all be around the 10Y point, which will lead to this sector experiencing the largest delta. This also implies the BoJ must

JGB supply can, of course, be tweaked to make it more digestible. And MoF's Finance Bureau has been conducting a comprehensive review to ensure the market retains sufficient depth and liquidity as the BoJ steps back. Its FY24 issuance plan makes no apparent concession and so sounds a confident note. ## 12. Global spillovers and short-covering may help

JGBs are quite sensitive to foreign rates, with 10Y US Treasury correlations around 25% on a 12m trailing basis. That's only a touch lower than the 30-ish percent average of the past decade-and-a-half, and implies upward local pressure could be dampened as and when the Fed (and ECB) are able to ease rates from restrictive territory later this year. Japanese policymakers are understandably hoping this will temper speculative selling, and are finding some validation in muted OIS pricing. They could also take solace in the recent experience that whenever speculative shorts become extended and the BoJ has been able to hold the line, domestics move back in, which in turn forces foreigners to cover shorts (Figure 26).

For all that, though, being at different points in the central bank cycle might encourage structural convergence or RV trades—paying Japan and receiving in the rest of the world —instead, particularly now as term premia are modest and fiscal sensitivities may be stoked afresh by the very process of exit. That would take us back to an earlier world where correlation with 10Y US rates was weaker (just 10% during the 15 years prior to the GFC) and periodically turned negative. ## 13. BoJ's auction plan suggests some QT

We may not have to wait long. The BoJ has replaced YCC with a quantitative bond monthly purchase target, set initially at around JPY6 trillion. This matches almost what rolled off their balance sheet last year (Figure 28). But given that the amount of maturing bonds they hold is steadily increasing, and that Q2 is the seasonal low point, it sets them on a course for stealth QT. Suppose they stick to this level, and the trend in maturing paper increases at trend for the next three years, their balance sheet would shrink by JPY16 trillion this year, 23 trillion next and 30 the year after (Figure 29), or JPY70 trillion cumulatively. That represents a big increase in net supply beyond annual new issuance for the market to digest.

JPY6 trillion in monthly purchases is less than the trend in maturing bonds held by the central bank.

Certainly, the Bank's JGB purchases are not about to be locked on autopilot. Much will depend on how yields behave, and there may be other ways to finesse short-term spikes in yields, by ramping up repos for example (Figure 30). But there is an underlying logic to setting the level here and encouraging the government to reduce net supply. Anyone who thinks that sounds far fetched should read former governor Shirakawa's Nikkei editorial from last December, not coincidentally published the day the BoJ held its Broad Review workshop. ## 14. Repatriation flows will not sink global markets

What then are the implications for overseas fixed income markets? In our benign scenario, domestic investors buy JPY15 trillion of JGBs by selling foreign bonds and foreigners put JPY5 trillion into Japan, presumably at the expense of similar investments elsewhere. That nets to JPY10 trillion of annual inflows, just over USD65 billion at the current exchange rate. In our stress scenario, this number grows only marginally because domestics withdraw JPY30 trillion from abroad and foreigners take JPY15 trillion out of Japan, summing to USD100 billion on net.

Set that against Japanese net sales of US Treasuries alone having totalled USD280 billion over the 13 months from September 2021 (Figure 31). US yields were then rising sharply, but the causality came from the Fed scrambling to hike rates and squeezing out life insurers, as we have documented above. In fact, USD100 billion is also attributable to Japanese banks who were plainly trend-trading. That can be seen from the brown line below which fluctuates around zero and is much more closely correlated with Treasury prices. The other way to demonstrate Japanese investors' muted influence on global rates markets is by examining the breakdown of the country's foreign asset holdings (Figure 32). NIIP data shows that of the USD10.7 trillion in gross claims on the rest of the world per end of 2023 only 2.4 trillion is foreign debt. Of that, 800 billion in bonds belongs to banks (broadly defined here to include Post Bank), and 1.5 trillion is owned by other financial corporations, i.e., mainly lifers and social security (Figure 30). The sum of these two figures is no bigger today than it was in 2009. Moreover, these numbers include what we estimate to be at least USD300 billion in corporate paper and a little less than that in agencies, which would be a bit less vulnerable to liquidation on the basis of relative sovereign yield spreads.

We do not worry about the potential for higher JGB yields to affect global rates markets by inducing Japanese fund withdrawals, concomitantly driving up the yen. The case for the latter is robust but rests with rate differentials narrowing to trigger an unwind in carry trades largely held by foreign investors.

For the record, the much bigger portion of Japan's foreign assets are tied up in foreign direct investment (USD2.3 trillion) and equities (2.1 trillion). Foreign reserves (1.3 trillion) are reported separately even though they comprise of 1.0 trillion in securities, almost all bonds. But that figure can only drop substantially as a result of big yen-buying intervention, which presumably would only come after the BoJ has liquidated a large share of its USD200 billion in cash held at the Fed's RRP. The remainder of Japan's international assets are long-term loans, substantially foreign funded, and short-term loans that primarily represent credit extension that underlies the FX carry trade (USD700 billion apiece). Derivatives (USD400 billion), accounts receivable (300 billion) currency & deposits (200 billion) and trade credit (100 billion) make up the rest.
