## Market commentary

Treasury yields whipsawed this week as they responded to economic data, renewed concerns over US regional banks, and the Fed. By week's end, 2y Treasury yields finished flat week over week, though they traded in a wide range from trough to peak of 4.19-4.37%. Starting with data, the January employment report stunned markets, coming in above expectations in several respects. Total nonfarm employment jumped by 353k, above consensus of 180k, and with upward revisions to the past two months. Average hourly earnings also rose by 0.6%, and there was a big downward surprise in the average workweek, which is a low figure for this series. Weather conditions likely impacted these readings, as 588k people weren't working due to bad weather in January. Even so, sifting through all the noise, the report continues to show still quite a strong labor market and an economy starting 2024 with plenty of forward momentum (see Groundhog Day for January job growth, M. Feroli, 2/2/24). This is consistent with the strong JOLTS report, which showed the number of openings increasing from 8.925mn in November to 9.026mn, with the level of openings remaining significantly above pre-pandemic norms (seeUS: Job openings move up; labor market differential jumps, D. Silver, 1/30/24). And while initial claims did tick higher during the week ending Jan. 27, overall filings remain low and look consistent with strength in the labor market (see US: Claims move up but stay low; productivity strong again, D. Silver, 2/1/24).

In light of the above, markets have significantly re-priced their expectations for March, though they continue to look for nearly 125bp of rate cuts this year (Figure 1). At this week's FOMC meeting, policy rates were left unchanged, Chair Powell pushed back on near-term rate cuts (i.e., March), and interestingly, both the statement and Powell's comments at the post-meeting presser suggested a more dovish bias, opening the door to cuts in the coming months. The statement acknowledged a“better balance" of risks to growth and inflation, and one of the more dovish comments from Powell suggested that strong employment gains won't necessarily prevent rate cuts, but weak employment gains will “absolutely" hasten rate cuts. With respect to inflation, he noted several times that he needs to see more good data such that the Committee has greater confidence inflation is moving sustainably towards 2%.

Against this backdrop, it might be hard to square the strength of the labor market with a sustained decline in inflation. However, our economists and inflation strategists see the future path of core inflation as one that should boost Fed confidence in the near term. In particular, they expect core CPI to step down from a strong 3.4% saar jump in 4Q23 to 3.1% in 1Q24 and 2.6% in 2Q24, driven by declining used car prices and rent inflation moderating through the summer. And while these factors weigh more on CPI than PCE, and PCE is the inflation measure that the Fed cares about, they also see core PCE inflation dipping below 2.5% oya as soon as the February report, even if core PCE reaccelerates modestly on a quarterly basis from 2.0% in 3Q/4Q to 2.2% in 1Q /2Q. If right, this should bolster Fed confidence that inflation is sustainably moving toward 2% and supportive of a cut in 1H24 (see US: Future core inflation to bolster Fed confidence, D. Silver & P. White, 2/2/24). Our economists continue to forecast the first policy rate reduction at the June FOMC meeting (See Maybe May, but March less likely, M. Feroli, 1/31/24).

As an aside, despite the negative headlines related to NYCB that sparked renewed concerns over the US regional banks, the event seemed to be an isolated event and not systemic in nature (see Here we go again? - The answer is No!, K. Caprihan, 2/1/24). Hence, it should have no bearing on the Fed's actions on monetary policy.

## MMFs now comprise a third of the T-bill market

In contrast to the excitement seen with the Fed and economic data, Treasury's Quarterly Refunding announcement was not market moving and largely delivered on expectations. Its statement affirmed that no further increases to nominal coupon or FRN auction sizes are expected for the next several quarters. Importantly, Treasury also expects to maintain bill auction sizes at current levels into late-March, resulting in an additional \$300-350bn of net bill supply over the next two months, before modestly reducing short-dated bill auction sizes going into tax filing season, making for a \$100-150bn reduction in net bill supply during April. All in all, our Treasury strategists see \$386bn in net privately-held borrowing in T-bills and \$1901bn in Treasury coupons for this calendar year, which would represent a 70+% increase in the net issuance of coupons compared to 2023. In addition, Treasury is preparing to implement a regular buyback program later this year and plans to announce the start-date at the May refunding. A limited set of small-value buyback operations will be conducted this April as a test for the program (seeTreasuries).

Away from the announcement, the TBAC meeting minutes revealed some discussion on T-bill demand. In general, dealers cautioned that lower short-term rates may present headwinds for the demand for T-bills in 2024 as they become less attractive relative to MMFs and other short-term investments. However, dealers also noted that MMFs are unlikely to face significant outflows this year and hence should continue to provide support for both T-bills and Treasury repo.

We would agree with the above points. As noted previously, we expect MMF AUMs to stay elevated in 2024 and that a meaningful shift from cash to longer duration in fixed income is unlikely. In fact, total taxable MMF AUMs are already up \$122bn YTD as of Thursday, defying the seasonal trend of outflows that is typical this time of year and challenging the view that cash will move out imminently (see MMF AUMs: We're not going anywhere, T. Ho, 1/25/24). And while MMFs significantly increased their T-bill presence over the course of last year as they rotated out of RRP, now comprising about 32% of the T-bill market (Figure 2), a closer look at the distribution of MMFs by WAMs and dollars remaining at the RRP show there is still room among some MMFs to increase their participation in T-bills. In particular, as Figure 3 shows, there are more than a handful of Treasury and Government & Agency MMFs with relatively low WAMs and high RRP balances that could potentially absorb more T-bill supply. While the data is based on year-end, where RRP balances are skewed to the upside, we suspect the January data will probably reveal a similar trend, albeit with lower balances. All told, MMFs should continue to be a meaningful investor in the T-bill and Treasury repo market, which should continue to faciliate a drawdown in RRP balances.

## Will bank CP/CD spreads tighten further?

Similar to IG, credit spreads in the money markets have continued their unrelenting march tighter. In January, 6m and 1y bank SOFR FRN spreads moved about 10bp tighter, consistent with the usual tightening we see that time of year (Figure 4). At current levels, spreads are rapidly approaching the lows seen just prior to the regional banking crisis and seemingly grinding tighter towards the lows seen post-Covid in 2021 (Figure 5).

Technicals have been the driving force behind the spread tightening. Even with an increase of around \$90bn in total bank CP/CD outstandings YTD, this was clearly well digested among liquidity participants (Figure 6). It helped too that prime MMF AUMs increased by \$56bn MTD, driven by both institutional (+\$32bn) and retail (+\$24bn) funds.

At current levels, 6m and 1y SOFR FRN spreads are trading around 15/20bp and 30/35bp, respectively, resulting in a 6m1y spread curve of about 15bp. Looking at the 1y point on the curve, FRNs currently swap to an equivalent 5.00-5.05% fixed yield. These yields are tight, reflecting more or less the market's Fed expectations for this year: while Wednesday's Fed meeting might have pushed back against a March hike, OIS forwards continue to price in close to 125bp of cumulative rate cuts in 2024.

Yet, as tight as those spreads and yields might be, we don't see a catalyst that would suggest the above dynamics are going to change anytime soon. Historically, bank CP/CD outstandings on average tend to be flat in February, and spreads tend to inch slightly tighter over the course of the month. And while there are some chunky bank CP/CD maturities due in the first half of February, it's unclear whether all of those maturities will be refinanced as loan growth continues to be subdued given the macro environment (Figure 7). On the margin, it's possible some US banks might consider tapping the CP/CD market as BTFP loans mature over the coming months, but we suspect supply here to be small relative to the overall size of the bank CP/CD market. Meanwhile, demand will likely persist as investible cash remains ample among liquidity investors.

On balance, we see technicals continuing to drive CP/CD valuations going forward. We would not be surprised if spreads continue to grind tighter and the curve flattens further.
