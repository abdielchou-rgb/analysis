## United States

Nonfarm employment growth stayed strong, with 275,000 jobs added in February

But household survey employment declined and unemployment rate rose to 3.9%

• Powell<sup>’</sup>s testimony suggested rate cuts are coming

We forecast February core CPI up 0.3% (headline: 0.4%) and solid headline retail sales gain (1.1%)

Fed chair Powell<sup>’</sup>s congressional testimony gave him an opportunity to update views following the strong round of January inflation reports. His prepared remarks largely stuck to the messaging that preceded the January inflation data, suggesting that the Fed is not taking too much signal from the latest round of inflation news on its own. Perhaps most importantly on monetary policy, he indicated that “it will likely be appropriate to begin dialing back policy restraint at some point this year<sup>”</sup> and also that “[w]hen we do get that confidence [on inflation] — and we<sup>’</sup>re not far from it — it<sup>’</sup>ll be appropriate to begin to dial back the level of restriction.<sup>”</sup> We continue to forecast the first rate cut coming at the June FOMC meeting.

Powell<sup>’</sup>s remarks preceded the February employment report and we think that news on the labor market and inflation will be very important in determining when (or if) the Fed starts to lower rates. The employment report gave somewhat mixed signals. The headline establishment survey figure showed 275,000 jobs added in February but there has been a much weaker trend for household survey employment lately and the unemployment rate rose to 3.9% in February, its highest level in about two years (Figures 1 and 2). There was also some mixed news in other labor market reports this week. Overall it appears that conditions remain solid but we continue to expect additional moderation in job growth and wage infla tion over time.

Next week<sup>’</sup>s February inflation reports should help give us a better sense of whether or not the January firmness in inflation was noise around a softer trend. We believe core CPI inflation stepped down between January and February, with the February report expected to show a 0.28% increase in the core index (down from 0.39% in January). Energy prices increased in January though, and we expect a solid 0.4% gain to be reported for headline CPI inflation. The upcoming retail sales report also will give important information on growth momentum in the middle of the first quarter. Despite efforts at seasonal adjustment, we think that the seasonally adjusted data will be strong in February in part because of the leap day that month. We estimate that total retail sales increased 1.1% in February, with sales of the important control group increasing 0.6%.

The economy<sup>’</sup>s momentum early this year has been stronger than we were expecting a few weeks (and months) ago. While we still expect growth to slow, we think the economy might perform better in the middle part of the year than we had previously forecasted. We now look for real GDP growth to average about 1% saar in the middle quarters of the year, about 0.5%-pt stronger than our prior forecast.

## One jobs report, two surveys

Nonfarm employment notched another impressive gain, increasing by a stronger-than-expected 275,000 jobs in February (Figure 1). However, that message of strength was muddled by other aspects of the employment report. The last two months<sup>’</sup> job gains were revised lower by a non-trivial 167,000. The unemployment rate increased by 0.2%-pt to 3.9%, the highest level in just over two years and 0.5%-pt above the cycle low (Figure 2).

More generally, many household survey indicators were on the softer side (Figure 1). Weather effects that likely depressed the workweek and boosted average hourly earnings in January look to have partly unwound. The average workweek increased one tick to 34.3 hours, after falling two ticks in January. Average hourly earnings rose only 0.1% following the downward-revised gain of 0.5% in January. Looking past the weather noise the trends in hours worked and average hourly earnings continue to moderate (Figure 3).

In some details of the household survey, the volatile household employment measure declined 184,000 last month. The household measure of employment adjusted to the establishment survey concept was down 271,000 last month and down 1.474 million over the past three months (Figure 1). It<sup>’</sup>s not obvious this has leading properties for establishment survey employment, but with the large revisions to the latter, this weakness in the household measure could raise some eyebrows.

There were also some mixed signals in other labor market indicators from the past week, although the tone of the data generally seemed consistent with strength in the job market. The latest claims report showed continued low levels of both initial and continuing claims, although continuing claims have been on the high end of their recently reported range over the past couple of weeks. The separate Challenger reported also showed a relatively high level of announced job cuts for an expansion in February. The more lagging JOLTS data showed a still-high level of job openings and a still-low level of layoffs relative to the norms from pre-pandemic years. But quits have been trending consistently lower lately and have moved back in line with figures seen before the pandemic began.

## GDP source data mixed

The limited GDP source data that came out this week largely provided updates/revisions to data that had previously been released. Most notably, the January trade report showed more widening in the deficit early in the first quarter than we and the consensus had been anticipating but upward revisions to the goods balance for earlier months were favorable with respect to 4Q GDP tracking. We forecast real GDP growth of 2.25% saar for 1Q, with solid growth in domestic final sales offsetting what is expected to be drags from net exports and inventories. Our 4Q real GDP growth tracking currently stands at 3.5% saar, a few tenths higher than the last official estimate of 3.2% released by the BEA on February 28.

As mentioned above, we still expect GDP growth (and job growth) to moderate ahead, but we raised our forecasts for the middle quarters of the year given more favorable momentum for the economy early this year than we had previously been anticipating. We think real GDP growth will step down from a 2.25% rate in 1Q to 1.5% in 2Q and then 0.75% in 3Q (all annualized figures). We had previously forecasted 0.5% growth in both 2Q and 3Q.

## Rate cuts still coming

We continue to expect the FOMC to start lowering rates at the June meeting, although this outcome will depend on how the economy develops over time, with focus in particular on inflation and the labor market. In its recent official policy statements, the FOMC hasn<sup>’</sup>t given a clear signal as to when the Committee may start to lower rates, but most communication has suggested that rate cuts will come at some point this year. This week Powell<sup>’</sup>s Congressional testimony on the semiannual monetary policy report contained no big surprises. In fact, large parts of his prepared remarks were repeated from the prepared remarks delivered at the most recent post-FOMC press conference in late January. In particular, the subsequent January data releases didn<sup>’</sup>t effect large changes in the way he<sup>’</sup>s describing the current economic situation.

Relative to his January comments on the “notable<sup>”</sup> slowing in core inflation, in his testimony he added that this slowing was “widespread across both goods and services prices.<sup>”</sup> On policy, he repeated that “it will likely be appropriate to begin dialing back policy restraint at some point this year.<sup>”</sup> He continued to caution that “Reducing policy restraint too soon or too much could result in a reversal of progress we have seen in inflation and ultimately require even tighter policy to get inflation back to 2 percent. At the same time, reducing policy restraint too late or too little could unduly weaken economic activity and employment.<sup>”</sup>

In the Q&A there were a lot of questions on Basel and fewer on monetary policy. In answering those questions on monetary policy or the economy there was, again, little new news. Powell repeated that the FOMC doesn<sup>’</sup>t need to see better data to cut rates, just more of the same to gain confidence. He also repeated that they don<sup>’</sup>t need to see inflation all the way back to 2% before cutting rates. He also offered that he expects over year-ago inflation rates to move lower in coming months.

We estimate the consumer price index (CPI) rose 0.4% in February. We look for a 2.6% increase in energy prices in the CPI to boost the February headline inflation print after declines in energy prices helped drag down inflation in the prior few months. Food prices are trending higher and we think that continued with the food CPI increasing another 0.2%. Away from food and energy, we forecast that the core CPI rose 0.28% in February. We don<sup>’</sup>t expect a particularly soft increase, but we think the February monthly change in the core will be modest enough to keep the related year-ago inflation rate trending lower; we think the core CPI will be up 3.7%oya in February (down from 3.9% in January).

The 0.39% jump in the core CPI in January was an upside surprise but we think that this will prove to be noise around a softer trend for inflation. Our forecast calls for the February core inflation rate to be a step down from January and close to the average figure reported for the few months before the January surprise. It is possible that price adjustments implemented at the start of the year led to significant firming in inflation in January but that these increases will not be continuously repeated over time. For example, an increase in the price of postage helped push communication prices up 0.5% in January, but we think communication prices will return to their preceding downward trend in February, with a 0.3% move lower. Prices for lodging away from home and airfares also jumped in January, but we look for much more modest increases to be reported for February, with lodging prices up 0.3% and public transportation prices rising 0.1%.

Apparel prices declined in January and generally have been moving lower lately. We think this continued in February, with a 0.3% move down in the apparel CPI that month. The increases in medical care prices in the CPI have been relatively stable lately at 0.4% or 0.5% and we think they behaved similarly in February, with a 0.4% move higher.

Owners<sup>’</sup> equivalent rent (OER) was another area that led to the upside surprise in the January CPI. The 0.56% January jump in that measure came despite industry figures pointing to a moderating trend for rental inflation over time and the related tenants<sup>’</sup> rent index showing softer price increases that month. We forecast a step down in OER inflation in February, with this measure up 0.44% that month. We also look for continued moderation in the trend for tenants<sup>’</sup> rent, with this price index rising 0.34% in February.

While overall core inflation was firm in January, the price changes for vehicles were soft, and industry data point to declines in related pricing in February. We forecast that new vehicle prices in the CPI moved down 0.3% while used vehicle prices dropped 1.1%.

Nonfarm employment notched another impressive gain, increasing a stronger-than-expected 275,000 jobs last month. But other aspects of the February employment report muddled that message of strength. The last two months<sup>’</sup> job gains were revised lower by a non-trivial 167,000. The unemployment rate increased by 0.2%-pt to 3.9%, the highest level in just over two years and 0.5%-pt above the cycle low. More generally, many of the household survey indicators were on the softer side. The weather effects, which likely depressed the workweek and boosted average hourly earnings in January, look like they partly unwound last month. The average workweek increased one tick last month to 34.3 hours, after falling two ticks the month before. Average hourly earnings increased only 0.1% last month following the downward-revised gain of 0.5% the month before. Looking through the weather noise the trend in both hours worked and average hourly earnings continues to moderate. After this report, we remain comfortable with our longstanding call for a first rate cut from the Fed in June. The rise in unemployment and easing in wage inflation should make the FOMC a little more confident that the labor market is moving back into balance.

In the details of the establishment survey, the government sector continues to aggressively increase headcount, up another 52,000 last month, most of those at the local level. The 223,000 increase in private employment was led again by health care and social assistance, up 91,000 last month. Leisure and hospitality added 58,000 jobs. Construction employment continues to chug along, up another 23,000. The few areas of weakness include manufacturing, down 4,000, and temp help, down 15,000. As mentioned above, the workweek recouped half of the January loss, but at 34.3 hours is at the lower end of the range seen over the past decade. The workweek for production and non-supervisory workers looks a little better, having recouped all the 0.3 hour fall that occurred in January. The soft reading on average hourly earnings left the year-ago increase at 4.3%, near the lows of recent years but unless you<sup>’</sup>re a productivity bull, is still probably a little too hot for comfort from an inflation standpoint.

Turning to the details of the household survey, the volatile household employment measure declined 184,000 last month. The household measure of employment adjusted to the establishment survey concept was down 271,000 last month and down 1.474 million over the past three months. It<sup>’</sup>s not obvious this has leading properties for establishment survey employment, but with the large revisions to the latter, this weakness in the household measure could raise some eyebrows. The labor force participation rate was stable at 62.5% for the third straight month. The female unemployment increased 0.5%-point last month while the rate for males actually ticked down. Similarly, much of the increase in unemployment occurred among younger individuals. Over half of the 334,00 increase in unemployment was due to job losers and that old Greenspan favorite, the ratio of job leavers to job losers, tumbled to a cycle-low of 0.22. While the U-6 broad measure of underemployment increased only one tick last month to 7.3%, it is now 0.8%-point above its cycle low.
