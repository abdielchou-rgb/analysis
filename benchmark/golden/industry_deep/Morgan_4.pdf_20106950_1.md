## A breadth of relief in the expansion

JPMorgan Chase Bank NA

The resilience of the global economy last year surprised many as the 521bp surge in global (ex-China) policy rates, tightening financial conditions, and spike in bank lending standards seemed a classic setup for recession. Yet the global economy continued to expand at an above-potential pace with employment growing faster than at any point in the past two decades (Figure 1). Absent the vulnerabilities inherent to late-cycle dynamics, tightening financial conditions alone couldn<sup>’</sup>t break the expansion. Whether this resilience points to a sustained soft-landing expansion remains unclear. It depends in part on whether central banks ease enough to limit the damaging effects of elevated borrowing costs on private sector health. While we look for DM rate cuts to begin around midyear, sticky inflation is a significant risk to limit the easing, absent a threat to the expansion.

The health of the expansion got a boost this week with a welcome broadening of its growth drivers. Much of last year<sup>’</sup>s performance was narrowly driven by a postpandemic surge in services and a significant strengthening in the US. Elsewhere, growth in Europe stalled and China<sup>’</sup>s initial re-opening bounce in 1H23 faltered. Global manufacturing struggled outside Asia as a large inventory drag held back production while goods demand decelerated. This imbalanced expansion challenged the resilience of global growth at the start of this year. However, recent news has allayed some of these concerns.

This week the global PMI advanced further in February and, after four consecutive monthly gains, is now aligned with our current-quarter forecast for 2.6%ar GDP growth. More encouragingly, the sectoral imbalance that opened wide last year is starting to close, with another strong rise in the manufacturing PMI outpacing a nudge higher in the services component (Figure 2). This improvement suggests a needed recovery in global industry after a string of soft actual manufacturing reports in December and January— underscored by this week<sup>’</sup>s US manufacturing payrolls data that imply a strong 0.5% gain last month.

The broadening of the expansion can also be seen across regions, as gains in much of the world have propelled the rise in the global PMIs. Most importantly, Europe now looks to be rebounding, having avoided last year<sup>’</sup>s threatened recession (Figure 3). Combined with a sizable upward revision expected next week to Japan<sup>’</sup>s GDP growth that would push it away from recession last quarter, the broadening expansion removes fears for a near-term setback to growth. At the same time, ongoing strength in US labor markets leads us to mark up 2Q US GDP a full %-pt to 1.5%ar just after revising up 1Q tracking last month. On net, we have marked up 1H23 US growth 1%-pt ar from our year-ahead outlook—continuing a theme of underestimating the strength of the expansion.

The health of the expansion is not enough, however, to secure a soft-landing. Absent a recession, inflation needs to fall sufficiently back toward central bank targets before rate cutting can commence. Strong near-term growth may ease recession concerns, but the news this year has done little to dampen the risk that a more resilient expansion will fan an undesired firming in inflation. This week<sup>’</sup>s commentary from the Fed and ECB underscore that more data are needed to gain confidence inflation is on the right track. While both indicated a start to easing cycles could come as early as midyear—in line with our call—the pace of cuts is much less clear, and could well be slower through 2H24 than what we are expecting (125bp for the Fed and 100bp for the ECB).

## DM CB communication: talks like June

Communication from the Fed, ECB, and BoC this week broadly supported our calls for easing to begin in June, whereas incoming data skew further toward later starts. Chair Powell indicated this week the Fed “was not far from<sup>”</sup> having enough confidence that inflation is on a sustainable path toward the 2% target to begin easing. February<sup>’</sup>s jobs report, with a 0.2%-pt move up in the unemployment rate to 3.9%

and cooling in average hourly earnings growth to a 3-month annualized pace of 4% provides some support. Continued strong payroll gains raise concern of overheating but this week<sup>’</sup>s further drop in the quits rate points to more meaningful wage disinflation (Figure 4). Next week<sup>’</sup>s February CPI report will be another important input into the Fed<sup>’</sup>s thinking; we look for a 0.3% monthly rise in core CPI that will keep the three-month run elevated at 3.8%ar (from 4.0% in January).

This week<sup>’</sup>s ECB meeting was more suggestive of a June rate cut than we had expected, as President Lagarde posited rising confidence in core inflation reaching 2% by 2H25, per the staff forecast. In addition, ECB “sources<sup>”</sup> reports suggested the Governing Council is very set on June. Yet the updated staff projections look optimistic to us, particularly for this year, given the upside surprise to February inflation was not incorporated. Lagarde noted that “a lot more<sup>”</sup> information will be released before the June meeting. With “high<sup>”</sup> domestic inflation and “strong<sup>”</sup> wage growth noted this week, further signs of slowing are needed not only to debate a cut in June, but to actually deliver it. For now, the ECB rhetoric is line with our June call, with risks still skewed toward later.

The Bank of Canada also left policy unchanged this week. But unlike the Fed and the ECB, the communication remained more hawkish and stressed more uncertainty around the inflation outlook. Softer data had increased the risk for an April cut, which this week<sup>’</sup>s communication threw cold water on. Solid February job gains alongside decelerating wage growth has us still looking for easing to start in June. But the latest rhetoric skews the risks toward later and we expect a shallower easing cycle than other DM central banks.

## Japan: What a difference a week makes

Incoming data suggest Japan<sup>’</sup>s economy is running hotter than previously thought, which is good news for the Bank of Japan. Price momentum is accelerating after a strong February Tokyo CPI report, while indicators point to a decadeshigh increase in the initial round of the Shunto spring wage negotiation (announced March 15) that aligns with our expectation for a 3%-4% rise in base wages, besting last year<sup>’</sup>s 2.3% gain. Also, the Ministry of Finance reported one of the strongest quarters on record for capex in its 4Q23 corporate

survey. We now look for 4Q GDP growth to be revised above 1%ar next week, from -0.4%ar previously. With wages and prices again firming and the economy entering this year on a more solid footing, the BoJ may be able to take its next step toward policy normalization. We continue to think that will involve changes to its forward guidance and balance sheet policy rather than an imminent removal of NIRP.

## Reprofiling UK fiscal adds some upside

The UK Budget this week contained a 2% cut in the rate of national insurance (a personal tax) worth £10bn, broadly in line with expectations. The easing was funded by offsetting measures elsewhere, but they kick in at a later stage and so there will be some lift to 2024 growth. The easing is still small in the grand scheme of things, and growth prospects for this year are much more dependent on whether the consumer starts to spend real income gains. We left our growth forecast unchanged this week, although the fiscal announcement nevertheless represents an upside risk. But the inflation implications are limited, and we continue to expect the first easing from the BoE to come in August.

## China’s 2024 targets to be challenged

Near-term growth prospects for China received a boost this week with a strong 7.5%m/m average jump in exports reported over January and February. Domestic demand also looks to have improved modestly. The full set of January-February activity data will be released in two weeks. In the meantime, this week<sup>’</sup>s NPC meeting stuck to the script, targeting a similar 5% pace of growth as last year. But without the significant re-opening bounce that had flattered growth in 1H23, policymakers will likely struggle to reach their target this year. Out right deflation is expected to end, but a return to demanddriven inflation will be challenged by overcapacity, depressed sentiment, a lack of policy support to consumers, and restrained business profitability in the face of weak pricing that is damping private sector investment. The government<sup>’</sup>s expectations of a return to 2.4% inflation this year looks unrealistic (JPM: 0.3%). Fiscal policies are expected to further lift the already elevated augmented fiscal budget by 0.2%-pt to 12.2% of GDP. Although there is increasing willingness for the central government to take on more debt to take pressure off local governments, it is hard to see how this fixes the ultimate problem of misallocated capital. On monetary and credit policy, we maintain our call for two policy rate cuts of 10bp, and a 25bp RRR cut in 2H, with stable TSF growth at 9.5%.

## India: Growth slowing, just not in GDP

Underlying growth momentum continues to slow in India, even if masked by a robust GDP report that bested all expectations with an 8.4%oya surge last quarter. However, with IP slowing sharply alongside the broader measures of gross value added (GVA), the GDP surge looks unduly flattered by a volatile Net Indirect Taxes (NIT) component. We fade these data and see the economy decelerating. Nevertheless, with next week<sup>’</sup>s report expected to show inflation elevated at 5%oya, we expect the RBI to take cover in the strong GDP outturn and remain on hold at next month<sup>’</sup>s policy review.

## Mounting pressures in EMEA EM

In Türkiye, headline CPI inflation came in well above expectations at 4.5%m/m in February, lifting the %oya rate to 67.1%. Core inflation rose by a high 3.6%m/m, to reach 72.9%oya in the month. The February data align with our view that the current monetary and fiscal policy mix is not tight enough to reduce inflation. We now expect the CBRT to lift its policy rate 500bp to 50% in April to arrest ongoing currency depreciation. We also expect macroprudential measures from the CBRT to increase lira deposit rates to stop rising dollarization among Turkish residents. We maintain our year-end policy rate forecast at 45% as we expect the CBRT to cut its policy rate by 250bp in November and December.

Meanwhile Egypt is struggling to reach fiscal consolidation and BoP sustainability despite IMF program support. But changes underway are boosting market sentiment. After taking significant fiscal measures, this week authorities broke with the past and moved further on critical reforms. At the same time, the CBE hiked an unprecedented 600bp (after 200bp earlier) while devaluing the currency 38% to close the official-parallel market rate gap. As a result, the IMF announced a Staff-Level Agreement (SLA) augmenting the program size to US\$8bn from US\$3bn. Additional support worth up to US\$12bn is also expected from other partners, including the World Bank and European Commission. In response, Moody<sup>’</sup>s upgraded Egypt<sup>’</sup>s credit outlook to positive from negative, and foreign portfolio flows are picking up. We see 27.25% as the terminal rate for CBE with a firm disinflation path and positive real rates expected from April.

## Andeans’ quest for optimal monetary policy

Although policy rates have come down since last year, real rates remain high among the Andean central banks given still elevated sequential headline inflation and expectations for firming growth. Moreover, February CPI prints surprised higher in Chile and Peru. Chile has also seen pressure on its exchange rate as interest differentials narrowed, with Peru similarly impacted to a point. Against this backdrop, Peru<sup>’</sup>s BCRP surprised this week by pausing its easing cycle, while we now look for Chile<sup>’</sup>s CBC to cut just 75bp next month (from 100bp). With Colombia<sup>’</sup>s CPI coming in line with expectations, we maintain our projection for the easing pace to accelerate to 50bp later this month.
