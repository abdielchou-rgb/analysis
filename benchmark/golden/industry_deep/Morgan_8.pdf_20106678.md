## US Fixed Income Overview

## Staying the course

● Treasury yields have risen further in the wake of a surprisingly firm inflation print and Fedspeak that has emphasized patience. We are hesitant to read too much into a single month of data and continue to think moderating inflation will allow the Fed to cut in June

● The FOMC minutes revealed less urgency to begin tapering QT. We now expect a formal announcement in June, with a slower pace of runoff beginning in July. We think QT can continue through year-end when ON RRP balances reach approximately \$300bn

● Economics: We expect next week<sup>’</sup>s PCE report to show a firm 0.47% m/m increase in the core index. We will also get a variety of January GDP source reports that we expect to generally be weak

● Treasuries: We raised our interest rate targets to reflect greater risk premium and a longer QT runway. We hold longs in 5-year Treasuries: Fed pricing is too hawkish and positioning is very neutral. We raise our breakeven targets by 5-10bp across the curve and continue to hold longs in 5-year TIPS. We recommend old 10s/30s breakeven curve steepeners

● Interest Rate Derivatives: Remain bullish on volatility on the back of heightened policy uncertainty. Turn neutral on swap spreads across the curve. We discuss three ways to construct well hedged carry trades on the swap curve - 2s/3s flatteners and/or receiving in the belly of a 2s/7s/20s swap butterfly paired with small longs in M4 SOFR futures, as well as 1s/5s conditional flatteners in a selloff. We detail our revised forecast for the Fed<sup>’</sup>s balance sheet

● Securitized Products: Lower coupon Agency MBS came under pressure amid concerns over possible bank positioning rotation, and a somewhat more hawkish Fed pushed out the timeline for deposit growth. The recent surge in prepayment speeds has brought focus on Ginnie customs, which we review. Within customs, we prefer 100% FHA pool floaters

● Corporates: High grade credit spreads are rangebound near post-GFC tights as heavy corporate issuance has been met with strong demand. In high yield and leveraged loans, the looming maturity wall has been significantly reduced, though we note remaining maturities skew towards lower quality credit. We revise down our YE24 HY spread forecast from 475bp to 450bp

● Near-term catalysts: 4Q second real GDP (2/28), Jan personal income (2/29), Jan JOLTS (3/6), Feb employment (3/8), Feb CPI (3/12), Feb retail sales (3/14)

Overall, we think the repricing in rates markets has been overdone, and we remain long duration in the 5-year sector. In addition to the shallower path of easing priced by markets, we think investor positioning is also supportive of this view. Figure 3shows the share of neutrals in our weekly Treasury Client Survey rose to 85% this week, the highest since the fall of 2011, and a level seen only twice over the last 25 years. If growth and inflation data show signs of cooling in the coming months as we expect, yields are likely to decline as investors add duration exposure. That said, given recent developments, we now look for a somewhat more gradual decline in yields as we approach the first cut. Indeed we think it will take multiple months of data in order for currently elevated uncertainty to moderate, and Treasury yields will stay elevated to reflect the potential for fewer cuts. Furthermore, as we discuss below, we now expect the Fed to begin slowing the pace of balance sheet runoff later than we previously thought, with QT continuing through year end, which should support higher yields on the margin. Accordingly, we raise our year-end 2-year Treasury forecast to 3.80% (versus 3.25% previously), and our year-end 10-year target to 3.80% (from 3.65%), implying less steepening than we had previously expected (Figure 4; see Treasur ies).

Consistent with these adjustments, we also see somewhat less room for TIPS breakevens to narrow into midyear, as recession risks continue to recede. We raise our breakeven targets by 5-10bp across the curve, and now expect 10-year breakevens to reach 220bp by midyear. Combined with our nominal yield targets, these forecasts still imply a significant decline in real yields over the course of the year, and we continue to recommend longs in 5-year TIPS: front-end TIPS offer a highly positive carry profile over the next few months and also offer attractive risk/reward given that stickier-than-expected inflation remains the primary risk to our Fed call. Moreover, we think there is room for the breakeven curve to steepen in coming months, particularly at the long end: supply is now out of the way, and we think the significant rise in 30-year real yields since the start of the year should draw end-user buying of long-end TIPS over the near term. Thus, we recommend adding old 10s/30s breakeven curve steepeners (see TIPS).

Turning to Fed balance sheet policy, the January FOMC minutes released this week revealed a general lack of urgency in regards to drafting a plan for the tapering of QT. Indeed, “many participants suggested that it would be appropriate to begin in-depth discussions of balance sheet issues at the Committee<sup>’</sup>s next meeting to guide an eventual decision to slow the pace of runoff.<sup>”</sup> Given this language, we think the FOMC will likely take more time to come to a conclusion, and we push our expectation for a formal QT taper announcement from the March to June meeting, for implementation in July. At that point, we expect the Fed to reduce the pace of monthly Treasury runoff from \$60bn to \$30bn. We think this patience with respect to slowing QT makes sense for two reasons.

First, conditions in short-term funding markets have remained stable lately; the upward pressure on SOFR experienced over the November and December month-ends did not resurface at the end of January. In fact, SOFR has trended lower this month as increased specialness in front-end Treasuries likely dragged the benchmark rate lower, similar to what occurred in 2022. Second, we expect the rapid decline in O/N RRP balances to stall around \$500bn in the near-term; demand for the facility should rise in coming weeks as T-bill supply turns negative and MMF AUMs remain elevated in anticipation of the April tax season (see Short-Term Fixed Income).

In options markets, the noise in the data combined with the Fed<sup>’</sup>s emphasis on patience has translated into significant policy uncertainty, and Z4 SOFR swaptions now imply a wider distribution of policy outcomes (Figure 6). Three key points stand out. First, since mid-December the modal outcome implied by options markets has remained a 5-cut scenario, even as OIS forwards themselves have traded in a very wide 80bp range. In other words, moves in the forwards can be thought of as stemming from shifting weights associated with alternate scenarios. Second, policy uncertainty as measured by the difference in weighting from the first to second most likely scenario, has risen significantly since mid-January. Third, markets are now pricing a substantial weight on a scenario where policy rates end the year higher than current levels. This elevated level of uncertainty has contributed to high delivered volatility in rates, and against this backdrop, implied volatility in 2- and 5-year tails appears cheap. At the same time, 30-year tails appear rich and we recommend selling 2Yx30Y swaption volatility versus buying 2Yx2Y swaption volatility (see Interest Rate Derivatives).

A delayed QT taper timeline and pickup in volatility is also unfavorable for mortgage demand, and OAS widened 2-3bp across the MBS stack on the week. In particular, lower coupons weakened on the back of concerns around potential bank sales. While rising rates have led to a noticeable drop in the latest VA refi index reading, the surge in speeds in recent months has brought renewed attention to Ginnie customs (pools comprised of a single issuer) and the call protection they offer relative to major pools (pools comprised of multiple insurers and deliverable into TBA). Indeed the custom share of Ginnie issuance took off in the autumn rally to reach nearly 30%, with a notable pickup in 100% FHA customs, likely driven by concerns around VA prepayment speeds. Moreover there is a self-reinforcing pooling dynamic here, as the removal of <sup>‘</sup>good<sup>’</sup> loans from major issuer pools lower their quality (through greater relative VA contributions) and increase the premiums for these higher quality customs (notably FHA). Within Ginnie customs, we prefer 100% FHA pools, and in particular, floaters which would disproportionally benefit in a rally where rising reinvestment risk on part of the issuer limits call risk on the security (see Agency MBS).

Meanwhile, in credit markets, despite high rates volatility and corporate issuance, HG bond spreads have found stability within a narrow 5bp range between 105-110bp on the JULI over the fortnight. To be sure the heavy \$194bn of supply in January only moderated slightly to a still historically-elevated \$135bn MTD in February. Meanwhile demand has remained robust. A strong risk-on environment in equities has flowed into credit markets, with the correlation between our credit-weighted S&P index and JULI spreads being quite strong recently. Moreover demand has likely been driven by attractive yields and widely held expectations that yields will be lower in the future. Despite negative total returns YTD, IG bond fund inflows have remained strong, with inflows averaging \$6bn/week YTD (Figure 7). Additionally, our FAB index suggests US credit has become more attractive for European investors while Asian investors have indicated that US IG remains a key source of riskadjusted carry despite rich valuations (see Credit Watch: Asia investor visit takeaways: some year of the dragon thoughts, Stephen Dulake, 2/23/24).

Looking ahead, we expect issuance to ramp up into March despite the heavy YTD supply. While technical momentum stemming from strong demand could push spreads lower in the near term, we continue to expect that spreads will widen through the year as gradually weakening credit conditions give investors some pause. A pickup in M&A activity alongside growing dividend/buybacks suggest the beginning of a re-leveraging trend and could indicate corporates are increasing their focus on shareholders after two years of conservative balance sheet management (see Corporates).

Further down the credit spectrum, high yield spreads tightened 15bp over the past week to 347bp, within 8bp of post-pandemic tights. Resilient growth, earnings, elevated yields, and improving capital market access all continue to support valuations. Notably 54% of the HY universe is now within 250bp, up from 19% at the beginning of November. CCCs are outperforming Single Bs and BBs YTD. Meanwhile, loans are outperforming the balance of fixed income in 2024 amid a record pace of CLO origination (\$27.6bn ex-refi/resets) and historic refi/reset activity. Indeed 2024 US CLO new issuance supply pace is the highest ever and we nudge our FY24 supply forecast 13% higher to \$130bn. Against this backdrop, we see limited room for further spread tightening, and maintain our 150bp MY24 T1 AAA new issue spread forecast (see CLOs). In high yield, given the upward revision to our rates forecasts, we revise down our YE24 HY spread forecast from 475bp to 450bp, but keep the FY24 total return forecast unchanged at 6.0%. Meanwhile we leave our YE24 spread and FY24 total return forecasts for leveraged loans unchanged at 550bp and 7.0%, respectively (see High Yield).

Notably, in the first 7 weeks of the year, \$123bn of leveraged bonds/loans have been refinanced following \$334bn in FY23. Given this, maturities in 2024, 2025, and 2026 have been reduced by \$472bn, or 45%, to \$585bn since YE22 (Figure 8). Still, the percentage of leveraged credit maturing in the next 3 years is at an elevated 21.4% of the index. An examination of the next few years<sup>’</sup> maturities reveals a higher necessity for capital market access by lower-quality issuers. As well, considerably more credit risk is priced into maturing loans than bonds. Indeed, roughly 36% of 2024 maturities, 32% of 2025 maturities, and 27% of 2026 maturities are of lower tier status (Split B, CCC, D, NR). While the average time between call date and maturity is historically 3.47 years, about a third of the 6,500 calls since 2000 our analysts reviewed occurred inside 2 years. Thus, issuers will continue to address maturities as opportunities arise (see High-Yield and Institutional Loan Maturity Schedule, Nelson Jantzen, 2/20/24).
