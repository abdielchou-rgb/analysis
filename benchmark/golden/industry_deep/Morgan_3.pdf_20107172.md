## The Fed, walking a tightrope, finds better balance

This week brought the much-anticipated FOMC meeting, with the Fed remaining on hold for now. But there was much to absorb in the statement as well as the press conference. Chair Powell struck a fairly dovish tone, repeatedly stressing that labor markets were coming into "better balance", and that cuts could be forthcoming "fairly soon". These comments, as well as the fact that the YE24 dots remained unchanged pointing to 3 cuts, dominated the somewhat more bearish signal from the upward revision to YE25 and YE26 dots. Also noteworthy was that Chair Powell alluded to slowing the pace of QT fairly soon (see The Jays beat the Hawks, M. Feroli, 3/20/2024). As a result, yields declined sharply led by the front end and forward OIS rates at upcoming meeting dates are lower on the week (Figure 1). The Fed's dovish bent was also echoed by other central banks this week - Thursday's BoE meeting produced a similar outcome (unchanged policy rates without hawkish dissents and an overall message that is similar to the Fed's waiting-to-ease stance - see Unchanged, but hawkish dissents dropped, A. Monks, 3/21/2024), and the SNB delivered a surprise rate cut.

The policy inclination appears fairly clear, but of course, it remains to be seen whether the data will in fact prove supportive. In recent weeks, we have utilized an analytical framework that seeks to illustrate the extent of policy uncertainty by decomposing the probability distribution implied by SFRZ4 options premia into a weighted combination of conditional distributions that correspond to specific scenarios (see What<sup>’</sup>s the rush?, 2/23/2024). Thus, even after this week's FOMC meeting, we remain in a state of considerable policy fog, that can only be remedied by the emergence of incoming data that provides the confirmation of disinflation that the Fed is looking for. This is a process that will inevitably take time.

As we await confirmatory evidence in the data in upcoming months, markets will likely remain mean reverting and rangebound, even if there is significant day-to-day volatility. But what does this mean for the swap yield curve?

As we discussed above, current forwards represent a blend of future possibilities that include bearish policy paths where the Fed under-delivers on cuts (e.g., no cuts or even 1 hike) as well as more bullish paths (e.g., 5 cuts) by year end. Armed with our empirical fair value model for various spot and forward swap yield curves (which we have discussed in detail elsewhere - see What<sup>’</sup>s the rush?, 2/23/2024), we can project the level of various spot and forward swap curves by year-end, in each such scenario, and compare that to what is priced into forwards as of a YE24 forward horizon. Specifically, we examine three stylized scenarios - one baseline scenario that assumes that future OIS rates will be fairly close to current forwards (where 3Mx3M OIS ends the year at \~4.15% and the 1st/5th 3M SOFR futures curve ends the year at \~minus 50bp), one "shallow cuts" scenario (3Mx3M OIS ends the year 75bp higher at \~4.9% and the SOFR futures curve goes to -90bp), and one "deeper cut" scenario (3Mx3M OIS goes to 3.65% and the SOFR futures curve goes to -35bp). In each of these scenarios we assume that the Fed's balance sheet declines as expected, and also assume that inflation swap forwards are realized. Under these assumptions, we project YE24 levels for selected 3M forward and 2Y forward swap curves (assuming for the moment that current mispricings relative to fair value do not converge to zero and will instead persist). These projections, as well as the YE24 forwards for each of these curves, are all shown in Figure 4.

As we look ahead to the rest of this year, policy uncertainty will be ultimately resolved and we will realize a policy easing path that is either deeper or shallower. What Figure 4 tells us is that when we compare what is priced into YE24 forwards for these various curves with projected outcomes in the future, we find that these projected future outcomes are likely to be flatter than forwards except in the case where the realized rate cuts prove to be significantly deeper. Moreover, that is even without considering any potential benefit from residual convergence, which would bias curves flatter still. Thus, yield curve flatteners, such as in the 2s/5s sector, are likely attractive except in the case where rate cuts prove deeper than expected.

This leads us to recommend two different ways of positioning for a flatter curve, while also guarding against the possibility of deeper rate cuts. One, we recommend initiating a forward 2s/5s swap curve flattener, but hedging the risk of steeper rate cuts with weighted longs in H5 and H6 3M SOFR futures (20% and 10% respectively - see Trade recommendations). The weighted longs in SFRH5 and SFRH6 contracts can be thought of as coming from a (i) 10% risk-weighted H5/H6 flattener and a (ii) 30% risk weighted long in SFRH5 contracts. These weights in turn come from the partial betas with respect to those factors estimated in our yield curve fair value framework. In addition to the main consideration discussed above, this trade additionally benefits from any residual convergence (since the 2s/5s curve appears too steep to fair value) as well as (somewhat more marginally) carry. Carry on the weighted box trade over a 1Y horizon is \~12bp, which lends additional support for this trade. Therefore, we recommend this trade.

Another way to position for a flatter 2s/5s curve while guarding against the possibility of deeper rate cuts is to do so conditionally in a selloff. As Figure 5 shows, the 2s/5s curve has been pretty uncorrelated with front end yields recently, but doing the trade conditionally in a selloff nevertheless has the attractive feature of avoiding exposure to the steepening that could arise in the event of deeper rate cuts being realized (or being priced in), since that would likely cause yields to decline. In addition, with 3Mx2Y swaption implied volatility falling to just below that of 3Mx5Y, the options market now permits the premium-neutral construction of a 2s/5s conditional bearish flattener struck at the forwards, which is also steeper than the 2s/5s spot curve. Given that we see the 2s/5s curve as likely biased flatter except in the event of a rally and rising expectations of deeper rate cuts, we therefore recommend this trade (see Trade recommendations).

Lastly, we also continue to favor well hedged positive carry structures that combine spot curve flatteners with forward curve steepeners. One such example is a box trade that pairs a 3M forward 5s/15s swap curve flattener with a 2Y forward 2s/20s swap curve steepener. As seen in Figure 6, these two curves have been well correlated, but the 5s/15s curve is currently a touch too steep making entry levels slightly attractive. But more importantly, the weighted box trade offers 3M carry of \~4.7bp, while the standard deviation of rolling 3M changes in the weighted box spread (measured over 2 years) is \~8.6bp, which makes the annualized carry-to-risk ratio fairly attractive at over 1.0. Therefore, we recommend this trade (see Trade recommendations).

Looking ahead, we turn neutral on shorter and intermediate tails as we find the risks balanced. We have mostly maintained a bullish stance on short and intermediate tails, due to the fact that implied volatility in those sectors appeared considerably cheap. In other words, although we recognized the bearish nature of a rangebound yield environment for volatility, we felt that was more than adequately priced into implieds. But with investors likely continuing to scale into carry-seeking short gamma strategies, the technical pressures appear difficult to overcome for long gamma positions. Therefore, with technicals continuing to appear bearish but with implied volatility appearing too cheap to entertain going short, we now turn neutral on gamma.

The exception to this is 30-year tails, where we maintain our bearish stance and recommend delta hedging less frequently to take advantage of likely mean reversion. As we noted last week, 30-year tails are the one sector where implied volatility remains rich, especially in longer expiries. Therefore, given relatively rich valuations, and given the fact that long end yields continue to exhibit strong mean reversion, we look for implieds to be biased lower in this sector and expect less-frequently delta-hedged short volatility positions to outperform.

## The Fed’s balance sheet and swap spreads

Swap spreads are wider on the week, with maturity matched swap spreads wider by 1-2 bp in most sectors but 3.5bp wider in the 20Y sector (Figure 8). In part, this week's widening appeared to be in response to shifting assumptions around the Fed's balance sheet. We begin by first discussing our outlook for the Fed's balance sheet.

Fed Chair Powell's comments this week hinted at a sooner than expected reduction in the pace of QT. Indeed, in response, our economists now expect a halving of the pace of QT beginning as early as May (see The Jays beat the Hawks, M. Feroli, 3/20/2024). This suggests to us that Dallas Fed President Logan's earlier comments are gaining ground as the Fed contemplates its balance sheet policy. In particular, Logan's acknowledgement of the merits of a floor on RRP balances as a safeguard against money market stresses (given the more inelastic nature of Reserves), as well as the notion that a slower pace of QT could be prudent as a means to extending the overall amount of balance sheet reduction, appear to have gained traction. Our Fed balance sheet forecast has indeed assumed both of these elements - we had previously thought that QT would be tapered beginning in July, and that QT would halt when RRP balances fall to \~\$300bn. In light of recent developments, we now look for the pace of balance sheet reduction to fall by half in May, and continue to look for QT to halt when RRP balances fall to \~300bn. Moreover, given the negative T-bill issuance in 2Q24, we also expect that RRP balances will become stickier in the near term. Based on these assumptions, and incorporating more recent balance sheet levels, our projections for the evolution of the Fed's balance sheet liabilities is shown in Figure 9. Our revised forecast continues to indicate that QT will likely continue for the remainder of the year, and will likely halt by year end at which point Reserves and O/N RRP balances will fall to \~\$3tn and \~\$300bn respectively.

These developments alone do not materially affect our views on swap spreads. Rather, the decision of how to reinvest MBS paydowns and maturing USTs will matter for swap spreads. But this week brought little new information on this score, and we may learn more when the minutes of this week's FOMC meeting are released.

Without much new information regarding the Fed's reinvestment strategy, we maintain our widening bias on swap spreads across the curve. At the long end of the curve, we remain biased towards swap spread wideners in the 30-year sector. As we noted last week, spreads remain below fair value in the long end, and the likelihood of receiving flows from VA hedgers (one of the main sources of spread narrowing pressure at the long end) appears low given the strength in equity markets (see (P)PI day, 3/15/2024). Moreover, as our cross sector strategists note, inflows into bond funds are running at a strong pace thus far this year (see Figure 5 in Overview). This is an important support for long end spread widening positions, because bond funds - whether active or passive - are typically managed versus an index and AUM growth tends to produce somewhat price-insensitive demand for long end assets (since they are part of the index). Indeed, we noted in our Outlook for 2024 that bond fund inflows will be an important source of widening pressure for long end swap spreads as we move through the year (see Interest Rate Derivatives 2024 Outlook, Trading Themes). With inflows coming in at a strong pace, we see the case for long end spread wideners as supportive and we maintain a widening bias.

We also continue to favor swap spread wideners in the 10Y sector, where spreads remain the most cheap relative to fair value. On a relative basis, one way to take advantage of this is to initiate exposure to 7s/10s swap spread curve steepeners hedged for the 7s/10s Treasury curve. As seen in Figure 10, the 7s/10s maturity matched OTR swap spread curve, adjusted for the 7s/10s Treasury curve using a -25% beta, has typically traded in a fairly tight range but is currently too narrow. With a nascent correction appearing to have begun, we now recommend initiating exposure to 7s/10s swap spread curve steepeners paired with 25% risk in 7s/10s UST curve steepeners (see Trade recommendations).

At the front end, our widening view is centered on upcoming negative T-bill issuance, which is likely to support wider front end swap spreads. As we noted last week, we prefer expressing this widening bias in the 3Y sector (or even in maturities just beyond the 2Y point) to take advantage of the steep inversion of the 2s/3s spread curve, since the 2Y sector continues to appear wide to fair value. In this vein, we had recommended initiating swap spread wideners using 2.125% May 26 last week, which has since widened by about 3.5bp. We therefore recommend unwinding this exposure, and instead recommend initiating widening exposure using 1.875% July 26, original issue 7Y notes, which offers better relative value (see Trade recommendations).
