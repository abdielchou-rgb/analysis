## The Risk of Higher Unemployment Rates is Real

The FOMC's Summary of Economic Projections (SEP) spoke to the supply-side story expected by our economists. At the same time, it contained a number of surprises, including the extent to which FOMC participants raised growth projections. Our economists expected the median participant to revise higher 2024 real GDP to 1.8% from 1.4%. Instead, the median increased to 2.1%.

This higher growth rate in 2024 likely contributed to the downward revisions in the 2024 unemployment rate projections. The SEP made clear that several FOMC participants lowered their 2024 unemployment rate projections – moving the median from 4.1% to 4.0% (see Exhibit 1 ).

These downward revisions fly in the face of the slow, but steady creep higher in the unemployment rate since mid-2023. A linear trend from the April 2023 nadir suggests an unemployment rate that averages 4.1% in 4Q24 vs. the 4.0% suggested in the SEP.

A nonlinear (polynomial) trend using data from February 2020 suggest a 4Q24 average unemployment rate of 5.3% (see Exhibit 2 ). Obviously, the unemployment rate does not evolve in line with either linear or polynomial trends. But history suggests that a rise in the unemployment rate occurs much faster than the subsequent decline generally.

While higher growth in the SEP may have weighed on the unemployment rate projections, the immigration-driven supply-side story would have otherwise lifted them, all else equal, according to our economists.

• The unemployment rate comes from the household survey of employment (Current Population Survey, or CPS).

• The household survey suggests much weaker job growth than the establishment survey (which comes from the Current Employment Statistics, or CES, program, see Exhibit 3 ).

• The household survey may be undercounting immigration-related employment, both outright and relative to the related labor force, thereby boosting the foreignborn unemployment rate.

Regardless of the household survey's shortcomings, it shows employment growth weakening over the last 12 months, even after smoothing for monthly volatility (see Exhibit 4 ). As a result, the unemployment rate came under upward pressure.

What if this trend continues? What could happen to the unemployment rate? Or, what amount of household employment growth would allow the unemployment rate to average 4.0% by year-end? In order to answer these questions, we ran various scenarios for household employment growth and the unemployment rate.

• A tool to perform this scenario analysis comes from the Atlanta Fed (see Jobs Calculator). The calculator allows you to calculate the average monthly payroll gains necessary to achieve a certain unemployment rate over a certain period of time, given a certain labor force participation rate.

The jobs calculator suggests that nonfarm payroll growth needs to average 168k/month over the next 10 months, assuming the labor force participation rate rises to its cycle highs (62.8%), to achieve a 4.0% unemployment rate at year-end. Given that nonfarm payroll gains averaged 250k/month in 2023, the necessary 168k/month seem well within reach.

According to the jobs calculator documentation, "Since the headline jobs number refers to the Payroll Survey and the unemployment rate comes from the Household Survey, the Jobs Calculator makes a simple transformation to the household employment calculation to produce the estimate of the number of payroll jobs needed."

• The transformation uses the ratio between establishment survey employment (CES) and household survey employment (CPS). The documentation suggests "The default CES/CPS multiplier (which is not adjustable by the user) is the current month’s ratio of payroll employment to household employment". But the jobs calculator allows the user to adjust the ratio anyway.

Unfortunately, the use of this ratio presents problems, given how much it has moved over time and more recently (see Exhibit 5 ). In addition, the calculation relies heavily on population growth and labor force participation. Put differently, the unemployment rate relies just as heavily on the size of the labor force as the average monthly employment gain.

More recently, the size of the labor force fell below its upward trend (see Exhibit 6 ). A smaller labor force, for a given amount of employment, would put downward pressure on the unemployment rate. As such, any rebound in the labor force, all else equal, would put upward pressure on the unemployment rate.

• Using an auto regressive process with a 1-period lag, AR(1), we project (not forecast!) the labor force over the next year (see Exhibit 7 ). We think a return to trend labor force growth, at a minimum, aligns with the immigration story our economists increasingly reference as a reason why breakeven employment may be higher than before.

If the labor force rebounds, the household survey needs to show a large rebound in monthly job gains in order to keep the unemployment rate at or below 4.0%. The necessary increase in job gains, conditional on a return to trend labor force growth, would mimic the rise in 1H23 (see Exhibit 8 ).

It's clear that the unemployment rate could easily rise above the median FOMC participant's 2024 projections – even if household employment growth rebounds. Perhaps more importantly, it could easily rise above the median participant's estimate of the natural rate of unemployment.

In that event, we would expect investors to worry again about a hard landing in the US. This worry would manifest itself in much lower US Treasury yields initially. Of course, FOMC participants may take the higher unemployment rates in stride – perhaps with the view that the natural rate rose as well, thanks to more immigration.

Nevertheless, we see risks to higher-than-expected unemployment rates, on account of increasingly weak employment trends in the household survey. And if those risks manifest in upcoming data, we think investors will be able to assess FOMC participant reaction at much lower yields than exist today.

## Stocks, Real Yields, and the USD

Why has the USD been negatively correlated with the S&P 500 since around the time of the global financial crisis?

Since 2010, the USD has reliably moved in the opposite direction of risk sentiment – particularly during the years immediately following the global financial crisis (GFC) and the onset of COVID. And this correlation has been on a long-term decline since the 1990s ( Exhibit 11 ).

The question matters for a range of investors – equity investors who hedge their positions with currency options, long-term institutional investors whose international portfolios carry both currency risk and global growth risk, and other investors who purchase derivatives such as correlation swaps.

One potential explanation for the USD's increasingly negative relationship to the S&P 500 is a shift in investor perception of rising real yields in the US ( Exhibit 12 ). Rising real yields have increasingly been associated with falling stocks (and vice versa).

As a result, market environments in which the USD is negatively correlated to risk sentiment have become increasingly common over the past 20 years. We characterize these market environments as Regime 1 and Regime 3 in our Real Yield USD Framework.

The US dollar moves dramatically in the opposite direction of investor optimism (i.e., breakeven yields, commodity prices, equity prices) when real yields and breakevens move in opposite directions.

Our analysis shows that USD and the S&P 500 reliably move in opposite directions in market environments where US real yields and US breakevens move in opposite directions – conditions we call Regime 1 and Regime 3.

In contrast, USD-S&P 500 correlation is mixed in Regime 2 and Regime 4. The correlation is weakly positive in Regime 2 and weakly negative in Regime 4.

Over the past 25 years, the increasingly negative correlation between TIPS yields and the S&P 500 has aligned with an increasing frequency of Regime 1 and Regime 3 months – in which the USD and risk demand move in opposite directions.

## EUR - Curve: What does stagflation mean for duration and curves?

In previous notes, we studied price action of the front end, duration and curves during ECB plateaux, i.e., between the last ECB hike and first ECB cut in previous monetary cycles (see for instance Circling the Airport or Landing?). One limit to such analysis is that growth and inflation dynamics were different than today's in most ECB cycles.

This week, we investigate duration and curves in stagflationary environments. As the history of eurozone data is rather short, we focus on Germany and more specifically on GDP and inflation yearly growth rates. To select appropriate periods, we screen for inflation and GDP growth according to (i) the levels, (ii) the 12m moving average, and (iii) the six-month change in the 12m moving average.

The closest periods relative to today (reported in Exhibit 32 ) are:

• Mid 1981: The 12MMA of German GDP was close to \~0% y/y and the 6m change was negative, i.e., growth was decelerating. On the other hand, 12MMA CPI was also \~6% y/y, however the latest print was still high and inflation was still accelerating when looking at the 6m change.

• End of 1993: German GDP was at -0.2% y/y, same as now, and had been decreasing in a similar fashion to the current cycle. On CPI, the 12MMA was \~4.5% y/y but it was also slightly accelerating.

• Mid 1983: The German CPI was in line with current levels (3.3% y/y) while growth in Germany had started rebounding.

• Late 1994: The German CPI came in at 2.5% y/y, closer to the current print of late 1993, and displayed some deceleration. Growth was rebounding as well.

Between the periods selected there is a distinction to be made: (i) the 1981 and the 1993 analogies were characterized by subdued and declining growth with inflation yet to find its bottom, whilst in (ii) the 1983 and 1994 periods, inflation had already bottomed with growth starting to rebound from its trough.

On the monetary policy front, in 1981, the Bundesbank hadn't started cutting rates yet (as it would do so in 82) while the BoE was still hiking and the Fed had just delivered its first cut. Conversely, in late 1993, the Bundesbank was already in its cutting cycle, lagging the Fed and the BoE, that started cutting in 89 and 91, respectively.

In 93, we were in the middle of the cutting cycle and Germany was in a recession but duration sold off. The catalysts for a rise in yields were twofold; (i) the Fed started its hiking cycle in early 94 and global

interest rates increased, and (ii) a tax reform led to a liquidity shock in markets, contributing to the rise in rates volatility.

Additionally, in 94, growth accelerated more than initially anticipated but this didn’t prevent yields from rallying later that year as mentioned above. The origin seemed in that case more exogenous.

All in all, price action on duration diverged between periods, nonetheless, cuts were systematically followed by a multi-month rally, even when growth was recovering. Cutting into a healthy growth environment is particularly topical these days in the US. This week, the FOMC raised the median 2024 GDP growth significantly from 1.4% to 2.1%, while keeping the median 2024 dot unchanged at 3 cuts.

This signals to markets that the strong growth of the past year is not as much a cause for concern as feared and will likely not prevent the Fed from cutting. Though there are many differences between the current situation and the 94 period in Germany, the analysis shows that rates can rally before and during cutting cycles despite healthy and recovering growth.

Results on curves are more consistent across periods. On average 2s5s and 2s10s steepened by \~60bp and \~80p in one year. Globally, in 81 and 94, curves steepened ahead of the cut. The steepening was less pronounced in 94, perhaps because the coming cut was a resumption of the previous cutting cycle and so some easing had already been delivered.

In 93, curves bear-steepened. With the Bundesbank still in its easing cycle, the rise in global yields likely spilled over to the back-end of the curve. Finally, in 83, there was no clear direction for curves.

All in all, on duration we found that cuts were systematically followed by a multimonth rally, even when growth was recovering rapidly. Additionally, growth and inflation dynamics don’t seem to be the only drivers of duration, with different patterns observed in similar macro environments.

On curves, results highlight that curves tend to steepen in a stagflationary environment - though monetary policy cannot be neglected. Additionally, when the Bundesbank was in its easing cycle in 83, external shocks had more impact on the 10y sector of the curve. Though markets were not as interconnected as today, this reinforces the possibility of a potential decoupling between Treasuries and Bunds, especially curvewise, when the ECB embarks on its cutting cycle.

## Cash EGBs: Enter 2s10s BTP steepeners vs Bunds

As highlighted in the curve section above, and from previous historical studies' takeaways, it should not come as a surprise that as we approach the start of the easing cycle a steepening bias in the curve should be warranted, as front-end rates get revised down.

At the current stage however, the uncertainties surrounding this cycle stem from how deep this cutting cycle will be, as we have not experienced a particular deterioration on the growth side yet. With inflation remaining sticky the risk of a reflationary spiral looms on the current global macro picture, if easing is not well calibrated. This concern is apparent in recent central bank commentaries.

Additionally, the willingness of the Fed to start cutting while being willing to tolerate an inflation rate higher than the 2% threshold, with economies being so interconnected, could pose bear-steepening pressures in the event that the stickiness in inflation continues in the context of sustained cross-market correlations. Moreover, the fiscal picture, where deficits are still abundant and with a good chunk of debt that will need to be refinanced in the next couple of years at higher rate, could demand additional compensation from investors down the line, in our view.

All of this is coming in a context where geopolitical tensions remain high, whilst the risky assets complex continues to be very well bid, with credit spreads at their tightest levels and US equities continuing to be at all time highs. The current volatility picture remains extremely subdued, tracking extremely well dynamics seen in previous easing years (see Exhibit 37 and Exhibit 38 ), feeding the bid for carry.

## Focus on differences in BoJ communications vis-à-vis future rate hikes and its balance sheet

In an outcome that came as no major surprise after having been so heavily telegraphed in the media, the BoJ terminated its negative interest rate policy (NIRP), “yield curve control” (YCC) framework, and so-called “inflation-overshooting commitment”.

The central bank did however signal that it will leave its pace of JGB purchases “broadly” unchanged for at least 2Q 2024, and also offered (conditional) forward guidance to the effect that “Given the current outlook for economic activity and prices, the Bank anticipates that accommodative financial conditions will be maintained for the time being”.

We found it interesting that Governor Kazuo Ueda went into some detail about the BoJ’s policy response function when asked about future rate hikes, but refrained from offering any specifics about future quantitative tightening (QT) other than acknowledging that it will obviously need to begin at some point. Ueda also emphasized that short-term interest rates will be the central bank’s primary monetary policy control variable going forward.

Asked about the circumstances under which a further rate hike might be considered appropriate, Ueda said that the BoJ does not yet see a 100% likelihood of the price stability target being achieved, and indicated that a further increase in the policy rate might be considered if that assessed likelihood should rise, or in other words underlying inflation should keep climbing towards +2%.

The overall takeaway is that while the BoJ is already prepared to further adjust its policy rate if it starts to feel more confident in the outlook for economic activity and prices, it does not yet feel comfortable offering any guidance on the QT front. Our economists have interpreted this as an indication that the next rate hike is likely to come before a commencement of QT (see this).

We thus expect conditions to remain basically supportive for cash JGBs for some time to come. Not only has the BoJ yet to offer any concrete guidance as to the timing and pace of QT, but we are also set to see a decline in fresh issuance through the 10y sector once the new fiscal year gets under way in April.

Some market participants have suggested that the BoJ might view the resultant tightening of supply/demand as an opportunity to start dialing back its purchases, but we would expect any such reduction to be quite modest at most.

As we discussed in "Macro Strategy: Turning Point For ASW", the BoJ has already tapered its monthly JGB purchases to the point where they are now on a rough par with redemptions (see Exhibit 54 ). A certain amount of variation from month to month is of course likely to be tolerated by the market.

However, a particularly sharp reduction without prior warning would be liable to cause “discontinuity” of the sort that the central bank has indicated that it will be looking to avoid (and might thereby end up driving long-term interest rates into uncomfortably high territory).

None of the world’s major central banks has taken a “stealth” approach to QT. And it needs to be recognized that any further reduction in the BoJ’s JGB purchases would constitute QT rather than mere “tapering” (curtailment of QE). Testifying before the upper house Finance and Monetary Affairs Committee on March 21, Ueda indicated that the BoJ’s JGB holdings would basically be held steady for the time being.

Expectations of further rate hikes do however look likely to build in the wake of the March meeting (see Exhibit 55 ). As mentioned above, Ueda has already acknowledged that additional policy adjustments could be on the cards if the assessed likelihood of achieving the +2% price stability target continues to rise.

JPY’s downtrend has continued despite the BoJ’s March 19 decision to bring an end to its negative interest rate policy (NIRP). This is somewhat difficult to explain in terms of fundamentals given that (1) pricing of US short-term r\* has actually started to decline over the past few days amid modest downward pressure on UST yields and (2) pricing of the BoJ’s future rate hike trajectory has meanwhile turned more hawkish (see Exhibit 89 ).

As discussed in JPY | Contemplating JPY Flows: A Better Explanation than “New NISA"?, March 2, 2024, a continuing rally in Japanese equities is likely at least part of the story. Domestic stock prices did indeed fall quite sharply for a period earlier this month as a March BoJ normalization move began to be viewed as more likely, but had already started climbing again prior to the meeting amid apparent expectations of the BoJ remaining dovish even after exiting NIRP.

The BoJ did not explicitly rule out further rate hikes, but meanwhile indicated that it “anticipates that accommodative financial conditions will be maintained for the time being” given the current outlook for economic activity and prices.

This appears to have reassured equity investors that neither rapid rate hikes nor a commencement of QT are likely any time soon. With stock prices having rallied by almost 5% within just a matter of days, overseas investors buying on a FX-hedged basis will have seen a further need to buy USD/JPY (i.e., sell JPY for USD) and may therefore have helped to drive it further above fair value (see Exhibit 90 and Exhibit 91 ).

Moreover, CFTC data point to somewhat of an unwinding of USD/JPY short positions from non-commercial investors ahead of the BoJ meeting on the back of various media headlines which hinted at a March BoJ hike (see Exhibit 92 ). As the BoJ confirmed no rapid hikes anytime soon, investors might reinstate JPY carry positions.

As we discussed in JPY | Contemplating JPY Flows: A Better Explanation than “New NISA"?, the relationship between Japanese stock prices and USD/JPY appears bidirectional. Equity investors will likely welcome weakening of JPY to the extent that it supports the earnings of exporters.

On the other hand, FX investors are liable to view a stock market rally as negative for JPY in so much as it will generate additional hedging needs. It would thus appear as though a “virtuous cycle” of rising stock prices and a weakening JPY could help to keep USD/JPY higher than would ordinarily be consistent with the current Fed-BoJ policy trajectory gap.

Fundamentals could conceivably start to generate more downward pressure if the Fed kicks off a rate cut cycle in June as our economists expect. However, as long as a “soft landing” is still viewed as the most likely outcome for the US economy, narrowing of the Fed-BoJ policy rate gap is liable to be so gradual as to leave the appeal of JPY carry trades only marginally tarnished.

Moreover, a “soft landing” consensus should of course be supportive for risk sentiment and thereby leave the aforementioned “virtuous cycle” of rising stock prices and a weakening JPY intact. We therefore see potential for any negative impact of Fed-BoJ policy rate convergence on USD/JPY to be cancelled out by FX-hedging flows as necessitated by further rises in Japanese stock prices.

It is difficult to envisage this situation changing unless a US “hard landing” starts to be viewed as more likely, in which case markets might begin to look for much more rapid Fed-BoJ policy rate convergence and risk assets would almost certainly face correction pressure.

The relationship between Japanese stock prices and USD/JPY might then turn into more of a negative spiral, with USD/JPY facing downward pressure from both fundamentals and technicals. In other words, it is important to remain mindful of the risk that USD/JPY could end up plunging if the market start to price in the risk of US “hard landing” scenario.

Given this risk, we suggest maintaining a neutral stance on USD/JPY rather than positioning too heavily for further upside.

## Might Japan intervene?

We have been fielding an increasing number of questions about FX intervention risk now that USD/JPY is approaching its levels of November 2023. JPY-supportive jawboning has yet to be ramped up all that noticeably, and are our own views are still unchanged from those explained in JPY | Is There a Risk of Potential MoF Intervention? February 17, 2024.

The government (MoF) would appear to have little incentive to intervene from a fundamentals perspective, with Japan’s terms of trade having improved as import prices have fallen so sharply from their peak levels, and the currency’s weakness having meanwhile buoyed exporter earnings sufficiently to support such sizeable spring wage hikes.

That said, the MoF has made it clear that it considers excessive volatility undesirable, and might thus see a case for “stabilizing” intervention if USD/JPY should keep surging towards (say) 155 with little sign of resistance.

## Continued MMF Appetite for Bills

Total MMF holdings of UST debt has returned to mid-2021 levels, although UST debt only comprises 40% of total assets ( Exhibit 103 ). This compares with an allocation of over 50% in prior years and suggests MMFs still have room to absorb more bill supply. In addition, still-ample balances in the RRP suggest additional demand for bills.

MMF bill purchases in February showed additions to bills in all buckets, except front-end bills (1m or less). 1m-2m bills garnered the largest growth amidst a substantial push-out of market-implied rate cuts during the month ( Exhibit 104 ). ## Is market pricing in line with the SEP?

We assess the fairness of swap pricing by applying a framework similar to the one we used for UST here. The premise is that swap pricing during a cutting cycle is driven by two factors: (1) the timing of the first cut, and (2) the pace of the cutting cycle. At a high level, the validity of these assumptions can be assessed by consulting the correlations in Exhibit 115 and Exhibit 116 .

Since June of last year, the regression coefficients have proven stable (see Exhibit 120 ). The signs of the coefficients generally correspond with our intuition:

With these tools in hand, we can consider the fair value of swap rates under various hypothetical policy scenarios. We start by consulting the March SEP dot plot, which has the median participant anticipating 3 cuts this year. While the starting point is not known, market pricing suggests that the cutting cycle will either start in June (\~80%) or July (\~20%).

Using a probability-weighted average of the model output in these two scenarios, we arrive at a fair value of 4.67%/4.11%/3.94% for the 2y/5y/10y swap rates. This compares with observed swap rates of 4.47%/3.93%/3.80% – suggesting current pricing is slightly rich to the SEP projections.

## A case for conditional bull steepeners

Last month, we argued against conditional bull steepeners. Our thesis centered on the steepness of forward curves vs. spot. While forward curves are still steeper than spot (see Exhibit 123 ), the gap between the two has shrunk (see Exhibit 124 ). This beckons the question – are conditional bull steepeners attractive? We think the answer is increasingly yes, for a few reasons:

Improved vol pick-up: For the past few months, bull steepeners have entailed buying elevated vols to sell depressed vols. Over the past month, the expiry term structure has steepened in vol space, meaning investors can enter ATMF trades nearly costless (see Exhibit 125 ).

Near flat 1m vega roll: A 3m2s5s bull steepener will be slightly vega negative, but the 1m roll-down on the trade is minimal (see Exhibit 126 )

## Trading Strategy 1 – "Trade Longs/Fade Shorts"

When the 5-day moving average crosses above the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract (long duration) and hold for a 25-business-day period. When the 5-day moving average crosses below the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract and hold for a 25-business-day period. In short, this strategy buys futures when the Simple Moving Average Crossover (SMAX) generates both a long and a short signal, given the historical outperformance of long signals traded long and underperformance of short signals traded short. Given that the SMAX could generate both a long and a short signal within the predefined holding period, an investor may have a 200% long position since each of the two signals would be traded in separate portfolio sleeves.

## Definition of terms

Buy/Long: The analyst expects the total or excess return (depending on the nature of the recommendation) of the instrument or issuer that is the subject of the investment

recommendation to be positive over the relevant time period.

Sell/Short: The analyst expects the total or excess return (depending on the nature of the recommendation) of the instrument or issuer that is the subject of the investment recommendation to be negative over the relevant time period.

Selling protection or Buying Risk: The analyst expects that the price of protection against the event occurring will decrease over the relevant time period.

Buying protection or Selling Risk: The analyst expects the price of protection against the event occurring will increase over the relevant time period.

Pay: The analyst expects that over the specified time period the variable rate underlying the swap agreement that is the subject of the investment recommendation will increase.

Receive: The analyst expects that over the specified time period the variable rate underlying the swap agreement that is the subject of the investment recommendation will decrease.

Unless otherwise specified, the time frame for recommendations included in the Morgan Stanley Fixed Income Research reports is 1 - 3 months and the price of financial instruments mentioned in the recommendation is as at the date and time of publication of the recommendation.

When more than one issuer or instrument is included in a recommendation, analyst expects one part of the trade to outperform the other trade or combination of other trades included in the recommendation on a relative basis.
