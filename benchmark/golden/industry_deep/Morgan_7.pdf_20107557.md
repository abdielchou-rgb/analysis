## Market commentary

Treasury yields rose sharply this week, with the 2y reaching its highest level since November and trading in a wide range of 4.75% to 4.97% from peak to trough on the back of strong inflation data. Notably, core CPI rose 0.4% in March, and the year-ago level came in above expectations at 3.8%. In particular, the closely-watched “super core<sup>”</sup> measure (core services ex. tenants<sup>’</sup> rent and OER) has been on a strong run lately, increasing 0.6% in March and surging 8.2% saar over the most recent three months (see US: March CPI up 0.4%, with another upside surprise for core, D. Silver, 4/10/24). Elsewhere, March<sup>’</sup>s PPI report revealed an increase of 0.2% for both headline and core, which is not particularly firm, but the headline and core year-ago rates were higher than their respective February readings. With three firm inflation prints in hand, it<sup>’</sup>s getting more challenging to dismiss those readings as aberrations. Even so, our economists still anticipate inflation to continue to moderate and look for core PCE inflation to be 0.27% (2.7% oya) in March (see US: March PPI is not so eventful, D. Silver, 4/11/24).

Meanwhile, markets have continued to dial back interest rate policy expectations, pricing a later start date to cuts and a shallower easing cycle. OIS forwards have now pushed back the first 25bp cut to September and are pricing in just a total of 47bp in cuts this year versus 64bp last Friday and 170bp in January (Figure 1OIS forwads hvenpu bckt i25 m g jl 47 y, 6 F 10 J). To be sure, markets are expecting rates to remain higher for longer. In fact, before this week, the probability distribution as implied by options on December 2024 SOFR futures indicated that the most-likely policy scenario was five cuts. However, post this week<sup>’</sup>s CPI inflation data, that most-likely scenario changed to one hike (Figure 2 . Similarly, even going into next year, the June 2025 SOFR futures option probability distribution has moved to fewer cuts, suggesting a shallower easing cycle and and the increased probability of a soft landing (see Interest Rate Derivatives).

Indeed, with recent economic news bolstering confidence in a high-for-longer path for poli cy interest rates along with positive developments related to balance sheets, US supply-side performance, and global financial conditions, the combination of these factors challenges the view that high rates will lead to a recession in the near term. Instead, there is a greater possibility of a “soft landing,<sup>”</sup> with more weight of a higher r\* than previously expected (see High maintenance, B. Kasman, 4/9/24). As of now, we continue to project the first cut in July and 3 cuts in 2024.

On the topic of monetary policy, the balance-sheet conversation in the March FOMC meeting minutes did not reveal any surprising information and supported our economists<sup>’</sup> view that the monthly cap on Treasury runoff could be halved in mid-May (see Fed balance sheet plans coming into better focus, M. Feroli, 4/10/24). Even with this anticipated May start to QT tapering, we think QT could continue to run through year-end.

Separately, in recent weeks, Treasury cut weekly T-bill auction sizes in 4-week through 13-week auctions as expected, as Treasury prepares for tax receipts around the April tax filing deadline. Our Treasury strategists expect a sharp rise to the TGA in the coming days due to the concentration of tax receipts around the tax filing deadline. Indeed, in the last two years, more than 50% of total non-withheld individual income taxes were concentrated in the four days around the April tax filing deadline. We could see the TGA rise over the next two weeks, which should allow Treasury to maintain smaller T-bill auction sizes until the cash balance declines to more normalized levels, and expect an additional -\$116bn in net T-bill issuance over coming three weeks. There are downside risks to these forecasts, as we might be underestimating the strength of upcoming tax receipts amid tight labor markets and strong asset performance last year (see Tracking the April tax season, J. Barry, 4/11/24).

To that end, we will monitor the money markets closely next week. So far this month, liquidity has remained abundant, as reflected in SOFR and TGCR setting at 5.31% and 5.30%, respectively, even as MMF AUMs declined slightly by \$43bn (from 4/2 to 4/11). We continue to think that outflows related to tax payments should not result in a funding crisis for a variety of reasons. First, there is still a positive balance at the ON RRP facility, which can take advantage of any dislocations in the funding markets. Second, the SRF is available for eligible counterparties to source liquidity, which should provide a soft ceiling for overnight rates. Third, the recent stickiness of reserves perhaps suggests that banks might already be preparing for outflows, thus minimizing the shock to the system. And finally, there is still plenty of liquidity in the markets; market participants are ready to take advantage of any dislocations they might see, limiting how high SOFR and FF could go. Overall, while we could see some funding pressures in the coming weeks, they should be relatively contained.

## March MMF holdings update: shifting into reverse

March generally saw MMF AUMs rise further, though they dropped considerably at monthend due to the Good Friday holiday. As in prior years, corporates tend to pull cash out of MMFs in the day leading up to Good Friday (given that it<sup>’</sup>s a holiday in the bond markets but not elsewhere) and redeposit the proceeds the day after. Indeed, cash flowed back into MMFs on April 1 and 2, when AUMs reached a new high of \$6.41tn, a \$238bn increase YTD (Figure 3The montary dpiMF AUsc- bu  lg fG , w k  12  \$6.4).

In any case, government MMFs ended the month down by more than \$70bn from February. The quarter-end gave way to a \$97bn drop in their Treasury and Agency repo, a \$71bn drop in their bill holdings, and a \$77bn increase in their RRP balances (Figure 4). This shift is not surprising—even as total bill outstandings rose by \$52bn in March, it seems many funds reached their capacity for bill absorption, as a majority of MMFs sat at WAMs of above 35d and held under \$15bn in RRP in March, with some funds decreasing their WAMs since February-end (Figure 5; see February MMF holdings update, T. Ho, 3/13/24). To that effect, growing uncertainty on the path of monetary policy might have inspired a degree of portfolio shortening. Indeed, government funds reduced bill holdings in the >2m space (-\$141bn) but increased holdings of bills maturing within 30 days by \$66bn month-over-month (Figure 6). Furthermore, looking at how WAMs behaved during the peaks of historical hiking cycles, government WAMs might not extend beyond their February-end highs of 38 days in the near term; in fact, they<sup>’</sup>ve come back down to about 35 days as of 4/11/24 (Figure 7 ). We<sup>’</sup>re unlikely to see WAMs meaningfully surpass these levels until the Fed signals that rate cuts are imminent.

On the prime fund side, AUMs were relatively stable month-over-month as funds shifted out of CDs and TD and into repo and Treasuries, though they only picked up about \$14bn in T-bills (versus \$50bn in February and January) (Figure 8). Somewhat notably, they kept their Fed ON RRP balances relatively steady. Institutional prime funds in particular have been gradually increasing their WLA (weekly liquid assets) over the course of this year, now averaging 73%. This shift to more liquid portfolios can be attributed to MMF reform deadlines, with the new 25%/50% DLA/WLA threshold requirements having come into effect earlier this month and with uncertainty over potential outflows as the new liquidity fee framework comes into play in October.

The Fed<sup>’</sup>s ON RRP balance in total rose to \$594bn on 3/28, a \$92bn step up from where it ended in February (Figure 9). Paired with the fact that balances largely remained within the \$400-500bn range over March, never falling below \$410bn, it is clear balances are keeping sticky as MMF bill pickup has waned/reversed and as Fed uncertainty has lingered. In April, with net bill issuance turning negative, it is likely MMFs<sup>’</sup> T-bill holdings will continue to come down. And considering that headline and core CPI each came in above expectations at +0.4% for March (see US: March CPI up 0.4%, with another upside surprise for core, D. Silver, 4/10/24), which led markets to push back rate cut expectations, MMF portfolio shortening could likely be a continued theme this month, and RRP balances should stay elevated in the near term.
