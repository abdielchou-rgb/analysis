## Market commentary

Another week of data, another week of higher Treasury yields. Markets once again have pushed back the timing of the first full rate cut, now to December, and are only pricing 68bp of rate cuts by June 2025 (Figure 1). To be sure, high-for-longer is increasingly becoming the narrative as inflation continues to remain firm and fails to deliver the needed confidence for the Fed to start easing. Indeed, the PCE price index increased 0.3% in March, with this measure up 2.7%oya, while the core PCE deflator rose 0.3% during the month (0.317% to three decimals) and 2.8%oya. While the core price index continues to moderate on a yearago basis, it has been running firm lately, jumping 4.4% saar over the latest three months, and the supercore jumped 5.5% saar over the three months through March. At the same time, the PCE report for March showed another strong month for consumer spending, with real consumption coming in at 0.5% and nominal spending jumping 0.8%, both above expectations. Given the strong momentum in consumer spending in late 1Q24, our economists now expect 2Q24 real US GDP growth of 2.5% saar (see US: Revising up our 2Q GDP forecast, D. Silver, 4/26/24), even following weaker-than-expected 1Q24 GDP growth of 1.6% (see US 1Q GDP disappoints as core PCE prices firm, M. Hanson, 4/25/24). Meanwhile, the labor markets continue to show their resiliency, as weekly initial claims data for the week ending April 20 registered at 207K, down by 5K from the prior week and remaining near historically-low levels (see US: Initial claims stayed low at 207,000, M. Tasci, 4/25/24).

Against this backdrop, at next week<sup>’</sup>s FOMC meeting, the Fed is widely expected to hold policy rates at current levels. We anticipate the post-meeting statement will be little changed from the March meeting, whereas the more interesting developments should occur at the press conference. It<sup>’</sup>s possible that Chair Powell<sup>’</sup>s opening remarks could echo recent comments in which he expressed that first quarter inflation data haven<sup>’</sup>t increased the Fed<sup>’</sup>s confidence of getting back to 2%. If asked about the possibility of further hikes, we expect the Chair will indicate that it<sup>’</sup>s not his base case, but also something he can<sup>’</sup>t rule out. Regarding the balance sheet, our economists believe there are now slightly higher odds of the FOMC reducing the monthly cap on Treasury runoff from \$60bn to \$30bn, for implementation in mid-May (see FOMC preview, M. Feroli, 4/26/24).

Next week is also Treasury<sup>’</sup>s May Quarterly Refunding announcement, and our Treasury strategists continue to forecast the deficit to be \$1.51tn for FY24 and anticipate \$245bn in net bill issuance in 2024, slightly lower than prior estimates. They expect Treasury to announce \$227bn in net marketable borrowings in the April-June quarter, assuming an endof-quarter TGA balance of \$750bn. Looking farther out, they continue to anticipate balances at the TGA to hover around current levels, particularly around year-end despite the debt ceiling suspension period ending in early January. While prior debt ceiling episodes have resulted in Treasury running down the TGA, which often leads to a significant reduction in net T-bill issuance, a recent re-released legal opinion written in 2021 from the Justice Department clarifies that Treasury can keep a prudent cash balance by the end of a debt ceiling suspension period. Considering the growth of the Treasury market over the last three years, and in particular how many additional securities roll over on a monthly basis, this leads our Treasury strategists to believe that TGA can continue to hover around current levels, thus limiting its impact on T-bills.

On buybacks, our strategists also expect Treasury to announce the date of the first regular buyback operation, as well as the schedules for the cash management and liquidity support facilities. Furthermore, they think Treasury could announce a cash management buyback operation in the very near term to help reduce the TGA, which currently sits at \$956bn following last week<sup>’</sup>s tax payments, and it<sup>’</sup>s likely Treasury announces another cash management buyback following June 15 tax payments. Finally, with regards to the SOMA portfolio following the end of the QT process, our strategists continue to see a risk that the Fed could move towards holding more bills, which would reduce the duration of the portfolio. Additionally, the Fed could reinvest MBS proceeds into T-bills, as opposed to coupon securities, which could prompt more T-bill issuance next year. However, we are not sure that would be the case for a few reasons. First, the T-bill share of the Treasury market should remain above the 15-20% range for some time. Second, primary dealer holdings of T-bills nearly doubled over the last month to an all-time high \$119bn. Third, MMF AUM growth has been slower this year, and if the Fed is in the middle of an easing cycle in 2025, as we expect, MMF demand might slow down (see May refunding preview, J. Barry, 4/24/24).

## MMF holdings update special edition: tax season flows

In a departure from our usual monthly MMF holdings update, we took a look at Crane<sup>’</sup>s weekly MMF holdings data to see how their portfolios evolved around tax season—a time period when MMFs tend to see larger outflows. Figure 2Of theaxbl MFswi rpodng  4/18(\~0%  uv),   T-  c k shows the composition of their portfolios as of March month-end, 4/11 or 4/12, and 4/18 or 4/19. This is based on 42 taxable MMFs with reported holdings data as of at least 4/18, representing close to 40% of the entire MMF universe.

Not surprisingly, the weekly holdings data reveal that MMFs shifted away from T-bills during the first two weeks of April, declining by \$11bn MTD through April 12 and by another \$22bn on and in the days following April 15. This observed drop in T-bill holdings makes sense—at the same time that net bill issuance had fallen MTD by a full \$134bn as of a week ago, MMFs were in many cases seeing tax-related outflows and/or shortening portfolios in response to growing hawkishness. On average, taxable MMFs have reduced WAMs by 2-3 days MTD. To the degree that MMFs were shedding T-bills to fund potential outflows, this might have also contributed to elevated primary dealer T-bill inventories and the resilience of T-bill/SOFR spreads. As our Treasury strategists noted, the negative T-bill issuance heading into tax season did not result in a richening in T-bill/SOFR spreads, as the higher-thanexpected primary dealer T-bill inventories might have counterbalanced the impact of reduced issuance (see Tracking the April tax season, J. Barry, 4/11/24).

Meanwhile, the weekly holdings data also reveal a notable pickup in non-Fed repo. It appears that numerous MMFs eagerly sought funding away from the Fed in the days just before April 15, with the above-mentioned subset of funds increasing non-Fed repo holdings by \$53bn from March-end. This suggests funds found non-Fed repo attractive relative to RRP, a fact that could partly explain the meaningful drop in ON RRP on April 12. This appears to be supported by the data: on a weighted-average basis, many funds (at least 21) secured overnight Treasury funding at a coupon rate of 5.31% or above on 4/11 or 4/12, versus RRP<sup>’</sup>s 5.30% (Figure 3On aweightd-vr bs,my MF(l 21)cu o T f p 5.3%  4/ RP’ 0).

Looking ahead, by the end of April, we<sup>’</sup>re likely to see a significant month-over-month drop in MMFs<sup>’</sup> total T-bill holdings, especially considering that we<sup>’</sup>ve now seen T-bill outstandings fall by a total of \$180bn MTD. Aside from bills, we also expect MMF flows to return to positive and ON RRP balances to remain fairly sticky in the near term.

## Get in the clear: more details on Treasury repo clearing

The below section is excerpted from our latest special note, FAQs on Treasury repo clearing.

Last December, the SEC finalized the Treasury clearing mandate, which would require covered clearing agencies to have direct participants submit all eligible repo and cash trades for central clearing. All repo and reverse repo trades done with a direct participant, other than those facing certain public sector entities, are scoped in under the rule, with an implementation date of June 30, 2026. This is a big deal, as only about 30% of the Treasury repo market is currently cleared (Figure 4Based onFt,ly bu30% fhTr pmk ic ), and most of the remaining \$3.2tn would need to be moved over to central clearing.

In order to access clearing at FICC, scoped-in market participants (MMFs, hedge funds, banks, insurance companies, asset managers, retirement funds, pension funds, etc.) must be/become one of the following: 1) a GSD netting member, 2) a Sponsored Member of a Sponsoring Member, 3) an Indirect Participant of an Agent Clearer, or 4) a direct limited member of FICC<sup>’</sup>s Centrally Cleared Institutional Tri-Party Service. Importantly, last month, FICC submitted an NPR change to the SEC in terms of how it plans on modifying GSD rules with respect to the separation, collection, and holding of margin for proprietary transactions and that of indirect participant transactions. Margin collection/posting varies depending on how a market participant accesses clearing and whether the repos are being transacted in a “done-with<sup>”</sup> or “done-away<sup>”</sup> model. For the most part, market participants have gravitated towards becoming a sponsored member and engaging in sponsored repo, which is transacted in a “done-with<sup>”</sup> model. Notably, the SEC provided a 5-year exemption for registered funds, including MMFs, to place margin at FICC to support their sponsored repo transactions, but because FICC<sup>’</sup>s current infrastructure prohibits end users from posting directly to FICC, dealers will likely continue to post margin on behalf of MMFs. While sponsored repo is currently the primary form of clearing for many market participants, there are still challenges with this access, particularly with respect to hedge funds and their inability to engage in portfolio margining or engage in repo with a “done-away<sup>”</sup> model.

In general, clearing repos should increase dealer repo capacity given balance-sheet netting benefits and capital efficiencies gained. However, there are meaningful operational and regulatory considerations that could limit the benefits gained from moving into clearing. At this time, it<sup>’</sup>s unclear whether FICC<sup>’</sup>s Agent Clearing Model is a commercially-viable one that will incentivize firms to become agent clearers to facilitate“done-away<sup>”</sup> Treasury repo transactions, which is uncommon in the current Treasury repo ecosystem. Even if they get Rule 15c3-3a relief by posting customer margin on a gross basis, the economics of running an agent clearing business could change meaningfully.
