# India Economic Perspectives

## 2024-25 budget: on track towards fiscal glide path

## Budget projects better-than-expected fiscal consolidation of 70bps in FY25

In today's interim budget announcement, the central government not only fared better by estimating the FY24 fiscal deficit (FD) at 5.8% of GDP (vs. 5.9% as per budget estimates (BE)), it also pegged the FY25 deficit at 5.1% of GDP. While the welfare focus remained on women, youth, farmers and the poor, the government refrained from announcing any outright fiscal populism in the run-up to the parliamentary elections. The finance minister reiterated the government's commitment to bringing FD to below 4.5% of GDP by FY26. Considering domestic economic activity remains largely resilient, the pursuit of counter-cyclical fiscal policy should help strengthen India's macroeconomic stability and create a foundation for strong medium-term growth. The robust government capex (despite high base) and lower-than-expected market borrowings would also create conducive conditions for a broad-based pick-up in private corporate capex which is key for sustainable growth.

## Moderation in capex growth, lower subsidies and reasonable tax assumptions

FY25 fiscal consolidation of 70bps assumes growth in revenue receipts of 11.2%YoY (vs. 13.3%YoY in FY24RE) and expenditure of 6.1%YoY (vs. 7.1%YoY in FY24RE). Indeed, the key source of fiscal consolidation projected in FY25 seems to be largely led by revenue expenditure (11.2% of GDP vs. 11.9% of GDP in FY24RE). The subsidy bill also eased further to 1.3% of GDP in FY25BE (from 1.5% last year) on lower fertilizer and food subsidy. The headline capital expenditure, on the other hand, is projected to pick up to 3.4% of GDP in FY25BE (from 3.2% of GDP in FY24RE). While headline capex growth of 17%YoY in FY25BE appears robust (following c30%YoY growth in the past three years), core capex growth (consisting of key infrastructure sector (Figure 14)) is estimated to moderate to 4.8%YoY (from 14% in FY24RE). We think the government's FY25 gross tax revenue growth estimate of 11.5%YoY and nominal GDP growth assumption of 10.5%YoY appear reasonable. The tax buoyancy works out to be 1.05 in FY25BE (vs. 1.4 in FY24RE) and there is room for some upside surprise later. The government continued to rely on one-off revenue receipts including RBI dividend transfer (Rs1trn) and the divestment proceeds (Rs500bn).

## IGBs: Lower borrowings add to the index inclusion optimism

FY25 gross/net borrowing estimates at INR14.1trn/11.8trn, as announced in today's budget, were c.INR1trn below market expectations. This positive surprise pushed bond yields lower by c.10bp, adding to the ongoing market optimism around upcoming bonds' index inclusion, and surging foreign debt flows (US\$2.2bn CYTD, 12m rolling at US\$10bn). We expect the latter to continue, with forthcoming index inclusion (starting June) attracting US\$25-30bn of further non-resident inflows (worth 20% of net IGB issuance, or c.13% of net IGBs/SDLs issuances). Forthcoming Fed rate cuts and generally contained domestic price pressures only add to this optimism. We expect 10y IGB yield to decline to 6.6% by end FY25. Equally so, however, we don't expect the post budget euphoria to extend in the very near term, as we find the market expectations of imminent liquidity easing by the RBI (i.e. equivalent to a c.20bp cut) at odds with resilient economic activity and credit growth. Pay 2y INR NDOIS as a tactical hedge.

## Equities: No Surprises

Overall the interim budget contained no major taxation changes (we may have to wait until the full budget post elections). The discipline in market borrowings should contain yields. There were weak takeaways for (bottom of pyramid) consumption and capex that matters for markets. Rural spending (Rural+agri+ Jal Shakti) is budgeted 6% yoy growth (in real terms it would be negligible), and could be sentimentally weak for rural focused consumption sectors / 2 wheelers. In India, the bulk of government spending is biased towards boosting consumption rather than investment. Interest payments, defence, pay and allowances and subsidies are the main components of the central government's revenue expenditure. The government's revenue expenditure will provide a short-term jolt to economic activity; however, it is the capital outlays that tend to have a lasting positive multiplier impact on overall growth. The government estimates revenue expenditure (net of subsidies and interest) to slow to 6.3% of GDP in FY25BE (from an average of 7.4% of GDP registered during FY21-24).

The government's subsidy bill is estimated to ease further to 1.3% of GDP in FY25BE from 1.5% of GDP estimated in FY24RE, largely on account of lower fertilizer and food subsidy bill.

Among global emerging markets (EMs), UBS estimates India will have the third highest public debt-to-GDP ratio in 2024E followed by Brazil and China. That said, compared with other high-debt EMs, India's public debt is largely domestic-funded and a significant share is held by local banks and the central bank, reducing the risk in a distress situation.
