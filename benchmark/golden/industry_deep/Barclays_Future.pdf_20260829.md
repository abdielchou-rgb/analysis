# Global Portfolio Manager's Digest Into the Future

We provide context and perspective on research across regions and asset classes, this week highlighting: our thoughts on the impact of renewables on gas-fired generation; the impact of tensions in the Mid-East and its implications on Israel and global markets; and a look into the future of predictive Earth observation data.

• Gas Hero to Net Zero: Power is having a moment, with rising electricity demand and higher prices (decoupling from natural gas). In the context of more renewables on the grid and data centers consuming more baseload generation capacity, the natural concern – or opportunity, depending on one's perspective – is whether much more dispatchable generation, such as natural gas, will be needed to supplement and smooth out the inherent intermittency of solar and wind. However, utility-scale batteries ofer a technology, already being deployed in some markets, that could provide an alternative to gas during demand peaks.

• Mid-East Tensions & the Global Markets: The unprecedented direct military confrontation between Iran and Israel has raised fears of broader escalation. We take stock of the implications for Israel's macro outlook, as well as for FX, rates, credit, and equities, both in the region and beyond.

Sectors such as insurance, energy, and agribusiness, that have started to harness observation data, have improved operational eficiencies and unlocked new products and services. We highlight five components of the Earth observation value chain: 1) physical infrastructure; 2) digital infrastructure; 3) data; 4) distribution; and 5) analytics.

## Higher, Fewer, Shorter: Gas Demand Peaks in the Age of Battery-Backed Renewables

Harry Mateer, BCI, US | Grace Ogden, BCI, US | Srinjoy Banerjee, CFA, BCI, US | Japheth (Jef) Otieno, BCI, US | Ryan Preclaw, BCI, US | William Thompson, BCI, US | Milan Mrazek, Barclays, UK | Vincenzo Pota, Barclays, UK | Theresa Chen, CFA, BCI, US | Eve Abraham, BCI, US | Joshua Park, BCI, US | Amanuel Seife, BCI, US | Nicholas Campanella, BCI, US

Excerpted from Gas Hero to Net Zero: Higher, Fewer, Shorter: Gas Demand Peaks in the Age of Battery-Backed Renewables, published on April 22, 2024

The pipeline industry's response to renewables has been to point out that the intermittency of solar/wind will require more gas as a backstop for power gen. Our California power model supports this, but only to a point: gas-fired demand peaks should be higher but fewer, and overall gas needs for power could be much lower.

Question we are addressing: Will the addition of renewables result in intermittency-driven demand spikes for gas-fired generation, or can batteries flatten out the peaks? Gas pipelines have suggested that even if overall demand fades for gas used in base-load generation, spikes in demand will maintain contracted capacity as customers solve for their maximum needs.

Why it matters: Power generation represents the single-largest domestic end-market for natural gas. The natural gas pipeline industry views electric utilities and power producers as some of their highest-quality customers, and these "demand-pull" pipelines often garner the highest valuation multiples and the most leverage capacity (facilitating the issuance of longdated securities). This reflects sticky end-user demand and multi-year, take-or-pay contracts.

What we did: Using our Power System Planning Model, we built out California's generation capacity (including battery adoption) based on current system operator expansion plans (under the state's SB100 framework), along with making assumptions about demand growth. We simulated what gas demand for power generation would look like across California's grid throughout the year, accounting for daily and seasonal changes in demand. Our model is based on the CAISO system, which captures roughly 80% of California's total generation capacity. The Appendix lays out some key assumptions and modeling decisions.

Takeaways: The pipeline industry's argument is right, but only in part: We estimate that by 2035, some gas demand peaks for power generation will be higher than our 2020 baseline. However, they also will be infrequent and short. Overall, we estimate that gas consumption for power generation will still be needed, but could be materially lower.

Implications: We think that utilities, their regulators, and their customers in certain regions could find it harder to rationalize paying (the same rate) for fully contracted gas pipeline capacity that is needed for perhaps a handful of days (or hours) per year. Merchant generators in the state could experience declining spark spreads. While we do not believe natural gas procurement strategies by utilities or power generators have changed to-date, we expect this to evolve if natural gas's share declines in the generation mix.

More broadly, California has the most accelerated and far-reaching renewables policy in the US, and although it ofers a useful test case for what could emerge in other states over a more extended time, each region has diferent energy policy and power demand trajectory dynamics. For investors in gas assets, changes in the power generation market are worth considering.

## What Mid-East tensions mean for Israel and global markets

Zalina Alborova, Barclays, UK | Brahim Razgallah, Barclays, UK | Amarpreet Singh, BCI, US | Themistoklis Fiotakis, Barclays, UK | Marek Raczko, Barclays, UK | Anshul Pradhan, BCI, US | Max Kitson, Barclays, UK | Andreas Kolbe, Barclays, UK | Fabian Herold, Barclays, UK | Valentina Stoykova, Barclays, UK | Stella Cridge, Barclays, UK | Tavy Rosner, Barclays, UK | Chris Reimer, Barclays, UK | Venu Krishna, CFA, BCI, US | Stefano Pascale, BCI, US | Matthew Joyce, CFA, BBI, Paris

Excerpted from Cross Asset Views: What Mid-East tensions mean for Israel and global markets, published on April 24, 2024

The unprecedented direct military confrontation between Iran and Israel has raised fears of broader escalation. We take stock of the implications for Israel's macro outlook, as well as for FX, rates, credit and equities, both in the region and beyond.

Geopolitical tensions have taken a new and dangerous turn. Contained conflict remains our base case. But the events of early April represent an unprecedented regional escalation. In particular, the role of several Arab neighbours in supporting Israel’s air defence during the Iranian attacks points to a greater chance of regional rifts. For now, the ongoing Hamas conflict and prospects of a ground incursion in Rafah should keep risks skewed to the upside. Within Israel, the anti-government mood has gained momentum and pressure on PM Netanyahu has intensified, with higher risks of early elections (a market positive scenario) than at the beginning of the year.

Fundamentals remain the key driver for oil.There are no signs that elevated geopolitical tensions are feeding pressures in oil markets, even though the overall trends since October last year are concerning. The relative calm is likely a function of robust fundamentals (low inventory cover) and elevated spare capacity. Risks to our \$90/b fair value estimate for Brent this year are skewed higher.

Israel Macro: The Israeli economy contracted by 21% q/q saar in Q4 23, bringing 2023 annual growth to 1.8% y/y (from 6.5% in 2022), and we expect 1.6% y/y growth in 2024. Since the end of the last year, economic activity has been recovering, while dynamics in construction, manufacturing, the hotel industry and services remain weak due to supply-side restrictions. Any wider escalation would pose downside risks for growth, with a potential acceleration of inflation (our base case is for it to remain within a 1-3% target range), an above-plan (ie, 6.6% of GDP) widening of the budget deficit  and a debt to GDP ratio above the 68.5% that we currently project for 2024, which could raise further concerns and action from the rating agencies. That said, apart from increased costs, we do not expect any dificulties with new issuance, as recent sales of domestic and external bonds saw high demand. We expect the BoI to continue cutting rates once geopolitical risks decline (three 25bp cuts in H2 24), and to be active (ie, intervening in the market, expanding support measures) in the scenario of escalation.

FX/local rates: The US dollar has benefited from the rise in tensions through various channels, and should maintain a risk premium in its favour in the medium run. This comes on top of the overall backdrop of exceptionalist policies that have underpinned a steady dollar rise over the past decade. ILS and Israeli rates already contain significant premia for geopolitical risks. We prefer to fade the premium embedded in the currency first, as EURILS has recently been more resilient than back-end rates. We maintain our short EURILS recommendation.

Rates: We expect a contained conflict to be largely ignored by US rates markets, or at most to result in very short-term price action which is quickly reversed. Meanwhile, we see a wider and more persistent escalation resulting in a decline in long-term yields, though the very front end would need to price in the possibility of higher policy rates for longer. Hence, we believe the yield curve is likely to flatten. In Europe, knee-jerk flight-to-quality dynamics could dominate in the short term, but the longer-term impact of escalating geopolitical tensions could be more ambiguous if higher oil prices drive renewed inflation worries.

EM credit: The events since October 2023 have led to a re-rating of Israel sovereign and corporate credit. Spreads in the broader Middle East region have also experienced some pressure. We see the risks for sovereign spreads in the GCC as broadly balanced, but recommend adding some risk hedges against a broader conflict, for example via a CDX EM versus iTraxx Asia IG decompression trade. The recent widening in Israeli corporate spreads has left the sector trading at the wides relative to other sectors within EEMEA. Our MW rating on the Israel Corporates sector reflects resilient fundamentals in banks and corporates; we recommend adding bonds that have underperformed recently.

Equities: If the Israel-Iran conflict remains contained (which is our base case), geopolitical risks remain less of a concern for US equities than the trajectory of inflation and corporate earnings. The drawdown thus far has been very mild. For European equities, we see bigger drivers in the form of reasonable earnings, rate-cut prospects and a brightening of the economic mood. For Israeli equities, fundamentals remain solid and we doubt that a contained conflict with Iran would change the paradigm. We see significant upside in real estate and flag downside risks in energy.

## Earth Observation: A sky full of data

Katherine Ogundiya, Barclays, UK | Hiral Patel, Barclays, UK | Milene Kerner, BBI, Paris | Ross Sandler, BCI, US | Tom O'Malley, BCI, US | Luke Sergott, BCI, US | Raimo Lenschow, CFA, BCI, US

Excerpted from Sustainable & Thematic Investing: Earth Observation: A sky full of data, published on April 22, 2024

An era of predictive earth observation data is on the horizon, giving companies unprecedented insights into global supply chains. As adoption levels continue to rise, we highlight opportunities for >30 public/private companies ofering solutions across five core areas.

Earth observation is emerging as a key vertical within the space economy industry, yet remains under the radar. Government and defence clients comprise 70-75% of the market, however we see this shifting. The combination of falling prices of satellite launches, the emergence of new data sets and advances in machine learning and AI, has created optimal conditions for the commercial earth observation market to grow. We are at the early stages of commercial adoption, yet the data is already being successfully used by multiple sectors (e.g. energy, finance, insurance, agriculture). In particular, we see this data enhancing global supply chains with services such as asset monitoring, intelligence gathering, productivity analysis and more. Ongoing technological advancements including new sensors (e.g. hyperspectral imagers and sensors) will bring unprecedented high-resolution datasets to the market, and software improvements are reducing latency and enabling the provision of predictive insights. At the same time, improving unit economics will help take earth observation from niche to mainstream.

Given multiple drivers supporting the earth observation market, we think it is an opportune time for investors to begin to better understand the value chain, given the multi-sector impact it will have once additional scale is achieved. In the Investor Guidebook our Earth Observation Company Landscape maps companies against the five core components of the value chain: 1) Physical infrastructure; 2) Digital infrastructure; 3) Data; 4) Distribution; and 5) Analytics. We highlight the pure-play earth observation companies (both public and private), which typically operate across all five components of the value chain and also companies providing individual solutions.

The listed companies ofering earth observation services fall within Aerospace & Defence, Internet, Software, Semiconductors, Life Sciences & Diagnostics and Satellite Services. When assessing thematic purity, we consider company exposure both from a revenue and impact perspective. Given the burgeoning nature of the theme, we expect to see additional industry consolidation and M&A as the market matures. We therefore provide a landscape of key private companies ofering diferentiated earth observation data and insights. To aid company engagement we also provide SDG mapping and questions for management.

Q1 earnings are beating estimates, helped by margins. Market fatigue, but don't blame earnings. ## Credit Strategy

## Americas

## Staring down stagflation

Bradley Rogof, BCI, US | Dominique Toublan, BCI, US

Excerpted from US Credit Alpha,  published on April 26, 2024

This week's stagflationary GDP print did little to improve macro sentiment. Equities have tumbled this month, and vol has yet to settle. Credit, however, has remained resilient amid constructive technicals.

Macro is back in the driver's seat. Thursday's weaker-than-expected consumption data caused rates to continue their month-to-date sell-of. 10y USTs rose 8bp on the week (50bp on the month), after trading with a firmer tone on Monday/Tuesday. While the GDP miss was driven by consumer spending and inventories, final sales to domestic purchasers rose a solid 2.8%, indicating strong demand conditions (see Q1 2024 GDP: Reasonably strong under the hood). Still, the headline numbers stirred concerns about stagflation, which tends to be a negative environment for credit performance.

Inflationary pressures have yet to subside. Core PCE prices rose 3.7% q/q saar in 1Q, roughly 0.3pp higher than expectations. Along with other strong price data released this month, this caused a wider probability distribution for the trajectory of the fed funds rate. One month ago, there was a 0.6% chance of zero rate cuts in 2024, and that has since risen to over 12%. There is also now a more balanced view between one and two cuts, with markets pricing in a 34% chance of either outcome by year-end, per the CME FedWatch tool. We maintain our baseline call that the FOMC will lower rates 25bp this year, at the earliest in September; however, slightly stronger prints could easily push out the timing to December.

Market volatility remains elevated. The MOVE index remains high at 110pts, while the VIX and CVIX (FX vol) are hovering near their highest levels in months. As discussed in this week's Focus, equity vol typically matters more than rate vol for credit: the beta of credit to VIX is more than 2x its beta to MOVE over the past 25 years. However, high yield and leveraged loans tend to be much more sensitive to rates vol than investment grade. Our Rates Strategy team suggests that rate volatility could stay structurally higher due to the fiscal picture (see Complacent: US fiscal issues remain an underpriced long-term volatility risk).

Credit has performed well in April, particularly versus equities. Despite some widening last week, spreads had tightened 2bp/16bp in IG/HY through Wednesday's close, and valuations remain fairly strong at 90bp and 307bp, respectively. Excess returns in IG and HY cash are now close to flat on the month, while the SPX/Nasdaq/Russell were down 3.7%/4.7%/5.8% as of Thursday's close. With over 43% of the S&P 500 having reported through Thursday (and another 35% next week), sales and EPS have risen 4.5% and 6.1% q/q, beating consensus estimates thus far.

Credit/equity correlations are changing in IG and HY. In this week's investment grade and high yield sections, we find that IG correlations to equities have fallen recently, consistent with previous tight-spread environments. In high yield, credit is responding to equities again after a sustained period of low correlation, but large moves in bond prices are taking place outside of earnings more frequently.

Performance across credit has not been uniform. In IG, the belly of the curve has outperformed this month, with the 7-10y bucket posting excess returns of 22bp, versus 0bp for the 1-5y tenor and 12bp for 25y+ bonds. ## Europe

Technicals vs macro volatility

Søren Willemann, Barclays, UK

Excerpted from European Credit Alpha, published on April 26, 2024

While cash technicals look set to remain strong, US macro headlines continue to drive volatility and concerns around higher-for-longer rates. This supports our preference for IG over HY; we also prefer loans to HY.

After the bout of macro and geopolitical volatility to start April, news flow—and markets—were quieter, at least to start the week. European economic data remain supportive, with the improved composite PMI (moving further into expansionary territory) supporting our economists' view that EA activity should continue to recover in Q2. After a modest increase in Q1 (+0.1% q/q), we expect growth to pick up in Q2 (+0.2% q/q), as increasing real incomes are set to support demand.

We think of the market as a tug of war between strong technicals and lingering macro and geopolitical uncertainty. Technicals remain strong, as supply is manageable (and low currently during earnings season), and high all-in yields keep attracting demand.

On the opposing side is uncertainty in the Middle East, albeit this quietened down over the past week, and continued macro volatility out of the US. Fittingly, US economic data out Thursday had an unpleasant stagflationary feel, with a meaningfully weaker Q1 q/q GDP print and core PCE printing above the consensus. Although the data can arguably be interpreted in a less stagflationary way, the market reaction was clear: yields were up and risk assets—both equity and credit—were selling of.

For now it is dificult to imagine that European macro data could drive this kind of market reaction, meaning that the US continues to be the driver of macro volatility. Although Europe cannot hide (much) from this, with the 20-day running correlation of daily 10yr UST-Bund changes being elevated (Figure 24), the current setup does mean that European credit markets, particularly IG, appear less risky. Famous last words?

Both lingering tail risk from the Middle East and the US macro volatility keep us wary of European HY on a relative basis; continued high rates alone is a headwind to chipping away at the refi wall. It is true that HY cash has underperformed IG lately, but if we look, for example, at the "middle 55" names (by spread level) of Xover vs Main non-financials, when we strip out the very wide names, HY CDS is efectively in line with IG CDS (Figure 25).

Fittingly, as we discuss in the HY section, we prefer B-rated loans to bonds, as valuations, even after adjusting for the downward-sloping yield curve and likely earlier take-outs in HY, are better in loans. Should rate cuts be priced even further out, loans (at least their valuations) would stand to benefit relative to bonds. Further, the loan market has done a better job at addressing the maturity wall than HY, and the ownership base of loans—predominantly CLOs—arguably reduces the uncertainty of the demand picture.

As a result of all this, we prefer cash longs to CDS (and we like hedging IG cash with Main options), and prefer IG (and loans) to HY.

## Economic Outlook

## Higher for longer...for sure

Christian Keller, Barclays, UK | Mark Cus Babic, Barclays, UK | Akash Utsav, Barclays, UK

After a week of volatile data, the bottom line is still strong US growth and stubborn inflation, which should make for a hawkish Fed next week. ## Hawkish data means hawkish Fed

Stickier inflation and still resilient growth...

After a succession of upside surprises in data releases this week, including payrolls, CPI, and retail sales, the baton passed to core PCE. Although PCE inflation is typically fairly predictable once its constituent CPI and PPI indices become available, the Q1 figure released on Thursday took markets by surprise, rising to 3.7% quarter-over-quarter from 2.0% in Q4, surpassing the consensus forecast of 3.4%. Ultimately, the data proved less alarming than at first glance, as the majority of the upward revision can be attributed to an adjustment to the January print. Nevertheless, March's figures also exceeded our projections, registering 0.32% m/m versus the anticipated 0.27%, and the revised January data will still push up the 6- and 12-month measures. In all, though the data turned out to be less discouraging than feared, their main message remains firmer inflationary pressures, particularly in non-housing services.

In contrast, Q1 GDP surprised to the downside, slowing to 1.6% q/q saar from 3.4% q/q saar in Q4; however, the details point to still-resilient activity. Private domestic final demand remained firm, easing only a touch, as softer consumption was ofset by better-than-expected investment. Instead, a large part of this miss relative to our forecasts was driven by the volatile expenditure components, primarily inventories.

Moreover, monthly real PCE released later in the week shows that the downside surprise in consumption came from revisions to January, while February was adjusted higher and March spending beat our expectations, implying a still-strong 2.0% q/q saar carryover for real PCE in Q2. Thus, we make no changes to our sequential growth outlook, expecting headline GDP to reaccelerate to 2.5% q/q saar in Q2, though the Q1 surprise mechanically lowers our 2024 annual average growth estimate to 2.6% from 2.8%. Next week, we expect services ISM to underscore resilient activity, rising to 52.3 from 51.4 in March.

We also expect next week's slew of data to reinforce continued strength in the labour market. We forecast nonfarm payrolls increased 250k, slowing from March, but maintaining a firm 3mma pace of c.275k/m. At the same time, average hourly earnings should post another 0.3% m/m (4.0% y/y) gain, while the unemployment rate could remain at 3.8%. Moreover, we forecast another elevated print for the employment cost index, accelerating to 1.0% q/q from 0.9% q/q, and only marginal easing in JOLTS job openings.

## ...imply a hawkish hold at next week's FOMC meeting...

Except for payrolls, the FOMC will have all these data at hand when it meets next week. We think the FOMC communications will be broadly consistent with recent speeches, indicating that the data have not increased the Committee's confidence that inflation is sustainably moving toward 2% and that it will likely hold the federal funds target range at the current level for longer than implied by the median dot in the March SEP. That said, this message is more likely to come across in Powell's press conference than the statement, which we expect to have only a few changes in its characterisation of the recent data, such as giving a nod to the firming in inflation prints, but leave the policy guidance unchanged.

In conveying this message, Powell will face a tightrope between tempering rate cut expectations while avoiding a sharp market reaction, which would extend the meaningful tightening in financial conditions that has occurred since March. For instance, we think the Chair will reiterate his comments from the previous meeting that "it will likely be appropriate to [cut] at some point this year"<sup>2</sup>  and that "the policy is likely at its peak for this tightening cycle". ## BoJ: The dog that did not bark

## BoJ refrained from adjusting policy ...

This week's much anticipated BoJ meeting concluded without significant changes to policy. The BoJ not only held rates steady, but also only simplified the language regarding its JGB purchases, rather than signaling a reduction, as some market participants had anticipated, thereby suggesting the oficial start of systematic QT remains a distant prospect.

That said, the BoJ laid the groundwork for policy adjustments. It revised its inflation forecasts upward, now projecting that core inflation measures will be broadly consistent with its 2% target for five consecutive years. In addition, Governor Ueda expressed growing confidence in reaching this inflation target and indicated that, should these projections materialise, policy rates would be gradually increased toward neutral levels. Given Japan's economic history, with no consecutive hikes in two to three decades, any such policy shifts are likely to be approached cautiously. Nevertheless, Ueda's comments suggest a readiness for multiple rate increases, and we expect the next hike in July. Meanwhile, on QT, this week's statement adjustments grant the BoJ leeway for small-scale, stealth tapering.

## ...inviting more pressure on the JPY and the potential for intervention

The main threat to whatever the BoJ's plans are, however, is now the currency. Against disappointed expectations for a more hawkish BoJ and with incoming strong US data further pushing out expectations for any easing from the Fed, the depreciation pressure on JPY extended, with it breaching the 157 mark (against the USD) as we go to press.

Such JPY weakness is reminiscent of September 2022, when the BoJ started three FX interventions in two months. It also comes against the MoF's recent escalation in its tone of verbal intervention and a joint statement at the trilateral meeting with the US and South Korea voicing concerns about JPY and KRW weakness. Hence, even if their efect would likely be only temporary, interventions or market concerns thereof could add to the JPY's volatility in the near term as Japan enters the Golden Week holidays next week. Ultimately, more substantial support for the JPY would have to come from higher Japanese rates, and that decision remains with the BoJ.

## EM central banks feel the pinch

Strong US activity and a more hawkish Fed also continue to put upward pressure on the dollar, and EM central banks have started to react. FX pressure has led Bank Indonesia to surprise

markets this week and hike 25bp. In our base case, this was a one-of move; however, we now no longer see any cuts by BI this year and think risks tilt toward additional tightening.

Most other EM central banks are also reacting to the Fed, in varied ways. Outside of Indonesia, we do not think other EM Asia central banks will follow with hikes of their own; however, we have pushed back or removed altogether cuts from our forecast profiles across most major central banks. Similarly, in CE4, the Fed (in addition to the ECB) is critical for the central banks' reaction function, and the NBH and CNB have recently turned more cautious in their easing cycles. In the same vein, the Fed has implications for LatAm central banks; for instance, we now no longer see room for Banxico to cut in May and forecast easing at every other meeting; the central banks of Peru and Chile are also sensitive to the Fed.

## Brightening outlook in Europe

The global manufacturing recovery remains feeble ...

One of the key shifts in Q1 was the manufacturing cycle finally appearing to improve after nearly three years of subdued activity. This week's flash manufacturing PMIs, however, failed to reinforce this. They declined across the US, euro area, and UK, and weakness was seen across the most components, including in the forward-looking new orders. Despite this, we hesitate to call a halt in the industrial sector rebound, as the decline in April DM PMIs falls within typical volatility and does not significantly reverse earlier gains.

Instead, upcoming releases will be crucial in determining the persistence and strength of the manufacturing trend. As soon as next week, we will be watching China's NBS manufacturing PMIs, which might decelerate slightly to 50.5 in April from 50.8 in March; the US manufacturing ISM, which should hold steady at 50.3; global manufacturing PMIs; and Korean trade data, which we expect to pick up.

## ... but Europe is slowly  gaining strength

Despite the disappointing manufacturing prints, incoming data continue to confirm a gradual recovery in Europe's activity, while disinflation remains on track. This week, April composite PMIs beat expectations, rising to 51.4 from 50.4, led by strength in services that ofset softer manufacturing. In addition, they point to a closing gap between the core economies and the periphery, mostly through Germany and France catching up, but also, though to a lesser extent, through slightly softer output in the rest of the euro area.

Next week, we expect the stronger soft data in recent months to translate to GDP growth returning to positive territory, expanding 0.1% q/q in Q1, led by consumption as real incomes rebound. Thereafter, we forecast further gradual acceleration in activity, thus ending the current 18-month period of stagnation. In addition to GDP, next week also brings the March unemployment rate.

At the same time, we also anticipate further positive news on core inflation, which could ease 0.3pp, to 2.6% y/y, in next week's April print, driven by a meaningful deceleration in services due to weaker Easter-related components and soft momentum elsewhere. That said, the headline print should remain steady at 2.4% y/y as weakness in core is ofset by stronger energy, due to higher oil prices and policy one-ofs.

Still, the fiscal outlook remains challenging. At the time of writing, we anticipate the French sovereign credit rating reviews by Moody's and Fitch, with the risk that Moody's downgrades the outlook to negative from stable.

## Single Stock

## Addus Homecare Corp. (ADUS; UW/Neu)

## CMS Finalizes 20% Gross Profit Cap, Downgrade to UW

We are downgrading ADUS to UW from EW after CMS finalized a materially negative regulation that was proposed last year and fell short of stakeholders' expectations for a more positive final ruling. Specifically, the rule requires at least 80% of HCBS Medicaid payments in a state for home and personal care services be spent on compensation for direct care workers. In efect, this would put a 20% gross profit cap on approximately 60% of ADUS’s revenue in a business where it currently earns a gross margin closer to 28%. While the rule is not scheduled to go into efect until 2030, if implemented today, we estimate the regulation would result in a 35% decrease (-\$48 mn) to 2025 EBITDA before potential ofsets. This results in an unfavorable riskreward that skews 1.4-to-1.0 to the downside. Further, we believe the long implementation period is likely to weigh on the company's valuation and complicate industry consolidation.

## Single Stock

## Li Auto Inc (LI; EW/Pos)

## Crack in the Moat; Downgrade to EW

The disappointing initial sales of Mega since the launch in early March was the first operational misstep in an otherwise impressive execution track record for LI management. As a result of weaker sales of Mega, LI management lowered 1Q delivery guidance around the end of March. Industry sales data show that sales of L series have been soft in recent weeks as well. Earlier this week, LI reduced prices across its entire L series of SUVs and Mega by RMB 18,000-30,000 (\~4-6% cut) for the first time ever in the company’s history. The fundamental issue here is competition, the intensifying competition, especially from Huawei (M5, M7, M9) and BYD’s higher-end subbrand Denza, has created a crack in LI’s moat surrounding its EREV luxury family-oriented SUV market. LI’s recent price cuts suggest capitulation to the intensifying competitive pressure. Investors have also become increasingly concerned about likely success of LI’s upcoming BEV launch later this year. We consider it prudent at this point to step to the sidelines while closely monitoring how the competitive landscape shakes out in the coming months. We downgrade LI from OW to EW, with a new price target of \$25.
