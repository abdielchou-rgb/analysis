# US Economic Perspectives May CPI Preview: Looking good

## Headline CPI: UBS US Econ 0.08%, UBS Nowcast 0.37%, consensus 0.15%

Next Wednesday's (June 12) CPI release is likely to show a notable slowing in the headline CPI compared to the recent months: We project an 8bps increase for May (seasonally-adjusted) following increases ranging from 31bps to 44bps in the first four months of this year. A 3% decline in gasoline prices will likely be the main driver of the weakness. Similar weakness was seen in energy prices in May last year, and as a result twelve-month inflation is expected to remain at 3.4%. Our 8bps headline CPI projection is below the current Bloomberg consensus average forecast and well below the projection from our Nowcasting models. The projected not-seasonally-adjusted core CPI level, at 314.379, is about 2 basis points above recent swaps fixings.

## Core CPI: UBS US Econ 0.24%, UBS Nowcast 0.38%, consensus 0.29%

The 24bps seasonally-adjusted core CPI increase that we project for May would be the smallest increase since October, and a second month of slowing following increases of 39bps, 36bps, and 36bps in first three months of the year. However, we feel that this comparison overstates the true slowing in May, and more slowing actually occurred in April: the April change was pushed up by around 8bps more than likely appropriate from revisions to seasonal factors since pre-pandemic and the May change will likely be pushed down by those same overly-sensitive seasonal factors (by around 4bp more than likely appropriate). (See our US Inflation Monthly for more on the seasonal factor impact.)

## Focus on NSA change compared to last year

As a result of the seasonal adjustment problems, we suggest paying less attention to the monthly seasonally-adjusted data and instead focus on comparing the May notseasonally-adjusted (NSA) core CPI increase to the increase in May of last year (or, equivalently, the evolution of 12-month inflation). We project the NSA core CPI increase in May will be roughly halfway between last year's pace and the upper end of the range in May for the pre-pandemic years of 2016 to 2019. (Figure 9) Such a reading would continue the progress seen in 17 of the past 19 months and result in 12-month core CPI inflation sliding from 3.61% to about 3.53%. However, it would also suggest that there is still a way to go before inflation is back to its pre-pandemic pace.

## OER likely to slow; apparel too, but used car prices rise

Across the components the slowing from April's 29bp increase to May's 24bp is largely from apparel and owners' equivalent rent (OER) — the two categories where we see the largest swing in the seasonal factor impact. In our projection these two categories combined contribute 7 bps less to the core CPI increase in May than they did in April. We expect that in the May CPI, OER will see its first sub-40bps monthly increase in roughly 2½ years. In contrast to the downward push from apparel and OER, we expect used car prices to increase modestly after falling solidly the past two months. The swing in used car prices should add close to 4bp more to the monthly core CPI change in May than in April.

## Vehicle insurance and food away from home also worth watching

Two other key components to watch in the May CPI release will be motor vehicle insurance (or the broader non-airfare transportation services category in our breakdown) and food away from home. Vehicle insurance has been one of the main drivers of elevated core CPI inflation: Over the past year the CPI for vehicle insurance has risen 23% and contributed 70 bps to core CPI inflation. CPI motor vehicle insurance is not used in PCE prices, and the analogous PCE price series has risen 7½% over the past year — a still elevated pace, but roughly ⅓ of the CPI vehicle insurance increase.

Meanwhile, the CPI for food away from home is not part of core CPI, but it is a contributor to the elevated readings for core PCE services excluding housing rents (what some have dubbed "supercore PCE prices"). The CPI for food away from home is also one of the more useful components when constructing a trend inflation measure. Those food prices have slowed notably over the past year but remain elevated and have shown an uptick in the past couple of months. Slowing in both prices for motor vehicle insurance and food away from home would be a positive sign for cooling inflation. ## Uncertainty still wide, slight downward skew

We feel the range of uncertainty remains elevated, though somewhat less than in recent months and we see a small downward skew to the risks. We would not be surprised to see a core CPI increase anywhere from 18bp to 29bp. Upside risks include OER continuing to increase in the 42 bps to 44 bps range it has been in for the past few months and motor vehicle insurance continuing to increase at or above 1½% per month. (We do not explicitly forecast motor vehicle insurance, but implicitly our nonairfare transportation services projection is consistent with an increase of motor vehicle insurance of around 1%.) Downside risks include a larger slowing in OER and a step down across core categories consistent with the full amount of the swing in the estimated May seasonal factor impact relative to April.

## Another moderate print projected for June

We currently project the headline CPI to increase around 4bps in June with early month gasoline prices suggesting another solid decline in the CPI motor fuels. Meanwhile the core CPI increase is projected at 18bps in June as seasonal factors once again push down the monthly change, the OER increase slows (temporarily) further, and used car prices resume their decline (as suggested by the June JD Power Used Car Guide). 12-month headline CPI inflation is projected to slip to 3.2% with core CPI inflation remaining at 3.5%. Looking further out, we project core CPI inflation will decline over the year amid the pass-through of market rents into CPI rents, rising vehicle inventories, and slowing wage growth all putting downward pressure on the monthly price increases.
