# Australia's stalling disinflation: clues from Canada...

When compared with Canada, the recent upswing in Australian inflation appears driven by factors immune to monetary policy

Australia's disinflation trend has seen an unexpected reversal in recent months, just as Canadian inflation has continued to slow. Market pricing has responded accordingly: \~10pbs of hikes are now priced in for the RBA by August. The BoC is fully priced to cut 25bps in July.

In that context, Canada serves as a helpful benchmark for illuminating Australia's inflation reversal. A closer look shows that the divergence between the two countries reflects differences across just three CPI components: (1) the cost of shelter for homeowners; (2) alcohol & tobacco; and (3) insurance premiums.

When the divergence in inflation trends is so recent, easy to pinpoint, and driven by factors mostly immune to monetary policy, we think there is a readthrough for the RBA. A policy response is not required. At least not yet.

## Details

A notable divergence has opened up in the underlying inflation trends between Australia and Canada in recent months. The disinflation in Australia's core CPI since early 2023 has come to an abrupt halt - after falling by 1.5ppts in year-ended terms from October 2023 to January 2024, Australia's trimmed mean inflation indicator has risen by ¼ppt over the two months to March. On the other hand, Canada's core inflation has continued to slow, falling by ½ppt between December 2023 and March 2024, Figure 1.

## 2. Diverging inflation trends

n One of the major points of difference in recent inflation trends in Australia and Canada relates to the inflation on the measure of 'homeowners shelter'. As Figure 9 shows, while inflation in this category has fallen from its peaks in both countries, it has stabilized at around +5%yoy in Australia, but has actually turned negative in Canada.

Figure 9: Homeowners shelter cost is the biggest expenditure class component of the CPI, and has been higher and stickier in Australia

Costof homeownership inflation\*

Source : Deutsche Bank, ABS, Haver

This category warrants a closer look.

Cost of shelter for homeowners is the single-largest expenditure class component of both the Canadian and Australian CPIs (5.5% of the CPI by weight in Canada's CPI, 8.07% in Australia's). While homeowners replacement cost is the single-largest category in Canada's CPI, the weight is somewhat smaller than Australia's equivalent because the Canadian CPI also includes a separate expenditure class covering mortgage interest cost (CPI weight of 3.8%). Mortgage interest costs are not included at all in Australia's CPI. There is an obvious practical reason for this: given Australia's variable rate mortgage market, rate hikes boost mortgage payments, directly adding to measured inflation.

Unlike food or utilities, however, at any given point in time the explicit cost of homeownership, at least as measured in the Canadian and Australian CPI's, is only realized by a relatively small number of households. While almost all households will, for example, eat food and consume utilities on a daily basis, the vast majority of households don't move into a brand new house every day, every month, or even every year. Many don't move for decades. Think of it this way: around 175,000 new dwellings were completed in Australia in 2023. Assuming one household paid for, and moved into, each one of those new dwellings, those households still represent less than 2% of the 9.3mn households in Australia. In any given month, then, less than 0.2% of Australian households are explicitly 'paying' the home ownership 'costs' that are included in the Australian CPI.

Moreover, while these home ownership costs are a very significant expense for those \~0.2% of households, is this really the best way to actually measure these costs? As noted above, mortgage interest payments are excluded from Australia's CPI, despite being a very significant 'consumer' item for mortgaged households. More generally, homeowner's shelter is widely recognized as the most difficult consumption 'price' to measure in any CPI basket.

In fact, Australia and Canada use completely different measurement methodologies. Australia uses a cost of construction basis to measure the cost of home ownership (essentially a producer price instead of a consumer price), while Canada uses a replacement cost method, which means the Canadian measure is linked to the house price cycle.

We are not suggesting here that one is necessarily better than the other. Indeed, with Australian house prices rising while Canada's are falling, adopting Canada's methodology would have made Australia's core inflation over the past year even higher. But the broader point we would make here is that measurement challenges do raise a relevant question about whether an RBA policy response is required.

## Bottom line

The divergence in inflation trends between Australia and Canada has been recent, it appears easy to pinpoint, and is driven by factors mostly immune to monetary policy. As such, we think a policy response by the RBA is not required. At least not yet.

The case for patience is most compelling around the rise in homeowner's shelter inflation. This is the single-largest expenditure class in both Australia's and Canada's CPI. But measuring its price is also the most methodologically ambiguous for any good or service in the CPI index, and the measurement approach used in Australia is directly relevant for less than 0.2% of Australian households in any given month. A reactionary rate hike to combat sticky inflation in this one component risks doing more harm than good.

That said, dissecting inflation trends in this manner also has limitations. Methodology issues aside, the RBA cannot ignore strength in homeowners shelter forever. The same applies to insurance, alcohol & tobacco, or any other CPI category. If strength in any segment of the CPI persists for long enough, it risks fuelling a rise in inflation expectations that will feed into the CPI more broadly. And given that the RBA's starting point is a headline inflation rate that is above target, the risks of that happening are far from trivial.

For now, the RBA can afford to wait for the overall disinflation trend to resume, but that disinflation trend needs to resume within months. As long as that happens, we remain comfortable with our base case for the next RBA move to be a 25bp rate cut in November.

If it doesn't, the RBA might need to hike again.
