# China Economic Perspectives Tracking Household Income and Consumption Sentiment

## New UBS Evidence Lab China consumer sentiment survey

UBS Evidence Lab conducted a Consumer Sentiment Survey (> Access Dataset) over 26 February to 6 March 2024, collecting answers from 1,000 respondents on questions about their income growth and expectations, consumption spending and future intentions. We compare the results from the latest survey to our July 2023 Consumer Survey and the February 2023 post-CNY pulse-check survey to track changes in consumer sentiment.

## Household income growth softened in the past three months

In the past three months, fewer respondents reported increased income than in the July survey, either from salary, financial investments or property rental. The reported weighted average salary increase was 3.3%, lower than the 4.4% in the July survey. That said, consumption spending was slightly less bad, with fewer respondents reporting cutting consumption than before. Respondents also showed a shift from risky investment to safer assets.

## Cautious income outlook, while saving intention cooled

Income growth and job promotion/stability were regarded as the top drivers of consumption. Respondents were cautious on their future income growth, in particular salary income growth, while expectations of income from rental and financial investment improved from actual outcome in the past three months, even though softer than the July survey and uncertainties remain. Despite cautious income outlook and limited government subsidies, the silver lining is that respondents’ saving intention has cooled compared with the surveys in February and July 2023, indicating further consumption normalization.

## Less bad consumption intention; staples preferred over discretionary

After a disappointing consumption recovery in 2023, there has been a marginal improvement in consumer sentiment in the latest survey. On a net basis, slightly more respondents expect to increase their consumption than in the July 2023 survey. Reflecting conservative sentiment, consumers continue to prefer spending on staples, such as F&B, daily necessities, and essential services, such as children’s education, sports and healthcare. Domestic travel and going-out related spending were also favoured. On the other hand, spending intention on outbound travel and large-ticket items remained weak. We expect consumption recovery to continue as property activities stabilize gradually in Q224, but the growth rate of real consumption will likely slow as favourable base effect fades.

One year after China’s economy reopened post Covid, labour market and household income growth remain under pressure and consumer spending has been more modest than expected. What factors could drive a consumption rebound? How do Chinese consumers view the outlook for income and consumption in 2024? Which sectors are consumers willing to spend more in and which sectors less? UBS Evidence Lab conducted a Consumer Sentiment Survey (> Access Dataset) involving 1,000 respondents after the CNY holiday between 26 February and 6 March 2024. We compare the results from the latest survey to our July 2023 Consumer Survey (> Access Dataset) and the post-CNY pulse-check survey conducted in February 2023 (> Access Dataset) to track changes in sentiment. Survey results and our UBS China Daily Activity Trackers help us gauge the underlying consumption momentum.

Household income growth softened from the July survey. 44% of the respondents reported an increase in salary in the past three months (35% on a net basis, share of increased salary minus decreased salary), with weighted average growth of 3.4%. This was weaker than our July 2023 consumer survey where 56% of the respondents (net 52%) reported an increased salary with an average growth rate of 4.4%, suggesting slower salary growth in the past quarter than mid-2023. Furthermore, echoing the weak financial market in the past few months, fewer respondents reported increased income from financial investment (gross 40%, net 24%) than July (gross 63%, net 55%). Those reporting increased income from letting property (gross 24%, net 19%) also dropped notably from July (gross 64%, net 56%), likely due to continued decline in property prices and weak rental prices. Weakness in income growth is also evident in official data – the 2022-23 two-year CAGR growth of nominal household disposable income edged down to 4.3% in Q4 from 4.8% in Q323, even though its YoY growth picked up on a low base effect.

Lower tier cities likely suffered more. Back in July 2023, both tier 1/2 and tier 3/4 had over 50% of respondents reporting salary increases on a net basis, while in the latest survey, tier 1/2 (40%) outperformed tier 3/4 (28%). Moreover, 68% of tier 3/4 reported income increase from letting property in July 2023, more than tier 1/2 (50%), but now only 16% in tier 3/4 still reported an increase, compared with 21% in tier 1/2. Lower tier cities were likely hurt more by property downturn and economic weakness than higher tier cities over the past few months, echoing a sharper housing price decline in lower tier than high tier cities in official statistics.

Consumption spending in the past three months was less bad than in the July 2023 survey, while saving cooled. Even though income growth weakened, the share of respondents that increased consumption in the past three months (gross 45%) was largely on par with the July 2023 survey, while the share of respondents cutting consumption dropped to 12% (from 22% in July 2023). As such, on a net basis, consumption spending was slightly less bad than the previous survey. Indeed, our estimate of seasonally adjusted real retail sales levels bottomed in July 2023 and has been on a modest upward trend since then, although its gap from the pre-Covid trend remained wide and only narrowed slightly. Meanwhile, the share of respondents that increased saving (narrowly defined, bank deposits and cash) still outweighed those that decreased saving (gross 35%, net 15%), but significantly less than July’s survey results (gross 60%, net 50%). Furthermore, reflecting a lower risk appetite and stronger desire for safer assets, fewer respondents increased investments (including stocks and housing) than in July 2023, while slightly more increased spending on insurance. On a macro level, household saving rate (broadly defined, ie, disposable income less consumption) saw a short-lived drop below the pre-Covid level (ie, small excess saving release) in Q323 partly thanks to strong summer travelling, but it bounced back again in last Q4.

Income and job security are the most important drivers of future consumption. 42% of the 1,000 respondents believe income growth is vital to boost consumption in 2024 and 40% picked job promotion/stability, far ahead of positive wealth effect (25%), tax reduction (18%), use of excess saving (18%), consumption vouchers (17%) and cash subsidies (14%). The ranking is very similar to the July 2023 and February 2023 surveys. One key change is that fewer respondents cited the use of excess saving as a key factor than the post-CNY survey a year ago (18%, vs 39% in Feb 2023), suggesting it may have become less important. Property price stops falling (wealth effect improvement) became a slightly more important factor than a year ago (9%, vs 4% in Feb 2023), though it is not crucial for consumption, at least for now. After two decades of housing price increases till 2021, most homeowners still have some paper gains even after the price decline since 2022.

What to expect from the above key drivers in 2024?

Income expectations remain largely cautious. 1) 47% of respondents expect salary income to increase in the next three months and 7% expect a decline, similar to the actual outcome in the past three months. This is much weaker than in the July 2023 survey where 62% expected an increase in the next year and 5% expected a decrease, indicating that households expect salary income growth to weaken a bit in the near term compared to 2023. 2) Meanwhile, expectations for property letting income to increase (gross 49%, net 46%) is much stronger than the actual outcome in the past three months, though softer than in July 2023 (gross 63%, net 54%). It seems households are expecting less bad movement in rental price and income, although housing prices have been declining in most Chinese cities. 3) Furthermore, partly owing to the stock market rally since early February, expectations of an increase from financial investment income (gross 69%, net 64%) was much better than the actual outcome in the past three months and largely stable compared with the July 2023 survey (gross 68%, net 61%). Overall, respondents remain cautious on future income growth, especially on salaries, which account for three-quarters of total disposable income (salary and the highly correlated business income), while income expectations from rental and the financial market have improved but remain uncertain. The results of the latest survey are slightly better than those of the February 2023 survey, where 43% expected total household income to increase in 2023 while 15% expected it to decrease.

Labour market remains stable with lingering pressures. The latest official unemployment rate edged up to 5.3% in February 2024, along its seasonal pattern and remaining below that in the same period of 2020-23. The “new” youth unemployment rate also picked up seasonally to 15.3% in February (which was a newly introduced series in December 2023 without a comparable history). That said, the employment index within PMI remained soft with a below-50 reading and on-the-ground evidence shows continued challenges for the labour market, pointing to lingering pressures.

 Saving intention cooled. Notably fewer respondents (gross 39%, net 28%) than the July 2023 survey (gross 60%, net 49%) expect to increase saving in the future (narrowly defined, bank deposits and cash), possibly indicating further normalisation of consumer behaviour, and interest in insurance products.

Limited external subsidies. The government announced a modest fiscal expansion at the NPC meeting and did not announce meaningful direct fiscal subsidies for household consumption. In particular, the government released an action plan to boost equipment upgrade and consumer goods old-for-new “tradein”, but without new fiscal stimulus from the central government. The plan urged local governments and corporates to contribute to the trade-in programme (see our comment). We think the size of direct fiscal subsidies for consumption (if any) will likely be limited this year.

Consumption sentiment is slightly less bad than in the July and February 2023 surveys. 38% of respondents expect to increase spending on consumption in the next three months, similar to the July 2023 survey, while fewer expect a decline. Therefore, on a net basis, the consumption outlook has improved at the margin (net 22% expect to increase consumption in February 2024 vs 14% in July 2023), after a disappointing year of consumption recovery in 2023. Meanwhile, intention to increase financial investment has softened modestly even though its return may improve, while desire for safer assets supports insurance spending. Compared with a year ago in the February 2023 survey, when 40% expected to increase total spending (including consumption and investment) on a gross basis and 16% on a net basis, overall consumption sentiment seems slightly better off.

Spending preference: staples still leading discretionary… The latest survey results reveal a similar spending preference to the one in July 2023. In light of cautious salary income expectation, staple consumption including food and beverages (net 28% plan to increase spending) and daily necessities (net 22%) continue to be the most favoured categories, even more so than in July 2023. Services, such as education for children, sports and healthcare, are considered essential, while more respondents plan to reduce spending on housekeeping services and helper services. Going-out related activities, including commuting, cosmetics and clothing have gained more traction, and consumers showed stronger intention to spend on domestic travelling (net 21% plan to increase spending) and dining out. Meanwhile, intention to travel overseas remains weak (net 2% plan to reduce spending), likely due to insufficient supply of international flights, higher travelling cost and lingering obstacles relating to visa applications.

…concerns over large-ticket items remains. 10% of respondents plan to reduce spending on property purchase, outweighing those expecting to increase such spending (5%), even softer than in July 2023 (net 1% plan to reduce spending). Sentiment on housing improvement and property renting is also quite weak. That said, slightly more respondents wish to spend more on home appliances than in July 2023, possibly on the back of expected policy measures (“trade-in”). Consumers also plan to spend less on handbags and jewellery.

We expect China's consumption recovery to continue, albeit with slower YoY growth. Our baseline expectation is for property sales and new starts to stabilise gradually in Q224, helping to prevent household confidence from sliding much further. Household income and labour market should continue to recover modestly. We estimate that China accumulated RMB 5.5trn of excess saving during the Covid years by end-2023; but with still cautious income expectation, we believe the release of excess saving is likely to be limited in 2024. If the equity market rally continues and/or housing prices stop falling, it should help to boost consumer sentiment through a positive wealth effect. Meanwhile, there’s likely to be limited consumption/income fiscal subsidies from the government, and income and inflation expectations remain soft. As such, we expect the post-Covid consumption recovery to continue, but the growth rate of real consumption (within GDP) will likely slow from 6.4% last year to around 5.5% as the favourable low base effect fades. Staple goods and consumer services may continue to outperform, following their robust performance in January and February.

Risk to our forecast is mainly biased to the downside from a prolonged property downturn. The latter weighs on consumer spending via a weaker labour market and income growth in sectors within the property supply chain, as well as through a negative wealth effect should property prices fall further. Consumer behaviour may take longer to normalise if expectations for deflation and economic weakness become more widespread. On the other hand, stronger-than-expected exports and meaningful direct fiscal stimulus (if any) could support the labour market, bolster consumer sentiment and lead to stronger consumption than we currently envisage.
