## Could the flattening momentum stop?

While steepeners may struggle until there is more clarity on the easing cycle, we think that current levels could offer attractive (tactical) steepening opportunities. Despite stop-outs caused by repricing in policy rate expectations since the beginning of the year, we acknowledge that positioning in steepeners may still be crowded. For the curve to steepen significantly, we would need to see signs of further deterioration in activity or a more rapid acceleration in the disinflation process, which, at the moment, does not seem like a very likely scenario. The latest Eurozone PMIs do not suggest any significant deterioration, while the labour market remains robust, limiting the need for the ECB to expedite its cutting cycle.

The front end of the EUR curve, such as EUR 2s10s, appears to be strongly correlated with policy rate expectations compared to curve segments anchored at the longer end. Hence, it will likely remain more sensitive to rate movements and more exposed to any further repricing. From a valuation perspective, EUR curves remain highly inverted historically. Recently, the front-end EUR curves have flattened more aggressively compared to other markets. With the repricing in the short end of EUR OIS likely running out of steam, we think that bull steepening is a likely scenario in the upcoming weeks. Steepeners anchored at the longer end of the curve, such as EUR 10s30s, are more attractive to us because they could offer better protection in case of further repricing in policy rate expectations and due to more favourable carry/roll-down.

## UK: Revising up our gilts gross supply to £258bn for FY24

With the Spring Budget likely to be expansionary, we expect the DMO to issue nearly £258bn of gilts in FY24, which is £10bn higher than our initial estimate of £248bn at the start of this year. This would mark the second-highest gross issuance since the pandemic, with £21bn of additional gilt supply compared to £237bn of gross gilt supply expected for FY23. Our forecast for the FY24 Gilt remit assumes a £7bn upward revision to CGNCR. The fiscal headroom that the OBR estimated after the Autumn Statement in November was nearly £13bn. However, recent reports suggest that it would be challenging for the Chancellor to utilize all available headroom for additional giveaways due to deteriorating forecasts, limiting the likely extra spending to £6.5bn.

Furthermore, the PSNB for the FY23 is currently £9bn lower than the OBR's projection in November. We assume that the gap between realised PSNB and the OBR's projection will remain at this level until the end of the FY. We expect NS&I demand to remain strong, and we have pencilled in £10bn for next year. Additionally, we assume an extra £5bn of bill issuance for debt financing. We expect the DMO to broadly maintain the issuance pattern going into the next FY, with gilt issuance remaining skewed towards the short and medium sectors. In particular, we expect the shorts gross supply to account for 38% (£98bn) of the gross issuance in FY24, while mediums, longs, and inflation-linked supply is expected to account for 30% (£77bn), 21% (£55bn) and 11% (£28bn), respectively.

## CHF: SARON front-end flatteners still appealing

Flatteners in the SARON March vs December curve have been our favourite expression for some time. This is due to the inflation outlook, dovish comments from SNB officials, and our economists' expectations for the first SNB cut in June. The market is still pricing in c.16bps of cuts into the March SNB meeting. Nevertheless, the stronger-thanexpected Q4 GDP data this week and repricing in policy rate expectations globally raise the bar for the SNB to cut its policy rate in March for the first time in this cycle. We anticipate that the March cut pricing could be pushed towards the second half of this year, leading to further flattening in this part of the curve.

We think that the SNB will use its balance sheet to address any unwarranted fluctuations in the CHF rather than depending on its policy rate. But with the real exchange rate back at levels seen in December when the SNB judged monetary conditions to be appropriate, the urgency for FX interventions or a policy rate cut (if deemed the appropriate tool by the SNB) has diminished. Furthermore, the divergence in the policy rate expectations between the ECB and the SNB will persist, maintaining a wide ESTR vs SARON spread.

The Q4 GDP exceeded expectations, while the release of the monthly SNB balance sheet data for January does not suggest any significant FX intervention from the SNB to counter the strength of the Swiss franc, observed at the beginning of the year. Following the better-than-expected GDP growth in Q4, our economists have raised the 2024 GDP growth forecast to 1.3% (+0.1pp) but have maintained the 2025 forecast at 1.5%. We anticipate that the SNB will start its cutting cycle in June, followed by two additional cuts in September and December, ultimately reaching a terminal rate of 1%.
