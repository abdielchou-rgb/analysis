# APAC Economic Perspectives

# ASEAN: Monthly CPI read-throughs—Assumptions and forecasts

## Everyone's back at or below 'target', except Singapore

In December, headline inflation cooled further in Indonesia (2.6% y/y vs. 2.9% in November, Cons: 2.7%), Philippines (3.9% y/y vs. 4.1% in November, Cons: 4.0%) and Thailand (-0.8% y/y vs. -0.4% in November, Cons: -0.3%). In Malaysia, headline inflation fell 0.3ppt to 1.5% (Cons: 1.7%) in November. In Singapore, November inflation fell 1.2ppt to 3.6% y/y in (Cons: 3.9%) (Figure 2). Inflation in Indonesia, Malaysia and the Philippines is within the central bank's target range or historical average. In Thailand, inflation is close to 2ppt below the BoT's 1-3% target range. In contrast, MAS Core Inflation at 3.2% y/y in November remains >1ppt above the implicit target of 2%, mainly due to the GST hike. Without the GST, we estimate core inflation would have been slightly above 2% in 2023.

## We expect ASEAN's inflation trajectory to surprise consensus on the downside

We are generally below consensus on inflation across ASEAN. Figure 1Key asumption frASEN-5 l c shows the key assumptions driving our below-consensus forecasts. For Singapore, we expect core inflation to come in at 2.8% in 2024 (consensus: 3.0%). The key assumption we make is a smaller pass-through of the GST increase relative to 2023, amidst less pent-up demand, cooling labour markets, and the ongoing restrictiveness of monetary policy. We also expect more monetary-fiscal coordination, and a budget that is much less inflationary than last year (see our 2023 budget report here). We note volatility in Singapore's core inflation on a monthly basis, with modest sequential price increases (0.1-0.2% m/m) in recent months, except for Oct (0.4% m/m). For Malaysia, notwithstanding our below-consensus inflation forecast, the uncertainty bounds are wide depending on the timing and extent of fuel subsidy changes. An additional 10% increase in fuel prices vis-à-vis our baseline could add 40bp to our inflation forecast, all else equal. In the Philippines, rice prices have continued to rise but gov't interventions have picked up, suggesting possible downside to prices in the coming months.

## We revise down our Thai CPI inflation forecast as a key assumption changes

A shift in our thinking with regards to the Thai government's approach to subsidies prompts us to revise down our inflation forecast for the second month in a row, to 1.2% (prev: 1.7%). Even then, risks are skewed to the downside, as our baseline includes the assumption that the digital wallet stimulus is introduced by May this year. Thailand is also an outlier when it comes to food inflation, which has continued to fall sharply despite the ongoing El Niño phenomenon, contrary to our earlier expectations. We maintain our inflation forecasts for all other economies in this report.

## More and earlier rate cuts by BoT

Cooling inflation and a Fed pivot by March opens the door for policy easing in Q2. Given very weak inflation in recent months (which we don't think will accelerate materially this year), as well as uncertainty regarding the digital wallet prompting BoT to revise its growth and inflation forecasts in November, we revise our policy rate call to a 25bp rate cut at the April meeting, followed by another one in August (50bp of cuts in total in 2024, ending the year at 2.0% p.a.). In our base case, BI cuts 150bp this year starting from June, and BSP cuts 100bp this year starting from August. Our forecasts for BI are conditional on a deeper Fed cutting cycle of more than 200bp this year. We expect BNM and MAS to remain on hold throughout 2024. We do not think BNM will react immediately to a level shift up in petrol prices, unless price increases threaten to broaden to other categories.

## Measuring the breadth of inflation in ASEAN

In this report, we include metrics of the breadth of inflation in ASEAN, such as CPI trimmed means and diffusion indices. Academic research (see list of References on page 15) has shown that conventional core CPI indicators based on exclusion based metrics ("ex food and energy") could be perceived to be less credible if, as in many emerging economies, they exclude important parts of the consumer basket such as food and energy. "Limited influence estimators" such as the trimmed mean have a higher correlation with key macro indicators such as the money supply. A description of the metrics can be found in the Appendix.

## Indonesia

In December, inflation fell to 2.6% y/y (from 2.9% in November), largely reflecting lower food inflation, which slowed to 6.2% y/y (from 6.7% in November). In sequential terms, food prices rose by a smaller 1.2% m/m (compared to 1.5% m/m in November). Rice prices seem to have stabilized at about Rp14,700 in December and early Jan, broadly unchanged from November. Prices of red chili pepper also moderated to Rp57-65,000/ kg, from a peak of Rp80,000/kg in early December. On the other hand, prices of red onions continued to rise. Core inflation also cooled slightly to 1.8% y/y in December (from 1.9% in November). On a m/m basis, the headline CPI rose by 0.41% m/m in December. Prices of goods such as clothing & footwear (+0.18% m/m) and household equipment (+0.10% m/m) were modest. Price increases of services such as dining out services (+0.03% m/m) and healthcare (+0.04% m/m) were also soft. We expect inflation to remain slightly below 3% in H1 2024 (within the central bank inflation target of 2.5±1%), which would not pose constraints on policy easing.

Food inflation slowed to 6% y/y in December, and we expect it to further cool in early 2024 on account of base effects.

## Malaysia

CPI inflation fell to 1.5% y/y in November, from 1.8% in October. Core inflation (excl. volatile items) eased to 2.0% y/y (from 2.4% y/y in October and 2.6% y/y in Q3). The breadth and the intensity of inflationary pressures remains contained, with less than half of measured goods and services prices experiencing m/m increases that are higher than average, and only less than a quarter of the CPI basket weight experiencing inflation of above 2.5% y/y. Goods inflation is mild, particularly in clothing and footwear (0.0% y/y), household equipment and furnishings (1.5% y/y), and housing and utilities (1.7%y/y). The government has announced that it would roll out a targeted RON95 (petroleum) subsidy in 2H 2024 (see link here). We assume a cumulative 10% rise in petrol prices, which means that inflation takes a step-up in H2 2024, to around 3% by the end of the year.

Inflation dropped to 1.5% in November. Subsidy retargeting expected to lead to a one-off upward shift in headline inflation in 2H 2024.

## Philippines

Inflation eased further to 3.9% y/y in December (from 4.1% y/y in November). Core inflation, which excludes volatile food and energy items, fell by 0.3ppt to 4.4% y/y (+0.1% m/m). Food prices rose by a modest 0.8% m/m, driven by rice (up 3.5% m/m). This was offset by sharply lower vegetable prices (-2.8% m/m), oils and fats (-0.15% m/ m), and sugar and confectionery (-0.4% m/m). The fact that rice prices rose again for a second straight month after the harvest came on market (Philippines' next harvest likely comes on market in March, see our previous report here), suggests that the rice supply situation is still tight for now. Last month, the government managed to broker an exemption to import rice from India (see here), and pressured rice traders to front-load their imports of rice (see here). The government is currently consulting with stakeholders on introducing the suggested retail price (SRP) of rice (see here), which aims to contain the increasing retail prices of rice. If these interventions are successful, rice prices are likely to moderate in the coming months. Rice imports in December increased by 29% y/y to 387,210 metric tons. The Department of Agriculture suggested that about 500,000 metric tons of rice are expected to arrive in the Philippines in December and January (see note), as the government is replenishing the rice reserves.

Rice supply situation remains tight, but increased prioritization of imports could help prices to moderate going forward.

Inflationary pressures are not widespread. Less than half of the CPI items are experiencing m/m price increases above their historical average, and the summed CPI weights of items experiencing >4% y/y inflation has been coming down sharply. Whilst we expect inflation to ease to below 4% in Q1, base effects would likely drive inflation higher above 4% in Q2 2024. We expect the BSP's hawkish stance to be maintained for now. Subsequently, a more sustained slowing of inflation below 4% will likely open the door to rate cuts in 2H 2024.

## Singapore

CPI inflation moderated to 3.6% y/y in November from 4.7% y/y in October. This was largely driven by cooling transport inflation (2.8% y/y, -3.9% m/m), particularly in private cars (5.2% y/y, -5.4% m/m), motorcycles (-10.1% y/y, -2.7% m/m), and point-topoint transport services (-2.4% y/y, -1.9% m/m). MAS Core inflation also edged down, to 3.2% y/y (from 3.3% in October). Food prices moderated to 4% y/y in November from 4.1% in October. Food servicing services inflation dropped to 4.3% y/y in November from 4.5% y/y in October, led by restaurant food (3.8% y/y) and hawker food (4.3% y/y). Goods prices also dropped, as seen in clothing & footwear (-1.3% m/m), household durables (-0.2% m/m), and recreational goods (-0.3% m/m), reflecting the fading of supply shocks and lower imported goods prices. Meanwhile services inflation remained elevated, as seen in healthcare (5.1% y/y, -0.2% m/m), holiday expenses (7.3% y/y, +0.7% m/m), and recreational & cultural services (5% y/y, +0.23% m/m).

Core and headline inflation moderated in November, due to lower private road transport costs and goods inflation.

In terms of underlying inflation, the breadth and the intensity of inflationary pressures are modest, with less than half of measured goods and services prices experiencing m/m increases that are higher than average. Still, price increases in utilities, taxi fares, and GST increase, are likely to keep supply-driven cost pressures elevated in the near term.

## Thailand

CPI inflation decelerated further to -0.8% y/y (vs. -0.4% in November). On a m/m basis, headline CPI was -0.5% m/m lower, driven by lower energy prices (-1.9% m/m). Retail prices of diesel and cooking gas have been kept frozen from 1st January 2024 to the end of March to alleviate living costs. Food inflation remains very soft (-0.9% m/m). Rice prices fell m/m for the first time since July (-1.4% m/m), while prices of meat went up m/ m for the first time after 14 consecutive months of falling (+0.6% m/m, though still down -16% y/y, due to illegal imports that had created oversupply issues). Core inflation, whilst still stable at 0.6% y/y, and in sequential terms picking up on a 3m/3m basis, remains very weak. The breadth of inflationary pressures is mild, with <40% of measured goods and services prices experiencing m/m increases that are higher than average. Goods inflation is subdued, particularly in clothing and footwear (0.0% y/y), household equipment and furnishings (-0.7% y/y), and personal care items (0.9%y/y).

Thailand's cabinet had announced to freeze the retail prices of diesel and cooking gas from 1st January 2024 to the end of March, as a continuation of the diesel excise tax cuts implemented from September to December 2023. Other cost alleviating measures include a cut in the alcohol tax and capping of train fares. The government also expressed support for keeping power tariff increases capped. As the government is likely to cap price increases in 2024 by more than we had expected, we lower our forecast for Thailand's 2024 CPI inflation to 1.2% from 1.7%. This factors in weaker realized food inflation, alcohol tax cuts, a smaller expected increase in the power tariff and diesel price adjustments. We have not factored in downside risk if digital wallet implementation does not go through.

Food and fuel prices are the main reasons for Thailand's deflation.

But core goods and services prices have also decelerated sharply throughout 2H 2023.

Lowering Thailand's CPI inflation forecast as the government is likely to keep prices capped, and as food inflation has been very weak.

## Appendix

Diffusion index: Measures the cumulative weight of CPI components whose monthly y/y price change are above a certain threshold. We aggregate some items to maintain a constant basket over time. In this report, we select 1%y/y (the bottom of the acceptable inflation comfort zone for the lower inflation economies in the region), 2.5% (close to the mid-point of inflation comfort zones across the region), and 4% (the top of the range for higher inflation target economies) as thresholds. A higher share for the latter two categories implies a greater breadth of inflation outcomes.

Trimmed mean inflation: Is calculated by excluding a certain percentage of the distribution of m/m price changes at the higher and lower ends of the distribution. In this report, we begin with sorting monthly individual price changes in ascending order from "fell the most" to "rose the most", trimming 10% of the most extreme observations at both ends of the spectrum, and then calculated as a weighted average of the remaining components. This can be used as an alternative of core inflation, but eliminates the influence of outliers or data points to gauge the breadth of underlying price pressures.

The share of CPI components rising: Indicates the number of CPI items the recorded mom % increases as the total number of aggregated CPI items in the basket. Again, we aggregate some items to maintain a constant basket over time. The metric highlights how much of the CPI index is or is not in deflation.

Core goods and core services: We break core inflation down into two broad categories: core goods (commodities less food and energy commodities) and core services (services less household utilities and other services). Core goods inflation may be more influenced by external developments. Both elements of core inflation rising together may suggest broad-based pressures. A caveat with this analysis is the revisions to CPI data. On balance this has been helpful since the data revisions have increased the granularity of available CPI data in the region in recent years. However, this also makes for challenges in defining categories consistently over time. Most recently this caveat has been strongest for Indonesia and Philippines, while the availability of detailed CPI data for Vietnam remains limited.
