## FX Strategy

## The outlook for G10 funders

• Last week we came out of the mid-year process with a view to be tactically short funders (JPY and CHF, link), but more for idiosyncratic bottom-up reasons rather than a broad top-down view.

Some similarities for CHF and JPY come, for instance, through balance of payments, with both having been dragged by FDI and debt flows. Ultimately core differences mean CHF should trade as more of a safe haven than JPY in times of stress.

USD/JPY has disconnected from rate spreads, and given the uncertainties surrounding BoJ policy, little intention from the MoF to defend any specific level of USD/JPY, and carry-seeking flows (even in the face of narrowing rate spreads), it is not clear to us that the time is right to be long JPY yet.

With a cyclically positive view on growth still intact at the global level, we are more of a mind to be short JPY versus cyclical currencies such as AUD, NOK and GBP on pullbacks in those crosses. Indeed, we took profit on our AUD/JPY long last week (link) and see any JPY rallies driven by possible MoF intervention as an opportunity to re-enter JPY shorts.

Last week we wrote (link) that as we exited June, our bias was for the political risk premium in CHF to reduce and more normal seasonality trends to allow CHF to weaken in July.

Beyond that things become more tricky for the bearish franc view, as the last round of European PMIs disappointed, the SNB isn’t as dovish on FX as it once was, tariff and FX policy risks will soon come into view, and the Fed approaches policy easing. So tactical CHF weakness in July makes sense, but growth and yield developments will then determine whether a good entry point for CHF longs presents itself.

## CHF vs JPY - some similarities but core differences

During H1-24 we argued that CHF and JPY were being driven by similar forces (link), but as we get further into H2-24 their differences should become more apparent. CHF and JPY occupy the bottom two rankings in G10 in terms of yield volatility, real carry and risk-adjusted carry, as well as having among the most negative risk beta in G10 (Figure 2). During H1-24 that made them ideal funders for the currencies of economies where growth was accelerating. It also increased their sensitivity to US yields. For investors debating whether markets were in the middle or the right side of the dollar smile, being short CHF and JPY provided some asymmetry in that they could weaken versus the USD in both of those environments.

Looking at the respective Balance of Payments, Switzerland ran a -4.2% of GDP basic balance in Q1, which is rare for a country that is used to solid BoP surpluses. Japan ran a 1.7% of GDP basic balance. Both positions are dragged down by FDI

JPY: Scale of H1 weakness won’t repeat as relative monetary cycles turn, but other risks emerge

Since the launch of the Fed rate hike cycle in March 2022, market expectations for the Fed’s monetary policy have been the main driver of USD/JPY. As a result, there has been a relatively strong correlation between USD/JPY and FF futures. Given that, our USD/JPY target as of end-September this year is 157, which is in line with one cut by the Fed this year. Our economic research team expects one cut by the Fed and two hikes by the BoJ within this year and divergence between the US and Japan to continue into next year and later. This divergence should lead to a modest decline in USD/JPY over the medium-term.

Given the recent rally in USD/JPY to above 161, the highest since 1986, the pair has now deviated from the level suggested by the past correlation with FF futures (Figure 3). One possible explanation for the recent deviation of USD/JPY from rates/rates expectations is a boom in JPY carry trades. For carry trades, absolute levels of short-term interest rate spreads and their sustainability are key factors, while long-term yields driven by monetary policy expectations become less significant for FX rate developments. Indeed, we saw weaker correlation between USD/JPY and long-end UST yields in 2006-07 when the JPY carry trade was booming (Figure 4). Although there has been little evidence suggesting that JPY carry trades have become more active at this time, at least we can point out that the attractiveness of the USD/JPY carry trade has improved to a level comparable with that seen in 2006-07 (Figure 5Curent acivs ofJPYy dmpbl wh 206-7). Moreover, history suggests that the long USD/JPY carry trade tends to remain well supported so long as the US-JP yield spread is above 4% (Figure 6).

More fundamentally, Japan’s deeply negative real interest rate has driven underlying JPY weakness in recent years (Figure 7), and in relation to this, there is some speculation that domestic outflows from Japan could even accelerate at some point, even as the Fed/BoJ policy gap begins to narrow. As mentioned above, our baseline scenario is that of a modest decline in USD/JPY driven by monetary policy divergence, and we thus think that the magnitude of JPY weakness seen in H1 this year won’t be repeated. However, recent developments suggest the possibility of an alternative scenario — outright JPY weakness driven by carry trades and /or domestic capital flow — has materially risen.

Meanwhile, anxiety around MoF intervention and/or actual intervention is liable to cap USD/JPY upside to some extent. As repeatedly stated, we believe that the key determinants of FX intervention decisions are the speed of Yen movement and the market context rather than any specific levels. Therefore, for example, were USD/JPY to trade at 165, the possibility of intervention would be significantly different if it were reached within the day driven by speculative JPY-selling compared to reaching it a few weeks later driven by rising US interest rates and a stronger broad USD (needless to say, the former has a much higher probability of forcing intervention). When JPY-buying intervention was implemented on April 29, USD/JPY rose on the day from the previous day’s low by more than 5 yen; hence if USD/JPY rises by 4-5 yen within 1-2 business days, the possibility of another round of JPY-buying intervention cannot be ruled out. Additionally, the threshold for intervention might be slightly lower this time around than it was at the end of April given the larger deviation of USD/JPY vs. UST yields and US-JP yield spreads that could make a plausible case for the Yen deviating from fundamentals (note).

According to the media, as widely expected, Mr. Masato Kanda, who is seen as a key person for FX intervention policy since his appointment in July 2021, will step down and be succeeded by Atsushi Mimura, the current Director-General of the International Bureau. Some market participants have speculated that the MoF stance on interventions might soften with this change. However, given that MoF's FX interventions are conducted based on G7’s FX commitments and Mimura is seen as valuing policy consistency, it is not expected that there will be significant changes in the intervention stance following the personnel change.

## CHF - Tactically bearish, things become more difficult deeper into H2

In our end-May note titled “Short term obstacles to medium term CHF weakness” (link) we said June would be harder to see CHF weaken given the strongest seasonality in global FX, SNB governor Jordan’s hawkish speech and the June SNB meeting. On top of that we got a surprise French election which pushed CHF stronger as well. As June now ends and seasonality normalises, we said last week (link) that our bias was to fade the political risk premium in CHF over the short term as we head into July. In our view this could see EUR/CHF test fair value (0.9769, Figure 9) over the short term. Beyond that things could get harder for the pair. The June PMI data signalled a set back for Eurozone growth, and it will be key to see whether that repeats in July. EUR/CHF is highly sensitive to Eurozone growth as shown in Figure 8. The SNB also isn’t as dovish as it once was. Jordan clearly expressed discomfort with the level of the currency at the end of May, and even though he appeared to backtrack on that somewhat at the June policy meeting press conference, the market has a much better idea now of where the line in the sand has been drawn. Indeed, the Q1 SNB transactions data revealed that the central bank actually did very little with the currency in Q1. Netting off the basic balance with SNB intervention in Q1 gives a net flows number which sits in bearish CHF territory to the tune of 6.8bn CHF, which is modest in historical context, but still negative.

Tariff risks are a clear bullish risk for CHF in H2-24. Its our view that once we get to August, it will be difficult for markets to pull their focus away from the probabilities around tariff risk. It will therefore become increasingly difficult for CHF to weaken and indeed there may be good opportunities to go long the currency if European growth surveys start to top out. Finally, at least as important a risk is that of global yields and the impact on CHF rate spreads. There appears to be a tug of war now between tariff (and related risks) pushing US yields higher, while soft US data surprises pull US yields lower. If that creates sticky US yields within a range, then investors may still be able to earn carry in USD/CHF even after accounting for some mark-to-market losses. Should the Fed be able to cut more aggressively, however, and USD/CHF rate spreads compress, that would be a key concern for CHF bears. For now we think the European political risk premium dominates the aforementioned factors, but then as markets approach the end of July, traditional drivers will re-assert. In summary, tactical CHF weakness in July makes sense, but depending on how growth and yields evolve, a good entry point for CHF longs may present itself.
