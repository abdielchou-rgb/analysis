# Capital market with Chinese characteristics III: How to support an ageing China?

## Pension shortfall: why, where, and what are the potential solutions?

As China's population ages, the country faces a pension shortfall. The Chinese Academy of Social Sciences forecasts the country's basic pension fund will be depleted by as early as 2035. This is partly because the country's pension system supports people who retired before its creation in 1997 and have made no contributions. On top of that, China has a relatively early retirement age. How can China bridge this looming funding gap? Apart from fiscal support, we think the solution lies in raising the retirement age and diversifying pension fund investments.

## Who might benefit from the development of the personal pension scheme?

China is also developing a third-pillar personal pension scheme (PPS), which we expect to reach Rmb172trn in 2060E, or 37% of the country's GDP (report). As Chinese households continue to raise their exposure to equity investments, we expect Rmb1.1trn of inflows into A-shares through the PPS by 2030E, accounting for 3% of the current free-float market cap, 21% of domestic mutual funds' equity holdings, and 15% of PPS AUM. We think such a long-term capital source could support the development of China's equity markets and raise institutional participation. We believe greater inflows from pensions would benefit stocks that are industry leaders with stable growth prospects and inexpensive valuations.

## State-owned capital transfer: dividends, reinvestment and capital operation

China began transferring state-owned capital to its social security fund in 2017. Details of how the fund is managed were released in March 2024. We estimate Rmb400bn of cash dividends from the transferred state-owned capital (9% of China's current annual basic pension income) could be used to supplement pension payments in 2030. As the pension deficit continues to widen, we think such reinvestment and capital operations are needed to create additional value.

## SOE valuation dilemma and a valuation system with Chinese characteristics

SOEs are trading at only half of the valuation of non-SOEs. In our view, this is unjustified from both the perspective of PE/earnings growth and PB/ROE (see "Capital market with Chinese characteristics II: SOE valuation dilemma and potential solutions"). To solve this, we expect China to accelerate SOE reform to boost profitability and payout ratios. We also note 'market value' has recently been added to the metrics used to evaluate SOE performance. Additionally, we think regulators' proposal to adopt a 'valuation system with Chinese characteristics' was created essentially to reverse investors' negative perceptions of SOEs. # Ageing population: What now and what next?

Getting old before getting rich

China is getting 'old'. According to the United Nations (UN), the median age of the Chinese population was 38.4 years in 2020, surpassing that of the United States (38.3). When the 65-plus age group reaches 7% of the population, a society is termed as 'ageing', per WHO definitions. When the ratio reaches 14%, a society is termed as 'aged'. The proportion of people over 65 years old in China reached 14.2% in 2021 and is expected to rise to around 30% by 2050F, according to United Nations Population Division (UNPD).

However, Chinese people were significantly less wealthy at the time the country reached aged status than other people when their nations reached the same watershed. By UN estimates, China reached 'aged' status in 2021 with a GDP per capita of US\$11,188, far behind US\$29,467 of Japan in 1994 and US\$55,675 of the US in 2014.

## Policy support has yet to boost fertility

In response to a declining birth rate, China implemented a selective two-child policy in 2013, a universal two-child policy in 2015, and lifted the birth limit to three children in 2021. More supportive fertility policies were implemented at the regional level in 2023. For example, Hangzhou plans to provide a one-time subsidy of Rmb5,000 to two-child families and a one-time subsidy of Rmb20,000 to three-child families. In 2024, Beijing, Guangxi, Inner Mongolia and Gansu provinces have included assisted reproduction in medical insurance reimbursements, according to the National Health Security Administration. In addition, Shandong Medical Insurance Bureau announced that assisted reproductive medical services have been officially included in Shandong's medical insurance from April 1.

However, the favourable policies have yet to yield promising results. According to the Seventh National Population Census, the average annual growth rate of the Chinese population has slowed to 0.53% over the past decade, down from 0.57% in the previous decade, and the total fertility rate fell to 1.3 in 2020, far below the 2.1 minimum fertility rate needed to maintain the current population.

The shrinking number of new births can be attributed to a number of factors. In a survey by the National Health Commission, 75.1% of respondents said they feel a "heavy economic burden", while 51.3% said they have received "no help to take care of the children". Fertility has also been affected by the COVID black swan.

To counter the increasingly urgent problem of its ageing population, Japan has introduced numerous supportive child-birth and child-rearing policies over the past three decades. Despite these efforts, however, Japan's birth rate has remained in steady decline, while its dependency ratio has been on the constant rise.

## How to fill the pension shortfall? Historical issues affecting China's pension system

According to the three-pillar pension system as defined by the World Bank, China has already essentially established a multi-pillar pension system. However, in 2022, the total balance of China's first pillar stood at Rmb8.3trn or 6.9% of China's GDP in 2022; in 2023 the total balance of China's second pillar stood at Rmb5.8trn or 4.6% of China's GDP in 2023; the third pillar remains nascent. In China, total pension assets are roughly 12% of GDP, much lower than the average level of 113.8% in OECD countries as of 2020.

The Chinese Academy of Social Sciences estimates that the annual balance (income minus expense) of China's basic pension fund could turn negative in 2028F, and that the deficit would keep widening (even taking fiscal subsidies into account). Thus, the cumulative balance for the first pillar looks set to peak in 2027F and be depleted in 2035F. Zhou Xiaochuan, former governor of the People's Bank of China, said in February 2023 that China's pension system faces serious challenges and there is no room for delay in addressing them. In December 2023, he further commented that postponing retirement is not about postponing it for as long as you want.

The problem of a potential pension shortfall is even worse at the regional level. The number of provinces that are net contributors to the central pension scheme shrank from 21 in 2022 to 18 in 2023.

When China established its national pension system in 1997, it was decided that people who retired before 1997 would still receive pensions despite having made no contributions to the pension system. As SOE employees who retired before 1997 had made substantial contributions to the country's economic development over their working lives when little progress had yet been made in market reform, it was deemed fair to provide them with retirement welfare. As a result, those who retired before 1997 have received pensions as if their personal pension accounts were fully paid over their period of employment.

However, with no personal account contributions from pre-1997 retirees or fiscal support for the pension system, China's working population is effectively financing the pensions of current retirees rather than their own. So since 1997, the reformed pension system has functioned as a pay-as-you-go model. As the size of the retired population without fully paid personal accounts grows and life expectancy increases, the deficit in private accounts is set to expand and lead to a severe pension shortfall in years to come.

## How to pay the elderly? Our mind map

In this section, we summarise what we consider to be the most viable solutions to China's pension shortfall.

Figure 17: China's pension system and potential solutions to the pension shortfall
![](images/48ee5d26c37ccf63c29f193a8a62694bf2cb96368d960cb9235a1b2d5c0a4e90.jpg)
Source: UBS-S

Apart from fiscal support, postponing the retirement age would be the most obvious way China could ease the pension shortfall, in our view. However, such a policy change is destined to be controversial and unpopular. As early as November 2008, the head of the Social Security Research Institute of the Ministry of Human Resources and Social Security said that the relevant departments were considering extending the retirement age. But 15 years later, even though China has reached 'aged' status, the debate is ongoing. In March 2023, State Council Premier Li Qiang said the government would carefully study the raising of the retirement age and that it would safely launch such a policy change when the time is right.

Meanwhile, the diversification of pension fund investments could help improve returns. The basic pension fund is 23% invested, with an average yield of 6.49% pa since December 2016 when entrusted operations kicked off. Returns are realised through both direct investment and entrusted investment. Direct investment is conducted by the National Council for Social Security Fund (NCSSF), with investments mainly in bank deposits and equities. Entrusted investments are conducted by trustees and mainly include onshore stocks, bonds, and other financial products. The combined share of onshore stocks, equity funds, hybrid funds, equity pension products is capped at 30% of the pension fund's NAV. We think that further diversification of the pension fund's portfolio could help enhance long-term returns.

Besides the above-mentioned potential solutions to a pension shortfall, below we highlight two other major potential solutions: developing China's third pillar personal pension scheme (PPS) and state-owned capital transfer.

## How large could personal pension scheme inflows into A-shares be? Saving vs investing

The IMF estimates China had the highest savings rate by far among all major economies in 2022. Although a similar savings culture among many East Asian countries might partially explain why some Asian economies have a persistently higher savings rate (35.3% in Korea, 35.0% in Vietnam and 36.4% in Singapore), we believe an underdeveloped capital market and pension system can also help explain the difference.

In fact, excess savings partly serve as people's preparation for retirement. We call the China model 'save for old age'.

In contrast, we consider the model adopted in the US as 'invest for old age'. Money put into pension accounts is invested in the capital markets, and the strong performance of US equities attracts more inflows into pensions, theoretically creating a virtuous circle. US residents receive both tax benefits and capital gains under this model.

## PPS accounts to reach Rmb172trn in 2060E

Starting from mid-2021, China's authorities began issuing notices and policies to support the development of the PPS. A list of 36 pilot cities and the first batch of PPS products were announced in 2022 and we expect the full implementation of PPS to happen quickly in the coming years.

UBS Securities financial analyst Haifeng Cao published a deep dive into China's PPS (see "APAC Focus: Personal pension scheme — an industry growth driver for decades to come?", 14 February 2023). With attractive tax incentives, we expect China's PPS to steadily accumulate assets (contributions greater than withdrawals) until breaking even (contributions = withdrawals) around 2060E, when we forecast the PPS to reach a cumulative size Rmb172trn, or 37% of China's GDP. We think that leading brokers with advantages in asset management and investment advisory, as well as mutual funds, are likely to benefit more from the development of PPS.

After comparing household asset allocations in China and other major markets, we expect Chinese households to continue to raise their equity investment exposure, driven by four factors:

(1) The Chinese government continues to discourage investment from flowing into the property sector and repeatedly stated that "housing is for living, not for speculation". In reality, the property prices have been on a downward trend since 2023.

(2) China’s new asset management regulations require financial institutions to offer yields based on the net asset value of the products, which increases the volatility of returns on fixed-income products. China has also tightened regulations on trust financing, which is used to offer investors "risk-free" yields higher than traditional wealth management products.

(3) Chinese households may buy more insurance and pension products incrementally, while insurers and pension funds are likely to invest more in equities in a low interest rate environment.

(4) The quality of Chinese equities is improving. More internet giants have returned to Hong Kong for secondary listings, and they may potentially list onshore in the future.

We think that investing in equities through the PPS with tax benefits is an attractive option for Chinese residents. In fact, we find that the scale of a country's PPS has had a positive relationship with the progress of capital market development in other countries. More developed capital markets can provide abundant investible assets for the PPS, while the PPS can provide a stable, long-term flow of funds into the capital markets, boosting equity valuations and stock performance. As a result, the PPS and capital market tend to be mutually reinforcing. For this reason, we think more Chinese household assets will likely be allocated through PPS to financial or equity-type assets.

The UBS-S financials research team's base case scenario is for China's PPS to reach an accumulated size of Rmb7.22trn by 2030E. We expect Rmb1.1trn of inflows into Ashares through PPS by 2030E, accounting for 3% of current free-float A-share market cap, 21% of domestic mutual funds' holdings of equities, and 15% of PPS AUM.

## Long-term funding source supports equity market institutionalisation

We believe development of the second and third pillars of China's pension system will be instrumental in increasing institutional investor participation in China's equity markets.

The experience of other countries shows that a substantial portion of the total second and third pillars would likely be invested in risky assets, such as equities, through mutual funds. For example, in 2020, direct-contribution plans in the US's second pillar had a 59% mutual fund allocation, while the third pillar IRA had 45%. In Japan, the Nippon Individual Savings Account (NISA) scheme, the country's second pillar, had a 57% mutual fund allocation, while the Individual Defined Contribution (iDeCo) programme, the third pillar, had 55%.

The US experience offers an example of how mutual funds can play a pivotal role in the process of institutionalising an equity market. In 1990, mutual funds only made up of 7% of the US stock market. But in the 1990s, pension funds started using mutual funds in the management of their portfolios. Thirty years later, mutual funds now make up around 27% of the US stock market. According to the Investment Company Institute (ICI), 83% of mutual fund holders invested through their retirement plans in 2020. Meanwhile, 63% of US households bought mutual funds outside their pension funds Among these households, 47% did so with the help of investment advisors.

# State-owned capital transfer and rerating of quality SOEs

## Growth in SOE assets implies further state-owned capital transfer

While China's pre-1997 retirees did not pay into their personal pension accounts, these former SOE employees are considered to have made important contributions to China's economic development at a time when the country had made little progress in market reform. Thus, it has been deemed only fair that they should receive retirement welfare. As many of these retirees dedicated their entire career to helping build SOEs, we think it would be appropriate for state-owned capital to be deployed to supplement basic retirement benefits.

In November 2017, the State Council unveiled an implementation plan to transfer a portion of state-owned capital to enrich the social security fund. With the goal of making up for the pension shortfall, 10% of state-owned capital (ie, the shareholders' equity belonging to the Chinese government) of SOEs would be transferred. At the provincial level, a number of local governments have announced similar plans. For example, the Shanghai government in 2020 issued an implementation plan to transfer part of Shanghai's state-owned capital to the social security fund with the transfer ratio set at 10%.

As of end-2020, a total of Rmb1.68trn of state-owned capital of central SOEs and central financial institutions had been transferred to the social security fund. The total capital transferred amounted to Rmb3.3trn by 2020, when including some local SOEs. The market value of the equities further increased to over Rmb4trn over 2020-2023.

As at the end of 2022, the total assets of industrial SOEs amounted to Rmb339.5trn. Total state-owned capital in industrial companies held by the central and local governments grew at a CAGR of 15% from Rmb18.5trn in 2010 to Rmb95trn in 2022, while total state-owned capital in financial companies held by the central and local governments increased from Rmb16.2trn to Rmb27.6trn during the same period. Thus, the total value of the central and local governments' SOE holdings (state-owned capital) came to Rmb122.3trn by end-2022.

In our base case, total state-owned capital held by the central and local governments is expected to reach Rmb200trn by 2030E. Assuming a transfer ratio of 10%, we estimate Rmb20trn of state-owned capital (though not all of the equities are listed on stock exchanges) could be transferred to help fill the pension shortfall, and the ratio could be increased further if necessary.

## Dividend payouts before 2030E; capital operation thereafter

The dividend payouts from state-owned capital transferred to the pension account could be used to supplement pension payments. Currently, the dividend yield from Ashare SOEs averages 3.4%, much higher than that of non-SOEs. Unlisted assets could also pay dividends. Looking ahead to the next decade, we foresee room for the dividend yield to increase further. We estimate roughly Rmb400bn of cash dividends – equal to 9% of total current annual basic pension income – paid by both listed and unlisted transferred state-owned capital could go towards pension payments in 2030E.

However, we do not think our above-mentioned Rmb400bn estimate of cash dividends would be enough to meet the pension shortfall in the long term, as the pension deficit is expected to widen materially over 2030-40 while SOE assets could grow at a slower pace. Thus, we think it is crucial that China obtains additional income through the operation of transferred state-owned capital. The government released a policy paper entitled "Measures for the operation and management of state-owned equity and cash income of the transferred capital for enhancing social security funds" in March 2024, setting out the operational and investment management rules.

## Why are SOEs undervalued by investors?

In "Capital market with Chinese characteristics II: SOE valuation dilemma and potential solutions", we point out SOEs are substantially undervalued by investors in both the Ashare and Hong Kong equity markets. Currently, the valuation of SOEs is only half of the non-SOEs in both PE and PB terms. In our view, the vast valuation difference between the SOEs and their non-SOE peers is likely due to three major factors, namely sector distribution, operating efficiency and investors' negative perceptions of SOEs. We believe the SOEs' valuation discount is not justified based on a classical valuation research framework.

(1) From a PE multiple point of view, non-financials SOEs have been trading at an increasingly steep discount to their non-SOE peers to the extent that the non-SOEs' PE multiple has been more than double that of the SOEs since 2021. However, the large valuation gap cannot be explained by differences in earnings growth.

To some extent, the SOEs' earnings growth is indeed slower. However, the lead is not as alarmingly wide, with profit growing at an average rate of 11% for non-SOEs compared with 9% for SOEs over a span of 10 years (2014-23).

(2) PE valuation aside, we note that a big part of the difference in PB valuation between SOEs and non-SOEs cannot be easily explained by differences in ROE.

## SOE reform is set to accelerate

Considering that the market generally assigns higher valuations to assets with high growth prospects and strong profitability, we think investors would likely need to see evidence of improving operating efficiency in SOEs. In our view, SOEs are unlikely to see a material re-rating if there is no concrete evidence of an improvement in profitability. Meanwhile, dividend payouts rely heavily on profitability. Higher ROE would lay the foundation for higher dividend payouts, which could be used to supplement pension payments over the long run.

The government has rolled out a series of policies and guidance on SOE reform. Related topics have been well researched in recent years; mergers and consolidation, mixed ownership reform, equity incentive plans, asset injections, and deleveraging have been widely discussed.

As China entered 'aged' status in 2021, we believe state-owned capital likely needs to serve as a supplement to support the retirement system for all generations. Japan's experience suggests that numerous measures and major reforms could be needed. China's pension system faces many challenges and the time to solve them looks increasingly limited. Thus, we think SOE reform should accelerate.

The total stake of the central and local governments in SOEs reached Rmb122.3trn by end-2022. From the perspective of cash flow/dividend, considering the substantial size of SOEs' assets and state-owned capital, we estimate that a 1ppt increase in ROE would bring Rmb1.2trn in additional earnings attributed to the Chinese government and billions in extra dividend payments each year.

From the perspective of valuation/market cap, higher ROE brought by reform would naturally lead to higher valuations for SOEs. We estimate that raising the PB ratio for SOEs as a group by 0.5x would boost the market cap of SOEs to Rmb61trn, assuming all of them are listed. This could create a substantial amount of resources for capital operation.

Many SOEs' assets are still unlisted. But as the asset securitisation rate increases along with the development of direct financing and a registration-based IPO system, we think more state-owned assets could be listed on exchanges.

## Ideological underpinning: a "valuation system with Chinese characteristics"

To improve investors' perceptions on the SOEs, on 21 November 2022, the CSRC proposed that “we will build a capital market and explore a valuation system with Chinese characteristics (VCC), so the market can allocate resources effectively.” On 23 January 2024, the CSRC revisited the concept of VCC in its 2024 work conference. Just a day later, the State-owned Assets Supervision and Administration Commission of the State Council (SASAC) announced that it would include the market value management results in the assessment of SOE executives.

In our view, the VCC as well as adding market value to SOEs performance review is likely aimed at boosting the valuation of SOEs and may help investors better understand the big picture of state-owned capital transfer.

In Japan, the re-rating of listcos has also been connected to the country's pension scheme. NISA, which is Japan's personal pension scheme, was established by the government in a low interest rate environment in an effort to channel personal savings into the capital market. According to documents from the TSE, the re-rating of Japanese stocks could help supplement the pensions of Japanese households and drive the development of NISA.

## Theme #2: Potential re-rating of high-quality SOEs

Admittedly, many fundamental investors are unlikely to start investing in Chinese SOEs without concrete evidence of an improvement in operational efficiency and profitability for SOEs. Historically, for individual SOEs, those that increased dividend payouts, announced meaningful management incentive schemes, or showed improved cash flow generation, have generally outperformed the broader market over the ensuing 12- month period.

As executives of SOEs will be judged on the outcome of market value management going forward, we expect SOE executives to have the advancement of corporate governance and operating results at the top of their agenda.

Looking ahead, other catalysts we think could support a re-rating of SOEs include: 1) higher dividend payouts, 2) share buybacks, 3) management incentive schemes, in particular the level of incentive provided to senior management with accompanying financial targets, 4) free cash flow generation, and 5) corporate governance improvement.

## Valuation Method and Risk Statement

For various stocks across the industries we cover in the Hong Kong and mainland China stock markets, we use a variety of valuation approaches, including DCF models, Gordon growth model analysis and relative valuation analysis using various multiples such as PE, EV/EBITDA and P/BV.

We think the risks facing China's equities include a hard landing for the property market, a capital exodus associated with currency depreciation and slow structural reform progress. In our view, any government policies that do not adequately address these risks could result in a shock to the market. For example, an excess of stimulus policies could pose a risk to the transition from an investment-driven to a consumption-driven economy and increase the debt of government and state-owned enterprises.
