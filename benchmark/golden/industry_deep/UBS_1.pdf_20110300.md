# APAC Economic Perspectives Singapore: The cost conundrum

## Clarifying the questions on competitiveness and restrictiveness

Since end 2021, Singapore's real exchange rate has appreciated by about 0-40+% vs. most of its major trading partners, or 12% on a trade-weighted basis by our estimates. But the rapid S\$NEER appreciation has not undermined competitiveness, in our view. One, manufacturing firms do not cite the S\$ as a factor inhibiting export orders. Two, FDI inflows to Singapore remain resilient. Three, our global supply chain analysis (link) shows Singapore's competitive position in manufacturing has been underpinned by structural factors, which remain intact. Four, absent the S\$ appreciation, domestic prices would have likely risen to an even larger extent, thereby undermining cost competitiveness anyway. We believe the S\$NEER's perceived impact on cross-border consumption switching has been overstated by the salience of bilateral currency movements. Going forward, as the trend of regional currencies' appreciation vs. SGD continues, outbound spending could be dialled back to some degree.

## Three reasons for a lower neutral S\$NEER slope

We believe the current 1.5% p.a. (UBSe) appreciation of the S\$NEER policy band could be restrictive in the medium term on the following basis. (2) Lower trend GDP growth implies a slower neutral rate of REER appreciation, given narrowing productivity differentials between the tradable and non-tradable sectors. 2) Global inflation has cooled materially. Amongst many of Singapore's key trading partners, CPI inflation has structurally declined. (3) UBS Evidence Lab's new outbound travel survey shows growing intentions of Singaporeans to spend in Johor, especially after the completion of the rail link between Singapore and Malaysia expected in 2026. UBS Singapore Research (link) forecasts S\$2.5bn of retail sales leakage (5.4% of current sales), which represents a structural competitive headwind on retailers' pricing power in the medium term.

## MAS to remain on hold in October but ease next year, likely in January

Recently, inflation has cooled sharply and on a broadening basis. We think it is because of lower import prices, consumers' increasing price resistance, and the unwinding of one-off factors, rather than weak underlying demand. That said, with growth and inflation both close to trend next year (i.e. an output gap of zero and core inflation at 2%), and upside risks to external inflation easing, we think MAS is likely to adjust its policy stance to a medium-term neutral stance in 2025. We now expect MAS to reduce the pace of S\$NEER policy band appreciation to 1% p.a. in Jan'25. We still think MAS will keep the current 1.5% p.a (UBSe) slope unchanged at the Oct'24 meeting as it awaits more data to confirm that the disinflationary trend will continue.

## Strategy: We recommend selling SGD vs. PHP

We prefer PHP over SGD as a countercyclical, late cycle, trade that should perform defensively in the event of a further global downturn. Three reasons: 1) In the context of broadening easing expectations' globally, SNEER's expensiveness on the MAS's crawling band (\~1.85% above the mid) is an anomaly. This suggests SGD's YTD outperformance is at risk of a modest reversal. In contrast, the PHP screen as 2-3% cheap on our models. 2) Philippines basic balance has risen to about neutral, with recent decline in oil prices likely improving it further in Q3. At the same time, to countercyclical Peso’s relief, most monetary and domestic indicators are implying limited pressure on the CA balance from imports growth. 3) Philippines economy is less susceptible to portfolio outflows, with NIIP portfolio liabilities being one of the lowest in ASEAN (at \~20% of GDP). For more, see: USD/ASIA: The End of the Beginning?

## A different transmission mechanism

## S\$REER's 12% appreciation since 2022 was anomalous

Since 2022, we estimate Singapore's real exchange rate has appreciated by 12% on a trade-weighted basis. Apart from MAS' strong S\$ policy (S\$NEER strengthened by 10%) since late 2021, higher inflation vs. Singapore's trading partners (2% higher over the last 2.5 years) was another factor driving REER appreciation. Structural REER appreciation tends to be episodic. The last time S\$REER strengthened sharply was 2007-2012 (\~3% p.a.). That was followed by a prolonged period wherein the real exchange rate was stagnant over 2011-20. The recent period of REER appreciation stands out in that growth is estimated to average just 2.1% in 2022-24, vs. a much higher 6% p.a. in 2007-12. Inflation averaged 3.9% in 2007-12, comparable to 4.1% in 2022-24.

The rapid rate of real exchange appreciation was anomalous, particularly in the context of weaker growth over the past three years.

S\$ appreciation since late 2021 has also been characterized by significant variation across currencies. On a price-adjusted basis, the real exchange rate of the S\$ has appreciated by around 40% against the Japanese Yen, 20% against the Malaysian Ringgit, and has barely budged vs. the US Dollar. This is because large interest rate differentials vs. the USD had weighed on lower-yielding Asian currencies.

The variation in bilateral real exchange rate movements was sizeable given strong USD environment.

## Not a loss of competitiveness, just a different transmission mechanism

Only 2% of manufacturing firms cite the S\$ as a factor affecting export orders, per EDB's business expectations survey. This could be due to a structural increase in US\$ vehicle pricing, which attenuates the impact of S\$ appreciation on export prices and demand. Another interpretation is that export competitiveness was not affected because the S\$ was relatively stable vis-à-vis the US\$ over this period. Thus, the strong S\$NEER policy did not affect exports as much as it could have. On the other hand, producer currency pricing is more prevalent in consumer goods imports, which are mainly sourced from China, Malaysia, and Europe. The relative strength in the SGD vs. CNH, MYR, and EUR helped to offset imported inflation.

Manufacturing exporters don't see the S\$ as limiting export orders.

Our global analysis, which takes a bird's eye view of the global supply chain playing field, concludes that Singapore's competitive position in global supply chains has in fact strengthened over the past decade. Improved positioning in specific, niche sectors with fewer competitors, might explain why Singapore's manufacturing exports are becoming less price elastic over time (as per EDB's survey).

Manufacturing focused on specific higher value add sectors. Singapore's manufacturing output is concentrated in a few productive and capital intensive sectors. These include electronics (front-end semiconductors and semiconductor equipment production), petrochemicals, transport engineering (aerospace), and pharmaceuticals. In fact, Singapore has gained stronger global export share in semicap equipment, air transport, and pharmaceuticals. The recent flattish growth profile in goods exports was largely due to cyclical reasons, unrelated to the

strength of the nominal exchange rate.

Proactive regulatory environment and incentives to attract investments. Singapore's government has been proactive in creating a favourable business environment for foreign manufacturing MNCs through various incentives, such as tax breaks and grants. This support goes beyond financial aid, as Singapore also helps with working visas and regulation issues. Strong intellectual property protection has also been a key factor in attracting investments.

 FDI patterns do not seem to suggest loss of competitiveness. Singapore's fixed asset investments in manufacturing and services continued to remain robust over the past three years.

## Consumption leakage overhyped?

## If the exchange rate is so strong, why is outbound travel still below its peak?

Historically, about 16% of Singapore's consumption comprises spending abroad. But as of Q2 2024, outbound travel spending has not recovered to its previous highs, and stands at just 13% of total consumption. Singapore's net travel services deficit is smaller now, at about 1% of GDP as of H1 2024 compared to 1.8% of GDP in 2019. Likely, higher air fares and/or lower flight capacity renders overseas travel uneconomical, even after accounting for a stronger S\$. This calls into question the notion that the exchangerate adjusted price of travel is so low as to incentivize significant cross-border expenditure leakage. Tourist arrivals have also recovered to about 90% of pre-COVID levels, with Singapore not far off the leaders Japan (110%) and Korea (97%). Based on UBS Evidence Lab's survey of China outbound travel intentions (>Access Dataset), the exchange rate ranks only 6th out of 8th of the key concerns deterring travellers to Singapore.

Spending overseas as a % of total consumption still below prepandemic levels.

## That said, the exchange rate likely influences choice of travel destinations

UBS Singapore Research noted that the number of Singapore visitors to Korea and Japan has increased sharply (by 40-60%) since 2019, in line with a massive 25-50% depreciation in their real exchange rates against the Singapore dollar. For other popular long-haul destinations, such as Australia and Taiwan, visitorship increased by a smaller amount, in line with more muted real exchange strengthening of SGD versus AUD and TWD. As we expect regional currencies to strengthen against the SGD over the next year as the Fed starts cutting, this could mitigate consumption switching effects to some extent.

## Near-term growth momentum remains steady

## Domestic consumption is not weak despite flattish retail sales

Based on national accounts data, private consumption has in fact accelerated in 1H 2024, belying a flattish profile for retail and F&B sales. Even after netting off travel spending overseas and big ticket items such as car sales, the rate of increase in private consumption was as much as 3.8% q/q sa in H1 2024, in nominal terms. How to interpret this dichotomy? First, private consumption includes a larger universe of spending data, including services such as education, healthcare, personal, and recreational services, and imputed rentals. Since the quarterly breakdown of consumption details are not available, we presume spending on the services items listed above has remained quite strong. Even as a percentage of disposable income, household spending has accelerated, and the unusually elevated savings rates over 2022-23 has come down. Thus, we believe the recent softening of inflation momentum is not an ominous sign of broadening demand weakness.

Despite flattish retail sales, we think underlying consumption momentum is robust and demand is solid.

## Is neutral lower in the medium term?

Nevertheless, over the longer term, a slower pace of domestic growth, weaker global inflation, and other structural factors, could mean the current 1.5% p.a. apprecation of S\$NEER may become increasingly restrictive. We think the neutral rate of S\$NEER appreciation in Singapore's context is between 0.5-1.5% p.a, implying the current 1.5% p.a. is not necessarily very restrictive, only slightly so. The rationale for our call for MAS easing in 2025 is premised on lower upside risks to inflation next year.

## Risks of increased competition and leakage overseas

Based on the new UBS Evidence Lab survey on outbound travel, UBS Singapore Research (link) estimates that up to S\$2.5bn of retail sales leakage is possible in 2026, when the rail link between Johor and Singapore is completed. Travel interest is underpinned by a meaningful cost differential, whereby F&B could be cheaper by 14-71% in JB when compared to Singapore, and 36-75% in the case of medical services. This is akin to the cost differential we have seen in Hong Kong versus Shenzhen. We do not expect a similar decline of retail spending. vs. Hong Kong, because of key structural differences including a different economic structure, and the RTS being less of a game-changing infrastructure improvement compared to HK's high-speed rail. However, the RTS completion will likely increase the structural competitive headwinds on retailers' pricing power, in our view.

## Lower trend growth = lower neutral S\$NEER slope

Singapore's trend growth rate has halved from an average of 6% p.a since 2010, to around 3% by the end of 2023. In our view, this mirrors slowing global growth from an average of 7% p.a in 2010 to about 4% by 2023, which directly translates to a smaller beta for an economy that is highly levered to global demand. Moreover, at higher GDP per capita levels, the room for catch-up effects arising from industry shifts to higher value added sectors has diminished. This means the relative productivity growth differentials between the highly competitive external sector and non-tradable domestic sector have narrowed and will narrow further, meaning a lower rate equilibrium REER appreciation. (Figure 27)

That said, we are not taking a fatalistic view of Singapore's growth. Patterns of city agglomeration and growth fundamentally reflect economies of scale and proximity. In our Future of Cities Q-Series (link), we made the point that land availability and usage, and not labour per se, is the main constraint to Singapore's growth. (Figure 26) Singapore's total factor productivity gains (TFP) have correlated positively with global GDP growth, and is not independent of labour force growth either. Should new initiatives such as the Johor-Singapore SEZ unlock potential complementary synergies and help Singapore gain scale, this could be a longer-term boost to Singapore's growth potential.

## The time is right for easing in January?

Based on the latest MAS Survey of Professional Forecasters in September (link), about 50% of respondents expect MAS to ease policy by reducing the slope in January, whilst 35% expect MAS to ease in April. We take the view that while S\$NEER levels are not fundamentally restrictive at this juncture, the conditions for easing the slope to a neutral S\$NEER appreciation pace would have been fulfilled by early 2025. By then, the level shift from GST hikes would have washed out from the y/y comparison, and core inflation would dip to just 1.7% in Q1 2025, close to its long-term average. We expect MAS Core Inflation to be 1.9% in 2025, compared to 2.8% in 2024.

## 1. Faster than expected decline in underlying inflation is the key signal

As of July, core inflation has cooled at a rapid pace to just 1.4% annualized on a 6m seasonally adjusted basis. To be fair, three months of data is insufficient to conclude that inflationary pressures have dissipated. A false start emerged last September, when the 6m average sequential run-rate of core inflation was also weak (1.5% p.a.), before picking up sharply in 4Q 2023. That said, the difference is that most items in the CPI are now showing weaker than expected trends - i.e. the disinflationary pressures are broad based.

## 2. Declining upside risks to imported inflation, more clarity post US elections

External inflation risks have also eased with China's weak PPI inflation imparting a global disinflationary impulse, and oil prices falling more rapidly in the past few months, with Brent oil futures at just US\$71 as of September. Singapore's goods inflation has been easing faster than services inflation. Global inflation is expected to cool from 5.8% in 2024 to 3.7% in 2025. UBS expects global growth to slow from 3.1% in 2024 to 2.9% in 2025, assuming a US soft landing in our base case. That said, we expect MAS to remain on guard against potential stagflationary shocks. The most important precondition for MAS easing is a stable outlook for commodity prices and global inflation, and more clarity on that would perhaps emerge post US elections.

## 3. The time is right: Core inflation finally back at 2% next year

There is no rush to ease in October. First, easing policy when inflation on a y/y basis still high, without an ostensible trigger from a negative output gap or weaker global growth, runs counter to the anchoring of inflation expectations which remain elevated. Our core inflation forecast for 2025 is 1.9% (below consensus forecast of 2.0%), and our CPI-All Items inflation forecast is 1.9% as well. The profile of quarterly core inflation is likely to dip to 1.7% in H1 2025, before an uptick to 2.1% in H2 2025. We assume oil prices remain at US\$75 for the rest of 2024 and 2025.

## Why not get ahead of the curve?

We highlight a few caveats to our call for easing in January. The circumstances for a reduction in the policy band are not typical of previous MAS easing episodes. The last time MAS had set the S\$NEER path at 1% p.a. (UBSe) in 2018, global inflation was also at around 2.1%, but domestic inflation was running at a lower 1.7%. In addition, even if MAS eases in January, it may not necessarily attribute its decision to a normalization to a neutral stance. The risks of some sort of policy miscommunication could be higher if so. Because 'neutral' is time varying, i.e. the extent of policy restrictiveness depends on the state of the economy, there is a possibility that what is cyclically neutral might be mistaken for what is structurally neutral. Rather, we think MAS would more likely characterize a decision to ease as congruent with a downward shift in the balance of risks to growth and inflation.

## #1: No trigger to ease, unlike previous easing episodes

Over the past twenty years, MAS has eased in 2009, 2011, 2015, 2019, and 2020. Each of those times was associated with either a recession (2009, 2020), significant weakness in the inflation outlook (2015), or a spike in global uncertainty (in 2011 due to the Eurozone sovereign debt crisis). Easing as a form of 'normalization' is rare, with 2019 perhaps being one of the more calibrated cases for easing when growth expected to be below potential but Singapore was not experiencing a recession.

## #3: S\$NEER slope, not the S\$NEER level, is restrictive

With the last recentering being two years ago in October 2022, and the S\$NEER appreciating at a modest 1.5% p.a over the past two years, it is unlikely that MAS views the S\$NEER level as having veered too far off fundamentals or extremely misaligned. Given that MAS has increased the frequency of its policy meetings every quarter, we think that setting policy can be a bit more data dependent than forecast dependent, ie. MAS can afford to wait. The analysis we did above suggests that the exchange rate might not be as restrictive as presumed, in terms of dampening demand for domestic goods and services vs. foreign substitutes.

## #3: Labour market is still at full employment, wage growth is solid

The labour market remains at full employment with a sub 2% total unemployment rate, and the outlook for hiring has in fact improved based on recent business surveys. Wage growth was 4.1% y/y in Q2, close to the 66th percentile of 10Y history. Lower interest rates are likely to be supportive of financial market activity and interest rate sensitive parts of the economy, including property transactions. We highlight the risk that the disinflationary trend reverses due to an unexpected quickening of demand side pressures, which would delay MAS' policy normalization.
