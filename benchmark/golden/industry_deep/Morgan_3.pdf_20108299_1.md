# Sunny with a Chance of Rain

The sunny macro environment we expect is good for risk assets in 2H24: A clearer path of disinflation, growth, and policy over the next few months sets up a constructive outlook across markets. But beyond year-end, many uncertainties drive a wider-than-normal bull versus bear skew, reflecting a greater range of potential outcomes over the next 12 months.

Global equities – prefer Japan and Europe: Attractive valuations and resilient earnings revisions support a relative preference for Japan and Europe. In the US, we forecast robust EPS growth alongside modest multiple compression, typical of a mid-to-late-cycle environment. Stay selective in EM – India and Semiconductors and Technology Hardware in Korea and Taiwan stand out.

G10 rates – a story of two halves: With eight of the G10 central banks forecast to start lowering policy rates ahead of the US election, we expect most investors in government bonds to close underweight duration positions. The set-up of cooling inflation and an optical rise in the unemployment rate is conducive to driving both lower rate expectations and term premiums.

G10 FX – carry domination: DXY rises given its bias to low yielders. Risk currencies outperform funders, aided by falling global rates. AUD leads the dollar bloc while GBP outperforms SEK in Europe.

EM fixed income – bond bonanza: Bond yields head down across the board, driven by USTs and benign EM inflation. We expect credit outperformance versus local bonds. FX should be the weakest as USD strength and US election risks feed through. We like CLP, PLN, and INR in EM FX and HUF, INR, and MXN in local bonds.

Corporate credit – in the zone: The fundamental and technical backdrop for credit remains much better than average, and supports tighter-than-average spreads. Loans offer the best risk/reward in US and Europe. We think that the US cash curve steepens.

Securitized products – stay bullish: Against the backdrop of lower tail risk, we expect agency MBS to lead the way given attractive relative valuations and correlations with lower volatility. Our highest-conviction call is long US CLO equity.

Commodities – selectively bullish in metals with a tactical bias in Brent: Toward year-end, good demand and a tightening balance drive copper, while tailwinds from falling rates and strong physical demand keep gold supported, and summer tightness lifts Brent prices.

## Key investment ideas

• We expect 2H24 to be a good environment for risk assets under our economists' forecasts for moderate growth, disinflation, and rate cuts. Fair fundamentals and supportive technicals act as additional tailwinds.

• The outlook is clouded by uncertainties in 1H25 given hard-to-model events (e.g., US elections). This is reflected in the unusually wide gap between bull and bear cases in our strategists' forecasts.

• The 'CCC's that matter - carry, convexity, and cheap optionality should remain key themes across stocks and bonds, providing a hedge against known unknowns in 2025.

• OW in global equities, OW in spread products (eg. agency MBS, loans) ; EW in commodities, UW in cash.

The sunny macro environment we expect should be good for risk assets in 2H24: While the first half of this year saw markets whipsaw as investors priced in and out soft landing, hard landing, and no landing scenarios with every data surprise, the second half should see the paths of disinflation, growth, and policy become clearer, setting up a constructive outlook for nearly all markets. However, what happens beyond that horizon is clouded by a wide array of uncertainties, and carry, quality, and convexity return to being key themes heading into 1H25.

## Easy does it for growth, inflation, and policy

In our 2024 Outlook, we argued that growth would be moderate and inflation would decline across DM economies. What caught us – and the markets – by surprise was that US growth has been more robust than we had expected, driven by immigration flows, and inflation across most countries has been stubbornly stickier than what we had pencilled in.

That said, the narrative of macro normalization and policy easing is intact, just delayed. Our economists see global growth steady at just over 3%Y, and inflation to fall from 2.4%Y in 2024 to 2.2%Y at end-2025. There is some regional divergence – growth recovery in export-led economies like India and China should offset slower growth in the US, but the lingering supply-side effects of immigration mean that US growth can still be robust even as core PCE decelerates from 2.7% 4Q/4Q in 2024 to 2.1% 4Q/4Q in 2025. This sets up an environment where central banks can ease toward neutral – we see the Fed starting to cut in September, the ECB and the BoE starting in June, with all expecting to cut by 75bp+ by the end of the year.

## Fair weather for fundamentals and flows…but not-so-fair valuations

Against this backdrop of good global growth and decent disinflation, fundamentals should be favorable. Our equity strategists are forecasting 2025 US earnings growth of \~13%, while our colleagues in Europe and Japan see higher earnings revision breadth sustaining those regions' bull markets. Consensus EBITDA numbers would suggest that US IG corporate can de-lever into 4Q24. Low-risk EM HY fundamentals should hold up.

Technicals should also be supportive for many assets, or at least not be a headwind: On the supply side, the US corporates pipeline has been unusually strong year to date, but we think that it's front-loaded. US fixed income should see renewed flows from Japanese investors as FX-hedging costs come down over the next six months. Bank demand for some spread products should pick up in 2H as the Basel 3 Endgame rules are finalized. And there's still \~US\$6 trillion in money market funds that will likely rotate into other assets – high-quality fixed income and equities likely in that order – as the Fed starts cutting.

## Maps or mirages? The mid-1990s and 2019 are useful references, not guides

The current backdrop is supportive for risk assets and past periods offer more bullish possibilities: Our economists noted that the patterns we're seeing now/we expect have parallels to what happened in the mid-1990s, when the Fed cut in small increments, US growth was sustained at high levels, and the labor market was strong. And between 1995 and the end of 1999, the ACWI rallied by 16% per annum, the Global AGG returned \~7% per year, and the broad commodities total returns index went up \~5% per year. If this is what awaits markets, then we should all be partying like it's the 1990s!

Another episode that our strategists have pointed to is 2019, when the Fed cut multiple times even as growth was good. This allowed global equities to rally by 30%, UST 10-year yields fell by \~80bp, HY spreads tightened by \~200bp, and VIX fell by 10 points. Again, an extremely bullish outlook if that's the playbook.

However, we think that these historical episodes are useful references, but not perfect analogies: In the mid-1990s, the macro backdrop looks similar to today but the market structure was materially different. For example, IG credit used to be dominated by buy and hold investors less concerned about daily liquidity and mark-to-market volatility. Today, there is a notably higher share of investors who require daily liquidity (mutual funds and ETFs) and for whom mark-to-market changes are a major concern and thus demand additional baseline compensation for liquidity/volatility (not just defaults); this suggests a higher floor for credit spreads relative to the mid-1990s.

Valuations also started off during that period at much cheaper levels than today. And the 2019 rally in risk assets was as much a function of the fact that there had been a rolling bear market through 2018 as it was about Fed policy.

While we're unlikely to reach those heady highs seen in 1990s and 2019, we'd also push back on the view that in an environment of rate cuts and falling yields equities can't perform well because the former usually maps to growth slowdowns: Exhibit 9 shows the annual performance of global stocks and UST 10-year every year going back to 1988, and the fed funds change we saw in those years. In the 13 times when the Fed cut rates over the course of the year, bond yields were lower and equities were up 43% of the time. And in those periods, stock returns averaged 18% while yields fell 100bp+.

It is important to recognize that policy rate cuts now, particularly in the US, are not to stimulate a sagging economy but to move toward a more normalized policy and reduce excessive restrictions in the economy. Good macro, fair fundamentals, valuations, and technicals, rate cut cycles and 1990s/2019 as framing – if not the exact playbook – all make for a great environment for risk assets, a reason for us to get more bullish on European and Japanese equities, leveraged loans, CLO equity, etc. But there are some strong caveats.

## Low visibility ahead – we're certain that there are uncertainties, but uncertain about how to quantify them

It's true that every cycle is different, but we'd argue that the current set-up is unusually uncertain. The next 12 months are filled with events that are: 1) Hard to model outcomes for (e.g., US elections); 2) Hard to model the consequences of the outcomes (e.g., resolution to ongoing conflicts or the prospects for fiscal policy changes following the US elections); or 3) Hard to model because long-standing relationships between economic and market variables have vanished in the post-pandemic world (e.g., household formation, locked-in and priced-out effects in housing).

This explains why our strategists' latest forecasts have an unusually wide gap between their bull and bear cases for bond and equity markets. While a bullish scenario where we retread the mid-1990s or 2019 is definitely possible – and arguably, the path to that outcome is a lot less narrower than to a bearish outcome – we have higher conviction that the positive returns forecasts apply to 2H24 rather than beyond.

## 2) Convexity as a hedge against known unknowns and unknown unknowns in 1H25

With a wide band of uncertainty beyond year-end, look for markets which give cheap optionality for bull and bear scenarios. MSCI Europe's \~25% revenue exposure to the US and \~30% exposure to EM mean that the market can benefit from hotter growth in either region, but at much cheaper valuations than the S&P 500, and much lower volatility than owning MSCI EM outright. Japanese and Indian equities and Taiwan and Korean Semis all have good secular bull stories that favorable risk-on sentiment can add fuel to, but can also limit downside should global growth slow.

Credit and most spread products also fit the bill of an asset with good convexity: We especially like the positive carry feature for a majority of spread products – corporate credit (both public and private), securitized credit, agency MBS, and emerging market sovereign credit. For agency MBS, if rates vol normalizes, spreads can tighten materially; if rates vol stays elevated, spreads are already at the wide end of a range. For corporate credit, if global growth turns out to be more robust than our economists' baseline, credit spreads – already close to prior cycles' tights – can go lower regardless of higher inflation; should growth grind to a halt, credit total returns can benefit from duration.

## 3) Carry, an umbrella

What helps with convexity is good carry – another theme we think would be increasingly important in 2H. USD and EUR high grade bond yields are close to 10-year+ highs – yields would need to rise by \~77-85bp before total returns go negative, which is not impossible, but unlikely against the moderate growth, disinflation backdrop our economists are painting. For a USD-based investor, FX-hedged dividend yields of Europe and Japan at 4%+ are above the historical average, and attractive. Rain or shine, carry can be your friend.

## US elections: Public policy strategy

## Ariana Salvatore

The 2024 elections introduce an element of uncertainty to the outlook, though impacts from policy changes likely will not matter to markets until 2026. Key variables that could have distinct macro impacts are trade policy (tariffs), immigration, and tax policy, all of which will be contingent upon the election outcome. Based on current candidate proposals, we expect the following:

Trade policy: As we've written, we expect President Biden to maintain relatively status quo trade policies should he win a second term. This stands in contrast to former President Trump's statements on the campaign trail, which suggest that he would seek to implement either strategic or broad-based tariffs. These changes can be implemented with relatively more discretion at the executive level, as we note the potential for broader trade frictions in our recent note here.

Immigration: Although immigration tends to be one of the most difficult policy areas in which to enact legislative change, we expect that these significant levels of voter dissatisfaction with the current state of play as well as both candidates' emphasis on the importance of the issue could provide an incentive for lawmakers to seek policy reform.

We note that there is scope for executive authority with respect to immigration policy, although several executive actions that were attempted during former President Trump's first term were challenged or blocked by the courts, including limiting entry points for asylum seekers and revoking temporary protected status for certain classes of migrants. Reports indicate that the former president is seeking to employ other avenues as well, which could include actions like Section 1182f of US code, which allows the president to "suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or non-immigrants" if entry would be "detrimental to the interests of the United States." President Trump utilized this authority in his first term and, although challenged, it was ultimately upheld by the Supreme Court, which interpreted the Section 1182f authority as granting the president broad power to suspend entry of migrants into the country.

Taxes: Given the diverging policy preferences of lawmakers, we would expect legislation in a Republican sweep scenario to reflect the House-passed Ways & Means tax bill (the American Families & Jobs Act) to effectively extend most of the TCJA (both individual & corporate), while a Democratic sweep scenario might see more focus on the individual tax measures and a few corporate provisions. Specifically, we would expect Democratic lawmakers in the latter scenario to pursue an enhanced Child Tax Credit and perhaps extension of some individual tax breaks, likely offset by tax hikes on higher-income individuals as well as allowing some TCJA provisions to roll off. In a divided government outcome, we would expect a narrower extension of some TCJA provisions relative to the Republican sweep outcome, assuming lawmakers can find agreement on some corporate extensions (a Republican priority) if paired with some incremental spending items like the enhanced Child Tax Credit, based on lawmaker comments around these issues.

Our economists lay out two potential shocks over the next 12 months – a scenario where US inflation stays higher for longer, and one where Chinese growth is stronger than expected. Our strategists' forecasts are informed by these views, but we also explore scenarios which we think can be bullish or bearish for risk assets.

## The bull case for risk assets – better than Goldilocks, with no bears in sight

Immigration to the US is stronger than expected again, lifting growth much higher with little inflation impact. The Fed starts cutting through 2025 as disinflation continues. Lower US yields and contained energy prices mean that the RoW can ease policy without the risk of higher inflation. The Chinese economy recovers strongly, supporting EM and Asian economies. Just the right amount of 'hot' growth, and inflation and policy rates being on a predictable downward path, creates a good environment for risk assets.

## The bear case for risk assets – in a tight spot

The impact of restrictive policy over the last two years finally hit the US and RoW after a long lag, but inflation in the US remains elevated as components of inflation – like shelter inflation – being 'sticky' turns out to be less than transitory. Central banks keep rates higher for longer and financial conditions globally become tighter. Consumption and investment fall, the job market slackens, and exports in Europe and EM take a hit. Elections taking place across various countries in 2024 mean that substantive fiscal easing won't be implemented until 2025. A growth slowdown in DM weighs more on China's recovery and it falls further into a debt-deflation loop, with spillovers into other Asian economies. The global economy falls into a recession within the next 12 months.

## US: A wider range of potential outcomes

Since the beginning of 2023, the consensus view on the macro backdrop has bounced between hard landing, soft landing, and no landing outcomes. Rotations under the surface of the market have reflected this frequently shifting view, with defensives, growth, and cyclicals all seeing periods of outperformance over this time horizon. The last couple of months have been a microcosm in this respect as economic growth data have once again cooled after a period of strength, while inflation data have been bumpy. In short, macro outcomes have become increasingly hard to predict as data have become more volatile. We see this environment persisting – a view that’s reflected in both our bull versus bear case skew (which is wider than normal) and our sector/style recommendations (more on this below).

In the base case, we forecast a 19x P/E multiple on 12-month forward EPS (June 2026) of US\$283, which equates to a 5,400 forward 12-month price target: Our 2024 and 2025 earnings growth forecasts (8% and 13%, respectively) assume healthy, mid-singledigit top-line growth in addition to margin expansion in both years as positive operating leverage resumes (particularly in 2025). Modest valuation compression (from \~20x to \~19x in the base case) as earnings adjust higher is typical in a mid-to-late-cycle backdrop (occurred in the mid-1990s, mid-2000s, and 2018 most recently). Normalization in the market multiple is also a function of a higher risk premium (lower rates are a partial offset), which reflects uncertainty around a wider range of potential outcomes as discussed previously. On this front, our bull (6,350) and bear (4,200) cases represent \~20% upside and downside potential versus the current index level, respectively. Our bull case reflects a strong (11-15%) EPS growth environment out to 2026 alongside multiple expansion to \~21x. Our bear case incorporates a hard landing next year (negative EPS growth and multiple compression).

In our view, the uncertain backdrop we face warrants an investment approach that can work as market pricing and sector/factor leadership bounce between potential outcomes. On this front, we recommend a barbell of quality cyclicals (which we see outperforming in a 'no landing' scenario) and quality growth (the relative winner in a 'soft landing scenario, in our view). We upgrade Industrials to overweight given improving earnings revisions and structural drivers and believe recent underperformance offers an attractive entry point. We also maintain long exposure to certain defensive areas to hedge against the risk of slowing growth – Consumer Staples and Utilities, in particular. Finally, large caps should continue to outperform small caps given stronger earnings revisions, more durable margin profiles, and healthier balance sheets.

## Europe: In the sweet spot

We stay bullish on European equities, fully incorporating our mid-1990s playbook and rolling forward our targets to June 2025. This leads us to raise our MSCI Europe target to 2,500 (18% potential upside) from 2,230 previously. Potential total return upside – with dividends and buybacks – rises to 23%.

We believe that European equities are in the sweet spot over 2H24, where a continued re-rating in line with our mid-1990s soft landing playbook meets the early stages of a European earnings growth recovery. We expect further re-rating to 14.8x (versus 13.8x currently) by year-end. Meanwhile, Europe’s earnings revision breadth has turned sharply higher, recently breaking into positive territory, in line with our earnings model. Our model projects 7.5% EPS growth by year-end versus consensus at 4%. By the turn of the year, we expect the re-rating to run out of steam and the baton to fully pass to earnings to sustain European equities’ momentum. We are only 2.5% above consensus for 2025 EPS, but we see upside front-loaded in the year.

Thematic tailwinds: European equities benefit from rising corporate confidence, an M&A cycle recovery which is leading the global trend, improving economic growth, the start to the rate-cutting cycle ahead of the Fed, high and rising capital distributions including an increasing share of buybacks, and underappreciated AI diffusion. We note that over 60% of MSCI Europe’s market cap-weighted revenue exposure is outside Europe, with the largest exposure to the US.

Sector and style preferences: We believe a recovery in bond yield-sensitive stocks has begun, as expected at this stage in our mid-1990s playbook. We expect this rally to be tactical but more powerful than the similar ‘Santa Claus’ rally from late 2023. We recently upgraded Building & Construction to overweight to play this rotation. Otherwise, we continue to prefer Europe’s quality growth sectors including Software, Aerospace & Defence, Pharma, and Semis, along with Banks. “What do rates mean for USD?” It depends: As our four-regime USD framework demonstrates, changes in US interest rates impact USD based on why they’re moving rather than their directionality. But in this case, depending on how we look at rates, we get very different answers.

US rates in isolation paint a mixed picture. On the one hand, rates are likely falling for benign disinflationary reasons and our rates forecasts are below market-implied forwards (USD-negative). On the other, rates are likely still elevated versus history and our forecasts are above consensus forecasts (USD-positive).

Yet, if we look at rate differentials, we get a clear USD-positive message: rate differentials are slated to widen in absolute terms and relative to market forwards and consensus as RoW yields fall faster than US peers.

So which matters more? Since 2023, changes in US rates in isolation have done a remarkable job of explaining USD changes whereas rate differentials have fallen a bit to the wayside. It's not to say that rate differentials are not important; rather it's because they haven't been moving much, thanks to monetary policy largely easing, and then tightening rapidly, in tandem.

Thus, despite the significant volatility in interest rates over the past few years, front-end rate differentials have been remarkably stable, remaining in a 100bp range for more than two years and effectively unchanged on net.

We think that rate differentials will return to the driver's seat as policy divergence becomes an increasingly important theme: While we expect central banks to begin cutting this year, the pace of cuts and ultimate destinations (terminal rates) are likely to vary widely, particularly between the US and the RoW. Indeed, on a DXY-weighted basis (excluding SEK and CHF, which we don't forecast), our rate forecasts imply that 2-year rate differentials will be 41bp wider relative to consensus forecasts and 30bp wider compared to market-implied forwards ( Exhibit 47 ).

Year to date, investors have become increasingly skeptical of the Fed's ability to cut rates this year due to strong growth and stickier-than-expected inflation. This increased apprehension means that, even as disinflation confirms the cutting cycle (as we expect), investors may prove reluctant to price in a meaningful cutting cycle.

In our 2024 Outlook, we said "the ideal environment for credit is a world of wide spreads, modest growth, low issuance, healthy balance sheets, steady or modestly easing monetary policy, and cautious management teams. Credit is an asset class vulnerable to extremes. Weak growth raises the risk of default that lenders are exposed to. Strong growth leads to corporate aggression that rarely works in a lender's favor. For credit, moderation is ideal. Looking at US credit excess returns from 1948-present (that is, returns versus Treasuries), credit does best when growth is 1-2%Y. It has done worse when growth is much weaker or much stronger.

Moderation is exactly what Morgan Stanley economists expect: We forecast \~1%Y growth in Europe and \~2%Y growth in the US, right in that 'credit-optimal' zone. We see moderating inflation and moderating policy rates. For credit, we continue to believe that the fact that central banks can ease (if needed) over the next 12 months matters more than exactly when they start or how far they go. ## Where could we be wrong?

Any outlook partly predicated on the view that tail risks won't materialize has a pretty obvious hole in its argument. Given our bullish outlook, we'll note that risks to the call are more skewed toward underperformance than outperformance. While clearly geopolitical concerns or a recession would impact spread sectors negatively, we're more focused on idiosyncratic risks. Specifically, we are worried about performance in the scenarios where the market prices in a higher chance of the Fed hiking. A higher-for-longer rate path would likely delay bank demand for MBS, see FI outflows, cause FX hedging costs to stay elevated for longer, increase concern around interest coverage ratios, and increase reinvestment risk for CRE. The bull scenarios are mostly related to more accommodation in a soft landing scenario, particularly if asset classes are buttressed by inflows.

## Energy

## Crude oil: Long-term anchor lies around US\$80/bbl, but summer tightness could drive Brent to US\$90

2024 has been something of a roller-coaster so far. At the start of the year, the consensus expectation in the oil market was for demand growth to slow down quite severely. With the post-Covid recovery essentially complete, demand growth would revert back to its long-term trend rate, which implied a slowdown. In addition, there were good reasons to expect that the future trend rate of growth would be lower than the historical rate.

With lower demand growth, and non-OPEC supply growing surprisingly strongly, OPEC would need to keep production restrained. OPEC cuts can balance the market, but this also means that OPEC loses market share and builds up spare capacity. At the start of the year, the market's fear was that OPEC members would either be unwilling or unable to coordinate the required cuts. Expecting a looming surplus, oil prices fell steadily late last year, which only came to a halt when WTI reached \~US\$70/bbl, a level where roughly 25% of US shale wells are no longer 'in the money'.

Since then, two things have surprised: first, demand has come in stronger than expected. Most forecasters have raised estimates for full-year demand growth by \~0.3 mb/d, with most estimates now clustered around 1.5 mb/d growth. Intriguingly, this is actually above the historical trend rate of 1.3-1.4 mb/d. Second, OPEC has extended its production agreement, and continued to restrain production. Compliance with production quota by individual members is not perfect, but it never is.

We expect that OPEC will extend its current production agreement at its upcoming meeting on June 1. Combined with the seasonal rebound in demand, which is typical between April and September, this hints at a period of tightness in the oil market over the summer. From June onwards, we expect global oil inventories to draw, possibly quite significantly so. In turn, this puts upward pressure on oil prices, with a return of Brent prices to US\$90/bbl a likelihood by late 3Q, in our view.

This implies meaningful upside from here, although we do not expect these prices to hold over the medium term. Our preliminary balances for 2025 again show non-OPEC supply meeting all global demand growth, leaving little room for OPEC to unwind production cuts. Later this year and into 2025, we expect Brent to revert back steadily to its longterm anchor, which we estimate lies around US\$80/bbl.

Asia-Pacific/EM quant factors – stay neutral between Value and Growth in APxJ/EM over 3Q, and prefer Value in Japan: In APxJ/EM, our model has shifted to classify the market regime to be in the late-cycle phase from April onward, meaning that market volatility and price actions are dominated more by flows and risk sentiment, while macro factors would tend to play a smaller role in driving style rotation. We continue to stay neutral on Value versus Growth in APxJ/EM given that the market is postponing the first Fed rate cut, and our historical analysis also suggests a marginal relative return of Value versus Growth in a Fed pivot year. The recent outperformance of Chinese Growth/Tech stocks amid rising UST 10-year yields was a good example of how flows could drive factor performance to go in the opposite direction of their long-term sensitivity with macro dynamics. Our model estimates a low conviction to go with Value or Growth now, at least in 3Q. We prefer to re-engage with Quality and Earnings Revisions to rebuild Momentum exposure following the price reversal trading over last three months.

In Japan, we continue to prefer Value as JGB bond yields are unlikely to go lower substantially, while the JPY volatility curve continues to turn to backwardation frequently. These are the signs of a moderate tightening of financial conditions in the market, which favors Value to outperform Growth per our model estimation. Still, the risk to our call here is a stronger-than-expected JPY outlook due to BoJ intervention.

This mid-year outlook involved intense discussions with the entire Morgan Stanley global economics and strategy teams. Here are some key issues we debated:

The Fed and US inflation – the path ahead: Despite the substantial reflation realized in 1Q24, our US economists have a strong conviction that significant disinflation is ahead, which in turn leads them to the narrative of three 25bp rate cuts in 2024 and four more in 2025. Given the many changes to market pricing of rate cuts year to date, driven by higher-than-expected inflation, the path ahead for inflation was heavily debated. Our economists argued that the acceleration in goods and financial services prices, which explains a substantial portion of the 1Q24 reflation, should decelerate from here. Furthermore, leading indicators point to weaker shelter inflation ahead. Finally, their analysis shows that residual seasonality contributed to the unexpected strength in 1Q24 inflation, suggesting a payback in 2H24.

Uncertainties galore: Crucial elections upcoming in several countries including the US and heightened geopolitical tensions point to a wide range of outcomes and a wider range of the consequences of those outcomes. Add to this mix the notion that several longstanding relationships between economic and market variables have vanished in the postpandemic world, jolted by aggressive policy moves in either direction and the response by households and corporations. The combination of these uncertainties results in substantial divergence between our bull and bear expectations across several asset classes, much more divergent than at any time in the last decade of our crystal ball gazing.

The China question: Relative to our expectations laid out in our 2024 Outlook, China's growth has surprised to the upside. Recovery in exports and modest supply-centric policy have led to higher-than-target real GDP growth in 1Q24. While there are a few policy positives on housing and green products, mitigating the debt-deflation spiral, growth remains unbalanced and subpar, even if credit growth and inflation stabilize. We debated our cautious stance on China equity markets against this backdrop and concluded that for China an equities market recovery is still challenging. We think that consensus mid-teens EPS growth expectations for this year and next are likely to be disappointed given our sub-5%Y nominal GDP growth forecast.

Why not more bullish on credit: While our credit strategists are constructive on corporate credit, given the combination of favorable technicals and solid fundamentals and a relatively benign economic outlook, we debated why the outlook for spreads is not tighter, considering that the set-up for corporate credit has elements similar to those during the mid-1990s when US IG index spreads were about 30bp tighter versus the current spread targets. Our strategists highlighted the significant differences between the market structure, index composition, and the duration of the underlying bonds today versus the 1990s. They noted the much greater daily liquidity demands from mutual funds and ETFs – investors with greater mark-to-market sensitivity – require additional

compensation for liquidity/volatility (not just defaults), which puts a higher floor on spreads. Furthermore, they noted that higher rates for longer create a tough set-up for very levered balance sheets. The tail names/distressed cohort need both good growth and much lower funding costs to become viable, which limits their exuberance toward the low end of the credit spectrum.

Interest rate volatility: Our constructive view on agency mortgages is in part based on the expectation of falling rate volatility. While rate volatility has declined in recent weeks from local highs, relative to long-term history it is still elevated. While uncertainty related to elections can certainly heighten interest rate volatility, it is also likely that when the market reaches a high degree of certainty about the timing of the Fed's rate cuts, interest rate volatility would subside. Thus, the debate about the trajectory of rate volatility and the consistency with our investment recommendations boils down to a question of timing and perhaps the volatility surface. Taking into account the 12-month investment horizon in conjunction with current valuations and the expected demand-supply dynamics, a constructive view on agency mortgages seemed a high-conviction recommendation, despite some of the divergent views regarding the trajectory of interest rate volatility in the group.

## Disclosure Section

Mortgage Backed Securities (MBS) and Collateralized Mortgage Obligations (CMO)

Principal is returned on a monthly basis over the life of the security. Principal prepayment can significantly affect the monthly income stream and the maturity of any type of MBS, including standard MBS, CMOs and Lottery Bonds. Yields and average lives are estimated based on prepayment assumptions and are subject to change based on actual prepayment of the mortgages in the underlying pools. The level of predictability of an MBS/CMO's average life, and its market price, depends on the type of MBS/CMO class purchased and interest rate movements. In general, as interest rates fall, prepayment speeds are likely to increase, thus shortening the MBS/CMO's average life and likely causing its market price to rise. Conversely, as interest rates rise, prepayment speeds are likely to decrease, thus lengthening average life and likely causing the MBS/CMO's market price to fall. Some MBS/CMOs may have "original issue discount" (OID). OID occurs if the MBS/CMO’s original issue price is below its stated redemption price at maturity, and results in "imputed interest" that must be reported annually for tax purposes, resulting in a tax liability even though interest was not received. Investors are urged to consult their tax advisors for more information. Government agency backing applies only to the face value of the CMO and not to any premium paid.
