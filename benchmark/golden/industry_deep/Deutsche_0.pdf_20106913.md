## Trump and markets

## Key market-moving themes this month:

## 1. Trump has become the defacto Republican nominee

➢ Markets and countries are now pricing in more expectations of potential Trump policies. The potential impact extends to the European defence sector and outsourcing destinations including India – where equity markets have outperformed.

## 2. Mitigating Big Tech equity risk

➢ Risks around the Magnificent 7 are too big to ignore but markets seem to be too complacent even in light of recent selloffs in key names. We take a look at ways to mitigate M-7 risk while trying to capture the upside from US large caps.

## 3. Dividends v treasuries

## Large cap concentration

➢ High-dividend US stocks have recently broken out of their range. That comes despite these stocks paying a dividend yield far below that of treasuries. We examine why capital appreciation is a bigger part of the draw.

## 4. Global large cap concentration

## Sector momentum

The lack of breadth in the recent global equity rally is well recognised but the spotlight remains largely on the S&P 500. We show that large-cap concentration is not a US-specific issue per se, but a number of US-only characteristics make it more worrisome.

## Cross asset summary

## 5. Sector momentum beyond tech

➢ Tech has been the top performing equity sector over the last year but this may be changing. We dissect what could be the first steps towards the greater importance of other industries and point to which ones may have the momentum to outperform.

1. Equities: Continue to like several markets in peripheral Europe on valuation / momentum scorecard relative to RoW EM markets. Japanese stocks face a BoJ test but tailwinds are strong. We are closely watching whether Chinese markets can extend their rebound.

➢ This week, the European Commission unveiled its defence industrial strategy. Among other things, it stipulates that by 2030:

• At least 50% of government defence budgets should be spent on products made in Europe. This is non-binding and is far from current levels of 22%

Macroeconomic fluctuations often account for most of the risks associated with exposures to instruments that promise to pay fixed or variable interest rates. The longer the maturity of a certain cash flow and the higher the move in the discount factor, the higher will be the loss. Upside surprises in inflation, fiscal funding needs, and FX depreciation rates are among the most common adverse macroeconomic shocks to receivers. But counterparty exposure, issuer creditworthiness, client segmentation, regulation (including changes in assets holding limits for different types of investors), changes in tax policies, currency convertibility (which may constrain currency conversion, repatriation of profits and/or liquidation of positions), and settlement issues related to local clearing houses are also important risk factors. The sensitivity of fixed-income instruments to macroeconomic shocks may be mitigated by indexing the contracted cash flows to inflation, to FX depreciation, or to specified interest rates – these are common in emerging markets. The choice of the proper fixing (or metric) is particularly important in swaps markets, where floating coupon rates (i.e., coupons indexed to a typically short-dated interest rate reference index) are exchanged for fixed coupons.
