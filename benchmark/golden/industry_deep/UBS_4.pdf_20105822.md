## Executive summary

## #1. Fed rate cuts and USD/Asia: What can we learn and (un)learn from history?

1. Global growth backdrop matters as much as, if not more than, the Fed rate cutting cycle. 2. Tech cycle is a positive, but China recovery is a necessary condition for a steep rebound. 3. ‘Precautionary’ US fixed income help is running out of steam. 4. Disinflation reprieve is stretched, risk premia even more. 5. Asian currencies’ role as a funder in 2022-23 is a welcome tailwind in '24. Target 2-3% backloaded spot returns in average EM Asia FX vs. USD; Risk-reward unattractive in Q1.

## #2. Which Asian central banks will cut after the Fed, and when?

UBS expects the average policy rate in EM Asia to decline by c.80bps, as Asian CBs follow the Fed amidst cooling CPI inflation, sub-trend growth. These cuts, which presume a mild US recession, are estimated to be largest in SK (150bps), ID (150bps) and PH (100bps), with 80-90% of forecast rate cuts materializing in 2H. Our analysis finds SG, HK front end rates stretched on the downside; about fair in IN/MY and marginally too high in SK.

## #3. Would China economic recovery lift regional assets in '24?

China’s real estate sector, amidst only a measured/targeted policy easing, pulled economic growth lower in 2023 for the third consecutive year. This risk remains elevated into '24, although the risk-reward in beaten up equity markets has improved; Property sales and PSL easing announcements, would be the key signposts to gauge any market sentiment inflections. We think any improvement in the surveys/data would be supportive for KRW, THB, TWD. In an exactly opposite outcome, we believe IDR is most at risk.

## #4. Which currencies are likely to outperform in ’24?

KRW, PHP, TWD are our most preferred currencies; CNY, IDR and SGD the least. We are tactically constructive INR as a dollar proxy.

## #5. How would regional elections in Taiwan/Indonesia/India/Korea impact markets?

2024 is a big election year globally with elections in IN, TW, ID and SK being key events in Asia. Taiwan’s elections have garnered most market interest with a focus on cross-strait relations. After a strong run in Q4 performance and positioning, TWD is exposed to a near-term correction. Polls suggest little risk of a surprise to PM Modi’s leadership in India. Indonesia’s three-way presidential race, with a focus on the policy continuity, would likely slow structural reforms implementation.

## #6. Will the tech cycle optimism lift KRW and TWD further in '24?

Tech cycle optimism lifted KRW, TWD’s performance in Q4, as tech duo's annual portfolio flows surged to highest since 2016. This stretchedness in performance/flows is at risk of a near-term correction, especially if NASDAQ (to which SK/TW are \~80% correlated) pulls back after posting its best year since 1999. Of the two, we prefer KRW at current valuations and amidst UBS expectations of a c.50% surge in DRAM prices in '24 and ongoing Korea’s BoP flows improvement. USDKRW 2024F: 1240.

## #7. How low can USD/CNH go with and without a recovery in domestic growth?

Despite optimistically leaning towards China internet stocks, we find RMB a weak link and are short CNY TWI targeting 94 on the CFETS basket by end 2024. Three reasons: 1) We find resident outflows risk elevated, acting as a source of sustained BoP flows weakness into 2024. 2) Demanding valuations, wherein RMB screens c.7% expensive on our preferred medium-term metrics. 3) Bleak exports outlook would potentially test policymakers’ stance of currency appreciation, as seen in 2H23.

## #8. How would IGBs' inclusion in EM bonds index impact bond markets and Indian rupee?

We expect IGBs’ inclusion in the GBI-EM index to attract c.25bn\$ of inflows in FY25. That’d be worth c.10% of annual net issuance of IGBs/SDLs, and c.40% of CA deficit. This sizable shock to BoP flows may not be enough to alter RBI’s unstated policy stance of FX stability (USDINR24F: 84);1y OTM digi puts screen as an attractive hedge to any change in this policy. In rates, we expect the downside pressure on yields to intensify in 2H, but see modest upside in short-end NDOIS in the near term.

## #9. Could KTBs extend their 4Q23 outperformance into '24?

KTBs rapid move in 4Q23 (a 5 S.D. change) brought forward '24 gains reducing the near-term risk reward. Nonetheless, unlike Oct-23, when markets were priced for almost a hike in '24, current market pricing of c.65bps cuts seems about fair to us on a probability adjusted basis. We remain structurally bullish for reasons discussed inside, but would recommend hedging against external risks. We would: a) hold hedges via 10y JGB futures, and b) switch KTB longs tenor from 10y to 3y.

## #10. THB recorded 15+% intra-year swings in 2022-23. Will '24 be any different?

2023 turned out to be one of the most volatile years for THB since '01. This volatility surge, catalyzed by USTs sell-off and borderline overall BoP flows, should ease in '24 likely pushing implied vols down by 2H. However, as the CA improves to only 2- 3% of GDP, baht is unlikely to revisit a one-way appreciation trend seen in 2017-19. Near term, we see risk of THB weakness between Feb-May, by when seasonality tends to be a headwind. Look to engage in ratio call spreads around late Jan.

## #1. Fed rate cuts and USD/Asia: Five lessons

UBS Asia FX views: Target backloaded 3% spot returns in average EM Asia FX vs. USD. KRW, PHP, TWD are our most preferred currencies; CNY, IDR and SGD the least. Near-term headwinds: With US soft-landing being largely priced in the front end rates, the next leg lower in rates would likely be catalyzed by bad news on growth. This growth deterioration would re-surface US recession concerns likely catalyzing a de-rating in DM equities/credit risk premia, to which EMFX tends to be highly sensitive. We prefer alpha over beta - Short CNH vs. KRW, Short SGD vs. PHP

1) Global growth backdrop matters as much as, if not more than, the Fed rate cutting cycles. In the charts overleaf, we compare how Asian currencies fared 12 months before and after the first Fed cut in the past 6 cutting cycles. Figure 1 shows that there is no clear relationship. For instance, Fed’s 475bps of rate cuts in 2001 saw a 4.5% decline in average Asian currencies (vs. USD), while relatively shallower cuts lifted average Asia FX by 6% between late 2007-1H08, and about unchanged over 75bp cuts of Q2-Q3 2019. The reason behind this divergence is the growth backdrop e.g. in 2001, global growth slowed dramatically with OECD's LEI declining by almost 10ppt over a year. 2006 was exactly opposite, and hence the Asia FX appreciation. Today's backdrop, amidst UBS’s expectations of 2024 global growth slowdown to the slowest level since the dot-com bubble (ex GFC, pandemic) is far from reassuring for cyclical assets like EM currencies (UBS24F 2.6% y/y vs. 3.2% in 23).

2) Tech cycle is positive, but a China rebound is a necessary condition for a steep rebound. UBS expects for a recovery in DRAM/ NAND flash prices over the coming quarters, supportive for KRW, TWD and at the margin relevant supply chains in the region. However, China’s sub-trend growth (UBS24F: 4.4% y/y) amidst an ongoing lukewarm/targeted policy easing response will keep a floor under USD/Asia. Partly because, a synchronized global growth slowdown would likely keep regional exports depressed around 0-5%y/y growth (even if better than 2023). And, partly because of expensive RMB valuations. As a reference, UBS’s top down growth-liquidity models suggest that in a scenario where US real yields decline by 100bps and regional exports growth is about zero in 2024, Asian currencies would only appreciate by 3% (Figure 4). In that context, rate cuts’ reprieve alone is not necessarily celebratory.

3) ‘Precautionary’ US fixed income help is running out of steam. FOMC’s ‘insurance’ messaging since late November has undoubtedly catalyzed a strong crossasset rally. Following this front loaded reprieve, however, the path for sustained fixed income help is narrower from here - at least in a ‘soft’ landing scenario. Three reasons: a) insurance cuts are now almost fully priced i.e. a 25bp cut at every meeting starting March. As a result, 2024 screens as one of the most anticipated easing cycles. e.g. the current market pricing of 200bps of rate cuts in coming two years compares with 15bp on average on the day of first Fed rate cut (Figure 8 ). b) 1y US breakeven is already pricing even lower inflation than UBS economists expect for the next 6-9m. c) With \~210bp of rate cuts priced over the next 2y, markets have already priced 70% of the policy rate easing that the Fed delivered historically (based on the last 6 cycles spanning 35 years), and this includes periods of deep recession.

4) Disinflation reprieve stretched, risk premia even more so. The rates rally led rerating, in our view, is now seemingly in advanced stages. We note that on three fronts: 1) 10y UST yields' 2 months change as of Nov-Dec is already most extreme since Nov-08 (i.e. after the GFC), 2) EM Asia (ex-China) equity portfolio inflows 'record' two month inflow in Nov-Dec has lifted 12m rolling equity flows in the vicinity of 2017 highs. 3) Our metric of risk premia, i.e. a simple average of US credit spreads, USD/Asia risk reversals, S&P’s extremity on fPE or even fPE (vs. bond yields), is extremely stretched vis-à-vis global growth momentum. Surely, the ‘precautionary’ monetary policy biases can help a further extension of such positions, but we do find the disinflationary reprieve is already in advanced stages - especially if the next mile on US yields decline is accompanied by weaker US consumption I.e. a bad news on US growth. These factors dim the path for a sustained risk rally returns.

5) Asian currencies’ role as a funder in 2022-23 is a welcome tailwind. Throughout 2022 and 2023, Asian currencies were global investors' preferred funders for carry trades, especially against high yielding EM currencies in LATAM and CEEMEA. Should Fed rate cuts realize as we expect in 2024, or/and FX vols rise, unwinding of carry positions could be a technical tailwind for regional currencies.

Asian currencies' sensitivity to Fed rate cuts is not independent of the manufacturing cycle. As such, UBS's forecast of a global growth deceleration in '24 would be a dampener.

UBS’s top down growth-liquidity model suggests that even if US real yields decline by 100bps and regional exports growth is about zero in 2024, Asian currencies would only appreciate by 2-3%.

The easy part of rates led re-rating is largely over, at least in a 'soft' landing scenario.

Q4 performance, record flows and our metrics of risk-premia suggest that the path for a sustained rally is dim.

Unwinding of 2022-23 carry trades - which were generally funded in APAC currencies - would be a tailwind in '24.

## #2. Which Asian central banks will cut after the Fed, and when?

Asian central banks to follow the Fed amidst cooling CPI inflation, sub-trend growth. Starting March, UBS expects the Fed to slash the policy rate at each FOMC meeting of '24. Although that’s broadly in line with the market pricing, UBS’s call for outsized rate cuts (>25bps) in 2H is motivated by our expectations of mild contraction of the economic activity in Q2/Q3. Nonetheless, a combination of at/below target CPI inflation and expectations of sub-trend growth in EM Asia would give leeway to the regional central banks to follow the Fed even in a scenario of less aggressive Fed cutting cycle of 125-150bps (market pricing) in 2024.

But, the cutting cycle will likely be quite shallow and backloaded. UBS economists expect the average policy rate in EM Asia to decline by c.80bps, led by rates cuts from SK (150bps), ID (150bps) and PH (100bps) central banks. Although that’s quite extreme vis-à-vis the market pricing, we’d note that a) the above mentioned rate cuts presume a mild US recession which ends up catalyzing much deeper rate cutting cycle (worth 275bps), and b) about 80-90% of our forecast rate cuts materialize in 2H. In the near term, we expect regional central banks to sit tight.

We expect yield compression in Asia to lag those in US and other HY EM... Having been anchored by relatively stable inflation, sub-par growth, and measured monetary policy tightening vis-à-vis RoW, EM Asia bonds outperformed EM LCY debt markets in 2021, 2022 and even during parts of 2023. ## #3. Would China recovery lift regional assets in '24?

China growth disappointed in '23 as the property sector recorded further weakness, offsetting the early year re-opening led optimism. Real estate investment contracted by c.10%y/y for second straight year, as the property sales contracted by 20%y/y (y/y of 3mma). In light of this sector’s macroeconomic significance (fiscal revenues included), its ongoing deterioration weighed on the overall economic activity. The consumer/business confidence remained depressed, while nominal GDP growth eased to \~4%y/y vis-à-vis \~11% growth in 2017-18. That said, the pace of sales contraction has recently eased from -60%3m/3m saar in Q2 to -15% as of Nov-23.

UBS expects slower growth in '24, with no big bang stimulus. In the context of above-mentioned economic activity weakness, authorities rolled out a number of policy easing announcements in '23, especially since the July Politburo. These announcements included fiscal easing including special LG bond issuances, rate cuts including lowered rates on existing mortgages, debt restructuring, macroprudential easing on the property sector including cuts in the down-payment ratio, and more recently announcement on the urban village renovation. Yet, the cumulative effect of these easing, when measured in terms of credit and fiscal impulse, screens pale vis-a-vis historical policy responses to the economic slowdown. UBS economists expect the growth stabilizing policy easing to continue but don’t expect a one-shot growth lifting big-bang stimulus. Accordingly, we forecast a 4.4%y/y real GDP growth in '24.

Nonetheless, the bar for positive surprises is now low.... There's little doubt that the market sentiment on China is quite downbeat with domestic stocks, after having recorded a 50% decline in the past three years, screening attractive on ERP metrics. Similarly, investors’ de-risking in the past couple of years reflects their cognizance of the structural headwinds facing China's economy. In this context, markets will be closely eyeing either further easing measures in the property sector, or a delayed/compounded reaction to 2023 easing in property sales or purchase intentions.

… especially if the property sales stop contracting. UBS equities analysts have recently turned less bearish on the property sector outlook for 2024/25 vis-à-vis 2022/23. They view the recently announced urban village renovations and social housing programmes as potential game-changers. Specifically, they expect a combination of demand lifting urban renovation (worth 5% of national new home sales), supply compressing social housing scheme and PBoC’s PSL scheme to break this sector’s negative feedback loop, with market’s excess inventory completely unwinding by mid-2025. That said, they aren't turning optimistic on near-term activity while looking for property sales, starts, completions and investment to respectively contract in 2024 by -7%, -7%, -3% and -7%y/y. When coupled with our expectations for a slower infrastructure investment growth this year of 5-6% y/y vs. 8-9% in ’23, we are less optimistic on China growth’s multiplier effects to the RoW.

## #5. Elections - India/Taiwan/Indonesia/Korea

2024 is deemed to be the biggest election year ever, with national elections in India, Taiwan and Indonesia, and legislative elections in South Korea being the key political events in Asia. UBS EM equity strategists and economists published a dummy’s guide to EM elections here. Below we highlight a summary of the APAC events.

## Taiwan Presidential Elections, January 13th

Market focus leading up to Taiwan elections is around cross-strait relations. As a background, Cross-strait tensions have escalated since 2016 elections, while the geopolitical risk premia attached to these tensions soared further in 2021-22. Hence key market focus into and after Jan-24 elections would be how the incoming government’s foreign policy shifts this narrative.

Three candidates have registered to run for the presidential election: Lai representing DPP, Hou representing KMT, and Ko representing TPP, while former Hon Hai chairman Terry Guo did not register. Per latest polls, the ruling DPP is holding the lead, followed by Hou (KMT) and Ko (TPP). The Economist discussed the three parties’ foreign policy stance here. Market generally expects improvements in cross-strait relations if the opposition wins.

For TWD, swings in equity portfolio flows will be the key driver of currencies' short term volatility in coming weeks and months. These flows, in our view, will be dominated by a combination of Fed rate expectations, confirmatory anecdotes of tech cycle recovery and geopolitics. These non-resident flows in the past couple of years were quite depressed, specifically until '22, with cumulative portfolio outflows of '75bn\$s between 2020-22 - c.22bn\$s more than Korea's. However, this started to shift in '23 as signs of tech cycle recovery firmed up and upside risks on US rates outlook eased. In fact, the latter - alongside swings in domestic elections polls/opposition alliance expectations lifted these flows to a record high in Nov/Dec (worth ann. 10% of GDP). A combination of these inflows, markets’ carry positioning (in Twd shorts) and insurance companies underhedged FX positions (i.e. long usds) helped TWD record its second best quarter in more than ten years as MSCI Taiwan also closed 2023 at pole position.

UBS View: We’ve highlighted TWD as our second most preferred currency into 2024 (mostly for macroeconomic reasons i.e. the tech cycle and TWD’s relative cheap valuations (More here), but would be wary of potential correction in 1H24 amidst the above mentioned event risk or/and a potential pushback by the FOMC to market expectations of steep rate cuts in the coming year. This is especially true as the FX risk premia (implied yields, risk reversals) in TWD also inched close to 10y lows. As such, we’d recommend investors wait for better entry levels, while tactically engaging in carry trades via structures that sell vol/riskies.

## India Elections, Apr-May 2024

The BJP government has been in power since 2014. With its right-leaning philosophy, general discipline on fiscal targets and recent focus on capex, policy continuity would be seen as a market friendly regime. Recent state elections and polls generally signal a continuity of this regime. For instance, Modi's BJP party fared better than expectations in the key state elections in November-December 2023 (BJP won three of the four important states), signalling good momentum for PM Modi heading into national elections in 2024. Put other ways, lack of a strong opposition to Prime Minister Modi's campaigns – which was also a defining feature of 2019 elections – could continue to be a key feature into 2024 elections.

The actual impact - positive or negative - from the elections could depend on the results. However, what can be tracked from history, irrespective of the election outcome, is the extent to which market behaviour changes around election dates vis-a-vis non-election periods. Our equity analysts note that the extent of the pick-up in market volatility, ahead of elections, has historically been high even though the recent elections have seen calmer markets even in election years. Typically the one month before and after election results announcements is when market volatility tends to be the highest.

## Indonesia Elections, 14-Feb-2024

President Jokowi has been in power since 2014, but because of the judicial law of maximum two terms he can’t contest in the 2024 elections. As such, key market focus into 2024 elections would be around the policy continuity in coming five years. The second key market focus into 2024 elections, especially from the perspective of business sentiment/government spending, would be if the Presidential elections process is prolonged upto second round or not.

In 2024, there are three candidates: Prabowo Subianto (the candidate seen as likely to continue Jokowi policies), Ganjar Pranowo (also a candidate seen to continue Jokowi policies), and Anies Baswedan (the change candidate). Prabowo Subianto is leading in the current polls, while Anies and Ganjar are trailing c20pp lower in polls behind Prabowo. That said, the coalitions backing these candidates do not have 50% of the parliament support yet vs the big-tent coalition (c80% of seat) backing the Jokowi government since the 2019 election. While a temporary parting of the coalition is generally expected given the election, it could be exacerbated by the combination of a) the high popularity of the president (c80% up to now) and b) tensions between Jokowi and his party given his son is the VP candidate of another party presidential candidate (Prabowo). ## Korea legislative elections 10-Apr 2024

The current Government leading South Korea is a divided one. President Yoon (conservative) assumed office in 2022 following victory in the presidential election, while the current National Assembly is led by the Democratic Party (DP), which together with its alliance has more than an absolute majority in the legislative chamber. This has likely been a hurdle for President Yoon to push through with his pledged reforms, though it's also noteworthy that President Yoon's rating has been falling since assuming office, from a high of 50+% to now 30+%. This year's legislative election will likely serve as a mid-term check on president Yoon's performance, as is the case with historical legislative elections, and we thus think the market impact will be muted.

The current opinion polls are guiding for DP to likely keep its position in the legislative chamber, and if so would likely keep a status quo outcome. This outcome would likely see a continuation of fiscal prudence, and alongside elevated household debt, could put domestic growth under pressure especially when global growth remains weak. With nascent signs of domestic growth slowing, we'd expect Korea's 2024 real GDP to fall below trend at \~2%, while recent BoK's speech for a more balance monetary policy could pave way for early rate cuts in 2024.

We thus expect KRW to likely remain stable in the event of a status quo outcome. Rate could however see the back-end selling off by about \~10bps should the PPP, the political party which president Yoon represents win given the more pro-business lean. The riskreward for engaging in NDIRS curve steepeners is also improving, especially with the BoK's shift to a more balanced monetary policy stance.

## #8. Impact of IGBs' index inclusion on bonds and FX

Indian government bonds (IGBs) will join global EM benchmark index (GBI-EM) in June 2024, lifting Asia’s representation in the index to c.50%. This index inclusion is expected to attract \$25-30bn of passive flows Figure 44, phased over a 10 month window from June 28, 2024 to March 31, 2025. This globalization of IGBs, in our view, also lifts the probability of India’s inclusion in Bloomberg’s global aggregate bonds index in 2024. That could be worth additional \$15bn of one-off inflows into Indian debt. Meanwhile, we don’t expect the inclusion in stricter rules’ based FTSE Russell’s WGBI to materialize over the foreseeable future.

Putting \$30bn flows into perspective: \$30bn of foreign portfolio flows account for >10x average yearly debt flows (\~4x equity portfolio flows) that India recorded in the past decade, or 4x debt flows that India recorded in 2023. This hefty number, however, would only make up for c.10% of annual net issuance of Indian government debt (IGBs +SDLs). When analyzing these flows vis-à-vis India’s external account, we estimate them to be accounting for \~40% of India’s annual CA balance (FY25 UBSe) or about similar quantum as average annual net FDI recorded in 2021-23. In short, when analysing flows vis-à-vis other supply-demand indicators, their marginal impact on rupee should be hypothetically larger than on bonds.

Implications on IGBs and NDOIS: As mentioned above, relatively smaller foreign flows into the bond market vis-à-vis net issuance should have a limited impact on fixed income markets over a 6-12m horizon. That said, their impact could intensify if such flows are crowded over a quarter or/and coincide with a shift in expectations around RBI’s monetary policy stance. Most of that is likely to be a late Q2, early Q3 phenomenon in our view – especially as headline CPI is expected to temporarily ease below 4% by then. ## #9 Could KTBs extend their 4Q23 outperformance?

10y KTB yields' 100+bps decline in Nov/Dec outpaced the ones seen in USTs was almost a 5 S.D. move. This rapid move – even though in line with our expectations and our top trade recommendations for 2024 - has arguably borrowed from potential 2024 returns, thereby reducing KTBs' risk reward vis-à-vis two months ago. This deterioration of appeal vis-à-vis October 2023, comes from a re-pricing of the market expectations of future monetary policy moves – from three months’ ago expectations of about one rate hike to about 2.5 rate cuts (25bps each) currently. Admittedly, while the former was a dislocation, the current pricing isn’t. Nonetheless, the risk-reward has also been accordingly compromised. We continue to find the bottom-up fundamentals supportive for KTBs targeting 10y yield at 2.80% in 2024, but would recommend hedging external risks/volatility into coming months via a paid position in 10y JGB futures.

KTBs recorded 5 S.D. gains in late 2023, marginally diminishing their outlook into 2024.

i) Underlying CPI inflation pressure is receding, with sequential core inflation easing to 2.7% respectively vs. 4.1% respectively in 2022. More importantly, the services inflation - a commonly watched metric for sticky demand-side price pressures – has also fallen to 1% on a 3m/3m saar metric. UBS forecasts the headline/core CPI inflation to ease below 2% by 3Q24/2Q24, faster than the consensus expectations by a quarter. Faster than expected disinflation would open up room for BoK policy rate cuts.

ii) Notwithstanding the tech cycle optimism, final domestic demand is already slowing, with y/y real GDP growth of 1.3% (2023F) less than half its pre-pandemic trend. Real retail sales, which peaked in 4Q21, continue to inch lower and is now running sub the 2016-2019 trend. Similarly we note a steep slowdown in the services sector, a sector that boosted domestic demand in 2022. Past three months’ services IP grew at an annualized rate of only 1.5% vs. 6.2% growth rate in 2022-1H23. We expect both to weigh further on final domestic demand, which in real terms barely grew in 2023 vs. 2.8% in 2022 and 5.1% in 2021.

iii) Lagged effects of tightening are likely still seeping through to this leveraged economy (total Debt to GDP of 380%, Households’ debt to disposable income ratio of 163%). The credit impulse is now at just the 3rd %ile of its 10y history while the lagged effect of 31% y/y contraction in construction orders, in 2023, should also pull down the investment growth in '24, which tends to lag with a 12-18m lead time.

US rates' re-pricing is a key risk in the coming months. KTBs’ c.90% correlation to USTs has been a source of non-negligible volatility in the past few months, although at current levels we see KTBs as roughly fairly priced. That said, if 10y UST yields back up to \~4.5% on Fed policy rate re-pricing or/and a resurfacing of USTs term premia, we’d expect 10y KTB yields to also rise to 3.6-3.8%. On the other hand, if Korean CPI disinflation continues towards 2% by mid-2024 and the US economy also edges towards recession, we’d expect 10y KTB yields to decline towards 2.8% by mid 2024 (with a bull steepening of 3s10s curve). Admittedly, that’s a balanced risk-reward. Hence, we’d refrain from running outright duration longs after a steep 4Q23 rally, with JGBs our preferred APAC market as a hedge; and tactically INR NDOIS within EM Asia.

Short JGB futures remain our preferred intra-regional hedge. The BoJ is likely be the only major central bank tightening monetary policy in 2024-25 amidst broadening price pressures. We expect the BoJ to end NIRP in early 2024, while lifting the policy rate to 1% by end 2025 (with upside risks), and target 10y JGBs at 1.85%. If the US skirts a contraction, we’d expect the BoJ’s policy rate to be closer to 1.5% by end-2025; about 125bps above short end OIS.

WGBI inclusion could be a potential tailwind, although unlikely in 1H: The FTSE Russell Fixed Income index team’s Sep-23 announcement did not include KTBs in the global aggregate bonds’ index amidst administrative and operational reasons ranging from tax information collection to FX operational hours. A FX pilot program planned for early 2024 is likely to sieve out operational issues, although this would take time to resolve. We thus think that KTBs’ inclusion is unlikely before the Sep-24 semiannual review. An inclusion would be a significant tailwind for the KTBs, likely attracting c.\$60bn of non-resident inflows staggered through 2025E. That’d be c.100% of annual net issuance of KTBs or \~8x of past twelve months’ portfolio flows.

i) Receding CPI inflation pressure, ii) slowing final domestic demand and iii) lagged effects of monetary policy tightening seeping through are key reasons on why we remain bullish KTBs.

After the steep repricing of the USTs in late 2023, the near-term riskreward in USTs has also deteriorated.

## #10. THB recorded 15+% intra-year swings in 2022- 23. Will '24 be any different?

THB recovered in 2023 as CA balance improved by c.4ppt vis-à-vis 2022. Despite an unimpressive recovery in Chinese tourist arrivals, THB outperformed median EM Asian currency in 2023. This stabilization was driven by a 4ppt improvement in Thailand’s CA balance from -3% of GDP in 2022 to c.1% of GDP surplus in 2023. About half of this 4ppt improvement was driven by a recovery in tourism, while other half was almost equally split between softer energy prices, lower freight imports costs and higher gold/precious metal exports (Figure 57). When compared to Q2-Q3 2022, when THB recorded peak depreciation pressures, the CA balance improvement is even larger by c.7ppt. This external balance recovery, alongside generally tepid resident outflows (vis-à- vis 2017-19), lifted the systemic net dollar supply in 2023 to 0.3% of GDP surplus vs. a 1.6% of GDP deficit in 2021-22.

But, 2023 also ended up being one of the most volatile years since 2001. Baht recovery in 2023, as discussed above, masks how volatile the currency was. In the past two years, USDTHB’s intra-year high-low ranges averaged c.17%, which on a relative basis was about twice median EM Asian currency (Figure 54), and on an absolute basis highest for the baht since 2001 (Figure 55). Most of this volatility can be attributed to two factors: a) THB’s elevated sensitivity to volatile UST yields via the channels of carry trades (THB/USD carry at 20+y lows) and idiosyncratic XAU-THB correlations (Figure 56 Bahtsiorcly beng v USTd (XA). ’z ,u DH); and b) borderline overall BoP and CA balance (i.e. systemic net dollar supply hovered between inflows and outflows), a macroeconomic dynamic that also tends to exacerbate exchange rate volatility as net dollar buying and selling flows tend to quickly flip between a surplus and a deficit.

We expect this volatility to ease in 2024, while also expecting 2024 to be a better year for the baht vis-a-vis 2023… Into 2024, we’d expect the USDTHB volatility (and intra-year swings) to compress from about 10% currently (3m implied vol) to about 7-8% range for two reasons: a) Shift in Fed interest rates narrative should moderately reduce the volatility in USTs, as market debate shifts away from rate hikes in 1H23 to the timing of the first rate cut in the upcoming months. Even though market pricing of rate cuts in 2024 is as stretched currently as it was in May, a shift in probability of actual rate cuts - aided by ongoing core PCE disinflation - should also result in narrower trading ranges, thereby dampening rates' volatility. b) UBS expects Thailand’s CA balance to recover further by about 1-2ppt led by an extended improvement in tourism services balance. Specifically, we expect Chinese tourist arrivals to recover by 20%y/y, lifting the source country’s tourist arrivals recovery rate from 40% of prepandemic levels to c.70% by end 2024 (more here). This recovery should help to improve the systemic \$ supply by c. \$5-10bn, which in turn should also dampen the realized volatility of USDTHB (Figure 58)

… but would not rule out a c.3-5% THB weakness between Feb-May when seasonality tends to flip into a headwind. Even though the overall BoP outlook is improving, and so is THB’s outlook vis-à-vis 2022-23, it's still settling at a level way below 2017-19 trend. As a reference, between 2017-19, Thailand recorded a BoP surplus in 30 out of 36 months averaging 3.7% of GDP vs. only 0.6% YTD. This delicacy of the CA flows shield for the baht is likely to be most exposed in Q2 and Q3. Specifically, we’d note that Thailand’s CA balance has historically been concentrated around the wings of the year (i.e. Q1 and Q4) and much weaker in Q2 and Q3. Specifically, the swings between Q1 and Q2 (typically between Feb to May) can be to the order of 4-5ppt (Figure 59). This is also why THB weakened between mid-Feb to mid-May in 9 out of past 10 years, by c.2% on average. Overlaying this seasonality with the consensus forecast of a borderline CA surplus in 2024 (cons: 2.8% of GDP), we expect a similar pattern to repeat between late Q1 to late Q3 - likely lifting \$THB back towards 35.5- 36.00 range. We’d look to opportunistically engage in USD/THB ratio call spreads around.
