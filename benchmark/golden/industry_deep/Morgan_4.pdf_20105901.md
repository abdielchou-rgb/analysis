## It’s all over now, QT

The FOMC spent much of 2023 content to let their balance sheet reduction plan operate on autopilot, with little discussion of the endgame for that plan. However, the issue garnered more attention at the most recent meeting in mid-December, when “several<sup>”</sup> people on the Committee voiced support for starting to discuss when and how to slow the pace of balance sheet runoff. Given this, we now expect that the FOMC will have the outline of a timeline to slow the reduction in the balance sheet at the late January meeting (communicated to the public in the mid-February minutes to that meeting). We expect that this plan will be formally agreed to at the mid-March meeting and will be implemented beginning in April.

We look for the monthly cap on the runoff of Treasury securities to be reduced to \$30bn/ mo (down from \$60bn/mo) and we expect no change in the \$35bn/mo cap on mortgages. A plausible alternative would see the caps stepped down to \$40bn/mo and then eventually \$20bn/mo. In either case we anticipate that balance sheet reduction will continue even if the Fed is cutting rates provided that those rate cuts aren<sup>’</sup>t in response to imminent recession concerns. Practically we think this means a funds rate below 3% would be enough on its own to end balance sheet reduction.

The projections from the 2022 annual SOMA report suggested the process would continue until the Fed judges that the reduction in its assets (and the corresponding reduction in liquidity supplied to the financial system) had gone far enough for Reserves to approach their lowest comfortable level (LCLoR). Also, the importance of maintaining some minimum RRP balance has become a more prominent thought amongst FOMC members lately, and we envision QT concluding at the end of November, while ON RRP balances still remain large enough to effect smooth functioning of money markets even as reserves remain somewhat above most estimates of LCLoR. We believe after that point is reached the Fed will allow mortgages to continue to roll off its balance sheet, with proceeds reinvested across the curve in Treasuries.

## Who am I? Why am I here?

Before digging further into the modalities of balance sheet reduction it may help to briefly step back and consider how the Fed got here and where it wants to go eventually. Between early 2020 and early 2022 the Fed<sup>’</sup>s balance sheet expanded by just over \$5 trillion. The vast majority was due to securities purchases, first to stabilize markets and later to provide additional monetary accommodation to the economy. In the case of balance sheet policy, the way up and the way down are not one and the same. Balance sheet reduction is clearly not meant to destabilize markets, and only tangentially is it meant to provide monetary restraint to the economy.

However, there are other reasons Fed leaders dislike an unnecessarily large balance sheet. A large balance sheet increases the potential risk of running negative net interest income. Large amounts of excess reserves could strain banks<sup>’</sup> leverage ratios, hindering their ability to lend. Mortgage holdings create their own issues, as Fed officials view that as a form of credit allocation, something they would like to avoid. There<sup>’</sup>s also concern that the size of the balance sheet could continually ratchet higher if the Fed doesn<sup>’</sup>t take steps to reduce its size away from emergency episodes. For these reasons and more, all FOMC officials agreed to start reducing the size of the Fed<sup>’</sup>s balance sheet

in January 2022.

When the FOMC first agreed to reduce the size of its balance sheet in September 2017, it was the culmination of a policy debate that had been taking place for at least three years. The debate leading up to the January 2022 announcement on balance sheet reduction was much more brief. Many of the decisions from that earlier debate were readily agreed to, for example, to reduce the size through slowing reinvestments rather than through outright sales, and to focus on the federal funds rate as the principal monetary policy tool during the period of balance sheet reduction.

By parallel reasoning, we think at the upcoming FOMC meeting the Committee will use the 2019 plan for slowing and stopping balance sheet reduction as the template for the upcoming endgame, making adjustment in places where the 2019 plan was found wanting. In 2019 the FOMC refined many of the balance sheet reduction details at the January meeting, formally agreed on a plan at the March meeting, and began implementing that plan in May. (We think this month-and-a-half gap can be shortened this time). The plan involved cutting the monthly caps on Treasuries in half and leaving the mortgage cap unchanged. The FOMC expected this slower pace of balance sheet reduction would go on for six months. However, the process wrapped up in July 2019, in part because the Fed found itself cutting rates into accommodative territory.

In September 2019 funding markets were briefly stressed, and too-low reserves were likely one contributing factor (see US Fixed Income Markets Weekly, 9/20/19). This may color how the FOMC modulates the 2019 plan. One response has been the creation of the Standing Repo Facility (SRF), though questions remain about how effective that facility will be in a similar stress period. That episode could also influence how the Committee thinks about slowing the pace of asset runoff and could motivate reducing the cap size in more than one step, so as to more finely tune the pace of reduction in banking system reserves.

With this in mind, it might seem early to consider a plan to slow and stop QT when reserves are at nearly 12% share of nominal GDP, well above the 8% threshold the New York Fed has used in its longer-run estimates. However, the latest 2022 annual open market operations report, released last spring, argued, “To slow balance sheet reduction, redemption caps are lowered when reserves reach approximately 10 percent of NGDP. To stop balance sheet reduction and maintain a stable SOMA portfolio size, full reinvestments resume when reserves reach approximately 9 percent of NGDP.<sup>”1</sup>. Since then, the regional banking crisis has likely had a lasting impact on the response function of banks, as they have prioritized liquidity build-up and capital conservation. This is apparent in the stability of reserves since the crisis in March 2023, as a cumulative \~ \$1.3tn change in liabilities (because of the rise in the TGA as well as Fed balance sheet runoff) was mostly absorbed by RRP with Reserves holding relatively steady (Figure 1 ). Thus, we think the Fed could be targeting reserves closer to a 10% share of nominal GDP, and it is prudent to begin this discussion now.

Moreover, there’s a growing consensus that RRP balances enable smooth functioning in money markets, thus allowing the continued effective transmission of monetary policy. Last week, Dallas Fed President Logan (nv) argued that “there will be more uncertainty about aggregate liquidity conditions as ON RRP balances approach zero...In my view, we should slow the pace of runoff as ON RRP balances approach a low level. Normalizing the balance sheet more slowly can actually help get to a more efficient balance sheet in the long run by smoothing redistribution and reducing the likelihood that we<sup>’</sup>d have to stop prematurely.<sup>”</sup> Indeed, RRP balances declined considerably around the brief “jumps<sup>”</sup> in SOFR the last two months, even as the Fed funds rate has held stable (Figure 2). This indicates MMFs are pivoting out of RRP to pick yields in repo markets, and suggest that this may prevent larger moves higher in money market rates. As a result, a positive RRP balance may keep money market rates more stable than the alternative, allowing for the continued effective transmission of monetary policy.

Looking out to the end-state, the FOMC appears content with the 2019 plans for the balance sheet<sup>’</sup>s resting spot. While operating with a regime of abundant reserves has plenty of critics outside the Fed, within the Federal Reserve system few have publicly questioned such a regime. This regime will continue to use administered rates to guide the federal funds rate, which will continue to be its primary monetary policy instrument. It will also seek to gradually return to an all-Treasuries balance sheet by continuing to allow mortgages to roll off its balance sheet. To meet the public<sup>’</sup>s (presumably) growing demand for its liabilities over time, the Fed will purchase Treasury securities across the curve, proportionate to their share of outstanding securities.

While in May 2019, the Treasury runoff caps were halved, the MBS caps remained at \$20bn. Eventually, when QT ended, any MBS paydowns up to \$20bn were reinvested into Treasuries, while any MBS paydowns above \$20bn would go back into MBS. \$20bn was a relevant cap at the time, roughly in line with the pace of runoff. Today, \$35bn is far higher than the \$15-20bn of actual paydowns, and so the probability of the Fed needing to reinvest paydowns in excess of \$35bn back into MBS is very low (Figure 7). The end state of this process will be to reinvest all MBS paydowns in order to keep reserve balances steady. However, the Fed wants to get to an all Treasury balance sheet, and reinvesting into MBS would be counterproductive. We think they<sup>’</sup>d only want to buy MBS if there were severe market functioning problems or a housing market collapse, and neither is a near term concern. As a result, they can probably leave the \$35bn cap where it is for now, and then state that at the same time as they end Treasury runoff, they will start reinvesting MBS paydowns fully into Treasuries.

Recall that in 2019 the Fed reinvested mortgage paydowns into Treasuries, via secondary market purchases, roughly matching the maturity composition of Treasury securities outstanding. Under our new QT forecast, this should result in \$15bn in secondary market demand for Treasuries in 2024, and approximately \$180bn in demand in 2025. As a result, this would help address the structural supply/demand imbalance in Treasury markets, but not until much later this year. Of course it is reasonable to ask why the Fed needs to hold a portfolio of securities that is longer in duration than the overall Treasury market, particularly given the events of the last 15 months. If the Fed wanted to reduce the volatility of its remittances, and the likelihood of repeating 2023, while more closely matching the maturity profile of the Treasury market, it could choose to reinvest these MBS paydowns into T-bills instead. However, given the simplicity of proceeding in the same way as in the past, we think the bar is high for such a shift, as rational as it may be.

Taking the slower pace of QT into consideration, we could see the Fed balance sheet holding steady starting in December, all else equal, removing one source of pressure on bank deposits. More deposit growth, in turn, probably means that banks increase their assets—and buy more securities. From a flow perspective, the net effect of ending QT is supportive of both Treasury and MBS yields, which could help both products outperform spread products that aren<sup>’</sup>t directly purchased by the Fed or banks (all else equal). MBS OASs are already on the tighter side however, and we don<sup>’</sup>t expect the Fed’s recent communication to drive much further spread tightening from here.

Additionally, if QT ends in November, we estimate a terminal O/N RRP balance of around \$300bn, implying a total RRP balance around \~\$640bn, assuming that foreign RRP level declines to its 2023 average, and reserves of \$3.2tn (Figure 8). More importantly, reserves would be significantly above our estimate of the lowest comfortable level of reserves at around \$2.75-\$2.80tn, which might suggest the Fed<sup>’</sup>s desire to secure some sort of buffer above LCLoR.

Finally, as the Fed returns the balance sheet to a more steady state, it<sup>’</sup>s also worth considering whether the Fed will make any technical adjustment to its administered rates. Recall in the run up to the Fed slowing the pace of QT in 2019, the effective fed funds rate (EFFR) was consistently trending higher in the fed funds corridor such that the Fed had to make technical adjustments to IORB. The IORB/RRP spread narrowed from 25bp at the start of 2018 to 10bp by May 2019 and has stayed there since. To that end, with EFFR currently stable at 5.33%, meaningfully below the upper bound of the fed funds target range despite the volatility we<sup>’</sup>ve seen in SOFR, it stands to reason there is no urgency to increase IORB and widen the spread. Alternatively, there also doesn<sup>’</sup>t appear to be any urgency to lower the ON RRP rate as SOFR is also trading meaningfully below the upper bound. Historically, the Fed has made technical adjustments when EFFR has traded within 5bp of the upper bound of the corridor.

Nor is there an urgency to cut ON RRP counterparty limits which were raised from \$30bn to \$80bn in March 2021 and raised again to \$160bn in September 2021. Usage of the ON RRP facility has consistently declined, demonstrating a natural decrease in market<sup>’</sup>s reliance on the facility as a source of backstop supply. If anything, given the growth of taxable MMFs over the past few years, it might make sense to maintain counterparty limits higher than \$30bn, as the ON RRP facility has proven to be an effective tool in providing a soft floor for money market rates. All told, assuming the Fed slows the pace of QT, this should moderate the upward pressure on SOFR relative to FF, as there is less collateral and more liquidity in the marketplace.

The prospect of a higher terminal RRP balance also has important implications for front end swap spreads. If RRP balances end the year \~\$200-300bn higher relative to previous expectations, our fair value model estimates this should push 2Y swap spreads wider 3-5bp all else equal, and markets are likely to widen front end spot swap spreads in anticipation. However, 2Y swap spreads are already trading \~10bp wide to our fair value estimate. Perhaps an even more important channel could be the effect that QT tapering expectations would have on bank demand for Treasuries. In addition to the implications of an earlier QT on bank deposits discussed above, other factors also suggest a more favorable backdrop for bank demand as we enter 2024. First, the stage of the rate cycle is more conducive, with the hiking cycle seemingly behind us and the next move likely to be an ease. Second, thanks to a decline in yields in 4Q23, banks that have reported earnings so far posted AOCI gains for the quarter, with the top 4 banks that we track (JPM, BAC, C, WFC) posting \~\$16bn in AOCI gains, which will likely help. Third, swap spreads remain significantly negative across much of the curve, and asset-swapping shorter maturity Treasuries is likely attractive as a way to banks to pick up incremental spread over IOR while mitigating interest rate risk. These factors should pressure 5Y swap spreads wider.
