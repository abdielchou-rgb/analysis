# FX & EM Fixed Income Strategy: US Term Premia Not Yet Impacting USD

James Lord

This week's FOMC meeting poses some downside risks for USD in light of how far Fed rate expectations have repriced over the past couple of months. Moreover, market positioning long USD has increased notably so far in 2024, mostly on a DXY basis but on a broader USD basis too. Our US team believes recent concerns about higher inflation in the US will abate, keeping the path to a July cut open. FOMC commentary this week that supports this view should help to put some downward pressure on USD. Recently carry trades have come under pressure, but should the above view materialise, we expect high-carry EM to benefit.

The continued expected outperformance of the US economy likely means that the market will struggle to price in a significant easing cycle anytime soon though, and so any near-term USD correction may be limited. This does not have to mean that carry trades would then come under pressure provided rates and FX volatility subside. Evidence of moderating inflation combined with continued good growth data should help.

Below we outline our view on why we are sceptical that fiscal risks in the US are playing a significant role in driving market (and specifically currency market) behaviour and re-emphasise the conclusions from our recent publication on USD's reserve currency status (Global Economics and Strategy: King Dollar Lacks Challengers).

## Term premia and USD

Market focus on the US fiscal trajectory remains high, with the IMF's fiscal monitor confirming the message sent from our own global economics team – debt/GDP in the US will re-accelerate due to wide primary deficits in the near term and in the absence of fiscal consolidation in the near term. Much of the market narrative on this topic suggests that it has been a factor behind the rise in UST yields, with attention now focused on this week's Treasury refinancing numbers (see here for the take of our US rates team).

We can see that term premia in the UST market has, according to the widely used ACM model, risen, and those pushing the idea that fiscal concerns are a factor in the rise in yields would likely point to this as evidence. However, we have our doubts that this has been a significant factor.

First, the US yield curve hasn't steepened. We should have seen some steepening if fiscal risk premia was behind the rise in yields and term premia. Relatedly, the rise in term premia at the front end of the curve has been just as significant as the rise in term premia at the back end of the curve. Again, if question marks over the sustainability of the US fiscal position were the driver of the rise in yields, we should have seen a bigger increase in long-term term premia.

Second, USD has been strong. USD's performance has been positively correlated with term premia over the past year. This pours cold water over the idea that the rise in term premia is related to fiscal issues. If concerns about fiscal credibility were in the driving seat, the most likely currency reaction would be weakness, not strength.

All this suggests that something other than fiscal concerns is behind the rise in term premia, and also the rise in UST yields. Most likely the strength and volatility of recent US inflation data have contributed. The fiscal position may well play a role in driving term premia, but the most likely transmission is via the uncertainty that the loose fiscal position creates for monetary policy and so the extra compensation that investors require for the risk that the Fed ends up having to hike by more than markets currently expect.

Over the long term, the correlation between term premia and USD is approximately 0, and we wouldn't necessarily rule out the possibility that fiscal concerns could weigh on the dollar eventually. Yet in our view the bar is high, particularly in light of the challenging debt positions of other DM and EM economies that keep US assets on a relative basis looking attractive. Indeed, as our recent report on USD's status as the world's dominant reserve currency suggests, we think that USD will continue to benefit from safe-haven flows and remain the dominant currency for trade invoicing and debt finance.

## King dollar lacks challengers

The influence of the US dollar in the global economy is the subject of significant debate, with some commentators predicting a reduced international role, and with it a bear market for the currency. We're far from convinced. As we wrote in Global Economics and Strategy: King Dollar Lacks Challengers, April 18, 2024, we expect USD to maintain its dominant position for some time to come.

USD's dominance and global influence are evident across multiple metrics – central bank reserve allocations, global trade financing, foreign exchange activity, cross-border lending and debt issuance. As the data show, the dollar's standing has remained unchallenged over the past decade ( Exhibit 3 and Exhibit 4 ).

Its continuing dominance, which drives additional external demand for USD from foreign investors and traders, has helped to keep USD's value elevated in real and nominal terms. Put simply, when money changes hands across borders for trade or finance, USD is most likely to be involved.

We expect USD strength in the short term versus EUR and CNH: In the short term, we remain bullish on USD largely on account of the fundamentals of the US business cycle. Indeed, we recommend short EUR/USD and long USD/CNH positions (held via long EUR/ USD puts and long USD/CNH calls).

The growth cycle in the US has moved to a higher plane in light of strong immigration, productivity and investment. The eurozone does not show a similar trend while the deterioration in China's outlook is clear and outlined in Will China Dethrone King Dollar? Provided these growth trends and differentials remain intact, which our economists think they will, the case for sustained USD weakness won't be convincing.

We also believe that USD will receive secular support through future business cycles (which could otherwise be negative for USD) as the dollar's reserve currency dominance proves more durable than some investors expect (see here, for one high-profile example, which although from last year reflects a sentiment that lingers and reappears over time in our conversations with clients).

This doesn't mean that future business cycles couldn't prove negative for the dollar. They could, of course, particularly if valuations become more expensive in the short term. However, enduring reserve currency dominance should remain a structural source of strength.

The consensus is too bearish on USD: Since around 2017 the consensus has been bearish on USD and largely wrong ( Exhibit 6 ). We think that consensus forecasts are too bearish on USD mostly due to our more constructive short-term view on the US business cycle, but to the extent that consensus forecasts have been influenced by the debate around reserve currency status, we would push back.

While concerns about USD's reserve currency status are unlikely to disappear entirely, recognition that there are no viable challenges to USD's status should help to ease concerns about its outlook. The US election campaign may offer a road-test of issues related to USD's value and reserve currency status, such as Fed independence and fiscal sustainability.

We think that USD's status as the dominant reserve asset will emerge intact, but there are risks to USD's value should, for example, markets intuit a more dovish path for the Fed.

## History suggests that three factors support the ascent to currency dominance:

• Economic use case: A third party is more likely to hold the currency if its economy is sufficiently large and globally integrated such that there's a ready and clear embedded use case;

• Political influence: Countries part of a political bloc (explicit or implicit) may be more likely to trade with each other in that hegemonic country's currency, making it easier for non-bloc members to trade with bloc members in that currency;

• Institutional protections: How safe is it for a third party to hold that currency, and what are the risks around that asset losing value either due to economic policies (e.g., inflation, devaluation) or political factors (e.g., expropriation, asset freezes).

Confidence in a country's economic credibility is an ongoing necessary condition – confidence in both the value of the currency itself and the country's economic and institutional policies.

The chief challenge to eroding USD's dominant status is finding a credible alternative: The rise and fall of the British pound (GBP) shows us that dominant currency status is neither a foregone conclusion nor permanent.

Yet, history shows that the dominant currency only loses its status once a sufficiently attractive challenger appears. The share of reserves held in USD may continue to grind lower back to the historically normal levels of 'first among equals' (as opposed to unipolar dominance), reflecting a cheapening in transaction costs.

However, in the absence of a credible competitor or a shock as profound as World War I, concerns about a rapid change in leadership (like GBP to USD after World War I), that also affected a broader range of reserve currency use-cases, may be overblown (see GBP Loses its Crown: What Does History Teach Us about Losing Dominant Currency Status?).

The stability in USD's global influence over the past 10 years demonstrates that no clear competitor is emerging: China is the most widely discussed candidate, given its growing economic, financial and political roles.

We expect a modestly more global role for CNY, primarily driven by the trade channel as per the historical norm, with capital flows less of a driver. But as we argue in the full report, the '3D challenge' to China's economy (debt/deflation/demographics) will likely limit its appeal. We see reserve holdings in CNY only rising to 5% in 2030 from 2.3% currently, though up to a third of Chinese trade could be invoiced in CNY by then.

The collapse of the explicitly dollarised Bretton Woods system in the early 1970s certainly created more space for competitors to emerge: But despite pundits predicting USD's demise at the hands of various competitors, none took the crown. Why? We think that investors underestimate the fact that dominant currency status is not a free lunch.

Dominant currencies tend to see artificial demand and strength, which isn't always welcome for export-oriented economies. Currency convertibility opens the economy up to potentially unwanted exchange rate volatility and an 'outsourcing' of financial conditions. Inflows to financial assets may keep borrowing costs undesirably low, fuelling a credit binge (e.g., US housing debt crisis, eurozone debt crisis).

Ultimately, countries have to want currency dominance – and must be willing to accept the consequences: But the most commonly argued contenders since Bretton Woods have chosen not to. Germany and Japan both viewed their own currency dominance with scepticism, given their export focus and preference for fiscal austerity.

Similarly, EUR was seen as a potential competitor to USD's dominance, particularly in the mid-2000s, though the limitations of the currency area were laid bare during the eurozone debt crisis, and policymakers since then have seemed reluctant to take the necessary integrative steps.

The US is well suited to absorb those consequences: Nearly all US imports are invoiced in USD and the US external sector is small compared to peers. Policymakers have generally supported the 'strong dollar' policy over time and appear content with the depth and liquidity of US capital markets. The Fed time and time again has demonstrated support for the internationalised USD such as through the FX swap lines in 2008 and the FIMA repo pool in 2020.

## IMF Spring Meeting takeaways

## Key top-down views

1) Both EM and DM fiscals in focus: While EM fiscals are always in the spotlight at the IMF, it was notable that this was nearly overshadowed by discussion about DM debt sustainability. From the IMF's perspective it was a matter of rebuilding fiscal buffers while for investors the issue is the extent to which risks are priced appropriately by rates markets into the US elections. For EM, there is still broad acceptance that most countries need to consolidate their fiscal positions further but also an acknowledgement that this is not easy, perhaps in part explaining why the EM structural primary balance is barely expected to change by the IMF in the coming years.

2) Long-term growth still the key issue, with structural reforms and AI potential saviours: The near-term growth outlook is a bit better than most expected six months ago, in the US for sure but also more globally. Growth having been more resilient than expected was thus an overall takeaway. That said, as the IMF usually highlights, its medium-to-long-term growth forecasts have kept falling since 2010. It estimates that total factor productivity has accounted for around half of this decline, driven by misallocation of capital and labour. Notably, the IMF reviews the required changes to see a boost to its latest long-term global growth estimate of 2.8%Y. Structural reforms reducing misallocation is the larger potential factor that could boost this, with AI adoption another key driver, even if more uncertain.

3) Beyond the headlines, progress is being made on debt restructurings: While down from six months ago, there is still plenty of talk of an EM debt crisis and the need to revamp the restructuring framework. Given the progress made, this seems exaggerated. Sure, fiscal balances are still wide and restructurings take too long, but at least it's heading in the right direction and market access has improved. Revamping the Common Framework at this point would be unlikely to help. The takeaways from the Global Sovereign Debt Roundtable were also more constructive – in particular the aim of making the comparability of treatment easier to measure and noting that nothing precludes a parallel track of negotiations between official and private creditors. There is also a clear focus on speeding up the overall process ( Exhibit 9 ).

4) On IMF changes, higher access limits extended until quota increases come through. Reducing surcharges making more progress but no change for now: The key bigger change remains the quota increase, as we indeed reviewed following the Annual Meetings in October (see here for details). This is still in the process of seeing individual countries going through their approvals, with the US itself a key outstanding one. In a positive scenario it may be completed by year-end, yet there are risks that it extends into 2025. Perhaps as an offset, the IMF extended the temporary boost to the access limits until end-2024 from March 2023, keeping the annual access limit at 200% of quota and the cumulative programme access at 600% (assuming normal access). Finally, there was more open talk of addressing the surcharges this time around, in our view. This is a clear issue for countries such as Argentina and Ecuador and they have many supporters in reducing the charges (see here for a surcharge overview). That said, there doesn't appear to be a consensus as to actually reducing them for now.

## Key country views

Argentina – positive all around yet the authorities need to keep delivering: You only needed to look at bond prices throughout the IMF week to see that Argentina was a winner. Overall we agree with the sentiment. Fiscal policy is proving to be a strong anchor together with the FX rate that is allowing for a faster-than-expected fall in inflation. Meanwhile, the FX reserve build-up is also ahead of IMF targets. The key concern all around is the sustainability of the adjustment and governability in the coming months. We did come away thinking that when it comes to future IMF programmes, there is no rush to increase the IMF exposure to Argentina by front-loading disbursements, with it instead being important to establish a track record of successful reviews. On FX and monetary policy, we didn’t find too much pushback to the current approach. However, there is agreement that the aim should be to move to a more market-based approach, with the question just being how quickly this can be achieved. On debt, we expect that it will still be classified as sustainable but not with a high probability, and that as long as there is enough FX reserve accumulation the 2025 payments can be made.

Ecuador – deal had to wait until after the referendum: We came away from the meetings thinking that a deal was close and perhaps only awaiting the details of the referendum. This turned out to be the right thinking as the IMF just approved a new EFF (see EM Credit Brief: IMF Staff Level Agreement with Ecuador on a US\$4 billion EFF, April 25, 2024). If disbursements total 200% of quota in 2024, then Ecuador sees net positive disbursements in 2024 but with 2025 likely already seeing net repayments, especially once factoring in interest. On a positive note, the IMF is coming with additional financing as CAF also just approved a US\$800 million loan. All details of the programme are still missing but we think that a key part will be to assess the extent of fiscal consolidation in the near term, and whether it's credible. When it comes to repayment risks, 2024 looks ok while the question mark will be what a new administration decides to do once it takes over in May 2025 and looking ahead to 2026 funding needs. Positive surprises would be delaying the closure of Yasuni and/or reducing subsidies more materially in the near term.

El Salvador – no sign of any IMF agreement: Post the issuance of the interest-only linked note just ahead of the IMF there was a lot of focus on whether any progress would actually be made on an IMF programme. As far as we could tell, little was made. The IMF emphasised that there are outstanding issues such as fiscals and bitcoin, and we don’t

think that there will be agreement on this anytime soon. So, with an IMF programme not in our base case, it makes the interest-only note attractive (see El Salvador Sovereign Credit Strategy: Interest-Only Note Review – It's Complicated, April 15, 2024). It has already increased in price to around 3 but if no positive IMF headlines come out in the following months, we would expect it to move higher into a range of 4-5.

Venezuela – still in stage 2 although with stage 3 perhaps not as far away as expected: Venezuela saw the highest level of interest in many years. So far we have seen the story in three stages. Stage 1 for Venezuelan bonds was for secondary trading sanctions to be lifted. Stage 2 was the index re-weighting which is now happening starting at end-April in a front-loaded manner, given that this will be done on an equal notional basis. Stage 3 is the actual restructuring. This has always been much further out, and we think that this should still be the base case. This is because it likely requires significant further normalisation in the relations between Venezuela and the West, a large further increase in oil production, an IMF programme and for primary bond sanctions to be lifted. However, as per recent headlines that Venezuela has hired financial advisors, there is a scenario where the process moves much faster. This likely would not come with the IMF. The IMF can publish an Article IV only if Venezuela has been formally recognised and data are made broadly available again. Finally, on sanctions, some oil experts see the recent change in the oil sanction regime – moving from the GL44 to the possibility of individual licenses – as potentially being positive as it offers greater clarity to firms wishing to do business with Venezuela.

Mexico – fiscals need a lot more work: Mexico has moved from being the country regarded as among the most fiscally prudent in the aftermath of Covid to there now being increasing concerns around the path. While there is a clear recognition from officials that the path needs to change, largely coming via significant spending cuts, these do not look credible at the moment. For now, all the market can do is await the elections. Meanwhile, external issuance is likely skewed to the upside, with our base case seeing at least another US\$4 billion in external sovereign bonds this year. On Pemex, the government is not planning on just including Pemex amortisations in the budget forever but does realise that there needs to be a more structural solution.

Colombia – a cautious outlook balanced by cheap valuations: Fiscals and potential reforms were key negative drivers when it comes to the outlook for Colombia amid an acknowledgement that broader macro has held up well compared to earlier fears. On fiscals, the revision earlier this year was seen very negatively, with the fear also being that it will be hard to subsequently reverse any increased spending. Whether Colombia even overshoots its own fiscal targets this year depends on spending constraints. On reforms, the pension reform is currently a big risk if it passes, particularly if the minimum wage threshold for funds to move outside of the new public sector pocket is again moved higher towards 4x minimum wages. The health care reform itself has been shelved but taking over two large private sector health providers was seen as very negative. Finally, it's worth noting that we found that the only way the prior IMF FCL drawing could be rolled would be via Colombia drawing down on SDR holdings, given that there is no way to renegotiate existing repayments.

Panama – sentiment was near unanimously negative, although different views around the potential mine reopening exist: Our sense is that some, including the IMF, see the mine reopening as possible as soon as next year, with discussions beginning in the second half of this year. One such path would be to convince people via a public education campaign and potentially an increase in taxes to pre-fund an arbitration loss fund. Still, we think that the majority, including us, view this timeline as very fast. We still expect the next administration to have to make considerable concessions to the public in order to begin to discuss this topic and see it as unlikely that either the president or the National Assembly will risk bringing up a mine reopening too soon into their term. If a discussion around a mine reopening begins, it would clearly be a credit-positive event, but it would still be a long process and it would not solve a lot of other fundamental problems that will require comprehensive reforms, including the pension system, low tax collection and high debt levels.

Dominican Republic – still a positive story, with growth expected to pick up this year and strong prospects for a tax reform in the second half of the year. We do not expect the IMF to be overly worried about the drop in foreign reserves in the first quarter of this year, given that reserves still cover five months of imports, above the three-month threshold. Also, it is worth noting that the IMF did not seem concerned about the impact of oil prices on external balances, which we expect to largely be because of the IMF's lower oil price forecast, averaging US\$78.6/bbl versus our commodity analysts' expectation of it peaking at US\$94/bbl in 3Q24.

Ukraine – most details still missing: Ukraine was perhaps the credit where investors had the most questions but unfortunately we don’t think that they found the answers they were looking for. As we enter into a period that should see a debt-restructuring proposal come out, most details are still missing. The baseline for the restructuring parameters has been set out in the latest review, with the hard levels being for debt/GDP to reach 65% by 2033 and the gross financing needs to average 8% in the post-programme period. The GDP warrants are now included as an annual funding need, with the amounts being implied by the IMF baseline scenario. If possible to buy back, a big question, then it would reduce financing needs and thus savings needed. The key questions still need clarity. First, are coupons allowed in the programme period? The IMF numbers suggest that they can be, but it then depends on the official sector. Second, how do you account for the downside scenario in bonds, and how much of the downside needs to be accounted for.

Egypt – improvement in the funding picture: Our engagement with both Treasury officials and the IMF reinforced our view that external liquidity has improved significantly since the UAE FDI investment. The latest data on the central bank NFA position now reflect this as the central bank deposits have been converted to FDI while the primary surplus is expected to be 5.75% in FY2023/24 from 1.63% the year before. The latter would result in public debt falling from 96% in June 2023 to 89% by June 2024 according to the Ministry of Finance. The authorities clarified that local debt will not be reprofiled as had been reported by various media sources, but rather the authorities will look to issue longer-dated paper upon maturity of local T-bills to local holders. The lingering question for now is whether the recent sizeable investments have disincentivised the authorities from sticking to the fiscal consolidation path. For now, the authorities are signalling their commitment to sticking with the reform path stipulated in the IMF programme.

Turkey – focus on inflation: The core message was that policymakers are focused on the disinflation path ahead of other objectives like accumulating FX reserves, reducing FXprotected deposits and supporting government revenues. It was clear that fiscal policy will be set in closer coordination with monetary policy in order to ensure that the inflation projection of 36%Y by end-2024 is met. There is a clear hawkish bias (as was also evident in the most recent CBT meeting) where policymakers note their willingness to hike further if inflation continues to surprise to the upside. On FX, control over the FX market could be relaxed gradually as inflation volatility comes down. We could see an easing in offshore FX swap restrictions before 4Q24 depending on how inflows evolve. If there are strong FX inflows, the CBT would unwind local banks’ FX swaps faster, which would improve its net FX position, and help with TRY liquidity management. The CBT could use tools to mop up excess TRY liquidity.

Zambia – drought concerns: Most investors’ questions were anchored to the impact of the current drought on the external accounts and growth. The latest WEO estimates partly reflect this, with the 2024 current account balance seen at 3.7% of GDP versus the 7.1% estimate in October 2023. However, the impact on growth seems to be limited, with the IMF revising its 2024 growth estimate to 4.7%Y versus 4.3%Y in the October 2023 WEO. It appears that the authorities are prioritising electricity access to the mining sector while also looking to tap the Southern African Power Pool for additional supply. On the debt restructuring, our Sovereign Debt Restructuring panel discussions left us with a base case that a deal will be concluded by June 2024 as official and private creditors are seemingly all aligned with the latest agreement.

Nigeria – steady reform progress, albeit with challenges: The authorities continue to show strong commitment towards macroeconomic reform, with FX adjustments and monetary policy tightening being the most talked about. However, our sense is that the pace of reforms is facing some challenges as consumers come under pressure. Indeed, the current negotiations with labour and trade unions on minimum wages suggest that any further adjustments (like higher fuel prices) will be met with some resistance. That said, the improvement in foreign portfolio inflows is a positive in rebuilding FX reserves ahead of external maturities in 2025.

Senegal – a critical U-turn: We came out of the meetings with a renewed sense of optimism as it was made clear that the concerns about introducing a local currency and renegotiating oil and gas contracts were merely campaign promises. The newly elected government has been proactive in engaging with the IMF, reassuring it of its desire to broadly maintain the current set of policies from the previous administration. To us this suggested that a lot of the political risks which had been built into the curve had to be priced out, which happened during the course of the week. The near-term emphasis remains on reducing subsidies, although it appears that there is some flexibility on the 1% target.

South Africa – near-term risks: The authorities continue to signal an intent to restore fiscal stability, but this will likely be tested in the near term with elections looming. The elections outcome, particularly in terms of any coalitions, should have a meaningful impact on the overall fiscal accounts and the debt trajectory by extension. Load-shedding and its impact on growth seem to be lessening compared to a year ago but logistical bottlenecks are a continued headwind to growth. The latest estimate from the IMF for 2024 is just 0.9%Y growth, revised down from 1.8%Y, underscoring the growth challenges. On the positive side, the government’s efforts on GFECRA are seen as a positive, although better understanding of the impact on inflation for instance is still to be better understood locally.

Earlier this month, we argued that MXN was due for a correction as our metrics pointed to extremely long positioning in the peso and global risk appetite was taking a turn for the worse.

While we maintain a cautious medium-term view on the peso, we think that the room for more near-term depreciation in MXN is now more limited from current levels, barring downside growth/NFP data surprises in the US or some tail risks around Mexico's general election. ## 3. What is the consensus view on China?

Similarly to our marketing trip to London in March, investors are still interested in talking about China, particularly CNY. But the majority of the investors we met with are hedge funds in fixed income and equity, who have shorter investment horizons. It shows that China's fundamentals need to improve a lot in order to attract inflows from more sticky real money investors.

On China's growth, investors' questions have been why China's growth is more resilient in 1Q and whether the stock market rally is going to sustain. Growth surprised on the upside due to strong exports (see China Economics: A Strong 1Q on Exports, But 2Q May Soften, April 16, 2024). Given that China doubled down on its industrial policy in new energy industry, exports should continue to sustain China's growth for the time being.

This should reduce the chance of additional fiscal easing in the upcoming April or July Politburo meetings. As discussed in EM Strategy: Asia Macro Strategy: Three Themes in Asia Local Markets, April 12, 2024, we would be watching: 1) Whether Vanke will be saved or allowed to default; 2) Whether there are any negative headlines around some regional banks or local governments; and 3) When the government will hold the Third Plenum and whether there is any personnel change in the Politburo.

We also need to watch if the US would sanction any of the Chinese banks, as reported by the WSJ last week.

## 4. When will the PBOC let CNY weaken?

Investors generally agreed with the direction of USD/CNY but the discussion has been around why the PBOC has kept CNY so stable despite a strong USD and when would the PBOC let USD/CNY move higher.

Regarding the timing, we have reiterated our view a few times that we believe 3Q is more likely. There would be three factors to support such a view: 1) USD could continue to be strong due to the US economy; 2) Trade tension could rise ahead of the US election; and 3) China needs a bigger export contribution to GDP growth in 2H24.

On the back of such an environment, China could have three choices: 1) Continue to keep CNY stable versus USD; 2) Let CNY weaken versus USD in 3Q in order to reduce pressure; or 3) Keep CNY stable and, after any potential actual tariffs in early 2025, let CNY weaken sharply.

We believe that the first choice would hurt China's export competitiveness, which would be negative for growth. The third choice could cause more market volatility as a one-off move in CNY in August 2015 cost the PBOC US\$1 trillion in FX reserves to reverse the depreciation expectation. The second choice makes more sense, in our view.

Of course, we can't exclude the possibility that USD starts to weaken sometime in 2H24 should the US economy begin to slow down and the Fed start to cut rates. This would be the ideal scenario for the PBOC as it would keep CNY stable versus USD, which would lower CNY CFETS should the broad USD weaken.

## 5. What are the risks to watch in India?

Long INR continues to be one of the consensus trades in Asia. Investors we met with want to stress test our view. We believe that the bar for the RBI to let USD/INR move above 83.50 is high because currency stability is very important during an election. Possible currency weakness could also import inflation, which in the end could trigger a RBI hike. That's what the RBI wants to avoid now, in our view.

Hence, the RBI would be comfortable using its FX reserves to defend the currency if needed. Of course, the risks could come from: 1) An election surprise; and 2) Oil prices.

We should also watch the CNH/INR level closely. If the PBOC is not willing to let CNY weaken in the short term, we think that the RBI would not let INR move as well given that India wants to compete with China in the manufacturing sector.

## 6. What to do with KRW FX and rates after the sell-off?

Investors' views on KRW FX were mixed. As USD/KRW broke above 1,360 and hit 1,400, some investors started considering the possibility of a return to the October 2022 highs of \~1,450, while others saw better risk/reward in playing for a correction lower.

We are more in the latter camp. Two weeks ago, we added short THB/KRW as KRW had underperformed THB significantly even though both are usually highly sensitive to US yields. USD/KRW was also overbought based on RSI and there was a sizeable short KRW positioning in the options market.

We think that there is still a bit more room for the trade to run. The fundamental story remains intact, with KRW benefitting from the exports recovery and foreign equity inflows (April month to date: US\$1.3 billion), while THB faces a slow growth and tourism revenue recovery. KRW is still one of the cheaper currencies within AXJ based on our idiosyncratic risk premia model.

In rates, investors still have a bias to receive in Korea. We remain neutral on Korea rates for now. Even though markets are pricing in a \~50% chance of the BoK restarting rate hikes, which our economists don't expect, we don't see an idiosyncratic catalyst for Korea rates to rally. Given the higher oil prices and weak KRW, the BoK may not send a dovish message imminently. Therefore, we think that it will require downside surprises in US data for Korea rates to rally.

## 7. Is it time to receive Thai rates?

After the BoT kept rates unchanged at its April 10 meeting – against some expectations for a cut – Thai rates sold off sharply. With markets pricing just \~10bp of BoT cuts by end-2025, some investors asked if it was a good opportunity to receive Thai rates again given the soft growth and inflation backdrop in Thailand.

Current market pricing for the BoT is very modest indeed, but like Korea we don't see an idiosyncratic catalyst for markets to price in more cuts in the near term. The rise in oil prices and weak THB reduce the scope for the central bank to turn dovish, with the BoT noting that the policy rate may be recalibrated if oil prices keep rising. The BoT also expects growth to improve.

Therefore, while current levels in Thai rates may look attractive to receive for the medium term, the strong USD and high US yields mean we stay neutral for now.

## 8. When does Hong Kong's dividend season start?

Long USD/HKD has been a popular carry trade among investors, which we could also sense from the number of queries we received on Hong Kong's dividend payment schedule for this year. Exhibit 25 and Exhibit 27 show the dividend payment schedule for the 100 largest Hong Kong-listed companies.

Dividend payments by Hong Kong-listed companies are set to start picking up in late May before ramping up further in June-August. This could generate some tightening of HKD

liquidity as companies exchange foreign currency for HKD to pay out to shareholders.

It is difficult to know how far ahead of the dividend payment will companies start to acquire the HKD they need. But at current levels, we think that the risk/reward of paying 1-year HKD versus USD rates looks decent. HKD rates have outperformed their usual beta to USD rates recently, particularly in the 1-year point, bringing the differential to the widest since May 2023 ( Exhibit 26 ).

Therefore, we like to pay 1-year HKD versus USD IRS, with a target of -20bp and a stop of - 85bp.
