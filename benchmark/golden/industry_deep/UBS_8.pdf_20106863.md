# US Economic Perspectives February CPI Preview: A wide range

## Headline CPI: UBS US Econ 0.47%, UBS Nowcast 0.36%, consensus 0.41%

We project next Tuesday's CPI release to show the headline CPI increased 47bp (seasonally-adjusted) in February as rising gasoline prices coupled with another solid increase in core prices should result in one of the larger monthly headline CPI increases since the peak increases in summer 2022. With the strong February increase twelvemonth headline CPI inflation is projected to tick up to 3.2%. Our 47bp headline CPI projection is a little above both the current Bloomberg consensus average forecast and the projection from our Nowcasting models. Our projection for the not-seasonally adjusted level of the February CPI, at 310.487, is a handful of basis points above recent CPI swaps fixings.

## Core CPI: UBS US Econ 0.39%, UBS Nowcast 0.42%, consensus 0.31%

The projected 39bp February core CPI increase matches January for the largest core CPI increase since May of last year. Nonetheless our projected 39bp February increase is below last February's 47bp increase, and we expect 12-month core CPI inflation will edge down to 3.8% — its lowest point since May 2021. Among the components we expect both housing rents and core non-rent services to slow from their strength last month, but the contribution of this slowing should be offset by a much smaller decline in used vehicle prices than occurred in January.

## Considerable uncertainty across a wide range of categories

The surprisingly strong increase in the January CPI has left us with considerable uncertainty surrounding the likely outcome of the February CPI. As we noted in the US Inflation Monthly, the January core CPI strength occurred in services, not goods. Prices for services are not normally where early year strength occurs. In fact, a large share of core non-rent services are not seasonally adjusted by the BLS because they do not find a significant seasonal factor. Additionally, the pick-up in owners' equivalent rent (OER) last month was odd as tenants' rent slowed, and new lease rents have been increasing at or below their pre-pandemic pace for the past 16 months. Despite the back-and-forth by the BLS last week on the cause of the January uptick in OER, and the divergence between OER and tenants' rent, we continue to be left without a clear reason for the January jump. With the January accelerations in OER and prices for core non-rent services largely unexplained, we could see these categories either post increases in February similar to those in January or for them to post lower-than-normal increases (such that their combined January-February increases are roughly in line with recent trends). Overall we would not be surprised to see a core CPI increase anywhere between 20bp on the low side up to 47bp on the high side — a much wider range than usual and one that is tilted to the downside of our baseline projection.

That said, indicator data for some of the more volatile components are, on balance, not suggesting much, if any, slowing in February. JD Power Used Car Guide prices suggest a much smaller decline in used vehicle prices in February than in January, which would boost the core CPI increase by around 7bp. Only ¼ of this push-up from used cars is offset by new cars, where JD Power transaction prices suggest a solid decline in February. Airfare and hotel price data both suggest another month of strong price increases for these components. A full category roundup of our projection is below in Figure 25 and additional details are available in the US inflation forecast spreadsheet on our website.

## March expected to show slowing

While we expect a fairly strong increase in the February CPI, March is expected to show slowing. Early March gasoline price data suggest a stronger not-seasonally-adjusted increase in March than in February, but seasonal factors will likely push that to more moderate seasonally-adjusted increase. Additionally, JD Power March Used Car Guide data is suggesting used car prices will subtract 5bp more from the core CPI increase in March than in February. Finally, we expect a further slowing in OER in March — in part as a delayed pay-back from the strong September increase (and the CPI's 6-month panels for OER). Currently we have a 27bp headline CPI increase pencilled in for March, along with a 23bp core CPI increase. These monthly increases imply 12-month headline CPI inflation would tick up 3.4% in March while core CPI inflation would edge down to 3.7%. Looking further out, we project core CPI inflation will decline over the next handful of months as slowing rent increases, falling vehicle prices, and easing supply conditions put downward pressure on price increases.

## Likely to need more than inflation to get Fed to cut

Except for January, inflation has slowed notably faster than the FOMC had been thinking it would. 12-month headline PCE inflation is more than 90% of the way back to the FOMC's 2.0% target from its peak in 2022. Meanwhile core PCE inflation is roughly three-quarters of the way back to target. But with the strong recent employment data, FOMC members appear in no hurry to dial back monetary policy restrictiveness.
