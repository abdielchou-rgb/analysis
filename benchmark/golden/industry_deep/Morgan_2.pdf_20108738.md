# Nikkei dividends 2H24 outlook

Nikkei dividend futures performed well this year, supported by favorable earnings trends and TSE corporate governance reforms, even as the Nikkei index has remained range-bound since March 2024 (MNDZ4 + 2.9%, MNDZ5 + 8.0%, NKY - 3.9% since end-March). The absence of dividend supply from structured product issuance has particularly benefitted longer-dated dividends over shorter tenors. The previously observed flat dividend term structure, which coincided with years of strong dividend growth, has now vanished, signaling a significant shift in market dynamics.

Participants in the dividend futures market have largely discounted conservative profit guidance from companies. This is evident in the recent rally of Nikkei dividend futures despite company guidance of a 3% year-on-year decline in net profit for FY2024 (see here). As a result of conservative guidance, our bottom-up estimates now suggest potential losses of 6% in MNDZ4 and 8% in MNDZ5 (Figure 3).

Analysts typically revise dividend forecasts upwards as fiscal year-ends approach, historically averaging a 4% increase for Nikkei constituents with a March fiscal yearend over the past decade (Figure 4 . Instances of downward revisions in dividend estimates are rare, with the most significant being a mere -0.5% in 2019. Dividend estimates for FY23 were revised upwards by 4% during the fiscal year. Factors contributing to better-than-expected earnings in FY2023 include foreign demand, yen depreciation, and pass-throughs of cost increases. These dynamics are likely to drive FY24 estimates higher to a certain extent, according to our Japan equity strategist.

However, we believe these upward revisions are already factored into current dividend futures pricing. Considering the percentage of dividends yet to be confirmed, we estimate bottom-up estimates for 2024 and 2025 dividends would need to rise by \~12% and \~5% respectively, to close the gap. This is a scenario challenging to achieve, as such revisions would exceed historical norms over the last decade (Figure 4 .

We revise our top-down model forecasts for 2024 and 2025 dividends to 679 points and 745 points, down from 688 points and 758 points. The top-down model is based on the premise that earnings growth is closely tied to economic growth and that corporate dividend policies are influenced by anticipated earnings growth. The downward revisions reflect weaker-than-expected economic indicators, particularly the 1Q24 GDP release (see here), which raises concerns about consumption weakness. The situation is further complicated by a weak yen, which limits expectations for real wage growth and subsequently discourages spending. Our economist currently projects Japan's real GDP to mildly contract by -0.1% yoy in 2024 before growing by 0.7% yoy in 2025. These estimates lead to forecasts of 679 points and 745 points in our top-down model for the Nikkei 2024 and 2025 dividends, suggesting a potential downside of approximately 3% from the current market level. To provide a more balanced view on dividends, we run our top-down models under various growth and payout scenarios (Figure 5Nike 204dvns top-w frca uy gh   , Figure 6Nike 205dvns top-w frca uy gh  ).

The risk of special dividends is worth attention, as cash-rich Japanese companies may follow Trend Micro’s case of paying a large special dividend. Currently, announced dividends for the calendar year of 2024 add up to \~5 pts. While special dividends are typically challenging to forecast due to their one-off nature, for context, 2018 witnessed the highest contribution from special dividends, when they accounted for \~4% of the total index dividend points. This potential for special dividends adds an additional layer of opportunity in our dividends futures forecast.

We close our buy recommendation for Nikkei 2024 dividend futures (see here). We believe the current pricing already reflects an overly optimistic scenario for dividend revisions, which is difficult to justify based on historical norms. Our top-down estimates suggest a potential downside of approximately 3% for the 2024 dividend futures. Furthermore, the steep upward-sloping dividend term structure makes longer-dated tenors unattractive. However, we remain fundamentally positive on Nikkei 225 dividends, supported by TSE’s corporate governance reform initiatives (Figure 7) and Japan corporates’ cash rich status (Figure 8). Notably, a significant number of companies still have a price/book ratio below 1x, which is far from the government’s goal of having 80% of the TSE prime market trading higher than 1x price-to-book ratio. We recommend that investors accumulate long positions if Nikkei dividend futures decline to our bottom-up estimate levels, which have historically served as a valuation floor.

## Recent flow dynamics in Japan cash equities market

Since May 2024, Japanese equities have traded range-bound as risk appetite remained restrained. Historically, domestic institutions' flows are more significant to watch in a range-bound market. Consequently, our risk framework has given more weight to domestic institutions’ flows, predicting market’s risk state based on their buying vs selling activities (Figure 9).

In early May, our framework indicated a risk-off period due to continued domestic selling (Figure 10 . However, as domestic buying strengthened and negative momentum failed to sustain, the framework alternated between a risk-on and risk-neutral state.

We think flat return and low volatility will likely continue in Japanese equity markets: With domestic investors continuing to buy equities in a range-bound market (Figure 12), our framework predicts the risk-neutral state to stay in the near term. Historically, the "risk-neutral" environment is characterized by a range-bound market, with index returns relatively higher compared to the risk-off state and lower compared to the risk-on state. Further, the risk-neutral state tends to exhibit realized volatility lower than risk-on and risk-off states. Interestingly, our gamma positioning proxy reveals a noteworthy shift in the derivatives market positions in recent months. Dealers' gamma exposure, which had previously leaned towards the short side, has rebalanced to a more neutral stance. This adjustment suggests a potential reduction in the technical factors that often magnify market movements (Figure 13).

## Risks of Common Option Strategies

Risks to Strategies: Not all option strategies are suitable for investors; certain strategies may expose investors to significant potential losses. We have summarized the risks of selected derivative strategies. For additional risk information, please call your sales representative for a copy of “Characteristics and Risks of Standardized Options.” We advise investors to consult their tax advisors and legal counsel about the tax implications of these strategies. Please also refer to option risk disclosure documents.

Put Sale: Investors who sell put options will own the underlying asset if the asset’s price falls below the strike price of the put option. Investors, therefore, will be exposed to any decline in the underlying asset’s price below the strike potentially to zero, and they will not participate in any price appreciation in the underlying asset if the option expires unexercised.

Call Sale: Investors who sell uncovered call options have exposure on the upside that is theoretically unlimited.

Call Overwrite or Buywrite: Investors who sell call options against a long position in the underlying asset give up any appreciation in the underlying asset’s price above the strike price of the call option, and they remain exposed to the downside of the underlying asset in the return for the receipt of the option premium.

Booster : In a sell-off, the maximum realized downside potential of a double-up booster is the net premium paid. In a rally, option losses are potentially unlimited as the investor is net short a call. When overlaid onto a long position in the underlying asset, upside losses are capped (as for a covered call), but downside losses are not.

Collar: Locks in the amount that can be realized at maturity to a range defined by the put and call strike. If the collar is not costless, investors risk losing 100% of the premium paid. Since investors are selling a call option, they give up any price appreciation in the underlying asset above the strike price of the call option.

Call Purchase: Options are a decaying asset, and investors risk losing 100% of the premium paid if the underlying asset’s price is below the strike price of the call option.

Put Purchase: Options are a decaying asset, and investors risk losing 100% of the premium paid if the underlying asset’s price is above the strike price of the put option.

Straddle or Strangle: The seller of a straddle or strangle is exposed to increases in the underlying asset’s price above the call strike and declines in the underlying asset’s price below the put strike. Since exposure on the upside is theoretically unlimited, investors who also own the underlying asset would have limited losses should the underlying asset rally. Covered writers are exposed to declines in the underlying asset position as well as any additional exposure should the underlying asset decline below the strike price of the put option. Having sold a covered call option, the investor gives up all appreciation in the underlying asset above the strike price of the call option.

Put Spread: The buyer of a put spread risks losing 100% of the premium paid. The buyer of higher-ratio put spread has unlimited downside below the lower strike (down to zero), dependent on the number of lower-struck puts sold. The maximum gain is limited to the spread between the two put strikes, when the underlying is at the lower strike. Investors who own the underlying asset will have downside protection between the higher-strike put and the lower-strike put. However, should the underlying asset’s price fall below the strike price of the lower-strike put, investors regain exposure to the underlying asset, and this exposure is multiplied by the number of puts sold.

Call Spread: The buyer risks losing 100% of the premium paid. The gain is limited to the spread between the two strike prices. The seller of a call spread risks losing an amount equal to the spread between the two call strikes less the net premium received. By selling a covered call spread, the investor remains exposed to the downside of the underlying asset and gives up the spread between the two call strikes should the underlying asset rally.

Butterfly Spread: A butterfly spread consists of two spreads established simultaneously – one a bull spread and the other a bear spread. The resulting position is neutral, that is, the investor will profit if the underlying is stable. Butterfly spreads are established at a net debit. The maximum profit will occur at the middle strike price; the maximum loss is the net debit.
