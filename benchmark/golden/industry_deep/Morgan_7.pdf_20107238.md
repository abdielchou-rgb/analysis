# Building Stronger Recovery

We upgrade GDP growth to 6.8% for F2025e from 6.5%, while we expect F2024 growth at 7.9%. Strength and stability are the hallmarks of the cycle with macro-stability outlook remaining benign. A shallow easing cycle can be in the offing, however, we remain watchful of stronger-than-expected growth.

## We raise GDP growth expectation to 6.8% in F2025 from 6.5% on continued traction in industrial and capex activity.

• We expect GDP growth to track at around 7% in QE Mar-24 with GVA growth of 6.3% and thus F2024e GDP growth of 7.9%.

• We expect growth to be broad-based and the gaps between rural-urban consumption and private-public capex to narrow in F2025.

• The cycle will have more years of steady expansion driven by improvement in productivity growth, which will ensure macro stability remains benign. Indeed, we expect CPI inflation to track at 4.5% in F2025e and F2026e and current account deficit at 1% of GDP in F2025e and F2026e.

• Moderating inflation trajectory and benign current account deficit opens up room for a shallow easing cycle as we have been highlighting. However, we expect the easing cycle to be delayed to 3Q24 from our earlier view of 2Q24. Further, we highlight risks of a potential delay and/or risk of no easing driven by better-than-expected trend in growth, capex and productivity. which will imply higher neutral real rates.

• Fiscal policy will remain in consolidation mode, driven by a pickup in tax revenue and rationalization in revenue spending, even as capex spending will likely growth above nominal GDP in F2025e and F2026e.

• Risks stem from global factors more than domestic. On the external side, slower-than-expected global growth, higher commodity prices and/or tighter global financial condition pose growth and macro-stability risks. On the domestic side, central elections and changes in policy mix need to be tracked.

Growth Outlook

We raise GDP growth to 6.8%YoY in F2025, a rise of 30bps from our earlier estimate of 6.5%, on continued traction in industrial and capex activity. On CY basis, we expect growth at 6.8%YoY for 2024, from 6.4% earlier.

## Drivers of Growth

We expect GDP growth to track at around 7% in QE Mar-24 with GVA growth expected to be 6.3% and thus F2024 GDP growth expected to be 7.9%. For F2025, we increase our forecast for GDP growth to 6.8% vs. 6.5% earlier and for GVA growth to 6.5% from 6,3% earlier.

## CONSUMPTION

## Where are we in the cycle?

Domestic demand growth has been steadfast and is a key driver of our constructive outlook for the economy. Consumption accounts for 60.3% of GDP and is the mainstay of the domestic demand story. While private consumption has recovered over the last four quarters, with growth tracking at 3.5% in QE Dec-23 vs. 1.8% in Dec-22, the trend in private consumption is just catching up to the pre-pandemic trend.

Consumption growth post pandemic has been mixed, with slow recovery in rural demand. In our view, this reflects: a) shift in consumption toward services while share of goods in wallet share has moderated; and b) low-income segments/rural households balance sheets are in the process of healing post the pandemic and inflation shock.

Current trends in high-frequency consumption data reflect that consumer demand for services remains robust, with both PMI manufacturing and services tracking above the 50 mark since Aug-21. Auto sales are picking up, with an improvement in passenger vehicle sales, which are tracking at 1.6x 2019 levels, while two-wheeler sales growth is showing steady improvement on a low base but remains a tad below 2019 levels. Consumer sentiment is recovering and is finally tracking close to pre-pandemic levels. While job growth has slowed, dragged down by the IT/BPO sector, recent data shows some stabilization at low levels. On the rural side, rural fast-moving consumer goods sales growth picked up gradually to 5.8% YoY in 4Q23, which while still lagging urban growth of 6.8% YoY represents a significant narrowing in the two segments compared to as early as 2Q23.

## Outlook

We expect private consumption growth to recover further as it narrows the gap between rural and urban demand and between goods and services. In our view, the factors supporting consumption will be: a) moderation in inflation, especially core inflation, which improves purchasing power; b) improving terms of trade for the rural sector; c) steady trend in net household financial saving rate 7% of GDP for QE Mar-23 as per RBI’s report vs. 7.7% of GDP in QE Mar-22; and d) improvement in labour demand conditions after a slowdown in 2023.

## INVESTMENT

## Where are we in the cycle?

This cycle, like the 2003-07 one, has been marked by a pickup in capex. Indeed, capex (real GFCF) growth has averaged 8.5% since June-22, with growth at double-digit level of 10.6% in QE Dec-23 vs. the average of 7.3% pre-pandemic (average of 2017-2019). Capex to GDP has recovered from the trough of 26.5% in Dec-20 at roughly 31% as of QE Dec-23 (four quarter trailing).

The recovery in capex since the pandemic has been led by the government’s thrust for capital spending. Indeed, central government capital spending has seen a 27.4% CAGR since 2019 and is tracking at 3.1% of GDP on a 12-month trailing basis (vs. 1.8% of GDP in 2019). As well, state capex spending remains robust tracking at 2.8% of GDP in Jan-24 vs. 2.6% in 2019. As such, public capex spending remains strong, with central government, state government and PSE’s front-loading capex spending in FYTD24.

Private capex, which has been on a weak footing for most of last 10 years is also showing signs of recovery as government’s push for infrastructure spending is improving the business environment and crowding in private investments. Indeed, private sector projects under implementation are growing at a robust 16.9% as of Dec-23 vs. -4.2% pre-pandemic (Dec-19). Further new investment announcements made recently, such as approval for three semiconductor chip manufacturing plants entailing an investment of US\$15bn and the new approved EV policy that could trigger investments in the electric vehicle space also indicate nascent signs of pick up in corporate capex.

Household capex, which accounts for approximately 37.6% of total capex, has been recovering over the last 18-24 months with a turnaround in real estate sentiment in both the residential and commercial spaces. Incoming data reflects that new sales of residential real estate were tracking at all-time highs in QE Dec-23.

## Outlook

We expect capex trend to pick up in a sustained manner creating a virtuous cycle of growth. Indeed, a pick-up in capex is key for the constructive outlook on the economy over the medium term. In our view, the nascent signs of capex revival will become more broad based and sustain the uptrend driven by:

a) Strength in private sector balance sheets – Corporate debt to GDP remains modest at 51.3% and impaired loans are tracking at a 16-year low of 4.2%.

b) Improving end demand – Improvement in end demand is being reflected in rising capacity utilization rates. Capacity utilization is tracking at 74 in QE Sep-23 vs. long-term average of 71.9.

c) Structural policy reforms – Reform measures since 2019, which have improved the environment for private businesses, have reached a stage where the real economy benefits are trickling in. For instance, India’s electronic exports are annualising at US\$28bn vs. US \$11.3bn in 2019.

d) Infra capex to sustain the momentum – The overall trend in infra capex tracked at 4.9% of GDP in F2023, below the peak of 6.9% of GDP in the previous cycle. In our view, infra capex will grow at double-digit levels over the next few years, supported by pubic and private capex spending.

e) Household capex to remain on an uptrend – Cyclical and structural factors such as improved housing affordability, higher income growth, urbanization rate and nuclearization of families will support real estate capex.

To conclude, we expect the overall investment rate to accelerate to 36.2% of GDP by F2027 from 32.2% of GDP in F2023.

## EXPORTS

## Where are we in the cycle?

Goods and services exports account for \~23.2% of GDP, and the trend in exports tends to influence domestic production and capex cycles. Goods exports account for 13.5%, while services exports account for 9.7% of GDP.

Goods exports have recovered since Aug-23 with five of the seven months recording positive growth. As such, export growth tracked at 5.2% YoY for the three months ended Feb-24 vs. -0.1% in the previous three months. Similarly, non-oil export growth tracked at 7.2% in the three months ended Feb-24 vs. 1.9% in the previous three months.

Even as exports have slowed in YoY terms, the trend in market share remains better than pre-pandemic, with the goods export market share tracking at 1.8% vs. 1.6-1.7% prepandemic.

Services exports have also seen a recovery from Oct-23, with services export growth since tracking at 8.9% YoY vs. 5.3% in previous six months. Further, gains on export market share on services side remain steady tracking at 5% vs. pre-pandemic of 3.7%.

## Outlook

With global growth expected to slow a tad to 2.8% in 2024 and remain steady at 2.9% in 2025 (from 3.2% in 2023e), we expect the export trend to stabilize and not be a further drag on growth. Further, over the medium term, we estimate an increase in export market share as India benefits from supply-chain diversification and policy measures supporting manufacturing. As such, we expect that export market share (goods and services) will increase from 2.4% currently to 4.5% by 2031.

## Macro-stability Outlook

Macro stability is expected to remain within the comfort range, as we expect policy makers to calibrate policy, such as price and external stability, to remain in control. Financial stability remains in check, with multiyear low impaired loans. In our view, CPI inflation will moderate to 4.5% in F2025 and current account deficit will track around 1% of GDP. Risks to inflation stem primarily from food inflation, while for current account balance the risks stem from geopolitics.

## Inflation

Inflation trend has softened recently: Headline inflation moderated to 5.1% in QE Mar-24 (tracking) from 5.4% in QE Dec-23 and 6.4% in QE Sep-23, driven by broad-based softness. Indeed, food inflation, which has a high weight of 45.8%, is tracking at close to 8.7% YoY vs. 9.4% in QE Sep-23. Further, on a sequential basis, food inflation has seen a moderation, especially post the spike in vegetable prices in Jul-23. However, food inflation trends will remain a source of volatility for headline inflation given the high weight.

Broad-based moderation in core inflation: As highlighted above, control in headline inflation is broad based, with core inflation also seeing meaningful moderation. Indeed, core inflation fell to a series low of 3.4% in Feb-24 vs. 6.2% in Feb-23. Further, sequential growth in core inflation also reduced to 0.3% MoM (non-seasonally adjusted) over the past 12-months from 0.5% MoM in the previous 12 months. This is driven by broad-based moderation across core goods and core services inflation. Indeed, easing in supply chains helped to temper core goods inflation to 3.5% in Feb-24 from 7.3% in Feb-23. On the other hand, core services inflation, which probably saw a reset higher during the pandemic, was tracking at 4.3% in Feb-24 from 5.1% in Feb-23. However, with respect to core services inflation, we highlight two points: a) services economy remains buoyant with PMI services tracking at an average of 60 in the past 12 months, indicating strong demand conditions; and b) housing inflation with a weight of 10.07% remained weak at 2.9% in Feb-24 vs. 4.8% in Feb-23, while three-city price average remains high 12.7% and anecdotal evidence regarding house prices and rentals also indicates higher growth. What about new housing consumption survey and its impact on CPI? The share of food declined to 46% in the latest round vs. 53% in 2012 for rural households, while it declined to 39% from 43% in 2012 for urban households. As such, the share of non-food items has risen to 54% for rural households (from 47% in 2012) and 61% for urban households (from 57% in 2012). The data will play a key role in reviewing critical economic indicators, including Gross Domestic Product (GDP), poverty levels and Consumer Price Inflation (CPI). The government is conducting two back-to-back surveys to produce robust results. The second survey is likely to be completed by the second half of the year (ET, Feb-24). With the results of this survey, it is likely that the revised CPI basket will have a lower weight for food and higher weight for non-food items; this should further reduce the volatility in headline CPI driven by changes in food CPI.

## Inflation Outlook:

As discussed above, the trend in headline inflation has been a function of supply-side shocks. In our base case, we expect supply-chain normalization to continue and commodity price pressures to ease. For food inflation, we are building in a normal monsoon rainfall trend, and thus expect food inflation on a YoY basis to decelerate. Further, oil prices are expected to be range bound at US\$85/bbl in F2025 (similar to F2024's levels), and with retail oil prices pegged to US\$ 85/bbl, we envisage no further pressure from fuel inflation. As such, food and fuel inflation, along with core good inflation, should exert a disinflation trend. We expect core inflation to remain muted in F1H25, and to see some modest upside in F2H25, driven by base effects and building in some increase in services inflation. We thus expect headline inflation to average 4.5% in F2025e vs. 5.4% in F2024, while we expect core inflation to remain muted at 4.1%.

## External Stability

Benign trend in current account deficit: The trend in merchandise trade deficit has moderated in F2024, as commodity prices have eased. Indeed, commodity trade deficit is tracking at 4.8% of GDP on a three-month trailing basis as of Feb-24 vs. peak of 6.3% of GDP in Dec-22. At the same time, the services trade surplus has strengthened to 5.5% of GDP in three months ending Feb-24 from 4.1% of GDP in QE Jun-23. On balance, the current account deficit remains range bound tracking at 0.9% of GDP in F2024e.

Current Account Outlook: We expect the current account deficit to remain in a range around 1% of GDP over the next two years. This is based on the assumption of oil prices averaging \~US\$85/bbl (MS forecast) in F2025. To recall, a 10% increase in oil prices can widen the current account deficit by 30bp of GDP. The funding of current account deficit has remained manageable as the capital account remains healthy with BoP in surplus over the last three quarters. Further, the inclusion of India in the GBI-EM index from Jun-24 will be a positive catalyst to trigger capital flows. We expect inflows of around US\$30bn between Jun-24 and Apr-25 as the index inclusion occurs in a staggered manner. This will improve the capital account surplus trend, add to BoP surplus and enhance external balance sheet strength. ## Financial Stability

Banks’ balance sheet strong: Bank balance sheets remain strong as gross impaired loans are tracking at a 16-year low of 4.2% and net NPA’s are tracking at an all-time low of 0.8%. As such banks, remain well capitalized and credit costs are still low. From a cyclical perspective, credit growth at 16.5% YoY continues to outpace deposit growth at 13.1%, even as the gap has narrowed from a year ago. As such, a pickup in real deposit rates as inflation moderates could help improve the deposit growth.

Pickup in leverage from low levels: Even as credit growth is tracking at a strong 16.5% YoY, it should be seen in the context of weak credit growth between F2020 and F2022, with nominal credit growth remaining meagre at 6.8%. As such, part of the pickup in credit growth is reflecting a catchup. Further, credit to GDP is currently tracking at 53.4% for India vs. 49.7% pre-pandemic, while it is at 185.4% of GDP for China, 71.8% of GDP for Brazil and 126.4% of GDP for Vietnam.

Credit growth could become more balanced: The rise in credit growth post the pandemic recovery has been driven by a pickup in retail segments, with retail credit growth at 18.4% in Jan-24 vs. non-retail credit growth at 15.3%. Within retail loans, unsecured lending is growing by 22.4% YoY in Jan-24 vs. 27.3% in Jan-23 and 22.9% in Oct-23 (before RBI announced risk weight adjustment). As such, the RBI used macro-prudential measure of increasing risk weights for unsecured consumer credit and credit to NBFCs in Nov-23. Given that we expect private capex growth to improve in F2025, non-retail loans or, in particular, industry credit – which is lagging at 7.8% – could see a pickup.

Short-term volatility in liquidity may continue: Given credit growth remains above deposit growth and reserve money growth remains weak at 6.1% YoY (reflecting RBI’s preference for tight liquidity), short-term volatility in liquidity could continue. Indeed, frictional factors such as seasonal demand for currency and/or government spending patterns could dictate liquidity trends. Inter-bank liquidity has turned into surplus of US \$3.1bn in Mar-24 after remaining in deficit since Sep-23. We thus expect liquidity to remain benign in F1H25 driven by supportive seasonal factors, moderating trend in inflation and support to BoP from capital flows such as bond index inclusion, which could lead to the addition of durable liquidity.

## Policy Response

In our view, an important support for growth expansion to be sustained is a well-calibrated policy response, which helps to maintain a sort of "goldilocks" environment with a healthy trend in growth, moderating inflation and a manageable current account deficit.

As inflation has remained largely within the comfort range of the central bank, the RBI has kept rates steady since Mar-23. Indeed, the rising uncertainty from external factors and tighter global financial conditions have kept the RBI on vigil with a focus on liquidity management. In our view, the RBI will remain cautious and focus on maintaining real rates in positive territory for the domestic economy. We expect real rates to track \~150bp in this cycle.

## Monetary policy outlook

In our base case, we expect inflation to moderate and current account deficit to remain manageable. As well, our US team expects the Fed to cut rates from the June meeting. As such, as the RBI gets visibility on gradual moderation in inflation toward 5% and below, we expect it to embark on a shallow rate cut cycle. We pencil in two rate cuts of 25bp each. However, we push the first cut forward from our earlier expectation of June to August/ October.

What could trigger no easing? We remain cognizant of the risk that RBI keeps rates steady in this cycle – i.e,, repo rate remains steady at 6.5%. Apart from global factors such as higher commodity prices, delayed start/shallower easing from the Fed and a stronger dollar, we anticipate a few domestic factors as well. On the domestic side, continued upside surprise in growth driven by capex and productivity could lead to no easing, as that will imply that equilibrium real rates are higher. Indeed, as per RBI’s working paper (Patra, 2022) post-pandemic real rates have moderated to 0.8-1%. However, the confidence gaps remain wide at ±90bp. Further, the paper also examines a modified version to estimate neutral real rates using the pace of leverage, and the findings indicate that a neutral rate could then be higher at 2-2.1%, even though the paper noted that the sensitivity of leverage gap to changes in real interest rate is estimated to be modest. As such, higher growth driven by improving productivity could warrant higher neutral real rates vs. our base case of 150bp. As we noted earlier, we believe that the current cycle is similar to the 2003-07 cycle, given growth was driven by capex and productivity. Thus, compared to the 2003-07 cycle, the average real policy rate averaged 190bp in that cycle.

## Fiscal policy outlook

On the fiscal side, we expect slow-paced fiscal consolidation, as we expect consolidated deficit to narrow to 7.9% of GDP in F2025 from 8.9% of GDP in F2024e. Indeed, the government surprised in the interim budget by stepping up the pace of fiscal consolidation. We expect the consolidation to be supported by an increase in tax revenues and rationalisation of revenue spending, even as capex spending remains robust. While improvement in the overall growth trend alongside administrative policy measures is helping to improve the tax collections, and this is likely to be sustained, we highlight the risk of fiscal slippage at the state level, driven by a recent increase in populist measures. The 12-month trailing fiscal deficit is tracking at 2.9% of GDP as of Jan-24 vs. 2.5% of GDP in F2023.

## Risks to Our View

In our view, the key risk in the near term stems from upcoming general elections in May-24. Besides this, cyclical risks stem from global factors, and idiosyncratic weather patterns.

Uncertainty in political environment and slowdown of reform momentum: While we assume a stable political environment to provide an anchor for effective policy-making, the key risk to India's growth outlook could stem from an unstable political environment, which could increase policy uncertainty. We believe that key risk thus stems from changes in policy mix that affect growth and/or macro stability adversely.

Cyclical risks from global factors, idiosyncratic weather events: Possibility of a weakerthan-expected trend in external demand would affect export income and weigh on capacity utilization, pushing back capex recovery. Further, changes in global commodity prices coupled with tighter global financial conditions could lead to monetary tightening having an adverse impact on domestic demand. Idiosyncratic weather events could affect growth-inflation adversely through the impact on crop production, given agriculture accounts for 18.3% of GDP and food has a weight of 45.86% in CPI.
