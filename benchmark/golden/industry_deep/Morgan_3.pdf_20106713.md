# The Viewpoint: Why Asian Central Banks Will Not Cut Ahead of the Fed

Easing inflation has opened up room for rate cuts, but Asia's central banks are holding back. We think this dynamic will shift in 2H24 as our US economics team expects the Fed to have started cutting rates.

## Key Takeaways

Inflation across Asia has been coming down and is settling back into central banks’ comfort zones.

But the latest rhetoric from central banks suggest they are considering but not quite ready to cut rates yet.

■ We attribute this mainly to the uncertainty in the market pricing of the first Fed rate cut, which is resulting in still rising US real rates.

If the Fed cuts in June as our US economics team expects, we see Asian central banks joining in fairly quickly.

The risks are skewed towards a delay to the start of the rate cut cycle in Asia.

## How the Fed influences Asia’s monetary policy

Not so synchronized on the way up in timing and magnitude: On the way up, central banks in Asia did not have to synchronise their rate hike cycles with the Fed in terms of timing and magnitude. Central banks in Asia had some degree of independence accorded to them by the fact that domestic conditions – i.e. macro stability indicators – were very much in check. For example, the Bank of Korea hiked ahead of the Fed in Aug-21 due to concerns over financial stability, while some (such as the Bank of Thailand) delayed rate hikes until Aug-22 – when the Fed was already well into its tightening cycle – as Thailand's post Covid recovery had lagged. To be sure, the extent of the Fed rate hike cycle eventually did lead some central banks to hike more than they would have just by looking at domestic considerations. But by and large, central banks had been able to respond mainly to domestic issues rather than moving in lockstep with the Fed.

But on the way down, a bit more synchronized as regards timing: We had thought that the different scale of the inflation challenge meant that the rate cut cycle in Asia could have begun even ahead of the Fed. For instance, in last year’s mid-year outlook (which was published in May-23), our forecast at that point was that the Fed would cut in Mar-24 and that some of the region’s central banks would have moved in 4Q23 or early 1Q24. But, by the time of our year ahead outlook in mid-November, we had moved the central bank calls such that the timing of the first rate cut would be either in sync with or later than the Fed, though the magnitude of rate cuts would still differ. Based on the latest policy documents and public comments from central banks around the region, they are not rushing to cut rates just yet.

Macro stability concerns have abated: To be sure, Asia’s inflation has continued to trend lower. As we have highlighted before, Asia is on track to transition towards its prepandemic low-flation environment. As things stand, six of nine economies in the region have already seen inflation return to their central banks' comfort zones. For economies like Korea, the gap between inflation and the central bank’s target has already narrowed to just 80bps, down from 430bps at the peak. Core inflation dynamics are benign for most economies across Asia but it is food prices that are causing near-term volatility in inflation. For instance, in the case of India, core inflation is now down to 3.6% but the runup in food inflation lifted headline inflation to a peak of 7.4%Y in Jul-23 before the reversal in food prices brought it back down to 5.1% in Jan-24. But, notwithstanding this progress, while we have seen central banks acknowledge the progress on disinflation, we have yet to see an outright dovish tilt that suggests rate cuts are imminent. We attribute this to the fact that inflation has just reached the target range or is still closing the gap. Moreover, Asian currencies have been relatively weak and potential depreciation pressure could yet arise given the trailing strength of the dollar, which adds to the concerns policymakers have about inflation staying within target.

# Reasons why Asia's central banks cannot move first

## #1: Uncertainty in market pricing over the Fed’s first cut

Market pricing for the Fed policy path has swung sharply: Market pricing of the timing and pace of rate cuts from the Fed has shifted drastically over the past three months. As late as mid-November last year, markets were still pricing in some small possibility of a rate hike. But in December, this very quickly shifted to pricing in a first 25bps cut by the March meeting ( Exhibit 1 ). Thereafter, stronger incoming data, notably Jan-Feb nonfarm payrolls and CPI prints, along with a pushback from Fed officials, have led markets to delay their expectations to a June rate cut instead. Similarly, markets have also sharply pared back the pace of rate cuts for 2024, from expecting 168bps of cuts in total in mid-Jan to just 84bps currently ( Exhibit 2 ).

Incoming dataflow suggests more near-term volatility ahead: The January FOMC minutes confirm that the Fed remains highly attentive to inflation risks. For the most part, policymakers are highlighting that they see rate cuts later this year but further progress with disinflation is needed. Moreover, our US economics team is now forecasting January core PCE to pick up to 0.43%M (vs. previous three-month trailing average of 0.13%) and the continued bumpy path for inflation in the months ahead ( Exhibit 3 ) could create more near-term hawkish repricing. They still see a gradual disinflation trend ahead with some payback in volatile series such as in core services components that drove the January acceleration (see US Economics: Our Inflation Forecasts - A Round Trip, Feb 22). As such, they continue to see a first Fed rate cut in June and a cumulative 100bps cut in 2024.

US real rates are rising again: With markets pricing in a later start to and more gradual Fed rate cut path, US real policy rates have continued to rise in the background. On a 12M forward core PCE basis, real policy rates have likely risen to 3.4% in January, the highest since Mar-01 ( Exhibit 6 ).

## #2: Real rate differentials are negative and have widened considerably

On an absolute basis, Asia’s real rates are high: In Asia, real policy rates have also risen sharply as the region has transitioned to its pre-pandemic lowflation environment (see The Viewpoint: Inflation concerns are dissipating, Nov-23). For Asia ex Japan ex China, real rates on a 12-month forward CPI basis have risen to close to a five-year high of 2.3% in January. This is well above the 20-year average real rate of 0.26%.

But on a relative basis, Asia’s real rates are still much lower than those of the US: However, Asia’s real rate differentials with the US have widened as the US has seen a larger rise in real rates. For AxJ ex China, real rate differentials with the US (on 12-month forward CPI) peaked in Feb-22 ( Exhibit 9 ). Since early 2023, AxJ ex China's real rate differentials with the US have turned negative (i.e. AxJ ex China has lower real rates than the US).

## #3: Asian currencies are still weak

The external backdrop matters: At the outset, we would note that it is not just real rate differentials that matter for the outlook for the currency. In particular, we focus on the period from 2005-1H08, when real rates in Asia were on average lower by 200bps as compared to the US ( Exhibit 11 ) but Asian currencies continued to appreciate against the dollar. In our view, the global trade cycle provided strong growth support to Asia and led the region into a virtuous, productivity-led growth cycle. This steadily pushed Asia’s saving rate higher and lifted the saving-investment gap (i.e. current account balances) into stronger surpluses ( Exhibit 13 ). Additionally, stronger growth differentials attracted capital flows into the region, resulting in large balance of payments surpluses.

Stronger USD a headwind for Asia’s rate cut cycle: But in this cycle, for a variety of reasons, the US dollar has been strong. In fact, the US dollar has tracked stronger year-todate, and the DXY has appreciated by 2.9% since the late December trough. Reflecting the dollar strength, Asian currencies – with the exception of INR – have depreciated by 0.7%- 4.2% against the USD in the same time period ( Exhibit 15 ). In that sense, the stronger dollar environment and the weaker starting point of Asian currencies could lead to caution on the part of central banks in the region, out of concern that they could stoke further depreciation pressure if they were to cut rates ahead of the Fed. This is especially as real rate differentials vs. the US are already unfavorable as discussed earlier.

For China, external considerations are an added constraint in this cycle: For China, we have been of the view that both domestic and external considerations are acting to constrain the rate cut cycle. On the domestic front, policymakers are likely concerned over the impact of further rate cuts on banks' net interest margins. Meanwhile, policymakers could be giving more consideration to external conditions in this cycle, in our view. This is given the weaker China growth environment and generally weaker investor sentiment on China’s asset markets. In that sense, currency depreciation pressures stemming from further policy rate cuts could weigh on investor confidence and capital flows, perpetuating a negative depreciation loop. Indeed, policymakers have been keeping the RMB fixing stronger ( Exhibit 17 ) and also keeping liquidity conditions tighter to guide interbank rates above policy rates since Aug-23 ( Exhibit 16 ). This indicates to us that PBoC has been trying to avoid excessive currency depreciation pressure.

## #4: Growth momentum holding up

Domestic demand stable, external demand improving: Across Asia ex China and Japan, growth momentum appears to be holding up despite the move higher in real rates. At the outset, we have also highlighted that real rates are not restrictive, per se. So far, what we are observing across the region is that domestic demand momentum seems to be holding up well, while external demand conditions are improving. For instance, the trade data reveals that import demand is holding up well ( Exhibit 18 ) and new orders have also improved ( Exhibit 19 ). Meanwhile, export growth has been rising and new export orders have also risen ( Exhibit 21 ). In this context, it does appear that better growth data will allow central banks to be more patient before they embark on cutting rates.

Conclusion

Asia’s central banks will still be held back in the near term: Domestic data is showing that inflation is already making good progress towards central bank targets. However, continued shifts in the market pricing of the Fed policy path, negative and widening real rate differentials, and potential FX depreciation pressures suggest to us that central banks in the region will maintain a wait-and-see approach to cutting rates.

The Fed holds the key: One of the factors explaining our central bank calls is our US economics team's view that the Fed will cut rates from June onwards. As the incoming data and Fedspeak begin to confirm our base case view, we think that it will provide markets with more certainty to price in a June rate cut, which in turn will provide central banks in Asia greater comfort in embarking on their respective rate cut cycles. To be sure, we still think that this will be a shallow rate cut cycle for Asia ( Exhibit 23 ), considering that we did not hike as much on the way up. The rate cuts we forecast reflect a situation where real rates don’t have to be higher than where they are now, considering the much better starting point of macro stability. The risks are skewed towards a delay to the start of the rate cut cycle. However, considering that real rates are not restrictive, a delay may only pose modest downside risks to the region’s growth outlook.
