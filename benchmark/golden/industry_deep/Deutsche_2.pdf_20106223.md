# CMBS

## Ed Reardon, Managing Director Bipul Sinha, CFA, Associate

CMBS: Lower conduit mortgage rates, the distressed CMBS opportunity, and rating downgrades

▪ Macro. DB still expects mild recession, but soft landing outlook has vastly improved. Fed cuts in 2024 to come in either outcome.

▪ Lower conduit rates ahead? We believe conduit rates can drop back below 6%, and that would help increase CRE transaction activity.

▪ Distressed CMBS. We believe distressed CMBS is a \~\$20bn opportunity.

Credit downgrades. We show downgrade activity over time and a life-todate SASB downgrade transition matrix. Severe AAA SASB downgrades create a CMBS reputation problem.

## <sup>▪</sup> Macro.

▪ Mild recession, still base case. DB expects Fed to cut rates starting in June (3.1% Fed Funds bottom in early 2025e). Mild recession with unemployment rising to 4.5%.

But…Soft landing likelihood improving. Rationale: declining inflation and better balance in labor market (lower labor demand).

▪ FOMC meeting outlook. Expect Chairman Powell to indicate "restrictive stance will have to be maintained for some time” and that officials do expect to cut rates this year – but “the timing and pace of those cuts will be data dependent.” (DB Economics research)

## ▪ Lower conduit mortgage rates ahead

▪ Combination of decline in 10yr UST to 3.8% (consensus year-end) and tighter new issue spreads point to conduit rates getting back below 6%.

<sup>▪</sup> Lower mortgage rates would help increase transaction activity.

## ▪ Distressed Opportunity

▪ We estimate CMBS is \~\$20bn of distressed opportunities. While small vs high yield corporate market, the buyer base for these bonds is also limited.

## Weekly Commentary

To say that last week’s new issue deal activity was “constructive” would be sheer understatement - \$5.8B priced across 14 deals, the highest single week total since December 2021. The continued spread tightening that began in Q4 of last year has carried over into the initial weeks of 2024, and with it the continued uptick in deal activity that punctuated the closing months of 2023.

▪ Although last week’s focus was new issue, refi / resets also contributed to the mix, accounting for \$1.7B across four deals, bringing the aggregate amount of primary activity to \$7.6B. Over the last three weeks, this category has accounted for 25% of primary deal activity, an extension of the focus that managers and investors have been paying to this part of the market since July of last year when spreads had tightened enough to make a repricing or full reset of recent vintages viable.

▪ We see this as a segment of the market that should play a significant role in 2024. If pricing for new issue remains near current levels, resets alone could contribute north of \$30 to primary deal activity on the year. Historically, resets have presented a slight spread pickup on average of roughly +5 bps for As and BBBs, and +15 bps for BBs when compared to new issue spreads. Conversely, refis have historically shown a tighter-than-market-average pricing of 15 bps at the top of the stack (AAAs), which should be accretive for equity investors. More broadly, however, the resurgence of this part of the market simply provides more supply of primary paper for investors during a period in which elevated base rates and mostly constructive credit metrics make CLO returns attractive.

▪ Secondary market activity was also outsized last week, with \$7.76B in Trace trade activity, more than double 2023 weekly average of \$3.7B. Shorter duration continues to be in demand, as well as junior credit – BBBs and BBs are receiving focus in 2024, accounting for 33% of total BWIC activity, compared to 24% a year ago. The attention has tightened average secondary BBB and BB spreads considerably since the start of the year, 20 / 56 bps, the most significant repricing across the stack YTD.

# Viable once again – Refi / Resets are set to accelerate

## ▪ Q1 2022 ushered in a period of heightened

uncertainty. The MOVE index, which tracks the level of volatility in interest rate swaps, went from a reading of 77 at the start of 2022, a level near the trailing 10-year weekly average of 75, to 93 by the end of February when Russia invaded Ukraine, and then to 125 by the end of March when the FOMC delivered its first interest rate hike.

▪ The market volatility caused a spike in CLO spreads, bringing the average BSL AAA from roughly 115 bps to 145 bps by the end of Q1, a 30bps (25%) widening for the quarter. This led to slowdown in new deals being printed, with Q1 2022 moderating to \$31.2B compared to \$56.8B a quarter earlier, and \$39.8B a year earlier. But for refis / resets, the poor deal economics resulting from the spread widening sidelined activity in Q2.

▪ This market context changed in Q3 of last year, however, as loan prices rallied and spreads tightened, opening the gates once again for refis / resets. Combined, the category averaged 30% of total primary deal activity in H2 and accounted for \$24.6B (\$5B refi, \$19.6B reset). The final two months of the year were especially pronounced, with \$13.4B pricing.

▪ Even if spreads were to marginally soften over the course of H1 – which we think is possible due to heightened geopolitical risk and decelerating GDP levels – we still anticipate market conditions to be constructive for refi / reset activity and are forecasting north of \$40B in 2024.

▪ Resets will take priority if pricing remains range bound and are therefore forecasting roughly \$30B (\$32B) as our base case. The average WADM of the five deals reset this year is S + 205 bps compared to roughly 220 bps at the end of last year. If funding spreads were to tighten meaningfully below 190 bps, we would anticipate closer to \$45B of reset volume as a bull case. However, pricing may drift wider during the year, and using an upper bound of 250 bps for the average WADM of new deals, we would anticipate somewhere closer to \$25B as a bear case.

▪ Our forecast is based on a few assumptions. First, we’re prioritizing the deals that are likely candidates as those that have not amortized more than 80%, essentially any deal that has not been outside of its reinvestment period for more than a year. Deals outside of their reinvestment period on average have softer credit metrics than those that are being actively managed (table, bottom RHS), and we assume that an investor would be wary of allocating to and extending a deal that is showing signs of credit weakness, particularly when considering the number of new deals being brought to market.

▪ The second assumption to our forecast is that spreads will be relatively range bound throughout the year, in between a context of S + 150 bps to a S + 185 bps average across all BSL deals, due to the anticipated durability of investor demand for CLO paper throughout the course of the year. Even if the US economy cools in H1, strong market fundamentals should stem spreads leaking wider than Q3 2023 levels. These AAA levels (150 – 185 bps) correspond to deal costs (WADM) in the S + 200 – 250 bps range.

At the same time, these elevated rates have made debt servicing costs more expensive for lev loan borrowers, a headwind that will likely extend well into 2024.

▪ With LIBOR’s cessation at the end of June, a synthetic LIBOR rate is being published until September 30, 2024, to provide additional runway for legacy deals to transfer to the new base rate. The difference between the two has remained locked to the ARRC recommended spread of 26.2 bps, eliminating any spread differentials that used to arise between the two due to the differences in pricing macro moves.

ABS Kayvan Darouian, Director ABS: Everything old is new again ▪ The structured finance industry has a deep and abiding love for acronyms. ABS, CMBS, CLO, CDR, CPR, … the list goes on. But some acronyms, like SIV and NCSLT, for instance, have fallen out of favor since the GFC, and rightly so.

▪ Imagine our surprise, then, to hear that there was a new CDO in the market, backed by ABS tranches and rated by Moody’s.

▪ According to Moody’s presale report, Bayview Opportunity Master Fund VII Trust (BVABS) 2024-CAR1F, is a CDO backed by the class C & D notes of two previously issued BVABS transactions, 2023-CAR2 and 2023-CAR3.

▪ The underlying deals are backed by prime auto loan pools with average FICOs of 757 and 791. The included Class C tranches are rated A3 by Moody’s and the Class D tranches carry a Baa3 rating. The Class A notes for BVABS 2024-CAR1 are rated Baa2 by Moody’s.

The sponsor of the transaction, Bayview Opportunity Master Fund VII, LP., has entered into a repo agreement to buy back the underlying bonds from the trust in September 2027 for an amount sufficient to pay back remaining class A principal and interest, as well as any transaction fees and expenses. If the repurchase doesn’t occur, the Class A noteholders can direct the indenture trustee to auction off the underlying ABS.

▪ The coupons on the underlying bonds are lower than the coupon on the CDO, so a non-declining reserve account has been included to provide support to the trust.

▪ Our take? While this transaction looks very little like any CDO we remember, the “CDO” acronym carries a well-deserved stigma and gives us pause.

▪ Private credit continues to make news, and one headline in particular caught our attention last week: Carlyle acquired a \$415 million private student loans portfolio and made a strategic investment into Monogram LLC, whose management team has originated over \$30 billion in private student loans.

▪ Monogram is the successor company of Cognition Financial, which was originally known as First Marblehead, a name familiar to anyone who has followed private student loan ABS market since the GFC.

▪ First Marblehead originated more than \$15 billion in private credit SLABS under its NCSLT and NCT shelves, and the 2006-2007 NCSLT transactions were among the worst-performing deals across the entire ABS sector during the GFC.

▪ The 2006-2007 vintage for the NCSLT shelf has experienced average cumulative defaults of close to 48%, compared with 43% for their deals overall.

In 2011, First Marblehead sold the resids for all of the NCSLT transactions to VCG Special Opportunities Master Fund, setting into motion events that ultimately led to the CFPB vs NCSLT case, which remains a significant regulatory concern for the structured finance industry today.

▪ In August 2023, Nelnet issued a private credit SLABS transaction, NSLT 2023-A, which included loans originated by Cognition under the Monogram loan program from 2010 through 2020. Performance for this deal has been solid to date, with a lifetime CDR of 1.68%

30+ Day Credit Card Delinquencies: Banks vs ABS master trust data (%, NSA)

![](images/4d815578b0300d22f63211d4e0500ea66c9f486780c4b47f90dc4f967df30a6a.jpg)

Credit card delinquencies are rising, but the magnitude of the increase depends on which set of data you’re looking at. Here we show Fed data on 30+ day credit card delinquencies for commercial banks vs S&P’s credit card ABS index, which is comprised of the major bank card master trusts.

▪ Overall bank data shows 30+ day delinquencies to be at their highest level since Q1 2012, and roughly twice the level of the S&P credit card ABS index at present. At 1.45% in the November remit period, credit card ABS 30+ delinquencies remain slightly below pre-pandemic levels. Why the difference?

▪ Following the GFC, banks moved away from using ABS as the primary funding method for credit card lending, opting instead to fund via deposits. Accounts designated for the master trusts tend to be more seasoned and better performing than the overall portfolios, hence the divergence in performance.

▪ ABS spreads have tightened considerably since we published our year-ahead outlook, so we are refreshing our relative value recommendations here.

▪ We remain confident that most ABS structures can handle a decline in performance associated with a mild downturn and recent spread tightening points to the increasing likelihood of a soft landing, making an even stronger case for ABS.

▪ Top-tier subprime auto seniors – Despite tighter levels, we still like the roughly 25 bp spread pickup for top tier subprime auto A3’s over similar WAL prime auto ABS. While there are performance issues for the 2022 subprime auto ABS vintage, top tier subprime auto ABS continues to perform well.

Retail lease auto ABS seniors – As much as we’ve liked prime auto ABS seniors over the last year, we don’t think there is as much room to tighten given current levels. We think 2-year WAL retail lease auto ABS seniors look compelling at present, offering roughly 20 bp of spread pickup against prime auto ABS, with similar credit risk.

▪ Rental car ABS seniors and subs – Rental car ABS triple B’s in the low- to mid-200 bp range look attractive to us, compared with some recent deep subprime auto ABS triple B’s pricing at 180 bp over the I-curve in new issue. We also like the more than 40 bp spread pickup for 3-yr rental car seniors over similar WAL prime auto ABS. Stabilizing used vehicle prices should be supportive of rental car ABS in the near term.

▪ Discount private credit SLABS: We continue to like discount private credit SLABS seniors at current levels and think that a drop in rates could speed up prepays if refinancing activity begins to pick up.

▪ Triple-net lease (NNN) ABS: Rating actions for the triple-net lease ABS sector have begun to filter through following S&P’s criteria update, and we see opportunity in the more well-diversified master trusts.

▪ Data center seniors: Data center ABS is getting a criteria update from S&P, which only rates the hyperscale transactions, adding an unwelcome technical for investors in the space. That said, we think the case for data center demand in the U.S. has only strengthened given the focus on AI. We like single-A rated hyperscale data center ABS seniors in the low to mid 200s spread area and think they have room to tighten from here.

▪ Timeshare ABS subs: Timeshare ABS typically price at a 15% CPR to call at new issue, but in reality, CPRs average close to 25%. We think that timeshare triple-B subs look attractive at 200-230 bps over the I-curve with weighted average lives closer to 2.5 years vs 3.4 years assumed at pricing. This compares favorably with subprime auto ABS triple-Bs, and, although you forgo the delevering seen in subprime auto, you get improved credit quality with higher average credit scores in the timeshare ABS pools.

The global market for commercial passenger aircraft continues to tighten – and we expect it to tighten further through the balance of 2024 and into 2025 and likely beyond. Production delays of new aircraft, particularly at Boeing, and reliability issues with new engines for the A320neo from Pratt & Whitney have caused supply shortages for airlines, greatly increasing the demand for mid-aged passenger aircraft. Storage of western-built mainline passenger aircraft decreased from 9.6% in November 2023 to 9.4% in December 2023, with an especially dramatic improvement yearover-year from 14.8% in December 2022. While storage of new widebody aircraft (those less than ten years of age) is now slightly below that of new narrowbody aircraft, storage of “teenage” widebody aircraft (those ten to twenty years of age) remains sharply elevated relative to teenage narrowbody aircraft, a situation we expect to continue in 2024-25.

Storage rates of narrowbody passenger aircraft has moved sharply downward over the course of much of 2023, improving from 15.5% in January 2023 to 9.5% in December 2023 as global air travel returned to near pre-pandemic levels. Storage rates of newer narrowbody passenger aircraft (those under 20 years of age) improved from 11.4% to 6.8% over the same period. Narrowbody storage rates would have been even lower for newer aircraft if not for the reliability issues plaguing the RTX’s Pratt & Whitney engine (the Geared Turbo-fan, or “GTF”). In December 2023, 400 of 1,300 GTF-powered narrowbody aircraft, or 30%, were in storage versus less than 3% of similarly-aged narrowbody passenger aircraft powered by the competing engine type. The difficulties with the Pratt GTF engine and the resulting elevated storage rates are expected to remain high in 2024 and continue well into 2025, reducing aircraft supply.

## Aircraft rent payments steady despite decline number of aircraft

hereAvg. Revenue (\$,000s)

The steady performance of average rent revenue through 2023 is particularly impressive given that the number of aircraft in benchmark aircraft ABS transactions has steadily declined over the course of the year. ## RMBS Ed Reardon, Managing Director Rupesh Shrivastav, Research Associate Smiti Kaul, Associate

RMBS: Heavy issuance and tighter spreads amidst strong IG fund flows

▪ Issuance. 2024 YTD issuance is \$7.7bn, 60% above the pace from last year. Non-QM issuance is at \$3.2bn, Prime \$2.2, SFR \$0.6bn (1 deal).

▪ Non-QM. AAA and BBB spreads have tightened vs comparable Corp IG spreads. Non-QM AAA value has eroded versus Agency MBS.

Fund flows. 2023 cumulative IG fund inflows reached +\$73bn vs 2022 outflows of -\$59bn. 2024 YTD inflows are already at +\$15bn, +64% YoY and closer to 2020 levels.

▪ Remit tracker. 60+ delinquencies and CPR for non-agency RMBS.

▪ Non-QM rally. Significant normalization of spreads vs IG Corp.

<sup>▪</sup> AAAs have rallied from T+200bp wides to T+145bp.

▪ BBBs rally from T+high 400bps to T+275bp.

▪ Rally means many recent vintage Non-QM bonds are trading at premiums to par.

## <sup>▪</sup> Relative value

▪ Non-QM RMBS relative value has eroded after the rally.

▪ #1: Basis to Agency MBS. Non-QM AAA spread basis to Agency MBS is historically narrow. Investors should add current coupon agency MBS over Non-QM, given the very low spread basis.

▪ #2: Prepay speed pickup. Prepay speeds can pick up for high WAC originations from the past year (bonds now trading at premiums) due to lower rates and tighter credit spreads.

## Still opportunities

▪ Look for Non-QM RMBS with prepay protection, such as deals with larger share of investor loan collateral that have remaining prepay penalties.

Penalties will provide some prepay protection. VERUS shelf typically has higher investor loan share; other shelves are more deal specific.

Macroeconomic fluctuations often account for most of the risks associated with exposures to instruments that promise to pay fixed or variable interest rates. For an investor who is long fixed-rate instruments (thus receiving these cash flows), increases in interest rates naturally lift the discount factors applied to the expected cash flows and thus cause a loss. The longer the maturity of a certain cash flow and the higher the move in the discount factor, the higher will be the loss, Upside surprises in inflation, fiscal funding needs, and FX depreciation rates are among the most common adverse macroeconomic shocks to receivers. But counterparty exposure, issuer creditworthiness, client segmentation, regulation (including changes in assets holding limits for different types of investors), changes in tax policies, currency convertibility (which may constrain currency conversion, repatriation of profits and/or liquidation of positions), and settlement issues related to local clearing houses are also important risk factors. The sensitivity of fixed-income instruments to macroeconomic shocks may be mitigated by indexing the contracted cash flows to inflation, to FX depreciation, or to specified interest rates – these are common in emerging markets. The index fixings may – by construction – lag or mis-measure the actual move in the underlying variables they are intended to track. The choice of the proper fixing (or metric) is particularly important in swaps markets, where floating coupon rates (i.e., coupons indexed to a typically short-dated interest rate reference index) are exchanged for fixed coupons. Funding in a currency that differs from the currency in which coupons are denominated carries FX risk.
