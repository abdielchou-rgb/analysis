# Of underpriced divergence and rising political risks

The direction of our views is intact. The market is underpricing divergent levels of capacity utilisation as political risks mount. Equally, however, we think this is the time for the dollar to shine – failure to do so would require us to review our frameworks anew.

Divergence is stickier than you think: Despite better European and Chinese activity, relative output gaps with the US have not narrowed, implying diferential easing paces in favour of the USD.

A market in denial: Our r\* and Taylor Rule-based frameworks suggest that the market continues to anticipate a relatively dovish Fed compared to G10 central banks.

Limits to divergence at play? We explore mechanisms that could limit policy divergence, including FX pass-throughs, a global inflation factor, sticky services inflation and spillovers from strong US data. We find they are not binding in the current context.

From Br-eturn to Fr-exit: Domestic politics in Europe are back in the limelight. We argue that they squarely favour the USD but also CHF and GBP relative to the EUR.

EM central banks face FX tradeofs...: The EM economic pain from rate hikes and the space in EM forward rates vs the US are both limited; policy easing may weigh on FX going forward. We think fundamentals compel easing in Czech, Colombia, Korea and Philippines, while fiscal dynamics require lower yields in Mexico, Brazil, South Africa and Indonesia.

...and constraints: Yet, we show, the stock of external debt may not permit excess FX weakness in Mexico, Colombia, South Africa and Korea. Political noise may further constrain easing in Mexico and Brazil. Czech, Philippines and potentially Indonesia may have a bit more room to manoeuvre rates to the cost of the currency, while politics are currently supporting ZAR.

## Persistently divergent ...

This quarter, economic data surprised to the upside in major regions such as Europe and China, and to the downside in the US.This has generated a narrative of macroeconomic and policy convergence between the US and the rest of the world – or at least one of no meaningful further divergence.

These trends are not entirely novel. For instance, relative data momentum implies that the Eurozone is about 12 months into the mega re-convergence trend that begun in the summer of 2023, following the ToT and China shocks. Equally, relative growth surprises have been favouring the Eurozone versus the US since the end of last year ( Figure 1).

That said, markets ultimately tend to focus on the degree to which this delta in the data feeds through to central bank policy. And, from this viewpoint, convergence is far from straightforward because the starting point of the various economies is not the same. For instance, convergence from a distant starting point needs not only growth, but firm above-trend growth to materialise and vice versa. Overall, the level at which various economies operate matters greatly.

The key macro economic variable defining an economy's initial conditions is the output gap. Although itself an unobservable variable, it conveys whether the economy is in excess demand or excess supply territory. Conceptually, this is key for the path of monetary policy because it is the size (and sign) of the output gap, rather than growth or data momentum, that defines the impact of demand conditions on inflation. Accordingly, a large positive output gap means that demand softness does not automatically mandate rapid easing. And, conversely, a large negative output gap means that rapid growth need not imply rapid tightening.

Even with better European growth and slightly less solid US growth, relative output gaps are not narrowing; indeed they might even be widening ( Figure 2). Eurozone growth rates of 0.42% in 2022 and 0.83% in 2023 are below potential growth estimates of between 1.0-1.4%; while US growth rates of 1.94% and 2.54% respectively are at, or above, most estimates of US potential growth. For all the uncertainty regarding post-COVID starting points and estimates of unobservable variables, it appears a reasonable assertion that in the past two years the US

output gap has either grown more positive (or at best has not moved much) while the one in the Eurozone has shrunk by c.1%.

It follows that in so far as the US output gap remains positive, demand continues to exert upward pressure on prices. The opposite case can be argued for the Eurozone, but not as straightforwardly. For instance, any decent increase to Eurozone growth levels would bring it closer to potential, which could prove tricky at the start of a cutting cycle. Nevertheless, in relative terms, it is fair to suggest that the operational level of the two blocks is still diverging.

## ... against a market in denial

This divergence in initial conditions ought to generate at least some degree of divergence in terms of the speed and depth of the respective cutting cycles between the US and other major G10 central banks. The underlying binding constraints for the US in its efort to ease policy will be larger, and last longer, than elsewhere.

Markets, however, have been very reluctant to trade this theme in earnest during 2024, with the exception of a short window of around four weeks in April. And even during that time policy divergence was a reflection of lower expectations of a US slowdown and accompanying Fed easing.

Accordingly, markets are pricing the Fed delivering more or less the same amount of cuts as the ECB and other major central banks for much of 2024 and 2025. Against the backdrop of a stronger economy, however, this leaves the US policy stance as less restrictive than a superficial survey of policy rates in the G10 might suggest. We demonstrate this more formally below, with the help of our proxy estimates of near-term neutral rates (r\*) along with an illustrative Taylor Rule exercise for G10 economies.

Figure 3 presents the latest iteration of a key metric of policy restriction we have used in the past, namely the distance of expected policy rates at end-2024 versus our r\* proxies. This update is based on the latest vintage of the 2024 output gap estimates from each central bank (or the IMF when the former are not available) as well as similar long-term neutral rate (R\*) proxies. Despite having one of the highest policy rates in the G10, US monetary policy does not screen as particularly restrictive. What is more, the assumptions underpinning our estimates (a real long-term neutral rate of 1% per the average NY Fed Laubach-Williams estimate for 2022 and 2023 and an output gap sub +0.5%) are, if anything, fairly conservative; a marginally higher R\* and/or output gap in the US would mean the ECB's policy stance is more restrictive than that of the Fed even after its recent rate cut.

Elsewhere, the RBNZ remains the most restrictive in the G10 space following its recent hawkish holds, with the BoE and the Scandies following suit. On the other side of the spectrum, the BoJ's slow hiking cycle means policy remains very loose; and the RBA is not far from neutral settings, which makes talk of more hikes seem not that unreasonable or unrealistic. Finally, the SNB's rate policy ought to be viewed in conjunction with its FX policy, which implies a much stronger degree of policy restriction than shown in Figure 3 (by some 75bp – for more details see here).

## Exploring the limits to policy divergence

The market's reluctance to price policy divergence with the US could reflect the belief that it is self-correcting. The economic mechanisms supporting such a stance, however, are rarely discussed explicitly.

Below, we examine three potential channels of transmission. These are: 1) the existence of a very strong FX pass-through to inflation; 2) a large common (global) factor in the background which gravitates towards macro convergence; and 3) strong spillovers from US outperformance to the rest of the world. We argue that a non-trivial degree of policy divergence is neither selfdefeating nor inconsistent with global trends. What is more, the influence of the global inflation factor seems to be receding and particularly so in services, a key variable that pushed markets against divergence recently.

A strong FX pass-through to CPI implies limits to policy divergence, all else equal. This is because macro divergence ought to lead to FX appreciation in the currency of the relative outperformer, which in turn compresses inflation and thus limits the degree of tightening. A forward-looking market can short-circuit this process. Although intuitive, we do not find this channel of transmission to be either uniform or particularly strong within the G10.

Figure 7 and Figure 8 show FX pass-through estimates for the period 2010-2019, during which QE policies arguably boosted this efect (thereby resulting in fairly generous coeficients). G10 economies are roughly split into two camps: one where pass-through is between 0.1-0.2 (denoting a 0.1-0.2ppt impact on CPI for every 1% move in NEER) and one where it is near zero. Small and medium-sized (non-commodity exporting) open economies comprise the former set, and commodity exporters along with the Eurozone the latter. These results are intuitive, as small open economies without domestic currency pricing ought to import inflation more easily; while large FX swings for commodity exporters typically fluctuate with the demand/commodity (super)cycle, dampening pass-through. As for the Eurozone, the small pass-through could reflect the region's size and more extended use of EUR invoicing.

Accordingly, there is a case to be made that countries in the first camp (in our case Switzerland, Sweden, the UK and Japan) face more constraints in terms of policy divergence compared to the other group. This, however, is not to say that there can be no divergence whatsoever. Instead, FX pass-through of that order remains quite small by international standards, reflecting the small share of food and energy in G10 CPI baskets. Early cuts by the SNB and the Riksbank are real-time examples of such policy divergence, and the same ought to also hold for countries/regions with lower pass-through, such as the Eurozone.

Policy divergence could also be limited if it reflects a common factor or shock afecting all economies similarly. There is little doubt that both the COVID-19 and energy shocks were such common disturbances, implying a similar response across economies. That said, as these initial shocks fade, country fundamentals ought to become more important in determining the appropriate policy stance across countries.

Figure 9 and Figure 10 show precisely that: as time passes, the impact of common drivers to various inflation components fades, and is replaced by local fundamentals and the degree of excess demand/supply in each country. The direction of travel is the same across headline and core inflation.

Interestingly, this common factor has been less pronounced in the stickier components (especially services and particularly in G10 economies). The market has traded "convergence" on the back of global services inflation persistence – and this does not appear backed by evidence in this context.

In the next section we take a closer look into services inflation in particular, where the "last mile" in the disinflation process is currently feeling the longest across most economies in both the G10 and EM.

## Exploring global services inflation further – is it a hurdle?

As we mentioned in the previous section, inflation stickiness appears particularly pronounced in the services sector across a wide range of EMs and DMs in CEMEA and the Americas. We argued above that the common factor in services inflation is receding. But while the common thread in services inflation wanes, the rate of services inflation can stay high and afect the rate of easing regardless. Can services inflation become a factor countering divergence?

To be sure, there are certainly common elements in the co-movement of services inflation across countries. For example, not only did the recovery in services demand lag the surge in goods demand post COVID-19 globally; but goods inflation directly passed onto services via input costs.

That said, services inflation persistence can also be linked to aggregate capacity utilisation. Indeed, one would expect, against the backdrop of the common global factors, countries with lower pressure on scarce resources to deliver lower rates of services inflation vis à vis countries with larger pressures.

We run a simple exercise comparing annual growth rates to levels of services inflation crosssectionally across G10 and EM countries. A simple scatter plot is indicative ( Figure 13). We find that services inflation tends to be associated with faster growing countries – with the exception of NJA countries (which never experienced enormous inflationary pressures to start, likely due to China's disinflation). The same conclusion can be drawn after substituting growth with output gap measures ( Figure 14).

In conclusion, our analysis supports the case for more policy divergence between excess demand countries (eg. the US and Australia) versus excess supply ones (eg. Sweden, Canada, New Zealand and the Eurozone).

## The price of divergence

With the above in mind, divergence (relative US outperformance) is still likely to favour the USD especially against the larger economies. And the closer we get to actual pivotal policy decisions, the more likely the market is to trade the relevant theme.

But how much can we expect the dollar to strengthen? A simple linear model mapping relative output gaps to the dollar ( Figure 2) would place EUR/\$ well below 1.10 but still above 1.00. Intuitively, this range makes sense. EUR/\$ was trading near parity when natural gas prices were exceptionally elevated and China was still in lockdown hurting European more than US growth. Large external shocks would be needed to revisit these low levels.

Equally, however, with the fair value of the EUR/\$ having declined to around 1.15 ( Figure 15), it is hard to think that the EUR should trade anything but undervalued in this cyclical happenstance.

Another way to cross-check this is by using our impulses model, which extracts the common factor in rates and equity markets for the US and the euro area respectively. We then map these common factors into EUR/\$ and identify the FX moves explained by each factor (which we refer to as "impulse"). As shown in Figure 16, we view a divergence between the euro area and the US as the diference between the two impulses, with EUR/\$ weakening the more negative the gap.

Looking back at four recent cycles since 2016, when the market traded the divergence between the US and Europe (Jan 2016-Feb 2017, Apr 2018-Oct 2018, Oct 2019-Mar 2020, Nov 2021-May 2022), our US and European factors drifted apart by 1.4%, while EUR/\$ dropped by an average of 4.2%.

Similar degrees of divergence would bring the EUR/\$ near or below 1.05, which is not far from our current forecast.

## The time is now

We argue that first principles imply that central banks will have diferent constraints in easing policy – favouring the USD. And that markets have not traded it. Also, that macro narratives regarding "limits to divergence" are not necessarily binding – especially for larger G10 economies.

The main loose thread to the thesis is why the market has not traded the underlying economic picture we describe so far.

We would contend that these trends may be hard for markets to pre-emptively price and that they are more likely to manifest as leads and lags in the actual process of easing policy.

In that sense, the closer we get to "live" meetings for each central bank, the larger the risk that the market adjusts in a step-wise function.

Yet, we are conscious that the time value of this view is limited. Within the second half of 2024, most central banks will have settled in their preferred mode and speed of easing. In that sense, we expect that our views will either play out in the months ahead – or we will be quickly proven wrong.

## From Br-eturn to Fr-exit; politics impact European currencies

Domestic political risk in the West was a key focus during the 12 years between the GFC and COVID-19 but has gradually given way to global geopolitics, starting with Trump's trade war then ramping up since Russia's invasion of Ukraine. In the past few weeks, however, surprise election results in selects EMs and the European Parliament election have refocussed attention on domestic political events.

We already analysed asset market implications of a Trump win at length in our last FX Outlook. Below, we take a closer look at upcoming political risk events in the UK and France, which entail contrasting fortunes for the EUR and GBP, respectively.

The upshot remains that EUR/\$ risks are mostly to the downside. French developments also weigh on CEE, while GBP and CHF tend to benefit.

## Sterling's "Br-eturn"

The 2016 Brexit referendum was a watershed moment for the pound. We have already argued that the disconnect between the currency and the Gilt curve since the referendum is a reasonable high-level proxy for the pound's Brexit premium ( Figure 17); which given Labour's current lead in the polls, for the first time in a while stands a reasonable chance of shrinking considerably following the UK general election on 4 July (if it is not already doing so in anticipation). We look at this in more detail below.

The fundamental intuition supporting the presence of a persistent Brexit premium for sterling is that the UK's departure from the EU amounts to a sizeable negative supply shock for the economy (per then-Governor Carney's 2018 IMF speech). This shock operates across all factors of production, from labour to capital, trade and productivity; it limits the economy's supply potential and trend growth; reduces the neutral rate; and, ultimately, results in weaker valuations for the pound versus the pre-Brexit equilibrium ( Figure 18). Even a partial reversal of that process would amount to a reverse positive supply shock and improve the pound's equilibrium, accordingly.

The UK labour market experienced a large squeeze as labour mobility was curbed since the end of the transition period in December 2020. That said, immigration flows from the EU dropped almost immediately after the 2016 referendum; and despite being ofset to some degree by an increase in non-EU arrivals, not all such arrivals ended up in the labour market ( Figure 19). As a result, there likely remains a sizeable gap versus pre-Brexit trends in labour supply, with its size a function of the share of EU migration absorbed by the labour market before 2016 ( Figure 20). Thus, COVID-related disruptions operated against, and amplified, an already constrained labour market backdrop.

In addition, this new migration mix may also be generating a "skills mismatch", to use BoE Chief Economist Huw Pill's term. The sharp shift outward of the UK's Beveridge curve since the pandemic could be capturing this dimension, along with other factors. The UK's Beveridge curve is only now beginning to re-converge with its pre-pandemic norm.

Investment also slowed sharply, and eventually stagnated, in the aftermath of the Brexit referendum, following a period of rapid re-convergence to the pre-GFC trend between 2012-16 ( Figure 21). Uncertainty regarding the UK’s eventual trade deal with the EU likely played a role, slowing down the expansion of the country's capital stock.

Lastly, Brexit weighed on trade directly across prices and quantities, by introducing direct extra costs to trade and, ultimately, reducing the economy's degree of openness. Additional costs and red tape are significant, with available estimates pointing to ad valorem tax equivalence between 10-40% depending on the trade barrier in question (see here) and a total cost from red tape c.10% of the value of exports (see here).

As for trade openness, although it has not dropped outright, it has lagged developments elsewhere. More specifically, the UK did not benefit from the surge in goods trade in the aftermath of the COVID lockdowns, with its degree of openness not very diferent from 2019, before the end of the Brexit transition period. By contrast, the Eurozone's own degree of openness is around 5% higher ( Figure 22). Unsurprisingly, subdued trade with the EU lies at the heart of this trend, which fails to track the pick-up in the openness with non-EU trade partners (most notably in services).

Inevitably, uncertainties abound with regards to the future of the UK-EU relationship: not only is the election outcome not known with certainty but the Labour party has also ruled out rejoining either the single market or the customs union. These pledges suggest that the pound’s Brexit premium is unlikely to be eliminated entirely and generate question marks with regards to the extent of improvements in the trading relationship that is either desirable locally in the UK or acceptable to the EU.

That said, the renegotiation of the TCA in 2026 and Labour’s declared openness to avenues such as a veterinary agreement or the mutual recognition of professional qualifications (which the current government did not pursue, for details of Labour's proposal please see here) suggest scope towards a closer relationship and a further modest – but non-negligible – Brexit premium unwind.

Consistent with the above, our new forecasts envisage sizeable gains for the pound versus the euro, with EURGBP reaching 0.80 in the coming quarters. French political risk is an additional tailwind for the pound as we explain below.

## Euro takes a wrong turn as France moves to the right

President Macron's decision to dissolve Parliament and call a snap Parliamentary election revived the spectre of political risk, this time in the very core of the Eurozone. For all the

uncertainty regarding the final outcome, initial polls suggest that Marine Le Pen's Rassemblement National is not far from absolute majority (for more details see here).

As we have recently argued, a fresh bout of French-driven risk will only square the risk profile for EUR/\$ to the downside. Our forecasts already envisage a trip down to 1.05-1.06 on the back of economic divergence between the US and Europe. But a fresh bout of political risk could allow for moves even below the recently established range of 1.05-1.11.

Essentially, a role of the RN in a next government could afect markets through four main transmission channels: 1) an erosion of EU institutions from within; 2) a bout of tensions between France, EU and member states; 3) contagion to other elections (eg. Germany in 2025); and importantly, 4) a deterioration in Russia-Ukraine-linked geopolitical risks and tensions.

Sovereign bond markets have catalysed (and invariably amplified) every single political crisis in the Eurozone since the GFC. This is no accident, as the creation of the euro without a fiscal union elevated the currency to reserve status but left a question mark in terms of sovereign state creditworthiness. In this way, sovereign spreads have become the main transmission channel to financial markets and other asset classes, including FX.

France has rarely been a source of political risk premium for the euro in the past 15 years, with the exception of a short spell around the 2017 Presidential election. Italy, however, could help illustrate the broad point equally well, given that its turbulent recent political past revolved around contagion worries and the relationship with EU authorities (predominantly regarding fiscal afairs) – all areas that are attracting market attention in this instance too.

Accordingly, we can directly detect periods during which BTP spreads became a statistically significant driver for the euro via risk premium swings in the intuitive direction (higher spreads, lower euro, and vice versa). These include the post-"whatever it takes" spread normalisation period after 2012; the Renzi constitutional referendum in late 2016; and, more strikingly, the 5 Star-Lega government in early 2018 ( Figure 23).

## EM FX: Higher-for-longer US rates becomes a real constraint

## Very little room for error for EMs as the Fed faces an economy above potential

Last quarter we turned cautious on EM rates on the view that markets were pricing enough without the all clear from US yields (see this note). The easy part of the disinflation process had already played out in many countries and the margins of safety were running thin without an impulse from the Fed. The story hasn't changed much since then. Emerging markets are stuck in an endless waiting game, where US data remains in the driver seat.

EMs are still (overall) pricing some normalisation in the policy stance, but the US has become a fundamental constraint for how much central banks can ease. Broad USD strength, driven by higher US risk-neutral rates and a still resilient economy, makes it hard for EM central banks to move too far (and especially too fast) ahead of the Fed. Figure 29 shows how the USD forced central banks to take a more cautious stance this cycle, with markets pricing out cuts in countries that are already easing and those that are still on hold. Currencies in countries that were already in the midst of a cutting cycle sold-of more as the easing cycles started and the USD strengthened last year, albeit behaviour has not been significantly diferent from those on hold, likely as cycles became more shallow across the region ( Figure 30).

Looking ahead, and as argued above, while the US economy is gradually moderating, it still operates above capacity. And any Fed normalisation will need to see policy rates stay above neutral for some time.

Central banks have opted to go slow putting more emphasis on mitigating FX volatility and preserving carry, especially as local pressures have not been screaming for fast cycles. There also appears to be some hesitation from the market to price more cuts, after all the swings we've seen in the past year and data dependence are keeping uncertainty high across the board.

In a world where terminal US rates settle higher, EM terminal policy rates are currently priced above some estimate of neutral across countries ( Figure 32), with most cutting cycles quite shallow (Brazil the only country pricing outright hikes).

## Fundamentally, high EM rates are not causing pain yet either

While asset prices are struggling with higher for longer, it's not clear that the same can be said of economic fundamentals. Real rates are optically high, but not so high relative to the US, and they have not been crushing growth or leading to a collapse in output-sensitive inflation measures.

The disinflation story has largely played out in EM, with inflation continuing to normalise ( Figure 33). However, base efects are unlikely to provide much of an impulse this year (see Embrace Goldilocks) and services still need to ease in many countries for inflation to converge to target. In the past, the level of services inflation consistent with periods when inflation was in the target range was lower in most countries, reflected in the red diamond in Figure 34. Median services inflation is typically a bit higher than this point, but also broadly lower than where it stands today. The main exceptions are South Africa, India, Philippines, Malaysia and, to a lesser extent, Chile. We caveat that Brazil's target today is lower than in the past, and thus it actually falls in the bucket of countries that require more efort. Thus, overall there are still risks to the last mile of the disinflation process.

## Where is the cycle compelling EM central banks to diverge vs the US?

Under the broad trend of EM stability, certain central banks are likely to face a larger domestic impulse for easing, driven by their own fundamentals.

In CEEMEA, we still believe in the decoupling story as German manufacturing remains weak ( Figure 36). Moreover, the region faces significant spillovers from European political turmoil led by France, weighing on the growth outlook via escalating geopolitical risk premium and less secure EC funding. Specifically in Czech, growth has been slow since the pandemic and core

services inflation has seen a dramatic drop in the recent print. Although wages remain sticky, we don't expect a major pass-through to inflation per our analysis. This leaves room for policy easing more than currently priced in by markets.

In Asia, Korea and Philippines are compelling candidates to out-dove market pricing. The BSP is a traditional inflation targeter and likely the bank to pivot to easing first in the region. The macro backdrop should turn more supportive – inflation falls into target range from August and as growth already shows signs of slowing on cumulative rate hike efects. Meanwhile, in Korea, concerns of a slowdown in the domestic part of the economy have intensified (project financing and macro-prudentials should have an economic drag). The BoK could be under pressure to ease, especially after Fed pivots, and markets are currently only pricing \~20bp of cuts this year.

In LatAm, recent political noise and deleveraging has added another layer to the story, which makes reading fundamentals more dificult. We still think Colombia has the most room to ease more than what's priced, with the central bank being the only one with a dual mandate in LatAm. Inflation is easing and has momentum in this direction, and growth has been slow. Authorities are still cautious, and likely to continue to be so as the region is under pressure, with Mexico's shock spilling over to risk assets. While in Mexico we had thought Banxico had space to price more easing, the ongoing political turmoil will make it hard for markets to price that.

A quick look at the recent cutting cycles suggests FX underperformance could be a better way to trade the theme vs lower rates, should easing pressures become more binding. In the past, the playbook was to chase easing cycles via rates from beginning to end, as neutral takes a while to get priced and central banks tend to go below neutral. This has clearly not been the case this cycle, partly on the US constraint and partly because markets have come to know this pattern and have repriced faster. Thus, we think the window to capture this move is short. Figure 37 shows that by the time the first cut (or further pricing) materialises, the cycle has been largely priced. We think this could be the case this time, as there is the risk that the shallow US cycle could again become the driver (assuming things in the US don't turn). And thus, it is better to get in the trade too early, in spite of high carry costs, if one has conviction.

Thus, we think that positioning for weaker currencies in these countries as the first cuts get closer is a good strategy to keep in mind.

Yet, as we discuss below, not all of the aforementioned economies can aford weaker currencies without triggering external debt pressures.

## Where are fiscal motivations compelling lower rates?

EM economies face the dilemma of whether to lower local policy rates in order to support nominal GDP growth and ease local debt constraints at the expense of the FX, or support the currency at the expense of growth and a heavier local debt burden. Policy easing could become constrained for countries facing pressures on the foreign currency debt front, but initial stocks look manageable across the board, such that this consideration is unlikely to bind.

We find that Mexico, Brazil, South Africa and Indonesia would see their fiscal stance benefit the most from lower policy rates. None of these cases look particularly constrained in the opposite direction by foreign currency debt considerations. Indonesia has a bufer to loosen policy rates without increasing the burden of debt in foreign currency, while Mexico, Brazil and South Africa would see their debt/GDP levels in foreign currency rise as the FX weakens, but the initial stock is low and unlikely to cause disruptive dynamics.

For countries where the cycle may compel easing (Czech, Korea, Philippines, Colombia), Philippines shows the highest foreign currency debt / GDP ratio, but low growth-adjusted real rates give space for manoeuvre. In Colombia, a weaker currency would likely increase the burden of a non-negligible foreign currency debt stock, but we also think this is unlikely to represent much of a constraint for the central bank at these still manageable levels.

In more detail, debt increased significantly during COVID across EM, and the period of elevated inflation has not been enough to bring a reduction in overall debt levels. Consequently, debt-to-GDP remains higher than it was before the pandemic for almost all EM economies ( Figure 39). At the same time, primary balances are expected to remain negative over the medium run, except for a few select cases ( Figure 40).

A basic debt sustainability assessment indicates that the majority of EMs are in a growing local debt-ratio trajectory given the expected fiscal and growth paths. The prescribed tool to address these potentially problematic dynamics is fiscal adjustment, while the central bank remains completely independent. But the reality can be diferent, and some central banks might face pressure to cut real rates and loosen the Treasury's fiscal constraint when it bites. Here, we apply the simple debt sustainability principle to local debt, and assume the fiscal deficit is financed in such a way as to keep the local to foreign currency debt ratio stable:

$$
\Delta D _ { t } = \bigg ( \frac { 1 + r } { 1 + g } - 1 \bigg ) D _ { t - 1 } - s _ { L }
$$

where r is the one year ex-ante real rate, g is the expected growth over the 2024-2028 period, D is the ratio of local debt-to-GDP and SL is the share of the 2024-2028 expected primary balance to be financed with local debt.

We take the primary balance and economic growth as given, and we calculate the theoretical real interest rate necessary to keep the local debt ratio stable. If the theoretical rate is lower than the current rate, this means that country is on a growing local debt path ( Figure 41). Our analysis suggests that Mexico, Brazil, South Africa and Indonesia need the bigger cuts in real funding rates to achieve a stable local debt path, given expected growth and fiscal deficits. On the flip side, Asian economies (Thailand, Malaysia, India, Philippines) have more fiscal space to prevent local debt from rising at current rates.

Emerging markets, however, use a mix of local and foreign denominated debt to finance their deficits. Thus, we also introduce a debt-sustainability formula for external debt:

$$
\Delta D _ { f t } = \Bigg ( \frac { ( 1 + i _ { U S D } ) * ( 1 + \Delta F X ) } { 1 + \pi + g } - 1 \Bigg ) D _ { f t - 1 } - s _ { F }
$$

For this analysis, we treat interest rates in foreign currency as given and focus on the FX. Economies where the debt ratios in foreign currency are in a declining trajectory (given rates, growth and primary balances) have more room to let their currency depreciate. Meanwhile, those where currency strengthening would be needed to keep foreign debt ratios stable are candidates to be more constrained. Figure 42 shows that South Africa, Brazil and Colombia are in growing foreign debt paths. However, both in Brazil and South Africa, the initial debt stock in foreign currency is below 10% of GDP (see Figure 43), making this specific angle unlikely to bind. At the other end of the spectrum, Asian currencies have more scope to tolerate currency depreciation while keeping foreign debt stable, given rates and the expected paths for growth and primary balances.

The local versus foreign currency financing mix varies significantly across economies ( Figure 43). Easing of local real rates would open more fiscal space from the local market, but at the same time the likely FX depreciation would tighten the space from the hard currency market. ## Where do politics pose additional risks in a world with no room for error?

Politics has also taken the stage in recent weeks, triggered by election surprises in Mexico, South Africa and India (in addition to already ongoing local noise in Brazil and geopolitical uncertainty in Israel), and now the political turmoil in France. Beyond implications at the country level, these shocks have led to a sharp FX carry unwind and positioning squeezes in rates, particularly in LatAm and CEE, with Asia largely spared. Election outcomes are proving to matter for markets that are heavily positioned, and we shouldn't forget that the one election this year that afects everyone is yet to come.

High US rates and a strong USD, along with a very heavily positioned market following a period of low volatility, means contagion through the portfolio channel is fast. We tend to think the market will need a breather, and volatility should come of in the coming weeks before focus shifts to the US elections. The questions then become how much these moves were an overshoot, and where the FX actually needs to find a new equilibrium that incorporates more risk premium; and whether countries that haven't moved yet need to catch up.

Moreover, when currencies undergo stress, it is natural to ask if there will be a policy response, either through rate hikes or FX interventions. Central banks typically use FX interventions when they consider the pressure to be the result of a market dysfunction. Hikes to stabilise currencies are usually used when they believe the shock could have a lasting impact that threatens the convergence of inflation to target.

Thus, as the dust settles, we consider the implications of the moves, given that the policy toolkit may be more limited with interest rates already high. Some central banks may just have to keep high rates for even longer, and the question then becomes if this is enough for the FX to stabilize without a global catalyst. Moreover, this higher uncertainty locally could also spill over to weaker growth. In these situations, the FX pass-through could be smaller as well, and any pressures on headline inflation should be dismissed as temporary given that core is unlikely to react as much in this scenario.

These factors could also be a source of diferentiation within EM:

• Mexico: The MXN has been under pressure since the presidential election, as the darling trade of EM gets unwound and markets incorporate the premium associated with AMLO's reforms. The correction came from very strong levels, and has not been completely out of line with fundamentals. We acknowledge the new level likely needs to be higher than before the shock to incorporate the high uncertainty around the new regime. Markets are pricing just one cut from Banxico this year now, from almost three before, reflecting higher-for-longer rates as well. Still, we expect some near-term relief in both rates and FX, given the magnitude of the moves and much negativism on the reforms already in the price, especially as markets will likely go back to pricing Banxico as a function of the Fed. However, the coming months will surely remain volatile.

We think the peso still has to weaken more for the authorities to step in with FX intervention. However, we see this as the more realistic policy response if the move remains exacerbated by a carry unwind, which needs to play out and are typically not stopped by rate hikes. FX intervention could help, if we get to that, but is unlikely to change the direction of travel. The real stabiliser needs to come from the politicians themselves, with reassuringly confident messaging. See this note for details.

• South Africa: The (unloved) ZAR was an exception in global carry unwind, benefiting from very light market positioning due to prolonged political uncertainty (this was the case for rates as well). Here, the elections were actually positively resolved: South African assets were boosted by formation of an ANC-DA coalition, which before the elections was believed to be the only positive tail risk. As a result, risk premium embedded in USDZAR compressed to less than 2.5%, the lowest level in two years. We expect this move to be extended, but the pace of further adjustment will likely be slower.

Moreover, the ZAR could experience the opposite of what we discuss above: a stronger ZAR amid better sentiment should open scope for markets to price in some probability of rate cuts, and continue to drive rates lower, in our view. The steepness of the ZAR curve is actually in line with the move in US rates, while local factors suggest it should be outperforming this global model, also pointing to room for relief in rates ( Figure 45).

• Brazil: The DI curve is the only curve in EM pricing the end of the easing cycle and a meaningful hike premium from there, with recent BCB actions lengthening the transition period to the next board (see this note for details). Real rate spreads to the US are close to the past end of cycles, where the BCB was thought to have over-cut as carry eroded and the fiscal outlook was deteriorating. The narrative today is not so diferent, with the country in the midst of a confidence crisis, as inflation expectations de-anchor and fiscal targets are under question. This is driving BRL underperformance as well.

The authorities are showing good will to rebuild credibility, but this will take time to be reflected in local assets, especially as local fund managers have limited firepower after recent moves. We thus think the BRL still needs a local premium in the FX and expect it to remain at these weaker levels for now.

While we don't expect hikes from the BCB at this point, we doubt markets are in a rush to price these out. Past cycles suggest the Jan 25/26 FRAs are pricing enough premium, and we expect front-end rates to remain in a range in the near term. An estimation of risk-neutral rates suggest there are still upside risks if break-evens continue to rise, even if levels look reasonable. Moreover, the term premium has lagged FX and fiscal risks ( Figure 46), suggesting the Jan 26/31s have room to steepen after largely flattening on the pricing out of cuts.

## USD

## FX Views

Themistoklis Fiotakis, Lefteris Farmakis

Concern about an abrupt US slowdown stands in the way of pricing more pronounced policy divergence and yet more dollar strength. We think macro fundamentals, particularly the starting activity levels of the US versus the rest of the world as reflected in the relative output gaps but also subdued global activity trends in the context of more protectionist economies, justify such divergence, with the start of easing cycles outside the US a likely near-term catalyst. European political risk adds to the dollar's appeal via risk aversion and the same goes for the US election given Trump's flagship policies. In all, we stick to our bullish dollar view for the coming quarters; and our bearish bias towards small open economies.

## Rates Views

## Anshul Pradhan

We have been neutral on duration for the past several weeks after being short earlier, as we believed ofsides short-duration positioning and inflation releases could result in a rally. We now recommend shorting 10y US Treasuries. We believe the economy is more likely to rebound than slow, as is widely believed, given both rising real income growth and household net worth. From a levels perspective, markets have already priced in a material easing cycle, with the real rate expected to fall well below the estimated short-run real neutral rate. While inflation progress should allow the Fed to ease this year, that alone would not justify delivering any more than adjustment cuts, in our view. The term premium has also fallen in the recent rally to levels not justified by fundamentals. We also recently took profit on our 5s30s curve steepener, as the curve has steepened materially and bullish catalysts are behind us.

## EUR

## FX Views

## Themistoklis Fiotakis, Lefteris Farmakis, Sheryl Dong

June's hawkish cut notwithstanding, given stickier inflation and wages data lately, the path of least resistance for the ECB remains for three cuts in 2024 — per our economists's call. This outlook is supported by stalling data flow from China, which casts doubts on the sustainability of the pick-up in the Eurozone's recovery momentum, and implies further policy divergence from the Fed (from practically none by end-2025 currently). What is more, French political turmoil adds to an already adverse political backdrop for the single currency ahead of the US elections in November. Our updated forecasts continue to envisage EURUSD hovering around 1.05-1.06 in the coming 12 months, with downside risks having increased due to the reemergence of Eurozone political risk.

## Rates Views

## Rohan Khanna, Max Kitson

The ECB's commitment issues to signal further policy easing had changed the mood-music in EUR duration with no clear bullish impulses, but the recent increase in political uncertainty has shifted the focus away from cadence and destination of policy easing. Once the French election hurdle is crossed (and without a market-unfriendly outcome), economic data and monetary policy path should again taken the front seat. We see EUR duration trading in ranges and the curve reflattening once the safe-haven bid subsides.

## JPY

## FX Views

## Shinichiro Kadota, Lhamsuren Sharavdemberel

USDJPY is likely to remain in an upward drift in the near term regardless of expected BoJ hikes/ QT and Fed cuts this year as the absolute foreign-domestic rate diferentials remain too wide to discourage relentless yen carry trades. Changes in capital flow dynamics, such as retail outward investment under New NISA tax exemption ofsetting more than half of the current account surplus and a structural digital deficit overshadow any positive efect from the FX-induced boom in inbound tourism. Concerns around FX interventions continue to be headwinds on the top side. In 2025, we expect the JPY to eventually escape the persistent downward pressures from carry as the BoJ and the Fed extend their hiking/cutting cycles to more meaningfully tighten rates diferentials. An upside risk to USDJPY is continued US exceptionalism and further delays in the Fed's cutting cycle while downside risks could materialize in case of global risk aversion given the elevated level of carry positions.

## Rates Views

## Shinichiro Kadota

We expect upward pressure on yields to continue on the back of BoJ rate hikes and QT. In the short- and medium-term sector, we continue to expect bear-flattening pressures to strengthen gradually with BoJ rate hikes. The long-term sector is vulnerable to overseas yields and QT and the boost to term premia from rinban reductions will likely continue to put upward pressure on yields, leaving the 10y sector prone to underperformance on the curve. Super-long yields have become more attractive relative to the liability cost of life insurers and to negative-carry FXhedged foreign bonds but life insurers have already largely filled their duration gaps, as indicated in FY23 results, and could therefore remain cautious with an eye on BoJ policy normalization. JGBs should continue to cheapen against swaps especially in shorter tenors (under 10ys) where BoJ presence is still large.

## GBP

## FX Views

## Lefteris Farmakis, Sheryl Dong

The UK's early general election is bringing forward the (partial) unwind of the pound's ample Brexit premium as markets begin to price in closer ties with the EU. BoE dovish risks continue to linger but we have found spillovers to the pound to be limited. With growth momentum improving and carry support likely to remain in place throughout the cutting cycle, we now envisage larger (and more front-loaded) pound outperformance. Our new forecasts envisage EURGBP finding a new equilibrium around 0.80 in coming quarters.

## Rates Views

## Moyeen Islam

In the UK, market pricing for the timing of the first cut has been complicated by both the forthcoming general election and also the relative resilience of services CPI and wages. With 40bp of cuts still priced for the second half of the year, the onus is on the MPC now to deliver easing that its own forecasts implied were necessary to return inflation to sustainably to target. Gilt yields face the challenge of a change of government as well heavy medium-term issuance. Given this backdrop, the resilience of longer-dated yields imply that term premium remains well-contained on the curve. However, with global fixed income facing significant event risk in H2 24, such stability should not be taken for granted.

## NZD

## FX Views

## Mitul Kotecha, Lefteris Farmakis

The RBNZ has so far been pushing out the start of its easing cycle due to sticky services inflation. In our analysis, however, it continues to face the largest dovish risks in the G10, consistent with a considerably larger slowdown in activity. As such, we see limits to the RBNZ's hawkish stance, with eventual capitulation likely amid faltering economic activity, a rising unemployment rate and falling wage growth. These factors, worsening terms of trade and a still-challenging environment for small open economies, result in our forecasts of underperformance for NZD versus both the USD and AUD.

## Rates Views

## Mitul Kotecha

We expect NZGB yields to gradually decline. Moderating inflation, and easing labour supply will likely support bond market sentiment. Dovish RBNZ risks, with potentially more rate cuts than markets currently price (currently just over one cut by end year), should also prove supportive. We expect the RBNZ to pencil in inflation returning to its target band of 1-3% by Q4 24.

Countering this are concerns over deteriorating growth prospects and fiscal prospects, implying supply challenges will persist. Accordingly, we forecast 10y NZGBs to stay closer to 4.5% by Q3 24 and reach 4.4% by end-Q2 25. We expect 2y NZGBs to reach 4.65% by end-Q3 24 and 4.5% by end-Q2 25. Moreover, rate diferentials versus the US have widened significantly, with the 1y1y diferential currently above its top decile since 2007. Further out, the market is pricing only one 25bp rate cut for the rest of 2024, and about four more in 2025. This would leave terminal close to 9.7%, way above Banxico's estimates of neutral (1.8 to 3.4% real, to which we would add 3.5% expected inflation) despite limited evidence of FX pass-through to inflation.

The fiscal impact of AMLO’s reforms in their current state is a question mark (particularly as to pensions and healthcare), with little available detail on the proposals. However, finance minister Ramírez de la O (who will remain in his position at the beginning of the new administration) has doubled down in his commitment to reduce the fiscal deficit from \~6% of GDP in 2024 to about half in 2025, and rating agencies have confirmed that institutional deterioration will be a slow burn, with no immediate implications for Mexico’s credit rating (Fitch has Mexico one notch above HY, while S&P and Moody's are two notches).

We think the belly of the curve could recover, as it should benefit from a downward repricing of Banxico’s rate path, while higher term premia associated with fiscal uncertainty and weaker institutions should be more evident in the back-end of the curve. We expect the ASW to remain wide for the time being, as foreign investors won’t rush into the local market until there is more clarity regarding the reforms.

## FX Views

## Marek Raczko, Sheryl Dong

We maintain our constructive structural view on the ILS because we see it as fundamentally undervalued. In the near term, however, ILS price action remains determined by geopolitical headlines. ## Rates Views

## Marek Raczko, Sheryl Dong

The BoI should err on the side of caution and not to ofer any more rate cuts this year, in our view.
