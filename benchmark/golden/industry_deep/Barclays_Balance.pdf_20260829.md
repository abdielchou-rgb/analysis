# Global Portfolio Manager's Digest Striking a Balance

We provide context and perspective on research across regions and asset classes, this week highlighting our thoughts on what could turn sentiment positive on the "AI trade"; adjusting our call following the Fed's decision to cut rates by 50bp; and what the Fed's move means for Asia markets.

• What's Next for the "AI Trade": The bull case around AI boils down to a simple concept: if scaling laws hold and frontier model performance continues to improve at great leaps over coming generations, then end-user products in consumer and enterprise will similarly improve greatly in terms of functionality and accuracy and adoption will increase. To that end, we see GPT-5’s upcoming release as a potential catalyst to get things going again. If successful, sentiment could shift greatly, AI hyperscaler capex would need to ramp further, and all this investment will likely end up being another expensive moat for mega-cap tech. Further, we think that another recent breakthrough around test-time-compute could usher in a new sub-category of frontier AI lab innovation around finding a more optimal mix of training and inference compute, which could dramatically reduce the capex needs of the overall industry, but it's too early to tell. An alternative view, and much more bearish take, is that scaling laws don’t hold up, the foundation models stop improving, and the capex wave stops. In this scenario, GPT-5 would likely be introduced in late 2024 or early 2025 to great fanfare, only to see the same kind of experimental enterprise AI workloads running on top of it but no killer apps or broad-based consumer/enterprise adoption.

## What's Next in AI? GPT-5, MOAR GPUs, Test-Time-Compute, and Avoiding the AI Winter

Ross Sandler, BCI, US | Trevor Young, CFA, BCI, US | Alex Hughes, BCI, US | Joseph Petroline, BCI, US | Michael DiSanto, BCI, US | Tom O'Malley, BCI, US | Scott Fessler, BCI, US | Kyle Bleustein, BCI, US

Excerpted from U.S. Internet & Semiconductors: What's Next in AI? GPT-5, MOAR GPUs, Test-Time-Compute, and Avoiding the AI Winter, published on September 17, 2024

The investment community has soured a bit on AI based on the slow end-user adoption, high cost to serve, and capex vs. revenue mismatch. While this kind of time lag is normal for big technology transitions, in this report we take a stab at “where could we go from here?” and what could turn sentiment back up.

The Key Take-Away: The market cap exposed to “the AI trade” is enormous, with five of the largest companies in the world duking it out for pole position. The fate of the NASDAQ (and to a lesser degree the S+P 500) depends on which way “the AI trade” goes over coming quarters and years. We have already seen a quick sentiment shift following the publishing of our first in this series of AI reports (see “FOMO or Field-Of-Dreams,” from June). Some in the investment community (including us) are currently asking: Are we entering a trough of disillusionment or just in an air pocket? What could bring back the animal spirits witnessed in 2023 and early 2024? This report attempts to predict what could play out next in AI. We unpack a bunch of new important things that help explain where we are on the AI capacity vs. uptake debate, key things that could impact future compute needs, and clear up some of the confusion given the limited disclosures from the biggest players in the space.

We were somewhat taken aback when Character.ai more or less threw in the towel and its senior staf joined Google. After all, their founder was a lead inventor of the transformer back in the day at Google (the “T” in GPT), and Character.ai was generally perceived to be the #2 company in the consumer AI space behind ChatGPT, measured by users and engagement. After similar high-profile flame-outs at Inflection and others, it begs the question of whether the cost of compute in AI is just too great, and more broadly if we are headed into an “AI Winter” or if these are simply normal bumps along the road for any new space riddled with start-ups. We think the latter. We see more catalysts, like the GPT-o1 model release from OpenAI this past week, coming over the next few months that could re-invigorate bullish investor sentiment.

## September FOMC: A 50bp re-calibration

Marc Giannoni, BCI, US | Jonathan Millar, BCI, US | Pooja Sriram, BCI, US | Colin Johanson, BCI, US

Excerpted from Federal Reserve Commentary: September FOMC: A 50bp re-calibration, published on September 19, 2024

The FOMC initiated its rate-cutting cycle today with a larger-than-expected 50bp cut and an SEP showing 100bp of cuts this year, followed by 100bp in 2025, 50bp in 2026 and a longer-run dot at 2.9%. We adjust our rate call, showing two more 25bp cuts this year, followed by three 25bp cuts in 2025.

The FOMC initiated its rate-cutting cycle today with a 50bp cut, against our expectation of a 25bp cut, in an efort to re-calibrate the policy stance and to maintain the labor market at full employment.

The FOMC statement suggested that future rate cuts are likely to be in 25bp steps, by showing no alarm about the cooling labor market, and mentioning that risks to achieving "employment and inflation goals are roughly in balance."

The new Summary of Economic Projections (SEP) also suggested a slower pace of rate cuts going forward, with two more 25bp rate cuts this year, four 25bp cuts in 2025, and two cuts in 2026. The overall projected rate path is lower than in the June SEP, reflecting lower inflation and higher unemployment projections.

FOMC participants lifted again their longer-run dot to 2.875%, from 2.75% in June and 2.55% in March. We think this formalizes an upshift in views about the neutral rate that has been signaled by the 2026 median dot for some time now.

In the press conference, Powell emphasized that the 50bp cut was designed to re-calibrate the policy stance, but did not signal large rate cuts in the future. He reiterated that the FOMC will make decisions meeting by meeting, that the timing and pace of rate cuts will depend on incoming data, the evolving outlook, and the balance of risks, and referred to the SEP for guidance about the baseline policy rate path.

In light of today's large cut and the new SEP, we adjust our rate call. We continue to expect that the FOMC will cut rates 25bp at the November and the December meetings, as well as three times in 2025 – in March, June and September. However, given today's larger-thanexpected rate cut, our forecast interest-rate path is now 25bp lower, reaching 4.25-4.50% at end-2024, and 3.50-3.75% at end-2025. Our baseline is predicated on inflation remaining moderate on a sequential basis and the unemployment rate edging up this year before declining to 4.2% in 2025.

## Fed cuts 50bp, what does it mean for Asia?

Mitul Kotecha, Barclays Bank, Singapore | Shinichiro Kadota, BSJL, Japan | Yimin Weng, CFA, Barclays Bank, Singapore | Kaanhari Singh, Barclays Bank, Hong Kong | Brian Tan, Barclays Bank, Singapore | Jian Chang, Barclays Bank, Hong Kong | Yingke Zhou, Barclays Bank, Hong Kong

Excerpted from Cross-Asset Research: Fed cuts 50bp, what does it mean for Asia?, published on September 19, 2024

We expect USD/Asia FX to trade softer in the short term and are biased to receive rates on any sell of. A more aggressive Fed should have direct implications for monetary policy in Indonesia. We expect Asia credit to trade with a firm tone. For Asia equities, this favors inflows into relatively cheaper valuation plays.

• The Fed started its easing cycle with a 50bp cut that was bigger than most forecasters expected. However, market pricing was veering towards a larger cut over recent days; pricing had been at around a 65-70% for a 50bp cut. In the event, Fed Chair Powell did not indicate that the Fed was about to embark on an aggressive easing path, with terminal-rate pricing largely unchanged.

• Our economists continue to expect that the FOMC will cut rates 25bp at each of the November and the December meetings, as well as three times in 2025 (see September FOMC: A 50bp re-calibration). However, given today's larger-than-expected cut, our forecast interestrate path is now 25bp lower. We examine the impact on Asian markets and central bank policy in this publication.

• Asia FX/rates: We do not rule out further bouts of USD weakness in the weeks ahead and expect overall downward pressure on USD/Asia FX to be sustained. Much had been priced in terms of Asian FX gains ahead of the Fed decision, yet the USD remains a sell on rallies versus Asia FX. We maintain our view that after further USD/Asia FX weakness in Q4, there will a reversal into H1 2025. In Asian rates we are biased to receive on any sell-ofs.

• JPY: The outlook for the pace of Fed cutting cycle and the path of broad USD remain uncertain, depending on the incoming US data. We think the presumed USDJPY recovery next year may have become front loaded if markets' peak dovishness proves to have already passed. However, LDP election on 27 September poses some risks to the JPY.

• Credit strategy: We expect Asia credit to trade with a firm tone. We expect the recent HY-IG decompression to pause and expect investors to add risk through longer dated bonds.

• Asset allocation: In the short-term, the re-calibration in bonds is likely to be the main driver of cross-asset price action. For global equities, we expect this to translate into a continued bid for lagging Value plays as growth stocks will remain challenged on valuations and crowding. For Asia heading into year-end, this favors inflows into relatively cheaper valuation plays in the region.

Value vs. Growth, Post-Rate Cut

Venu Krishna, CFA, BCI, US | Japinder Chawla, CFA, BCI, US | Rex Feng, BCI, US | Madhuresh Rai, CFA, BCI, US | Young Chen, BCI, US

Excerpted from U.S. Equity Strategy: Food for Thought: Value vs. Growth, Post-Rate Cut, published on September 20, 2024

In past non-recessionary rate cutting cycles, median Value returns surpassed those of Growth immediately after the "first" rate cut (t+1m to t+3m), but inconsistently. Past t+3m, Growth outperformance takes over and with more consistency, i.e. there could be more Growth upside ahead if we are on track for a soft landing.

Fed rate cuts have begun, which imply more upside for Growth vs. Value over the medium term if historical trends hold. We use Barclays L/S equal weighted sector-capped factor baskets for this analysis. In our previous work, we found that Growth typically outperforms Value heading into the "first" rate cut of an easing cycle; however, style performance has been much more volatile this time around. Looking ahead, we focus on historical instances of nonrecessionary rate cuts, since a soft landing for the US remains Barclays economists' base case. In such scenarios, median Value returns surpassed those of Growth immediately after the "first" rate cut (t+1m to t+3m), but this excess performance was not very consistent, as shown by the standard deviation range in the chart above. Growth outperformance begins to take over after the t+3m mark, with more consistency. Our interpretation of these results is that while both Value & Growth stocks can benefit from rolling of peak rates, the upside for Growth stocks is likely higher in non-recessionary scenarios. Risks to our thesis include the upcoming US Presidential election and further upside in SPW/SPX returns, both of which have been historically beneficial for Value.

## Europe

Don't fight the Fed, don't short Cyclicals

Emmanuel Cau, CFA, Barclays, UK | Matthew Joyce, CFA, BBI, Paris | Magesh Kumar Chandrasekaran, CFA, Barclays, UK | Emmanuel Makonga, Barclays, UK | Arihanth Bohra Jain, Barclays, UK

Excerpted from Equity Market Review: Don't fight the Fed, don't short Cyclicals, published on September 20, 2024

It seems clear now that the Fed has shifted to growth preservation mode. Time will tell, but with incoming data holding up, a soft landing stays on track. The rebound in activity surprises and positive year end seasonality may give legs to the short squeeze in Cyclicals. Now focus turns to Sep PMIs and upcoming Q3 earnings.

Don't fight the Fed. Fed's surprise 50bp cut and accompanying message seem clearly designed to pull out all the stops to achieve a soft landing. Risk assets' initial reaction has been positive, which along the bear steepening of the yield curve, suggest markets are buying into this scenario - the Fed put is in play. However, compared to the 'new dots' showing a slower pace of rate cuts going forward, with only two more 25bp cuts this year and four in 2025 (see September FOMC: A 50bp re-calibration, 19 Sep), the rates path reflected into current market pricing looks rather aggressive to us. Incoming data will impact these expectations, and if it confirms the recent bounce in the US, we doubt the Fed will cut as much as currently priced in. But for the time being, absent a catalyst to challenge the soft landing script, we believe the path of least resistance for equities and Cyclicals is to the upside. As we discussed in our report earlier this week (see What next after the first cut, 18 Sep), equities and Cyclicals typically rebounded steadily after the Fed started its rate cut cycle...as long as it was not followed by a recession.

## Calibrating the cycle (part II)

Christian Keller, Barclays, UK | Mark Cus Babic, BCI,US | Akash Utsav, Barclays, UK

Excerpted from Global Economics Weekly, published on September 20, 2024

The Fed initiated its easing cycle with a 50bp cut, signalling its focus on achieving a soft landing. The BoE and BoJ stood pat, as expected. Next week, US PCE, European PMIs and Japan's election are key as markets assess the Fed's next steps, chances for an October ECB cut, and the future of BoJ policy normalisation.

## Global easing cycle in full swing

## The Fed 'recalibrates' with a 50bp cut and focus on employment

Over a year ago, the central bank of Chile became the first larger central bank to cut rates. It was then joined by other EM central banks, such as Brazil, and increasingly those from advanced economies, more recently the ECB and BoE. This week, the global easing cycle entered a new stage, with the Fed joining. While the move was well flagged, its importance is hard to overstate, given the Fed's role in USD liquidity conditions worldwide.

This makes it all the more significant that the Fed started the cycle with a 50bp cut. Although market-implied odds had shifted towards 50bp after the press reports late last week, the market had priced only 31bp on the eve of the Fed's blackout period a week earlier and just 27bp following the firmer core CPI inflation print last Wednesday. Apparently, the Fed's own communication, in combination with the incoming data, had not led markets to expect a 50bp step.

Whether the Fed's reliance on using journalists to prepare markets – even in the absence of any major data surprise during the blackout period – could backfire at some point is a separate question. For now, we note that initiating a cycle with a 50bp move without an imminent financial crisis or jobs actually being lost is quite unusual for the Fed. It also stands out in the global context: out of 21 larger central banks that have begun easing in this cycle, 16 started with a 25bp cut. We thus think the step reveals the Fed's determination to avoid a deterioration in labour market conditions as inflation returns to the 2% target, or, in market jargon: to achieve a soft landing.

For his part, Chair Powell characterised the 50bp cut as a "re-calibration", aimed at bringing policy to a more neutral stance, given that the risks to inflation and employment are now seen as balanced. But the notable rise in the unemployment rate likely contributed to the rush to recalibrate. In the six months ending in August, the US unemployment rate had risen 0.4pp, which is a larger increase than the majority of other central banks were contending with at the point when they first cut rates. Moreover, while every cycle is diferent and historical regularities have limited predictive power, Figure 13 shows that when facing such an increase in unemployment in the past, developed market central banks have on average cut by 50bp. Tellingly, the Fed changed the statement to indicate its strong commitment to supporting maximum employment.

## 25bp cuts from here to reach 3.50-3.75% by September 2025

Regarding the pace going forward, the Fed signalled that 25bp steps are the base case. The median dot in the SEP, which Powell explicitly endorsed as the base-case path, shows 100bp of cuts this year (incl. the 50bp this week) and 100bp next year. In addition, the median projection for the unemployment rate at the end of this year was lifted to 4.4%, which gives the Fed some breathing room before further deterioration in the labour market would constitute a surprise requiring a forceful response. In the same vein, Powell characterised unemployment rates "in the low fours" as a good labour market.

We agree that 25bp per meeting is the most likely pace for the rest of the year. Next year, we expect 25bp cuts once a quarter, in March, June, and September, with the cycle ending at 3.50-3.75%. Beyond communication, which can be fickle, the primary reason for this is that we still see the US economy as very solid. In fact, given the larger-than-expected cut and the broader easing in financial conditions this week, we revise up our US growth forecasts, nudging up the quarterly pace between Q4 24 and Q4 25 by 50bp each, implying 2.1% growth in 2025, compared with 1.7% before (2024 annual average is unchanged at 2.6%). Amid healthy activity, we continue to expect the unemployment rate to peak only 0.2pp higher than it is today (in Q4 24 and Q1 25), before decelerating over the course of 2025.

This is backed by incoming data, which signal resilience. This week, control group retail sales were reported to have risen a solid 0.3% m/m in August, following a July estimate that was nudged up 0.1pp to 0.4% m/m, positioning consumer spending for another quarter of firm growth in Q3. In addition, industrial production rebounded strongly, rising 0.8% m/m, showing clearer signs that that the weak July reading reflected various disruptions from Hurricane Beryl and that the sub-50 ISM manufacturing prints understate true industrial activity.

Next week, we expect August real PCE to print at 0.0% m/m, slower than the likely upwardly revised and elevated readings in the prior months, but still implying a very solid increase in Q3 as a whole. We expect core PCE inflation to print at 0.14% m/m.

## BoE holds now, but likely resumes cuts in November

The Bank of England held Bank Rate steady this week at 5% and afirmed its intention to cut gradually. More broadly, this week's BoE meeting brought little news. With the Monetary Policy Summary and Minutes remaining cautious, we think the modal voter is still inclined to vote for a 25bp cut in November, but absent a large surprise, we think a 50bp cut is of the table. That said, with GDP growth decelerating, the labour market continuing to loosen, and the Committee gaining confidence in wage and price disinflation, we think the MPC will accelerate the pace to sequential 25bp cuts in December and February.

One factor afecting this could be a headline inflation forecast that is revised down in the November MPR on the back of lower energy prices. Indeed, in our forecast, we largely fade the small upside surprise in this week's August CPI print and adjust lower our headline projections, with energy being the largest driver.

## What could trigger an ECB cut in October?

For the ECB, the all-time low unemployment rate together with stubborn services inflation have contributed to a cautious cutting cycle of only 25bp per quarter so far. However, with signs that the Governing Council is becoming more concerned about the growth outlook and subtly more confident about the disinflation process, the decision to accelerate the pace to 25bp per meeting in October remains live.

We think that a September HICP print in line with the ECB's forecast (1.99-2.28% y/y for the headline and 2.86-3.15% y/y for core) is a necessary but not a suficient condition for this. Instead, back-to-back cuts could be brought on by a notably lower inflation print or one that is line but combined with weaker PMIs, an increase in the unemployment rate, or negative growth surprises in global demand. Conversely, an upside surprise in September HICP compared with the ECB's forecast would likely rule out an October cut. We think the Fed's 50bp cut and the ECB's expectation of the Fed's future pace of cuts will play a second-order role in the decision of the Governing Council. Regardless of the October decision, we expect the ECB to move to sequential cuts from December to March.

In this context, all eyes will be on the range of activity indicators released next week, including September flash PMIs, the European Commission survey, the German ifo survey, and French household consumption of goods. We will also focus on the preliminary HICP readings in France and Spain, where lower energy prices could bring a notable deceleration (due partly to base efects).

## Some EM central banks have already reacted to easier Fed policy

In emerging markets, the more aggressive Fed cut this week may have direct implications for monetary policy. Already this week, Bank Indonesia (BI) surprised with a 25bp cut, citing that the Fed would likely cut more aggressively than it had expected earlier. The South African Reserve Bank also cut 25bp this week, as expected, while the Central Bank of Turkiye (CBT) kept the policy rate stable, but delivered a statement with some dovish tilts, signalling that it is getting prepared to start easing in Q4 24.

We now think BI will cut in all three of its remaining meetings this year. The 50bp Fed cut also cements our view for an earlier-not-later BSP cut, it allows the BoK to front-load easing, and it should further redirect the CBC away from additional tightening. That said, we maintain our view that it will have little efect on the monetary policy calculus for the RBI, BNM, BoT, and MAS. In smaller developed markets, Sweden and Switzerland look set to cut 25bp next week, while the Reserve Bank of Australia is likely to remain on hold.

## More PBoC easing to come, even if only incremental

For China, things are more complicated. On the one hand, its data are going from bad to worse. Following last week's record-low credit growth data, this week's activity data for August also surprised to the downside, showing broad-based deterioration, with weaker domestic demand spilling over to the supply side as manufacturers begin to scale back. Retail sales growth stayed below 3% for the third straight month, weakening a significantly 0.6pp to only 2.1% y/y. Fixed asset investment also slowed more than expected, to a year-to-date low of 3.4% y/y, with moderating manufacturing and infrastructure investment and a continued double-digit contraction in property investment. Amid the deterioration in domestic demand, industrial production growth weakened by 0.5pp to a six-month low of 4.5% y/y, marking the fourth consecutive month of slowdown. Overall, we think the risks to our Q3 GDP growth forecast

(4.5% y/y) are skewed to the downside. Moreover, the 50bp Fed cut creates a window for the PBoC to ease without undesirably depreciating the currency.

On the other hand, the PBoC still sees itself as constrained by financial stability concerns (therefore likely not welcoming a further decline in CGB yields this week) and banks' low net interest margins. In addition, unlike former governor Yi Gang, no sitting PBoC oficials have highlighted deflationary pressures.

On balance, we thus think the PBoC's policy easing will remain incremental. We expect the PBoC to deliver a 50bp cut in banks’ reserve requirement ratio (RRR) next week or in the one after, followed by another 50bp cut in H1 25. We also stick to our earlier rate cut call, expecting another three 10bp cuts in the 7d OMO, 1y MLF and 1y LPR rates, in Q4 24 and in Q1 and Q2 25, with the first of these likely after Q3 GDP data (18 October).

## The few who hike

## BoJ aims to continue normalising policy after political uncertainty fades

The central banks of Japan and Brazil are the only ones hiking. That said, for the former, this is a delicate cycle, as the BoJ has to navigate not only economic data, but also politics and market volatility, as demonstrated by the large swings caused by its previous hike in July. In light of this, the BoJ held the unsecured overnight call rate steady this week at 0.25%, as expected, accompanied by communication that included a mix of hawkish and dovish elements. For example, while the BoJ is clearly still committed to policy normalisation, Governor Ueda took a cautious stance toward early rate hikes, citing the recent correction of earlier JPY weakness and the need to assess economic, price and financial conditions.

This implies that the BoJ will take the time to assess the pass-through of labour costs to services prices, which will be seen in CPI data from October onward, and will also want to wait to gather information on the outlook for FY25 "shunto" wage hikes, to be reported at the January branch managers' meeting. In addition, it will wait for political uncertainty to clear. Part of this should happen next week, when the LDP race comes to an end. Still, whoever is the new leader may front-load the general election to this autumn, creating another obstacle to a move in October, besides the US election on 5 November.

In all, we thus still believe the next BoJ hike will not come until in January, though there is a risk of an earlier move in December, should October nationwide CPI, October and November Tokyo CPI, and December Tankan data show strong signs of pass-through of labour costs to services inflation.

## Brazil's BCB starts a shallow hiking cycle

In Brazil, the BCB kicked of a tightening cycle this week with a 25bp increase in the Selic rate to 10.75%, in line with expectations. While it did not provide guidance on the pace or total size of the adjustment, we continue to expect a relatively small cycle of 150bp, with the pace accelerating to 50bp in November and the Selic rate topping of at 12.00% in January.

## Avis Budget Group Inc. (CAR; EW/Neu) / Hertz Global Holdings Inc. (HTZ; UW/Neu)

Same old rentals or transformed industry? Initiating coverage of US rental car industry, EW CAR, UW HTZ

We launch coverage of the public US car rental stocks Avis Budget Group (CAR) and Hertz Global Holdings (HTZ). We believe the central debate for the US car rental industry is whether it’s now a transformed industry or the same one we saw when covering the industry in the prior decade. After a robust post-COVID era in 2021-‘23, in which the industry saw elevated profits given a “best of both worlds” combination of elevated pricing and low fleet costs, results have reversed in 2024, putting the path forward into question. In evaluating whether the industry has structurally improved vs. pre-COVID, we believe it is worthwhile to evaluate the industry along six debates – 1) asset maximization, 2) pricing power, 3) fleet costs/depreciation, 4) competitive dynamics, 5) disruption risk, and 6) technology adoption. In sum, while we believe the industry has made some improvements, additional proofpoints are needed to demonstrate that the industry has truly gone through a transformation. We believe it will take time to demonstrate these proofpoints, thus underpinning our Neutral view on the US car rental industry.

Upgrade to Overweight; 5 Catalysts We Want To Be Ahead of
