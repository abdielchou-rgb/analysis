# Global Rates Strategy

## Rates Map: Stay long as central banks move slowly

Since we wrote that longs are attractive again at the start of March, UBS has shifted its expectations for the first rate cut by the ECB and the Fed to June (Figure 2). Nevertheless, updated ECB macro forecasts showed larger-than-expected downgrades in core inflation and the revisions to US jobs data wiped out January's strength.

UBS expects another hot US inflation print of 0.47% mom (consensus: 0.4% mom) on 12 March but for now we expect the Fed's dot plot to still show 3 cuts in 2024. Many clients believe that a shift to 50bps of cuts in the 2024 dot plot would lead to a significant repricing higher in global rates. It will take time to get more clarity but there are good reasons to believe US disinflation momentum will gather pace again after February (housing inflation is set to slow, wages are trending down, and the economy is slowing). This would be consistent with what we see globally.

## Long across the euro area bond curve but more value in front end

Last week was a big week for macro, but our views are little changed since the start of the month. Longs look increasingly attractive, but we have higher confidence in the front end, especially in Europe. We pushed back our timing for a first cut by the ECB from April to June and now expect 75 bps of cuts in 2024 and 100 bps of cuts for 2025 but with a risk of more cuts in 2024. We had not expected the ECB to downgrade its core inflation projections across the forecast horizon, by 0.1pp to 2.6% for 2024, 0.2pp to 2.1% in 2025 and 0.1pp to 2% in 2026 without a strong signal for a cut in April. In any case, we see risks of more cuts and like to receive the Dec '25 meeting as a slower start of the ECB will delay the transmission of easier monetary policy.

## Earlier cuts if needed after a relatively sharper year-to-date rise in euro area yields

Still subdued growth and incoming inflation data are setting the stage for an earlier cut by the ECB compared to the Fed, if required (Figure 4). For now, we think that our earlier arguments on why the10y US Treasury vs Bund spread is set to stay wide still hold. We have pointed to the relative resilience of the US economy, relative inflation dynamics, and central bank outlook. In the most recent World at a Glance, we emphasized that the gap between US real GDP growth and the rest of the G7 over the past 6 months is at a 20-yr high. The central thesis of most of the UBS research trading calls is that this cannot last, but so far the data disagrees. In our view, the risk reward over the next few months suggests taking a cautious view on equities and re-establishing received positions in fixed income. Euro area yields have also seen a relatively sharper increase, with 2y bund yields 35 bps higher in 2024 compared to a 23 bps increase in 2y US yields.

## Could the flattening momentum stop?

Current levels could also offer attractive steepening opportunities in the euro area as we are getting more clarity on the ECB's easing cycle. The front end of the EUR curve, such as EUR 2s10s, has moved together with policy rate expectations compared to curve segments at the longer end. EUR curves remain highly inverted historically, and less able to withstand the current level of inversion compared to the US (Figure 7). We think that bull steepening is a likely scenario as our baseline of a first ECB cut in June materializes. Steepeners at the longer end of the curve, such as EUR 10s30s, could offer better protection in case of further repricing in policy rate expectations and due to more favourable carry/roll-down. In any case, growth and inflation data suggest that the current level of yield inversion in German 2s10s (at -50 bps) is materially tighter for the economy compared to US 2s10s at -42 bps.

## Net sovereign supply - Waiting for news on the structural bond portfolio

Ms Lagarde said that the ECB would likely present its new operational framework for steering short-term rates on 13 March and investors will pay close attention to how the ECB plans to provide short-term, medium-term and longer-term ("structural") liquidity.

We have previously made the point that there has been a relatively smooth absorption of net supply of EGBs, including ECB QT in 2023. Sovereign bond holdings of banks remain near historical lows compared to capital and higher yields have attracted foreign and domestic investors, with holdings suggesting further absorption capacity. Nevertheless, a key question is how the ECB plans to maintain a "structural bond portfolio" to provide liquidity on a more permanent basis. For now, the supply of "10y duration equivalents" to the market as the ECB’s balance sheet rolls off has been manageable (Figure 9 .

## UK: SONIA December 24 vs 25 curve could flatten further

We still favour SONIA December 24 vs 25 curve flatteners, anticipating more cuts to be priced in for 2025. The MPC’s focus on services inflation, wages and the health of the labour market implies a more gradual easing cycle from the BoE. This segment of the SONIA strip has flattened by more than 10bps since last week but still looks relatively steep compared to the US and Eurozone.

Earlier in February, we also recommended to fade the steepening of the SONIA March 24 vs December 24 curve vs EURIBOR. The trade moved in our favour since then but reverted back to the trade's opening level following the ECB meeting last week and a downward revision in the ECB's inflation forecast, which was larger than we had expected. The market is currently pricing in around 65bps of cuts from the BoE by yearend, which does not seem excessive in our view. Ahead of the MPC meeting on 21 March, labour market data (due on 12 March) and the inflation report (due on 20 March) could provide some support to the UK OIS front end. However, it may be harder for the trade to outperform in the upcoming weeks. Hence, we turn neutral on this recommendation.

Alongside the Spring Budget this week, the DMO published the Gilt remit for FY24 (more details below). The remit is forecast to reach £265.3bn for the upcoming year, which is nearly £33bn more than the gross gilt supply in FY23. In our view, the increased supply would keep the cheapening pressure on Gilt ASWs, while the remit's greater allocation towards medium sector (in relative terms) is expected to cheapen rates in the belly relative to the wings of the curve.

## Gilt issuance to reach £265bn for FY24, £33bn higher than in FY23

The gilt remit is projected to reach £265.3bn for the upcoming financial year, approximately £7bn higher compared to our £258bn estimate. This marks the secondhighest gross gilts issuance after the pandemic, and £33bn of additional gross gilt supply compared to FY23. Following the announcement, gilts rallied across the curve, with shorts leading the moves. This likely indicates that the market had built expectations of a somewhat higher gilt remit. The rally in shorts likely reflects the decision to slightly reduce the share of shorts in the overall allocation and no new bill issuance.

The largest share of the gilt remit, approximately £226.4bn, will be allocated to conventional gilts, around £28.9bn in the index-linked gilts, while the remaining is provisioned as unallocated (£10bn). Using our subjective judgement and recent historical gilt issuance pattern, we expect around £97.5bn in the conventional gilts shorts (37% of the total), around £85.8bn in medium conventional gilts (32%), around £52bn in long gilts (20%) and around £30bn (11%) in index-linked gilts in FY24.

This breakdown implies an increase in the share in mediums in the overall allocation compared to FY23 (i.e., 32% vs 28%), while the share of shorts, longs and index-linked gilts will be around 1-2% lower compared to FY23. Furthermore, we estimate an £11mm increase is gross gilt issuance in DV01 terms relative to previous FY. In particular, we estimate the shorts gross supply to increase by 3.5mm in DV01 terms, mediums by £14mm, while we expect inflation-linked supply to remain unchanged and longs to reduce by £6.5mm.

## CHF: SARON Mar 24 vs Dec 24 flatteners now more balanced

We have been recommending flatteners in the SARON March 24 vs December 24 curve for some time due to the Swiss inflation outlook and dovish comments from SNB officials earlier this year. We previously argued that the repricing of policy rate expectations globally, driven by more resilient data, would make it more challenging for the SNB to cut rates in March. This week, the market pricing has removed nearly 7bps from the March SNB meeting, resulting in further flattening in the Mar 24 vs Dec 24 curve. While this part of the strip could flatten more, as the market is still pricing in c.9bps of cuts for the March SNB meeting, we think that most of the move is behind us. The market is currently pricing in around 70bps of SNB cuts by year-end, which is close to our baseline scenario of 75bps. Considering that a March cut cannot be ruled out, we now see a more balanced risk/reward profile for this trade.

Although the ESTR vs SARON spread has tightened in the past few days, particularly in the front end with the 1y1y ESTR vs SARON spread c.6bps tighter this week, we think the ECB's focus on the persistent components of inflation could further weigh on EUR rates. The divergence in policy rate differentials has been the key driver of the widening in the ESTR vs SARON spread since the beginning of the year. Given there is a risk that the SNB cuts rates in March, the spread is likely to remain at relatively wide levels leading up to the March SNB meeting. From a valuation point of view, the ESTR vs SARON spread is currently at historically wide levels. The SNB's capacity to significantly cut its policy rate is more constrained in comparison to that of other major central banks, including the ECB. Therefore, we expect the ESTR vs SARON spread to compress further over time, and we will monitor developments to enter into ESTR vs SARON tighteners.
