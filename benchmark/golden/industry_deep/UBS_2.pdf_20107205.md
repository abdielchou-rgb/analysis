# Global Inflation Strategy Global Inflation Monday

TIPS breakevens. Low vol, diversifying, cheap.

US breakevens look attractive along the curve; risk sentiment is the dominant theme in global markets right now and breakevens are diversifying in this regime. They are also low volatility and supported by cheap fixings.

Markets are getting revved up again about rate cuts. US front end real rates (1y1y more than 1y) has room to go further. UK nominals are better than reals because RPI fixings are challenging. EUR has least room to follow through.

Fixings still look attractive in US and Europe.

We see higher EUR real rates as a strategic theme and like expressing that as broad curve steepeners or in long-end forwards against the UK.

The UK DMO's gilt calendar is bullish for UK breakevens in the 30y. We like RPI steepeners.

## Reals to the races

## Stay long front end real rates… in the US. Nominal in the UK

Markets seem to have decided that it's okay again to hope that central banks will be dovish this week. When optimism about the start of a central bank cycle starts, there is still a lot of room for that excitement to follow through, especially in the UK and probably in the US. But unless something 'breaks', the room is mostly in the reds and greens and not so much in Europe. And in the UK, the value is nominal rates: RPI fixings are challenging.

## Phantom pension hedging. Dutch funds sat out of the Q4 rally

But before moving on, pent up demand. Last week the DNB published its update of pension schemes' actual hedging of interest rate risk for Q4. It seems that far from contributing to the rally in rates, pensions were discouraged by it. We estimate that in aggregate they may have increased hedges by just €10m/bp, perhaps less (Figure 3.) Meanwhile they likely gamma'ed shorter (due to mismatched convexity between assets and liabilities), leaving their hedge ratios slightly lower as an industry (Figure 2.) So far this year we believe that hedging activity has been strong. Curve flattening and asset swap tightening strongly suggest so. Q4 may have also served to remind that markets can be volatile and gains snatched away quickly. We guess that schemes are probably 60-65% hedged now. Just how far further will schemes want to raise PV01 coverage? We remain confident that pensions hedging activity will slow substantially this year and we believe that positioning in steepeners is less likely to compound what activity there is. Real rate curve steepening is still a strong view.

## UK pensions maturity is coming to Europe

We also maintain a strong UK vs Euro spread tightening view in real rates. The UK has transitioned to maturity in pensions hedging very quickly, but Europe will also get there. While we would expect UK rates to outperform euros along the curve as the BoE eases and markets realise that the UK's growth potential is more German than American, we are most confident in the long end. We also expect RPI outperformance of HICP in the long end to support UK-Euro real-rate narrowing.

## Value in US fixings

There has been a lot of inflation information to process in the past two weeks. We'll tick it off quickly. Our US economics team upgraded its forecast for the US economy. They no longer project a recession, although the risks are still skewed downward. The Inflation forecasts were also raised, more in PCE than CPI, but the bottom-up profile for CPI (Figure 21) is clearly above market fixings. We would only add the observation that US CPI services ex-rents appear to be running at a SA 3m/3m annualised rate of close to 6% in recent months (Figure 4.)

## … and in Euro fixings

We also project Euro Area fixings above the market (Figure 22). The gap is around 0.2pp in one year. The upside risks appear to be gaining weight. Services inflation bounced across the region in February (Figure 5) and PCCI has also turned back up. Business surveys were slightly stronger, even if they are still at low levels, reinforcing the idea that recession risk might be receding. Consensus for 2024 growth has flatlined at 0.5%, while US expectations have lept. A pick-up in H2 is expected for Europe, but this might still be too pessimistic. Lower rates; lower, more stable energy; and demand pull from the US are all upside risks for euro area demand. China has even looked a little healthier recently (video).

## But not UK. Short 10y, and RPI steepeners.

The bounce in services CPI in the Euro Area contrasts somewhat with the UK, though, where momentum continued to fall to about 3.6% (SA 3m/3m annualised) in February. Front end UK breakevens are expensive and we like breakeven steepeners. We also retire our 5y RPI vs nominal 4:1 ratio recommendation. But data over the next two months still has much to convince the markets in the UK. April is the strongest month for priceresetting and March/April are the strongest months for wage inflation. There is also another increase in the minimum wage of nearly 10%.

## Inflation is still on a higher track compared to before the pandemic

If there is not to be a hard 'reset' for inflation through recession, what does medium term inflation pressure look like in the US? (Or elsewhere in DM?) Consensus is that inflation pressure will be higher. There is no shortage of theories to support the idea and that is reflected both in breakevens compared to 2014-19 and above all, much higher real rates expected to be required to contain that pressure. What evidence do we have now, if any, that the trend in inflation has actually increased compared to the prepandemic period? In his Inflation Monthly UBS Economist Alan Detmeister accepts that most metrics do point to a higher inflation trend for now, although he's dismissive of model-based (filtered inflation metrics, Philip's Curves). In the end perhaps it boils down to inflation expectations. Charts 6 and 7 take a broader global view (although the US provides more surveys). Feedback between inflation and inflation expectations is in both directions, so expectations will probably continue to fall. But it is hard to conclude that we are close to a the pre-pandemic environment on this.

## US breakevens: Bullish background. Bullish base case...

As in previous weeks, the base case, at least for the path ahead in the fixings, is bullish for breakevens. The skew of risks also looks bullish for breakevens. And the risk environment looks bullish for breakevens. So what is the problem? In the absence of an answer, we will add another reason to be long.

## Rates are risk...

In principle TIPS look particularly useful right now in a multi-asset portfolio. Rates remain strongly positively correlated with risk. Rebalancing of stockmarket gains back into rates and tech's preference for lower rates are a virtuous cycle that has been seen before, while a Fed put has probably been moving out of the money for most people, as downward inflation momentum has appeared to slow. Rates, in other words, don't appear to diversify in this environment.

## … but breakeven bulls are stockmarket bears

But breakevens do diversify. Figure 8 shows how unusual this is. The logical ex-post rationalisation is that risk markets simply like signs of lower inflation because ultimately that is what will underpin stronger ongoing risk sentiment. This set up is only likely to remain as long as inflation pressure is seen as being high and something the Fed is on guard against. (For most of 2001-2020, in our view, the Fed was arguably more concerned about financial markets as the most leading indicator of demand.) It may seem odd to say that higher inflation pressure should be good for breakevens, but more often than not it is mostly relevant for real rates. For now though, it should be very supportive for breakevens.

## TIPS are cheap, low volatility diversification

Diversification doesn't necessarily require that TIPS be 'cheap', but clearly it helps and we think they are. Others taking a value view and hoping to beat treasuries should be encouraged that breakevens are currently also very low volatility (Figure 9).

## The ETF tide is slowly turning

ETF outflows are continuing, but barely. The absolute value of these flows is small - around \$2bn so far this year. Normally we are interested in ETF flows not for their size but as a sample of wider sentiment. On that note, it may be worth noticing that outflows have been shorter duration on average. Overall, then, we do not see this as negative, but another an indication that investors have not appreciated the value of the asset class. Figures 10 and 11 illustrate.

## UK Q1 supply is bullish for long end linkers

## No syndications, no long linkers, one ultra

The UK gilt supply calendar was published last week. It should be bullish for linkers. No syndications are planned for the quarter - just five auctions: a 10y (twice), a 15y, a 20y and the 50y. For real rates, what matters is that duration supply will remain elevated, but not more than recently (Figure 13). That is perhaps surprising given the total supply entering the market across the curve (Figure 12) but the skew toward mediums and shorts has taken a lot out of that pressure for the market as a whole. For breakevens, the pressure of IE01 will remain historically very low (Figure 14.) This was a calendar that strongly reinforces our breakeven steepening view in UK linkers. (Our ideal expression was 10y vs 20y20y.)

## Stay in RPI steepeners

The steepener has not been working, however: we are already nearly 10bp offside as the 10y has led a global rally in breakevens this week. We still like this trade and push our stop back by 10bp (to -94bp) to reflect that. We prefer the steepener, but we would also be short 10y RPI outright. Front-end breakevens are very expensive: we see next to no prospect of RPI realising above 3.9%, over the next several years, as front end valuations imply. In addition to UK inflation gradually falling into line with the global trend toward better-behaved prices, when rates begin to fall, mortgage interest payments (MIPS) will subtract from the CPI-RPI wedge. There may not be a turn in the cycle in time for this to add back to before RPI reform in six years.
