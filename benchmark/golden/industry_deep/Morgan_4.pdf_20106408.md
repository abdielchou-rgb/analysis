Dispersion, large capital structures, and a sizeable distressed universe make gauging value in CCCs challenging at the index level. However, we see opportunities in the details. Our deep dive into CCCs.

## Key Takeaways

CCCs continue to screen attractive vs. the rest of credit, with spreads at or above long-term averages. We see better risk-reward in CCCs than BBs/Bs.

In HY CCC, we see opportunities in the "mid-beta" cohort, while the tightest subset looks rich, and the tail is concentrated in names with secular challenges.

We prefer unsecured bonds from higher-rated issuers that have cheapened vs. secured bonds from weaker names.

We see better/broader upside in CCC Loans, which are skewed to higher notches. Valuations are cheaper; Fed cuts should help ease funding and cash flow stresses.

In Europe, CCC valuations are cheaper, but the opportunity set is more limited. We recommend AT1s and CLO mezz as liquid alternatives for yield pickup.

In derivatives, we like pairing longs in CDX HY 41 equity vs. index to position for CCC compression.

Introduction

Risk assets continue to embrace a soft landing, bolstered by strong growth data and continued evidence of disinflation. Credit spreads briefly approached our bull case in both IG and HY, as good macro data and cooling inflation has been supplemented by very strong technicals to start the year ( Exhibit 2 ). Underneath the optimism implied by valuations though, our client conversations suggest there is a sense of caution. While the soft landing view seems to be consensus, it is one that lacks conviction and feels somewhat shaky.

This lack of conviction is most evident where the highest-beta part of corporate credit trades i.e., CCCs. After compressing sharply into year-end – consistent with our call (see Picking Our Spots) – some of the outperformance has reversed YTD. In US HY, CCC spreads are at long-term averages, while in loans they are in the top quartile (i.e., high spreads). In Europe, current valuations appear more dislocated and point to significant upside in European CCCs, but the small market size means capturing this may prove challenging.

Client concerns regarding the CCC preference have centered on several main arguments:

1. Concern #1 - Our macro view remains too optimistic, and many clients are in the weaker growth and/or stickier inflation camp.

2. Concern #2 - The CCC universe in HY is dominated by large capital structures facing secular challenges.

3. Concern #3 - Excluding the distressed universe, there is not much value left in HY CCCs.

4. Concern #4 - In Loans, the level of downgrades/defaults has been meaningful, and hence justifies wider spreads. Also, it is difficult to see CCCs compressing given lack of CLO sponsorship.

5. Concern #5 - If leveraged credit spreads widen, regardless of the catalyst (weak growth or high inflation), CCCs will be the first to see selling pressure. In other words, a preference for CCCs is simply a bullish call on markets.

We acknowledge that these concerns are not without merit. Case in point, defaults rates rose in 2023 while recovery rates declined closer to cycle lows (see Defaults - Looking Back to Look Forward). In addition, the frequency of large price moves remains high, especially around earnings misses and/or poor guidance. And over the long term, the value proposition in CCCs tends to be weaker. For instance, the Sharpe ratio for CCCs is lower than that for BBs and Bs, despite the higher spread that is on offer.

That being said, we see an investible window for CCCs to outperform, given the combination of strong growth data, PMIs bottoming out, very rich valuations in high-/ mid-quality leveraged credit, and continued strength in technicals. The CCC universe by its very nature is idiosyncratic, and rigorous credit work is especially important. But it is also a fairly broad universe, comprising 180+ issuers each in HY and in Loans. Looking at the bottom-up picture across such a large swath of names is clearly beyond the scope of this report. We will look to address micro takeaways and bottom-up opportunities in a separate note. Our goal for this report is to identify high-level top-down takeaways that can help inform investor views.

Our key responses to the client concerns outlined above are summarized below.

1) With respect to the growth outlook, we refer readers to our economics team's call for a soft landing for the economy (a view they have held since the Fed started hiking rates). For investors that have a more cautious take, we would argue that, while CCCs should underperform in a weaker economy, the magnitude may not be as severe as prior cycles, given cheaper starting levels and the likelihood of significant Fed easing.

2) The CCC "tail" in HY today is undoubtedly large, with the top 25 names accounting for 40% of amount outstanding. However, the distressed tail is also less cyclical and more "asset" heavy than prior default cycles, e.g., 2015/16 or COVID, when the concentration of Energy + Consumer names was significant. This implies that defaults can be more drawn out and the odds of very poor recoveries are low.

3) CCC index spreads are no doubt skewed by large names, but the tighter subsets are not priced for perfection. For example, the "mid-tier" issuer (e.g., 25-75th percentile by spread) within the CCC index looks wide to where single-Bs are trading. In other words, there is value outside the tail, if the macro picture remains resilient.

4) CCC Loans have grown sharply in size but are skewed toward higher notches (CCC+ accounts for 68%), which include names recently downgraded from B- because of interest coverage and free cash flow erosion. As we approach rate cuts, we believe a subset of this cohort should see fundamental stresses ease. Our CLO strategists expect issuance to recover in 2024, which should help the technical picture as well. Finally, the growth in private credit provides a release valve for some lower-quality names (as we saw in 2023), which is likely to further smooth the pace of defaults.

5) CCC outperformance is often seen in a rallying market, but it is not always the case. We look at periods when CCCs outperform even as the broader HY market weakened. Not surprisingly, most of these periods were associated with very cheap relative valuations (similar to today) and/or a negative surprise on the monetary policy/rates front which drives outflows.

In summary, we see an attractive risk-reward skew for CCCs across a range of scenarios:

Scenario 1 – "Goldilocks": Resilient growth, disinflation, and Fed easing would give CCCs room to catch up with BBs/Bs.

Scenario 2 – "Soft landing": Weaker growth but no recession, coupled with easier Fed policy could reduce risk premia across a broad enough cohort for CCCs to outperform.

Scenario 3 – "Higher for longer": Strong growth accompanied by re-accelerating inflation and rates volatility would result in HY outflows and weigh on BBs and Bs more meaningfully.

Scenario 4 – "Hard landing": A (mild) recession would likely drive a broad-based decompression, but expectations of a quick Fed response coupled with cheaper starting levels would likely limit the extent of CCC underperformance.

## What's in the HY CCC Bucket?

• The HY CCC universe is skewed towards large capital structures, with the top 25 names (out of 180) accounting for nearly 40% of all CCC debt, by par.

• The "distressed" tail today is sizeable, but less cyclical than prior default cycles, dominated by Healthcare and TMT, unlike Energy and Consumer (in the 2014/2020 periods).

• Quality within the CCC universe has been deteriorating, with the CCC2 (i.e., mid-CCC) bucket expanding at the expense of CCC1.

• The proportion of secured debt within the HY market (35%) has grown sharply in recent years, but has lagged within CCCs (\~27%). Within CCC Secureds, 35% by amount outstanding are below first lien.

• A majority of unsecured CCC debt comes from higher-rated single B issuers (71%).

## CCC HY universe is much smaller than 10 years ago, but has been steady in recent

years. For this report, we use the ICE BAML HY index as our benchmark for analysis of the CCC HY universe. Note that the CCC classification is based on bond-level rating (not issuer rating), taking the average of ratings by the three agencies. The CCC sub-index currently is \$141bn in market value and roughly \$175bn in par value outstanding, as shown in Exhibit 6 . Looking back, the CCC index peaked in size at close to \$240bn in June 2014 and steadily decreased until the COVID recession.

Compared to 10 years ago, CCCs account for a much smaller part of the HY index, although their weighting has increased slightly over the past year. CCC concentration peaked to 25% just after the GFC, but has been on a steady decline since. Over the past 10 years, CCC weights were the highest in 2014 before the Energy crisis. Since then, though, as weaker names exited the CCC bucket via defaults, this proportion has generally been shrinking until very recently. By market value, CCCs comprise \~11% of HY currently, which is slightly up compared to last year. The recent growth of the CCC share has been more about BBs moving up to IG, rather than downgrades into the CCC bucket.

Sector-wise, CCCs are skewed towards Media and Healthcare. The sector composition of the CCC universe has changed meaningfully over the past decade (see Exhibit 12 ). Media and Healthcare in total account for 22% of the CCC universe. Media has seen a sharp increase in CCC bonds since 2H22, as higher rates aggravated their free cash flow pressure amid secular headwinds from cord cutting and decline in pay-TV distribution. Healthcare names have faced macro challenges from factors such as labor shortages, rising wages, and adverse operational impacts from legislation like the "No Surprises Act" (see Moody's US Corporate Default Monitor – Fourth Quarter). Meanwhile, the share of Energy in CCC HY has dropped from 20% (at YE 2016) to 5% now. Energy is well below average as weaker names have left the index with defaults, while survivors have displayed strong capital discipline and fundamental improvements post-COVID. Retail also comprises a smaller share of CCCs today than the post-GFC average.

If we only look at just the distressed bucket within CCCs – defined as names trading above 750bp – Media and Healthcare are large, but Telecom is also significant. 22% of the distressed CCC bucket comes from Media names, 16% comes from Healthcare, and 10% comes from Telecom. Without going into detail, our credit sector analysts are cautious on some of larger Telecom names due to increasing competition, market maturation, elevated leverage, weak demand, and event risk with individual names. Their outlook is detailed in the HY Telecom & Cable Credit Outlook. Commscope (COMM) is one name they like within the sector, on expectations of better performance, demand picking up in 2H24, and attractive valuation based on their recovery analysis.

One takeaway from the above is that the distressed bucket is large and faces secular operational challenges. However, it is also less cyclical than the distressed buckets seen prior to the bear market in 2015 and the COVID recession in 2020.

Less secured bonds within CCCs than the HY market overall. Over the past few years, the percentage of secured debt within HY has increased significantly. As depicted in Exhibit 14 , roughly 35% of the overall HY market is in secured debt. Similar to the overall HY market, the concentration of secured bonds within CCCs is higher than it was a few years ago. However, in level terms, secured debt comprises a much smaller part of the CCC universe, at \~27% by par outstanding.

Not surprisingly, secured CCCs tend to be associated with weaker issuers that won't have access to unsecured debt in the primary market, or would have to fund at very punitive levels. We further find that within CCC secureds from a single B issuer, 60% of the debt is second lien or lower. Within this cohort, roughly 32% debt by amount outstanding does not have unsecured bonds subordination.

On the other hand, the good news for investors is that the unsecured portion of CCC debt from Single-B issuers is high (at around 70%). So, while subordination remains a risk in the case of a credit event, the underlying quality of the issuers in aggregate has increased over time.

## What's in the Loan CCC Bucket?

• The universe of CCC loans has expanded following a sharp wave of downgrades from B3s, as higher rates led to elevated funding costs and weaker free cash flow.

• Relative to HY, Loan CCCs are skewed toward higher notches (CCC+), which should see fundamentals improve as the Fed cuts rates.

• Technology is a much bigger part of the CCC bucket in Loans than HY, and combined with Healthcare, accounts for \~50% outstanding. Digging into the tail (price lower than \$80), we see CCC loans even more concentrated in Tech and Healthcare.

• Trailing defaults and downgrades have been worse in loans than in bonds. However, rate cuts and our expectation for better CLO issuance are both constructive for CCC loans over time.

CCC loans are comprised of \~180 issuers, similar to what we see in the HY market. But the universe is different in 2 key ways. The CCC loan market is less skewed towards large capital structures. While there are several names that have more than \$2bn in debt outstanding, these are Loan heavy capital structures, unlike the HY market, where the largest names are mixed capital structures (where total balance sheet is substantially higher than index-debt).

The CCC concentration within the index has doubled in size, to 9% today from 4.7% in January 2022. We can attribute this growth to an intense wave of downgrades as rate hikes eroded debt service capacity for floating-rate heavy capital structures. As a result, the CCC cohort is heavily skewed toward high CCCs, which make up 68% of the CCC cohort now compared to only 42% at YE 2019. In fact, we identify 76% (by amount outstanding) of names in the CCC+ bucket were downgraded from the B- bucket in the last 2 years.

## Digging into Dispersion Within CCCs

• Dispersion within HY CCCs is very elevated: the share of distressed CCCs (trading at OAS over 750bp) has increased with spreads widening, while the tightest trading quartile has come in.

• We are constructive on mid-tier CCCs within HY as they screen relatively cheap to single-B spread levels and believe they have room to compress.

• Within Loans, the CCC+ cohort looks cheaper vs. B- loans, with the price gap almost at widest levels in the post-GFC period

As discussed earlier in this report, one of the challenges with being constructive on CCCs and a concern we hear from clients, is that the index is skewed by a few large names. This has merit as the concentration of large capital structures is increasing as discussed above. The share of the CCC bucket trading at distressed levels remains fairly high (see Exhibit 20 ). Further, the largest CCCs in HY are also the ones trading at more distressed valuations. Four of the largest 10 names have the average price of their CCC bonds trading at deep discounts (below 60pt) (see Exhibit 21 ).

## Despite high dispersion, "performing" CCCs are not at extreme levels.

The question, though, is where are CCC valuations once we strip out the impact of these large names? Below, we look at a cross-sectional CCC analysis along a few different lines. In Exhibit 22, we break down CCC spreads into different quartiles. The top (widest) quartile, where the distressed names dominate, remains at post-COVID wides, well in excess of 1000bp in spread. On the flip side, the tightest quartile, is likely back to the tights. The 25th percentile constituent within HY CCCs, for example, trades as tight as 400bp. Therefore, investors looking at the "wings" are either buying significantly stressed names, or a fairly rich tight-trading subset.

However, if we look the mid-beta segment, the median CCC issuer trades at a spread of 650bp. The tights for the median name within CCCs was closer to 510bp in 2021, so this cohort clearly is not back to extreme tight levels.

Similarly, the basis between the mid-tier CCC names relative to single Bs is around 450bp, well north of the post-GFC spread basis average of 380bp (see Exhibit 23 ). Overall, while distressed names clearly skew the index considerably, we disagree with some client concerns that "ex-distressed names, there is no value within the cohort". We think the performing part of CCCs – while it has rallied significantly – has room to compress further relative to single Bs.

In a nutshell, while the "cheapness" of CCCs does get inflated by the distressed names across HY and Loans, we think CCCs are not priced to perfection more broadly. A soft landing for the economy may not be enough to help the deeply-distressed names, but likely helps the non-distressed cohort with additional runway to recover. This "nondistressed" segment of CCCs, in our view, should have room to compress vs. where BBs/Bs are. Furthermore, given the asset-heavy and less cyclical nature of the distressed tail, there are two incrementally positive considerations for investors: 1) Defaults are likely to be drawn out and the coupon remains significant, even if only for a few quarters, and 2) While recoveries might be lower than average, the tail risk of very low recoveries across a significant part of the distressed universe may not be that high.

## Historical Performance and Valuations

• CCC spreads adjusted for market beta and defaults look reasonable. Lossadjusted carry vs. BBs/Bs looks poor only in worst-case default/recovery outcomes.

• CCCs performance into a rate-cutting cycle is mixed based on a limited sample size. In 2019, CCCs underperformed into the first rate cut, but held up better into the first rate cut in 2007.

• CCCs do not only perform in risk-on or bullish markets. We identify periods when CCCs outperformed despite weak HY returns.

Are investors getting compensated for higher volatility and default risk in CCCs? Using ICE BAML index data since 1997, CCCs have a beta of 1.4 (vs. HY) in terms of monthly excess returns, compared to 0.8x and 1.1x for BBs and single Bs. But even adjusting for beta (CCC spreads - beta\*HY index spreads), the carry profile looks attractive in CCCs. This carry figure has been rising in recent months to \~300bp, vs. \~200bp on average from 2021 ( Exhibit 27 ).

While volatility is one risk metric to consider, the bigger risk for CCCs over time is from losses and defaults. But here as well, we think a lot is in their price. To validate this, we estimate loss-adjusted carry of the CCC cohort by assuming the lowest cash price bonds default first (and therefore do not pay any coupons) within the ICE HY index, and we assume our peak 5% default rate (note our base case for 2024 is actually closer to 4% defaults, but we make conservative assumptions here). If recovery rates come in line with the historical norm, the loss-adjusted carry in CCC is 160bp higher versus Bs. If recovery rates end up in the lower quartile of their historical range, the extra carry is still \~50bp higher than higher-rated issuers. Only if LGDs reached historical highs does a 5% default rate erase all the excess carry in CCCs versus higher-quality HY bonds. This analysis points to better convexity in CCCs unless we experience a bearish recovery outcome.

## Positioning Themes Within CCCs

## Better and broader risk reward in CCC Loans vs. bonds

CCC valuations in loans are cheaper than in bonds. The LSTA CCC Loan Index trades at a dollar price of \$80 and a spread-to-maturity of \~1300bp. On the surface, the cheapness in loans might make sense considering: 1) Loan borrowers bore the brunt of higher policy rates given their floating-rate liabilities, 2) The reliance of the CLO buyer base, where appetite for CCCs is muted, and 3) Defaults rates have been higher among loan borrowers than bond borrowers, with recovery much lower than typical.

Overall, however, we think the CCC loan market is priced for much worse default outcomes than CCC HY, and thus has a better convexity profile if the economy stays strong. Although we expect higher defaults and weaker-than-average recoveries in loans (as outlined in Defaults - Looking Back to Look Forward), the market seems to have already priced this in. Exhibit 34 summarizes the loss-adjusted spreads across default and recovery assumptions, and the circled scenarios are broadly consistent with our one-yearahead default targets and recovery rate assumptions. Adjusting for expected losses, CCC loans are still trading at least 300bp more than similarly-rated HY bonds.

However, here we use the actual default and recovery rate targets (rather than riskneutral measures), while investors should be compensated for the uncertainty around defaults and recovery outcomes. Loan investors may also demand a premium for illiquidity and smaller issuer size. Naturally, these considerations generally argue for higher lossadjusted spreads for loans vs. HY. But at these levels, we think the spread profile appears more attractive for loans than HY.

The stress for loan borrowers – especially in the CCC+ bucket – is more directly a function of higher interest rates, vs. HY borrowers (where industry- and company-specific factors play a larger role). Thus, if a policy-easing cycle is imminent, it is very likely that the worst of quality migration within loans is behind us.

## Down in capital structure over down in quality

Given the lower-quality issuer mix, CCC secureds typically trade cheap to CCC unsecureds. For context, the spread basis of secured vs unsecured CCCs averaged at \~340bp in the post-GFC era. As a result, unsecured CCCs screen increasingly cheap – secured vs unsecured basis are now tracking about the 20% percentile in the post-GFC range. At these levels, we like CCC unsecured over CCC secured.

## The European Perspective

## Aron Becker, Ellie Dann

• Dispersion within the weakest HY cohorts has been particularly pronounced in Europe, with the CCC-B basis rising to a post-GFC wide. Such a gap has historically been followed by strong outperformance of CCCs.

• The size of the universe remains well above any pre-Covid era levels, although the absolute volume of CCC bonds at €25 billion is a relatively small opportunity set.

• The recent underperformance within CCCs was not led by any single names or problem sectors. In fact, concentration measures suggest a relatively more diverse set of issuers and sectors than at any time in the past.

• Similar to the US, the average ratings quality within CCCs has deteriorated, with the share of CCC+ bonds dropping to a 10-year low. On the flip side, senior secured is becoming the dominant structure within the ratings cohort.

• We think loss-adjusted yields and low cash prices should compensate investors for any further increase in default rates, consistent with the experience seen in 2016 and 2020. For benchmark driven investors with limited appetite for CCCs, we see subordinated debt (particularly AT1s) and CLO mezz as more liquid alternatives to improve portfolio carry.

Dispersion within the weakest tails has been particularly strong in Europe. The spread basis between CCCs and Bs has reached levels last seen in 2009. And as we show in Exhibit 41 , this index-level valuation differential has demonstrated a strong correlation with relative performance in the following twelve months. In fact, when the index-level basis traded wider than 800bp, the 'median' total return of CCCs outpaced Bs by 10 percentage points in the following year. The only time that this relationship meaningfully broke down was in the early 2000s, when defaults and distress remained elevated for an extended period of time, keeping performance in the weakest cohorts under pressure for longer. As a result, we believe spread valuations in Bs are approaching extremes relative to almost all other parts of credit markets, and recommend a barbell of BBs and CCCs over Bs.

We acknowledge that executing a CCC overweight may prove challenging in practice given the relatively limited size of this universe. But as we show in the following sections, the CCC market has remained relatively large versus history, as well as less concentrated in single issuers. And pricing dispersion is also not as elevated, suggesting that opportunities may indeed be available in the universe.

More importantly, the above pricing dynamic should matter for benchmark driven investors, particularly if they are not allocated to the weakest ratings cohorts. While EUR HY index spreads are 100bp wider than the 2021 tights, excluding CCCs from the index

shows that valuations are now significantly closer to their five-year tights, with 2021 lows now just \~50bp away from current levels. Put differently, not including CCCs in a HY portfolio costs 60bp in portfolio carry now, close to a cycle high ( Exhibit 42 ). Therefore, for benchmark-driven investors with limited CCC exposure, we recommend AT1s and CLO mezz as more liquid alternatives to offset a potential underperformance versus the benchmark if CCCs rally strongly. For more details on our views on the relative value, please see our recent publications (The Path to Normalisation and Tiering Up).
