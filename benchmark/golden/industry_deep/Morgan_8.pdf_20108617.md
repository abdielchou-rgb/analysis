## United States

• Job growth still chugging along as nonfarm employment increased by 272,000 in May...

• ...but the unemployment rate ticked up to 4.0%, and job openings moved lower in April

• In next week<sup>’</sup>s May CPI release, we look for another firm core reading of 0.33%

• We expect a Fed on hold next week and now look for a first hike in November

Ahead of next week<sup>’</sup>s FOMC meeting this week brought a checkup on the labor market. The biggest news was the May jobs report: employment growth rebounded smartly last month, with nonfarm jobs up 272,000 (prior months revised down 15,000 on net). Pay growth also firmed again as average hourly earnings rose 0.4%, taking the year-ago increase to 4.1%. The average workweek was unchanged at 34.3 hours.

Meanwhile, the unemployment rate ticked up to 4.0%, the highest since January 2022, and most other metrics from the household survey were less favorable or outright weak. The household measure of employment contracted by 408,000, unemployment increased 157,000, and the participation rate slipped two ticks to 62.5%. Over the past 12 months the unemployment rate is up 0.4%-points, and during the same period nonfarm employment growth averaged 230,000 jobs per month. The confluence of these two facts points to (i) the importance of immigration in boosting trend, or breakeven, job growth, and (ii) the fact that the monthly establishment survey is likely overstating the pace of job growth.

Sending a message more akin to the household report, the April JOLTS report pointed to a continuing moderation in job openings, and the vacancy-to-unemployment ratio came down to 1.2, from a peak of 2.0 earlier in the cycle. Likewise, the quits rate remains lower than earlier in the cycle, when workers were job-hopping more in search of higher wages.

After the May FOMC meeting, when the unemployment rate was 3.8%, Chair Powell said “a couple of tenths [increase] in the unemployment rate<sup>”</sup> would not be sufficient to ease as he would need to see “broader<sup>”</sup> weakening. Last month<sup>’</sup>s perky job growth and persistent strength in wage growth are still very far from meeting that broader test, and a July ease now looks highly unlikely. It<sup>’</sup>s possible that the weakness in the household survey broadens out in the three employment reports between now and the September FOMC, but given the recent momentum in job growth we think it may take the five reports available between now and the November FOMC meeting to meet the test. We now see the first ease in November, followed by a quarterly cadence of rate cuts next year.

## Labor market still hot to the touch

In the details of the establishment survey, health care again led job growth, adding 84,000 jobs in May. After cooling in April, government employment rose 43,000 last month. While these industries led the way, job growth was broad based with 63.4% of industries increasing employment, the strongest diffusion index reading in over a year (Figure 1).

## Household puzzles

Turning to the household survey, some of last month<sup>’</sup>s rise in the unemployment rate was due to higher teen unemployment, though the 20+ rate also increased a tick to 3.6%, versus a cycle low of 3.2%. The 376,000 year-ago increase in household employment still contrasts markedly with the 2.786 million rise in the establishment survey (Figure 3). Moreover, the payroll concept-adjusted measure looks even weaker, down 456,000 last month and up only 216,000 over the last year. Mirroring quits in the JOLTS report, job leavers continues to decline to a near three-year low while job losers constitute a larger share of the unemployed (Figure 4).

## Job openings resumed their decline

Job openings declined by 296,000, and downward revisions to prior months brought the level down to 8.06 million, the lowest since February 2021. Chair Powell frequently references the vacancy-to-unemployment ratio as a measure of labor market tightness, and in April this ratio moved down further to 1.24. This ratio peaked at 2.0 earlier in this cycle, and a reading in the range of 1.0-1.2 is considered consistent with a healthy balanced labor market.

## CPI and Fed ahead

The May CPI report will be released the morning of next week<sup>’</sup>s FOMC meeting. We look for a relatively firm 0.33% increase in core CPI inflation that keeps year-ago inflation unchanged at 3.6%. In the details, we look for OER and rents to remain elevated, alongside some continued strength in medical care and a sizable reversal in used vehicle prices growth from April. While we continue to expect further cooling in inflation this year—and notably the FOMC is focused on PCE inflation, which is running below CPI—the current pace may leave various participants still concerned about a lack of further progress.

Next week the Committee is almost certain to leave the target range unchanged at 5.25-5.5%. The big question is the median 2024 dot. We think it will show two eases this year, down from three at the March FOMC meeting. With almost half the Committee expecting two or fewer rate cuts in March, this is a close call and the median could easily go to one if Chair Powell and the leadership are so minded. For both 2025 and 2026 we think the median remains at three eases, while the longer-run median dot could drift up to 2.75% as several Fed speakers have opined in recent months that the neutral funds rate is likely moving higher.

As for the post-meeting statement, the description of activity growth could be moderated given recent data. And next Wednesday<sup>’</sup>s CPI report could influence how they tweak the inflation language. At the press conference, we expect Chair Powell will express confidence that the economy is still on a path toward a sustained recovery with inflation heading toward two percent—and that they can remain patient as they expect to gradually gain more confidence in that outlook.

We forecast that the consumer price index (CPI) rose 0.2% in May, with the year-ago inflation rate holding steady at 3.4%. We look for a 0.1% increase in food prices, in line with the relatively soft growth in recent months, and a -1.5% drop in energy prices on the month led by a -3.4% monthly decline in gasoline prices. Away from food and energy, we are looking for a firmer core CPI reading, however, with a 0.33% increase in May. If realized, this print will be strong enough to hold the year-ago core CPI inflation rate steady at 3.6%.

Shelter is the largest piece of the CPI index and is closely watched for signs of easing. We look for only modest cooling from the still-firm April readings, despite signs of prices fading in certain rental measures. Specifically, we look for a 0.40% rise in Owners<sup>’</sup> Equivalent Rent and a 0.36% rise in tenant<sup>’</sup>s rent, which are similar to the paces they<sup>’</sup>ve run in recent months. Elsewhere within the shelter component, after seasonal adjustment industry figures point to a second month of moderate cooling in lodging. We forecast the lodging CPI slipped -0.1% in May.

One of the larger shifts in CPI components is expected to occur in vehicle prices in May. Most significantly, based on industry data we look for used vehicle prices to rise a sharp 1.5% last month, more than reversing the decline recorded for April. We also anticipate more modest firming in new vehicle prices, up 0.2%, after contracting in six of the last seven months. In other transportation-related news, we look for a flat reading in recently volatile public transportation prices.

Communication prices are expected to resume their downward trend in May after postage stamp prices pushed up this measure earlier in the year—although additional increases have been approved to take effect in July. While medical insurance prices took a step down in April and should continue to run at a more moderate pace than earlier in the year, continued firmness in other parts of the medical CPI leave us looking for a 0.4% rise in this component in May. After several months of notable price increases, we look for the apparel CPI to tick down in May.

The two main monthly surveys of manufacturers—the ISM and the PMI—gave conflicting signals for the near-term health of the manufacturing sector, although both have drifted upward since late last year. But whereas the PMI now stands comfortably above its level at the start of the year, the ISM is down from recent highs and only slightly above where it started 2024—with a sizable reversion in new orders back to more depressed levels seen last year. The broad trend in manufacturing output has been mostly sideways in the industrial production data, and averaging through these reports suggests that trend is likely to continue for now. In particular, ordersto-inventory ratios in both surveys have slipped back below one in recent months, which suggests muted momentum for output.

The May ISM manufacturing composite index fell 0.5-pts to 48.7, disappointing expectations. The details were generally weaker across the board, with the employment index being a notable exception as it rose to its highest reading since August 2022. By contrast, the production index fell 1.1-pts to 50.2 while the new orders index took a big step down (-3.7-pts) to 45.4, a 12-month low. Meanwhile, the composite manufacturing PMI for May was revised up to 51.3 from the earlier flash release of 50.9, which represents a 1.3-pts gain from the value recorded in April. Although still below its recent high in February, it remains consistent with a choppy upward trend in the manufacturing sector over roughly the past year. In the details, the output PMI improved to 53.0

from 51.1 in April; new orders, employment, and future output all also posted increases last month.

Consistent with the broad upward trend in both surveys<sup>’</sup> pricing components, goods price data have recently firmed somewhat after deflating outright during the second half of last year. Falling prices in China and a strengthening US dollar should exert some downward pressure on goods prices, whereas recent jumps in shipping costs and continued support on the demand side (which has weakened some in recent spending reports) point to upward pressure. The May ISM prices index ticked lower while import prices moved up in the PMI survey even as output prices were again slightly lower on the month.

Job openings dropped 296,000 in April to a seasonally adjusted 8.06mn, a larger than expected decline that beat expectations and brought it to its lowest level since February 2021.

The prior month<sup>’</sup>s level was revised down to 8.36mn from the previously reported 8.49mn. This drop brings the openingsto-unemployed ratio, a useful gauge of labor market tightness, to 1.24 for April, down from 1.52 a year ago and very close to its pre-pandemic level of 1.22 in February 2020. As we have argued recently, with this move in job openings, the US labor market seems to be moving along the pre-pandemic Beveridge curve relationship, raising the possibility of a gradually increasing unemployment rate.

Under the hood, it looks like job openings in April declined in health care and social assistance (-204,000), leisure and hospitality (-109,000), state and local government education (-59,000), and information (-43,000), while vacancies increased in professional and business services (+122,000) and private educational services (+50,000). The construction sector<sup>’</sup>s large drop observed in March did not reverse itself, highlighting the softer demand in this sector. Finally, the underlying detail by establishment size continued to indicate relatively stronger labor demand among smaller establishments, which collectively posted more job openings in April relative to March, whereas establishments with more than 50 workers reduced openings.

Beyond openings, hires and total separations changed little and quits stayed relatively flat at 2.2%. The quits rate has been at 2.2% since November and is similar to the levels we have seen at the end of the prior expansion, which marked a fairly tight labor market by historical standards. Relatively stable quit rates over the recent past seem to be consistent with other survey data, such as the mean probability of quits from the New York Fed<sup>’</sup>s Survey of Consumer Expectations. Higher quits rates are generally associated with higher wage gains by workers, and as such, a flat quits rate might be a concern that wage growth could stay elevated, although the relationship has not been very tight lately.

Job growth rebounded smartly in May, with nonfarm employment increasing 272,000 (prior months revised down 15,000 on net). Pay growth also picked back up as average hourly earnings increased 0.4%, taking the year-ago increase to 4.1%. The average workweek was unchanged at 34.3 hours. Meanwhile, the unemployment rate ticked up to 4.0%, the highest since January 2022, and most other metrics from the household survey were less favorable or outright weak. The household measure of employment contracted by 408,000, unemployment increased 157,000, and the participation rate slipped two ticks to 62.5%. Over the past 12 months the unemployment rate is up 0.4%-point, and during the same period nonfarm employment growth averaged 230,000 jobs per month. The confluence of these two facts points to (i) the importance of immigration in boosting trend, or breakeven, job growth, and (ii) the fact that the monthly establishment survey is likely overstating the pace of job growth.

After the May FOMC meeting, when the rate was 3.8%, Chair Powell said “a couple of tenths in the unemployment rate<sup>”</sup> would not be sufficient to ease, as he would need to see “broader<sup>”</sup> weakening. Last month<sup>’</sup>s perky job growth and persistent strength in wage growth are still very far from meeting that broader test, and a July ease now looks highly unlikely. It<sup>’</sup>s possible that the weakness in the household survey broadens out in the three employment reports between now and the September FOMC, but given the recent momentum in job growth, we think it may take the five reports available between now and the November FOMC meeting to meet the test. We now see the first ease in November, followed by a quarterly cadence of rate cuts next year.

In the details of the establishment survey, job growth was led again by health care, which added 84,000 jobs in May. After cooling in April, government employment increased 43,000 last month. While these industries led the way, job growth was broad based last month with 63.4% of industries increasing employment, the strongest reading on the diffusion index in over a year. The average workweek was unchanged near the low end of its recent range at 34.3 hours (34.296 hours to higher precision). The strength in headline earnings was mirrored in the production and non-supervisory worker series, where average hourly earnings increased 0.5% last month and the year-ago gain ticked up to 4.2%.

Turning to the household survey, some of last month<sup>’</sup>s increase in the unemployment rate was due to higher teen unemployment, though the 20+ rate also increased a tick to 3.6%, now up from a cycle low of 3.2%. The 376,000 yearago increase in household employment continues to contrast markedly with the 2.786 million increase reported in the establishment survey. Moreover, the payroll concept adjusted measure looks even weaker, down 456,000 last month and up only 195,000 over the last year. Mirroring the quits number in the JOLTS report, job leavers as a share of the unemployed continues to decline to a near three-year low. In contrast to the headline participation rate number, prime-age (25-54) participation ticked up last month to a cycle high 83.6%, and prime-age female participation hit an all-time high of 78.1%. The gains were offset by big declines in over 55 and under 25 participation rates.
