## Executive Summary: Too early to position against US exceptionalism

2024 is tracking a high-for-long soft landing, but US election risks are looming larger

US exceptionalism defined the first half of 2024, marked by outperformance of the US economy and financial markets. The current narrative for disinflation supports a soft landing and easy financial conditions alongside expectations for resilient and broadening growth, sticky inflation, and moderate policy easing. While our confidence in the key 2024 outlook macro themes largely holds for the remainder of the year, it does not extend to strong conviction about where the expansion will stand 12-18 months from now. Record high debt and deficits, sticky inflation, and rich equity valuations with narrow leadership, alongside heightened geopolitical risks and slowing US growth, could come to a head in 2025. The risks from the upcoming US elections are key, and there is a clear lack of consensus on the 2025 outlook, as the elections have the potential to bring higher tariffs, higher inflation and increased fiscal spending. Our global economics team continues to see a reasonable risk that the economic backdrop could interact with political dynamics to reignite inflation pressures, prompting additional central bank tightening.

• At the mid-summer point, we revisit our strategic ideas and recommendations from the beginning of the year.

• Markets have moved to a consensus around a high-for-longer soft landing, with a view that restrictive for longer will not morph into even higher for longer.

• Yet confidence in the key 2024 outlook macro themes—resilient and broadening growth, sticky inflation, and moderate policy easing—does not extend to strong conviction about where the expansion will stand 12-18 months from now.

• This year’s record election cycle has brought surprises, which have led to short-term spikes in volatility, but the upcoming US elections are marked by historic developments and carry the most significant consequences in the wake of the assassination attempt on Trump and the ongoing debate within the Democratic Party on Biden’s viability to remain in the presidential race.

• The US election outcome could bring substantial changes to trade policy, foreign policy, regulation and fiscal/tax policy through higher tariffs and increased fiscal spending, with the risk of higher inflation and a stronger dollar to persist.

• The industrial policy revolution is gaining momentum, trumping trade policy and anti-trust risks.

• US-China competition intensifies beyond a tech war with growing concerns about the weaponization of commodities and critical minerals now in focus.

• Non-oil markets are among this year’s standout outperformers, and the rally could still have further room to run, particularly for sugar and grains, alongside the continued support for precious and base metal prices.

• We stay with the view that the classic 60/40 equity bond strategic allocation is making a gradual comeback, but momentum continues to favor equities over bonds.

• We retain Strategic overweights of Tech and Health Care in equities but exit Financials, as the expected rise in bond yields has largely played out.

• We exit Climate Change funds, which is one input in our EM strategic underweight.

• We now include Securitized Debt in our allocation to Credit in our long-only Strategic Asset Allocation model portfolio.

• We retain an overweight on inflation-linked bonds and keep an allocation to hedge funds, as higher macro volatility should benefit active managers in 2H24.

Revisiting Our Long-term Strategy Core Views and Strategic Asset Allocation Model Portfolio

Macro policy trumps US election uncertainty and broader geopolitical risks for now, as recent data confirms the disinflation trend and our US economists now expect the Fed to ease in September, but there is considerable uncertainty surrounding the 2025 outlook. The upcoming US elections are marked by extraordinary and historic developments in the wake of the assassination attempt on Trump and the ongoing debate within the Democratic Party on Biden’s viability to remain in the presidential race. We see ongoing risks around the elections and reiterate our view that the potential for far greater market disruption in 2025 is not yet priced. The US election outcome could bring higher tariffs, higher inflation and increased fiscal spending, with a stronger dollar to persist, alongside changes to domestic regulation and a shift in US foreign policy priorities.

We revisit the top 10 strategic and long-term investment themes driving global markets and economies since we published our Top 10 Strategic Investment Themes for 2024 and Beyond on January 9, complementing the Global Research 2024 Midyear Outlook published on June 28. Equity markets have rallied strongly, credit spreads have tightened, USD strength has endured and Treasury yields have risen 40-60bps over 1H24 as expectations for material Fed easing this year have faded but conviction for a soft landing has increased. While there is more conviction around a September ease, there is less conviction that this marks the start of a cycle that will extend through 2025 and 2026. That said, despite the rise in uncertainty around the scenarios for the US elections, we would not be surprised to see a summer lull set in with low vol as long as the expectations for a September Fed ease are not derailed, with 2025 the year that central banks are tested. We expect fixed income markets to remain relatively rangebound for the remainder of the year (Figure 1).

We recently updated our Strategic Asset Allocation based on long-term return and risk signals (see Updating our SAA, Jan Loeys and Alexander Wise, 21 June 2024).

In equities, we retained strategic overweights of Tech and Health Care. While we have no strong evidence that AI will augur in a productivity revolution, we rely on the old adage of “in a gold rush, sell shovels” as a better motivation to stay overweight Tech companies that are selling the AI “shovels”.

We exited our strategic overweight of Climate Change funds, as we have not yet found any with a decent return, as most assets in this area look expensive. The investment opportunity from Climate Change is more in what not to buy, which is largely a function of the location of fixed assets, close to the equator or seashore where there is higher risk of losses due to extreme weather. Climate risk is one input into our EM strategic underweight but is harder to execute cleanly through broad asset class exemptions—aside from excluding a whole country or region. We have not seen funds that cleanly differentiate assets by extreme weather risk locations.

We also exit our strategic overweight in equities of Financials that was motivated by the expected rise in bond yields, which has been realized for the most part by now.

We fine-tune our allocation to Credit by including also Securitized Debt, as the prime motivation for the allocation is the structural expensiveness of central government debt due to financial repression driving banks, insurers and pensions to hold a lot more government debt than warranted by its yield. We retain an overweight on inflationlinked bonds, but fine-tune our advice in that these should be held only in one’s base currency.

We stay with an allocation to Hedge Funds, as higher macro volatility should benefit active managers. We prefer to have active tactical risk taken by the specialized risk-takers that hedge funds are and continue to prove to be by outperforming a portfolio of bonds and equities with the same volatility.

Note that our Strategic Allocations consider only longterm return and risk signals and are unrelated to J.P. Morgan Research’s tactical recommendations, which advise on deviations from an investor’s own SAA. We keep a generic fixed 60/40 equity bond allocation, as this should be driven by the investor’s own investment horizon, return objectives, and risk tolerance. We follow broadly our “Keep It Simple” KISS principle. Allocations within bonds and equities each start from global outstandings for maximum diversification. From this, we cut parts we do not want to invest in strategically and add others we like to hold more of. Allocations do not distinguish between public and private asset classes. We have no reason to either exclude or overweight private assets. Most of our suggested asset classes can be held through mutual or exchange-traded funds (ETFs), except for private assets and hedge funds. We do not distinguish between passive or active funds, leaving that to the investor. Our portfolio does hold tactical risk through specialized managers—hedge funds. All allocations are FX unhedged, except for credit and securitized bonds.

## Top 10 strategic themes for 2024 still intact, but 2025 holds greater challenges

1. Easy financial conditions and a broadening of the global expansion support the high-for-long softlanding scenario for 2024, but strong conviction does not extend through the next 12-18 months.

Our global economics team shifted their view to greater odds for a soft landing back in early April, highlighting the uniqueness of the current state of the expansion, with late cycle interest rates but mid-cycle fundamentals (see High maintenance, Bruce Kasman and Joseph Lupton, 9 April 2024). Looking at 1H24 outcomes, global growth has moderated to a still-solid 2.4%ar and is less dependent on a US demand engine, as recoveries in Western Europe and emerging markets ex. China find firmer footing. Our US economics team expects growth will continue to moderate in 2H24, with a 1.0% average growth pace for the remainder of the year.

The persistence of inflation stickiness in 1H24 has endured, with core inflation remaining well above a 3%ar and cumulatively. Our US economists highlight that it is important to recognize that two straight low core readings after four high prints leave core CPI still up at 3.3%ar thus far this year. We had highlighted the risk that the Fed could dial back after the dramatic ease of financial conditions, and our global economists now see just 35bps of DM easing (ex Japan) this year while Treasury yields have risen 40-60bps over the course of 1H24.

Treasury’s funding needs also remain a concern given expectations of wider deficits over the coming years, and our US Rates strategists highlight that the US Presidential and Congressional elections could impact the fiscal outlook and Treasury market (see Treasures: 2024 Mid-year outlook, Jay Barry, 28 June 2024). A Trump victory with a Republican sweep could lead to higher yields and steeper curves due to elevated volatility and fiscal concerns, while a Biden victory could result in modestly lower yields. In light of the Congressional Budget Office’s latest 10-year projections, our Treasury strategists now expect that any additional financing needs over the balance of 2024 will be met primarily with T-bills. Additionally, there is risk that the Treasury could begin another round of coupon auction size increases sometime in mid-2025, slightly earlier than anticipated (see CBO’s 10-year budget projections: less than meets the eye?, Jay Barry, 18 June 2024).

Our Long-Term Strategy team maintains a decade-ahead forecast for 10y UST real yields at 2.25%. Profligate fiscal policy is absorbing substantial amounts of capital and is incentivizing additional private investment, while the en masse retirement of baby boomers is shifting a substantial share of the population from a high-savings period in life to a low-savings period, depressing the supply of capital. At the same time, capital needs for the mitigation of climate change, and the adaptation to it, are growing rapidly. The period after the GFC was also aberrational in the sense that there was tremendous damage to balance sheets, which forced household and corporate deleveraging. Thus, the central longer-term scenario is for a 10yr UST yield of 4.5% or higher in nominal terms, while inflation is likely to average 2.5% due to the Fed’s asymmetric tolerance of inflation surprises. The second round of the French lower house elections held on July 7 resulted in a big surprise with a hung parliament. The far-right National Rally (RN) fared worse than expected, while both NFP (centre-left to far-left alliance) and ENS (Macron’s alliance, centre) did better than expected in the second round of voting. This could be explained by a high turnout of voters and the decision of many candidates to drop out of the second round of voting in favor of the candidate best positioned to win against the RN. The outcome of the French lower house election is a hung parliament with 7-8 different factions, which has no precedent, making it unclear how this political configuration will operate. Macron has asked MPs to come up with a cross-party majority (grand coalition) that would back a stable government and ensure a more normal working of the institutions. Our French economics team sees a grand coalition (spanning centre-left to the centre-right) as the only stable solution, with the alternative a technocratic government until a grand coalition emerges or until the next lower house election (see France: Politics in a hung parliament, Raphael Brun-Aguerre, 12 July 2024).

The weak relative majority scenario in the French elections is a messy one, but not necessarily a disruptive one. However, the market will likely keep pricing some political/ fiscal uncertainty premium until there is more clarity on the government and fiscal agenda. Our European rates strategists believe the 10Y France-Germany spread will likely stay in the 60-70bp range until there is clarity on the path ahead (see French election outcome: messy but not disruptive: 10Y France-Germany to stay in 60-70bp range; turn OW intra-EMU ex France/€-SSA spreads, Aditya Chordia, 8 July 2024).

In the UK, the Labour Party’s landslide election victory had no rates market impact but raised questions about how the party will deal with a challenging fiscal backdrop (see UK: Election preview, Allan Monks, 1 July 2024). While no meaningful unwind of Brexit is in the cards, our UK economist sees that benefits from reforms will either be small or take time to bear fruit (see UK: An uphill struggle for Labour in government, Allan Monks, 12 April 2024). Absent some good luck, something will likely eventually have to give. UK authorities could try to find leeway around the fiscal rules that would likely involve the five-year debt target rolling forward each year to open up an extra £10bn or so. Alternatively, Labour may seek a consensus to alter its debt target, creating additional room for public investment (see UK: Exploring Labour’s subtle Plan B, Allan Monks, 5 July 2024).

The upcoming US elections are marked by extraordinary developments and carry the most significant consequences for the macroeconomy next year and beyond. We see rising risks around the elections, as trade, immigration, industrial, and competition policies will be shaped by the presidential election. The past two weeks have seen historic and unpredictable developments in the wake of the assassination attempt on Trump and the ongoing debate within the Democratic Party on Biden’s viability to remain in the presidential race with almost 110 days until election day on November 5.

Expectations for the return to a divided Congress are now seen as uncertain, with increased focus on the policy implications of a Red Wave. Democrats are struggling to regain the narrative beyond Biden as a candidate, and some factions of the party are fearful that the elections could slip away from them, as Republicans have gained momentum going into the election. Polls continue to show narrow margins within the margin of error for Biden vs Trump at the national level, but Trump has maintained a lead in the key swing dates. We note that even though there are 244 million citizens who are eligible to vote, the election outcome will come down to a handful of key swing states. In 2016, Hillary Clinton was only defeated by 77,000 votes in Pennsylvania, Michigan and Wisconsin. In 2020, Biden defeated Trump by only 44,000 votes in Wisconsin, Arizona and Georgia.

A Trump victory could bring higher tariffs, higher inflation and increased fiscal spending, with growing concerns on stagflation risks. While there is greater certainty around a September ease, there is less conviction that this marks the start of a cycle that will extend through 2025 and 2026. The size of the US fiscal deficit remains a source of market consternation, but there is general acceptance that the issue will not be on the political agenda in an election year. The outcome of the election will shape the possibility and nature of any fiscal consolidation, including whether some tax cuts in the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 will be expanded, extended, made permanent, or allowed to expire at the end of 2025. Broad extension of the tax cuts was estimated to increase the cumulative deficit between 2025 and 2034 by \$4.6trn according to the Joint Committee on Taxation and the CBO.

Conservative groups and Trump supporters are increasingly focused on Day 1 readiness in the event that Trump wins the presidential election. A Trump 2.0 cabinet could include names whose policies would be considerably market friendly for the US Treasury and the Fed, but there is greater uncertainty about foreign policy, trade and defense appointments (see Washington Policy Perspectives: Rising risks to 2025 outlook as US election uncertainty looms larger, Joyce Chang, 15 July 2024).

A Trump victory could manifest in the form of raising existing tariffs, and our US Economics and FX strategy teams highlight the risk of higher inflation and a stronger dollar. A Trump 2.0 administration has promised a 60% tariff on all Chinese imports and a 10% universal tariff on all other trading partners and/or a 100% tariff on auto production in Mexico by Chinese automakers. Higher tariffs would have significant implications for US equities across sectors/styles (see here and here). While independent estimates project that the 10% tariff alone could raise \~\$300bn in revenue, this would be offset by second and third order effects of their implementation (see Global Thematic Strategy: Key Upcoming Elections, Equity Implications, Policy-Linked Stock Ideas, Dubravko Lakos-Bujas et al., 7 May 2024). While tariffs during Trump 1.0 raised the basket of consumer prices by around 0.3%, a 60% tariff on all Chinese imports could raise the price level 1.1%, while a 10% universal tariff could raise the price level by 1.5% and could lead to 5% appreciation of the dollar. In standard models, the economic growth impact of tariffs is surprisingly small but does not capture policy uncertainty (see US: Economic policy implications of the election, Michael Feroli, 29 June 2024 and FX Mid-Year Outlook: Ten questions (and answers) on currencies in 2H24, Meera Chandan et al., 28 June 2024).

Under a Trump administration, the fiscal deficit would also come back into focus, as the extension of the Tax Cut and Jobs Act (TCJA) amounts to 1.3% of GDP, but a divided Congress would likely demand compromise for a higher rate. If all these features of the TCJA were allowed to expire as currently written in law, tax receipts are estimated to increase by just under \$400bn per year, or around 1.3% of GDP. Biden 2.0 would see certain TCJA provisions extended for those making under \$400,000. Those making over that limit would be reverted to older, higher rates. Biden has also proposed raising the corporate rate from 21% to 28% and increasing the Global Intangible Low-Taxed Income (GILTI) tax rate to 21%. These provisions would raise around \$150bn per year, or about a half percent of GDP. Under Trump 2.0, it is likely all of the provisions of the TCJA would be extended, but he may need to compromise if it is a Democratic-controlled House, which could possibly see the corporate tax rate move higher.

Our US Equity strategy team sees the possibility of a range of policy outcomes from the US elections, some with meaningfully negative implications. The team has laid out scenarios for the election outcomes, divided into Bluelock Scenario (Biden victory and divided Congress), Red Top scenario (Trump victory and divided Congress), Red Surge scenario (Trump victory and Republican House and Senate) and Blue Wave Tail (Biden victory and Democratic House and Senate) (see Global Thematic Strategy: Key Upcoming Elections, Equity Implications, Policy-Linked Stock Ideas, Dubravko Lakos-Bujas, 7 May 2024).

In the event of a Red Top or Red Surge, there is risk of substantial changes to US trade policy, foreign policy, energy policy and domestic regulation. Key legislative achievements under the current administration would face scrutiny, such as provisions of the Inflation Reduction Act. Given the amount of funds going to Red states, significant changes in IRA spending may not be in focus, but the openended provisions such as tax credit bonus adders could face a slowdown. A Red Surge could open the door for permanent individual tax cuts that are sunsetting at the end of 2025. Further, the statutory corporate tax rate would be maintained (currently 21%, previously 35% pre-TCJA passage in 2017), with the potential for new business incentives/subsidies funded by proceeds from the tariff increases. Under a Red Surge Scenario, an additional debate on trade policy could focus on the repeal of permanent normal trade relations (PNTR) with China, which would require congressional support. While no clear position has yet been staked out by Republicans regarding Defense spending, rising geopolitical tensions could trigger calls for additional outlays, especially for domestic infrastructure.

A victory by the incumbent Democratic party is the most market-neutral scenario, as a Biden victory would cement key legislative achievements of the past few years, including the Bipartisan Infrastructure Law, Inflation Reduction Act and CHIPS and Science Act. Under a split Congress scenario, follow-on legislation to the CHIPS and Science Act, Child Tax Credit expansion, changes to corporate R&D deductibility, and industrial policy addressing Critical Minerals could still be passed on a bipartisan basis. The incumbent could also look to prioritize policy initiatives on trade, anti-trust and student debt, which could be actioned by executive authority alone (e.g., recent steel/aluminum tariff proposal).

With the exception of value vs. growth and financials, our global markets strategist Nikolaos Panigirtzoglou points out that markets do not appear to have priced in much of a risk premium for the inflationary implications of Trump’s campaign policies (see Flows Liquidity: How are markets currently viewing the US election?, Nikolaos Panigirtzoglou et al., 26 June 2024). Tariffs across all US imports are likely to lead to higher US prices, while severely restricting immigration is likely to put upward pressure on wages. And extending the 2017 Tax Cut and Jobs Act (TCJA) that are set to expire at the end of 2025 is likely to induce a more expansionary US fiscal and debt trajectory, thus also posing inflation risk. Our Equity strategists highlight that the momentum factor remains the key driver of equity gains, while the benign macro environment has kept volatility low. Momentum remains concentrated in Mega Caps (median Momentum decile market cap current: \$68bn vs. \$40bn last year) and High Quality (P/E spread relative to market at 99%ile vs 35-year history). The surprisingly low equity volatility YTD is driven by fundamental and technical factors, including rising markets, risk complacency, low realized correlation, and large volatility selling flows.

Our Global Commodity strategy team believes that a second Trump administration would likely see a major realignment of federal energy policy via executive actions to lighten the regulatory burden on oil and gas companies. A Trump victory could see significant barriers lifted by selling more federal land and eliminating climate regulations. Under Trump 2.0, it is unlikely that Biden’s IRA law would be materially altered, given that 75% of investment in clean energy manufacturing pledged since passage is destined for states with Republican governors and 83% to Republicanheld congressional districts. Tax credit policy for clean electricity and large-scale renewable power production, along with investment incentives for carbon capture, nuclear and hydrogen energy, as well as sustainable aviation fuel and biofuels are the least at-risk elements of the law. Republican states are leaders in clean energy deployment, attracting 80% of all the funding, with Georgia, Texas, and Oklahoma the main recipients. Texas led the nation in 2023 for new solar installations, eclipsing California for the second time in the last three years, followed by Florida. Meanwhile, Republicangoverned Texas, Oklahoma, and Iowa were among the top three states for wind installations. A Trump administration, however, would likely attempt to scale back the consumer elements of the IRA, such as incentives for the purchase of EVs and home heating (see Global Commodities: 2024 US Election Watch – Implications for Commodities, J.P. Morgan Commodities Research, 7 May 2024).

The bullish USD view remains intact in the medium term, driven by the dollar’s relative “safe haven” status and high carry. The dollar's considerable yield advantage persists, with 40% of global central banks already cutting rates. US growth exceptionalism still lingers, despite moderated US growth and an improving outlook in Europe and stabilizing growth in China (see FX Mid-year Outlook, Meera Chandan

et al., 28 June 2024).

Turning to the recent EM elections, Mexico has experienced the greatest volatility as the fiscal outlook has come into focus, while the formation of a Government of National Unity (GNU) in South Africa has reassured markets and India has returned to BAU. MSCI Mexico was down 11.3% in June, as MXN depreciated by -5.0%. The strong mandate for Morena (i.e., Legislative supermajority) and its intentions to pass a reform agenda that could alter the equilibrium of forces among the Judiciary and the Executive have raised concerns about the concentration of power, while the incoming government is not aiming at a short-term fiscal reform, although it has confirmed the continuation of key policy makers. South Africa has cleared a key hurdle with the formation of the GNU, and ZAR appreciated by 3.3% in June. India has returned to BAU, as the BJP-led NDA formed the government for the third consecutive time. BJP has successfully run a coalition government from 1999-2004 and 2014-2024, and our economists expect the current economic and fiscal policies to continue. India’s growth outlook remains robust, with 8.2% real GDP growth forecast in FY24 . 3. Three Ds and counting—de-globalization, de-dollarization and de-population remain long-term secular trends that do not appear to be in prices or well understood.

The long-term risk of de-population has come under intensifying scrutiny, as fertility rates continue to fall further below the long-term replacement rate of 2.1 in most countries outside Africa. In 2024, fertility rates are projected by the UN to be below 2.1 in 70% of countries outside of Africa, which jointly account for 68% of the world’s population (83% ex-Africa).<sup>2</sup> In many of these countries, fertility has fallen well below this long-term replacement level. In the most extreme case, the average woman is now expected to have only 0.68 children in her lifetime in South Korea as of 2024.<sup>3</sup> Thus, as shown in Figure 8, some countries now find themselves on the cusp of a dramatic decline in population

through the 21st century. If fertility rates continue to fall apace, the world as a whole is at serious risk of population decline starting in the next 10-15 years. Countries experiencing sharp population declines will likely face permanent economic contraction, declining global influence and rising government debt burdens relative to economic capacity.

## 4. Industrial policy has become ubiquitous and trumps trade policy and antitrust risks.

Industrial policy has become universal and institutionalized as “economic statecraft.” The use of industrial policy has risen dramatically in recent years, with 2,500 industrial policy interventions worldwide in 2023 alone, or three times the number in 2019, with greater concerns about the tradeoffs and growing fragmentation into geopolitically distant blocs (see The great debate: Macro and market questions by the dozen, Joyce Chang et al., 2 May 2024 and Changing Global Linkages: A new cold war, IMF, 5 April 2024). More than two-thirds of the interventions last year were trade-distorting, as they likely discriminated against foreign commercial interests. US trade policy is rapidly evolving with the return of buy American provisions and the formation of trade blocs with countries that are deemed as friendly allies. In a polarizing US political environment, trade policy is one of the few issues garnering bipartisan support. Irrespective of the election outcome, there is risk of a meaningful increase in the use of industrial policy to address trade tensions with China. 2024 could be the calm before the storm for US-China relations as US elections and China’s domestic issues provide a short-lived reprieve to the US-China strategic competition.

The Biden administration has taken an aggressive approach to antitrust enforcement, while a second Trump administration is perceived as likely to take a more lenient approach to enforcement of the relevant statutes. For the remainder of this administration, additional regulatory measures and White House Executive Orders are unlikely to be proposed (see 2024 US Election Watch: Post-presidential debate still sees a toss-up but next three weeks critical for Biden, Joyce Chang et al., 3 July 2024). Trump’s VP running mate, JD Vance, has outlined a much more aggressive approach to Big Tech antitrust enforcement to level the playing field for smaller tech companies. Some conservatives are becoming more receptive to a vigorous competition policy, particularly an antitrust agenda aimed at Big Tech, earning the name “Khan-servatives” in reference to current FTC Chair Lina Khan. While this may not be a macroeconomic issue at least in the sense for the contour of the business cycle, many economists believe increasing industrial concentration has longrun macroeconomic impacts, including increased profit margins and a lower labor share of income. But even after more than three years of the Biden administration, the labor share remains near all-time lows, indicating that these industrial trends won’t turn on a dime with a change in the White House (see US: Economic policy implications of the election, Michael Feroli, 28 June 2024).

EM dedicated bond funds have seen -\$12bn in outflows YTD after more than \$31bn in outflows seen in the second half of 2023, split evenly between hard and local currency, while EM equity outflows are at -\$10bn YTD. 6. US-China competition intensifies beyond tech war, with critical minerals now in focus.

The latest tariffs announcement by the Biden administration confirm the increased use of industrial policy, which initially targeted semiconductor supply chains and has now expanded to encompass clean energy technology. The new tariffs are noteworthy for imposing import tariffs that restrict access to the US market, whereas the earlier tariffs focused on export restrictions to deny access to US advanced technology. The latest tariffs also targeted critical minerals for the first time, with tariff levels set to increase from 0% to 25%, which elevates the debate on whether China’s dominance in the critical minerals supply chain will emerge as the latest battleground for US-China strategic competition. Indeed, the three industrial policy legislative acts that passed in the US, i.e., the Bipartisan Infrastructure Law (BIL), CHIPS and Science Act (CHIPS+), and the Inflation Reduction Act (IRA), in combination, have included investment exceeding \$135bn to build out the critical minerals supply chain, including sourcing and processing and battery manufacturing. They have also helped spur over \$500bn in private investments. In our view, any prolonged export restriction on critical minerals will accelerate the rise of alternatives to China.

However, the expansion of industrial policy to critical minerals is also igniting a debate on whether these actions are at odds with the goals to advance the clean energy transition, since Chinese EVs, batteries and other products are globally competitive. Tariffs could delay EV adoption, and the contradictions are similar to past policies, including the Biden administration’s temporary 24-month reprieve to allow duty-free imports of solar modules and cells from ASEAN countries in June 2022, which expired on June 6, 2024. This was to ensure that the US has access to a sufficient supply of solar modules and cells while domestic manufacturers scale up production. The latest US tariffs also led to Europe introducing their own tariffs on Chinese EVs. The European Green Deal includes domestic content requirements similar to the IRA, and the European Commission formally launched an anti-subsidy investigation into the imports of battery electric vehicles (BEV) from China in October 2023 and has imposed provisional tariffs of up to 37.6% on imports of BEVs from China. China has threatened to respond through imposing retaliatory tariffs as high as 25% on imported cars with large engines.

7. Non-oil markets are among this year’s standout outperformers, and the rally could still have further room to run, particularly for sugar and grains, alongside the continued support for precious and base metal prices.

Geopolitics and powerful structural shifts are behind the rally in commodity markets, with demand for some of the world’s most vital commodities continuing to climb. Our commodity strategy team keeps their long-held view that Brent oil will average \$84/bbl in the third quarter and hit another \$90 by August/September, underpinned by expectations that global demand will outpace supply in the summer quarter. Non-oil markets have led the 1H24 rally, with copper, gold, cocoa, and frozen orange juice reaching all-time highs. Our Commodity strategists see an additional 12% upside for the BCOM Index until year end.

Our Commodity strategists remain structurally bullish on base and precious metal prices over the medium term. They see 4-6% upside for gold and silver prices into year-end, with targeted highs of \$2,6000/oz and \$34/oz, respectively, in 2025. Structural bullish drivers of gold include concerns over the US fiscal deficit, central bank reserve diversification into gold from de-dollarization, inflation hedging and ongoing geopolitical risks that will endure regardless of the US elections outcome. A substantial step up in tariffs and a tougher foreign policy stance on China could reignite US inflationary concerns, continuing to fuel increased gold demand from the PBoC (see Metals Weekly: Rallying base and precious metals prices have further to run in 2024, Gregory C. Shearer, 14 April 2024).

Although copper has returned \~15% YTD, JPM sees room for a 7% appreciation in copper prices by December, with more upside in 2025, and a base case for copper to reach new all-time highs of \$11,500 by 3Q25. Long-term drivers of copper demand will stem from the energy transition aligned growth in NEVs, renewable power, and grid enhancement. Supply tightness and the uptick in global manufacturing PMIs have driven copper prices to record levels, but fundamentals in China likely need to improve to sustain price momentum, with the base case for copper to reach new alltime highs of \$11,500 by 3Q25.

The highest returns could actually come from some segments of the agricultural commodities complex despite concerns of risks from a change in the US presidency and the potential for tariffs and dilution of US export share. Specifically, our strategists forecast that sugar and grains could generate projected 27% and 19% returns, respectively, by year-end, while volatility is depressed across the complex and offers value, particularly across the longer dates. Agri market performance has been predominantly led by rising grain and oilseed inventories in the US through 2023/24, and associated heavy investor short positioning, despite a deterioration of exportable surplus elsewhere, after weather-related production challenges. Looking into 2024/25, we remain constructive agri commodity markets on a fundamental basis amid rising US export market share and, more recently, also on a value basis following the June sell-off. Sugar prices are projected to rise amid a stagnant supply side after years of underinvestment across the sugar & ethanol and beet sectors, amid consistent demand growth. Now, a decline in cane volume in major sugar producer and exporter Brazil in addition to dry conditions are compounding the deficit market balance. Drought across the Black Sea Bread Basket has dampened production and export prospects of grains, with wheat exports projected to decline to a three-season low and corn exports to decline to a seven-season low following losses across Russia and Ukraine. Similarly, corn production losses in Argentina following the prevalence of corn stunt disease have yet to be fully accounted for. Higher-than-average temperatures and citrus greening disease is throttling orange-producing regions in Brazil and Florida, curbing supply, and driving up citrus prices. Cocoa prices have risen sharply amid a global supply shortage, driven by structural factors including chronic underinvestment in cocoa farms and disease pressures, with prices likely to remain higher for longer. Like cocoa, palm oil supply faces structural challenges, limiting output growth over the medium term, due to ageing stock, weather and the natural time lag between investment and output in tree crops (see Global Commodities: Got commodities this summer?, J.P. Morgan Commodities Research, 20 June 2024).

From a policy perspective, our agricultural commodity strategy team examines the circumstances and consequences of the 2018-2019 US-China trade war, which reduced China’s purchases of US agri products after retaliatory tariffs were applied on US imports (see Agricultural Markets Weekly: Tracking China’s purchases of US ag products and rehashing the US China Trade War, Tracey Allen, 9 July 2024). They point out that the proposed tariffing under a Trump 2.0 scenario would play out at a time when the world’s exportable inventories of corn and wheat outside of the US are at critical lows following a decline in exportable surpluses across the Black Sea and South America, leaving the US with a rare opportunity to increase export market share. Positioning across US centric agri markets—grains, oilseeds and cotton—is trading at cautiously low levels, amid limited risk appetite and tight grain stocks. During the 2018- 2019 US-China trade war, Brazil was a net beneficiary, accelerating the growth of its agricultural export market share into China. China’s soybean imports in 2024/2025 are expected to remain steady on the year, amid comfortable stock availability. Brazil is once again well positioned to continue to grow market share to China.

8. Climate transition faces a trilemma—financing challenges, debt sustainability, and political feasibility—and sustainable assets under management have flatlined and underperformed.

Our ESG and Sustainability Research team reports that sustainable fixed income and equity funds across both US and Europe have underperformed their respective broad fund universes and the performance outlook for sustainable funds remains unclear. Sustainable AUM grew by 2.3% in the first five months of the year to surpass \$3trn, driven by returns of 2% YTD and inflows of 0.3%, the latter entirely in fixed income. Sustainable equity funds have seen outflows in the first five months of the year, driven by the US, while Europe remained flat. Superimposing our global strategists’ preferred sector allocation with the sector tilts found in the average sustainable fund does not yield a clear conclusion on the direction of performance. Similarly, our quant framework suggests a mixed outlook for ESGQ stocks: with three of the four regional indicators (Europe, US and Japan) pointing to an environment that favors cyclicals over defensives; tailwinds for ESGQ in the short term appear unlikely.

Beyond the asset class performance, the EU Parliament’s “shift to the right” portends a slowdown of the green agenda, not a U-turn, with a change of “tone” towards economic growth, supply-chain resilience, and regulatory simplification. Backlash to the green transformation is evident, and overregulation needs to be addressed, as the EU is increasingly seen as excessively regulatory, even punitive when it comes to ecological transition, with a Green Deal that exacerbates territorial divides and increases dependence on China (see Viewpoints from Germany: Global economic optimism meets rising local anxiety, Joyce Chang, Greg Fuzesi Alexander Wise, 12 June 2024). The European Commission has proposed reducing financial reporting requirements by 25% for small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) and doubling the employee threshold for European Sustainability Reporting Standards, which will significantly reduce the number of SMEs that fall under the European Sustainability Reporting Standards.

The UK, by contrast, is likely to see a reacceleration of green policies in the polls. Monitoring future discussions on the 2040 climate target will be crucial to gauge the level of green ambition in the EU. We still expect the EU sustainable finance agenda to move forward across several fronts: reporting (CSRD), risk management (CS3D) and marketing of financial products (SFDR reform, development of “transition” strategies).

In the US, elections will likely be a binary event for climate policy, given the opposed views between the Biden administration and former President Trump. A Trump victory could see the US withdrawing from the Paris Agreement once more, with more negative consequences than in 2017, when the science was less developed and the consensus advocating to keep the global temperature increase below 2C. The scientific community has since moved on, having coalesced around a more ambitious 1.5C target. We also expect US election campaign rhetoric to create volatility in the alternative energy space, with some “non-core” elements of the IRA, such as ITC adders and incentives for longer-dated technologies, at risk. However, bar a Red Wave, we view a full repeal of the IRA as unlikely, given much of the announced capital investment is to be deployed in red states.

## 9. Investors still aiming to rebalance towards bonds but are in no hurry, as momentum still favors equities.

We stay with the view that the classic 60/40 equity bond strategic allocation is making a comeback as end investors recognize that equities have become expensive and are thus set to earn below historic returns over the coming 5-10 years, while the rise in bond yields has made fixed income a competitive asset class again. At the same time, it is recognized that while valuation is an excellent signal for long-term future returns and strategic assets, it has little signaling value for short-term returns and tactical asset allocation. Price momentum tends to dominate on a horizon of 6-12 months. Hence, most investors think in dollar-averaging terms, making modest efforts to slowly but steadily move towards their strategic target while paying constant attention to fundamentals and price momentum.

Year to date, the US and world economies have surprised to the upside, and so have corporate earnings and inflation. That provided positive macro momentum to equities and negative momentum to bonds. World equity prices are up 10% YTD, with the US outperforming at 15%, while bonds yield have continue to rise, \~50bp on the US Aggregate index, pushing its price down 3%. We thus find that in the alldominant US market, end investors are now even more invested in stocks, to a new all-time high (Figure 15), while long-term valuation signals (Figure 16) are telling us US bonds and equities are set to produce about the same return over the coming decade, just over 5%.

Over the coming 6-12 months, one should expect that investors will continue to buy more bonds than equities, at a steady pace and without any hurry. Global credit markets remain in ‘The Just Enough World,’ where all-in yields remain just enough to offset thin spreads (see Spread Strategy Spotlight: ‘The Just Enough World’, Stephen Dulake et al., 12 July 2024). Year to date, global bond ETFs and mutual funds have seen \$525bn of net inflows, versus only \$293bn in equities (see Chart A1 in Flows Liquidity: Updated bond demand and supply for H2, Nikolaos Panigirtzoglou, 3 July 2024). At that pace, 2024 bond fund buying should come in at \~\$1trn, similar to the high flow levels we saw in 2019 and 2021 and a decent acceleration from the \$630bn seen last year.

## 10. Risk appetite for private credit remains strong and offers higher returns, but alternative assets are now growing at a slower pace than public markets.

The universe of alternatives (private equity, private real estate, private credit, hedge funds, digital assets) has expanded to exceed \$30trn in Q1 2024. The share of alternatives in the total asset universe remained stable at 14.9% in Q1 2024. Fundraising for alternative assets had a strong start in January but has slowed in subsequent months, bringing the YTD fundraising across alternatives to \$430bn. Annualized, this YTD fundraising rises to \$1,145bn, the slowest pace since 2018. By comparison, bond issuance in the HY and leveraged loan space within public markets stand at \$169bn and \$726bn YTD, respectively. Our global market strategy team remains overweight on alternatives vs publicly traded traditional assets, as private credit still offers yields of around 11%, while hedge funds are on track to generate positive alpha for four years in a row in 2024. However, their stance is cautious on private equity and private real estate due to stillelevated valuation gaps for legacy private equity and legacy non-transacted private real estate. They also remain cautious in crypto markets (see Alternative Investments Outlook and Strategy: Private credit activity slows as public market take back share, Nikolaos Panigirtzoglou et al., 21 May 2024).

Private credit is now nearly as large as the high yield bond market, with demand unlikely to abate. The IMF estimates that it has reached \$2.1trn in global AUM, while J.P. Morgan’s Credit Research team estimates it is closer to \$2.6trn (\$1.8trn in the US) and \$3.1trn (\$2.1trn in the US), including dry powder. Our global credit team’s updated direct lending fund figures show longer-term average annualized returns of 8.3%. There is a wide dispersion in return, which suggests to us that diversification across managers is more important for investors in the private credit asset class compared with public credit markets (see Credit Watch: Private Credit Uncovered – uncovering even more, Stephen Dulake et al., 5 April 2024).
