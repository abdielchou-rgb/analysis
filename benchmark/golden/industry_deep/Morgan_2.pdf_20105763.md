# 2024 Outlook: Two to Own; Three to Avoid; Sector back to 'In-Line'

Heading into 2024, we lower our sector rating back to 'In-Line'. However, we see opportunities in selected stocks. We reiterate Overweight ratings on TotalEnergies and BP, offset by Underweight ratings on Equinor, Galp and OMV.

Sector rating back to In-Line: The fundamentals of oil & gas markets have turned relatively 'soft' and interest rates and inflation expectations are unlikely to provide the tailwinds they did in 2023. Also, we foresee modest negative revisions to consensus estimates for earnings and shareholder distributions. The sector is still highly cash generative and valuations are not demanding, particularly on certain metrics. However, with this set of factors, the case for the sector outperformance has weakened.

Total (Overweight; Top Pick) - resilience in a weaker environment: TotalEnergies offers a differentiated portfolio consisting of both a thriving oil & gas business with growth prospects and a new-energies segment that is profitable, growing, and turning FCF positive in the not-too-distant future - a rare combination.

BP (Overweight) - large upside potential in case catalysts emerge: Two consecutive quarterly earnings misses and the lack of a permanent CEO have taken the wind out of BP's sails. Yet, BP is the only major where shareholder distribution as % of CFFO can rise and the distribution yield has room to compress. That combination offers substantial upside if quarterly performance were to improve and concerns over strategic directions were to diminish - still a compelling risk/reward.

Equinor (Underweight) - lower distributions ahead in weak European gas market: We have a relatively bearish view on European gas, which is typically the key driver of relative performance for Equinor. Also, consensus earnings estimates have most downside, we'd argue, and we see a faster-than-consensus decline in distributions.

Galp (Underweight) - distributions likely to lag peers: Galp's shares reflect most of the operational positives seen in 2H23 and a much softer oil price environment will likely lead to consensus downgrades. Galp shares are more correlated to oil prices than peers and both free cash flow and distribution yields are amongst the lowest in the sector.

## OMV (Underweight) - material downgrades as petrochemical headwinds

continue: A continued weaker outlook for petchem markets in 2024, combined with OMV's high exposure to this segment, will likely lead more consensus downgrades. However, once consensus estimates come down, we'd see little valuation support - on our estimates, OMV is on one of the lowest FCFY and DY in the sector.

## Two to Own, Three to Avoid, Sector to 'In-Line'

## Looking back, looking forward

In the end, 2023 was a year in three stages for European Energy. The sector underperformed the market from January to July as oil & gas prices declined, inflation expectations rolled over, interest rates stayed under pressure and the market shrugged off some of the geopolitical risk premium that was built into Energy stocks in 2022.

However, this turned around sharply in July as the Saudi 'lollipop' started to support oil prices, European gas prices found a floor after various supply disruptions, interest rates rallied again and geopolitical risk events boosted the sector once more. For a brief while in early November, Energy was the second-best performing sector in the European market year-to-date.

Still, this was not to last and much of these relative gains were given up in the last two months of the year. For starters, 3Q earnings reports were mostly underwhelming. Also, the OPEC meeting of late November failed to inspire the oil market, putting pressure on oil prices late last year. Expectations for cold winter weather in NW Europe have remained elusive, driving TTF prices lower. As interest rates and inflation expectations peaked and rolled over, other parts of the market started a powerful year-end rally, which Energy failed to keep up with.

In the end, the MSCI Europe Energy generated a total return of \~12% in 2023 (in US\$ with dividends reinvested), trailing the MSCI Europe by \~800bp.

## Stock picks for 2024

## TotalEnergies (Overweight and Top Pick)

TotalEnergies became our top-pick in 2023 and this remains the case today. Over the years, TotalEnergies has built up a track record of strong execution, careful capital allocation and making fewer mistakes than others. This has been reflected in its share price performance, which has compounded at nearly double the sector's average over the last 30 years, in a remarkably steady fashion.

Looking ahead, TotalEnergies offers a differentiated portfolio: the company has both a strong oil & gas business with a deep resource base that offers (far) above-average growth opportunities compared to its European peers. At the same time, the company's new energies business - consolidated in its Integrated Power segment - is profitable, growing and has line-of-sight on turning FCF positive within a few years. This combination of both a thriving 'old' and 'new' energies is relatively rare.

TotalEnergies' earnings and share price have historically been relatively lowly correlated to oil & gas prices. Together with its strong balance sheet, these are attractive characteristics for a period of softness in commodity prices. TotalEnergies' shares are not the cheapest amongst amongst its peer group. Still, we still see a route for TotalEnergies share price to reach our new price target of €72.4 per share, implying \~16% upside.

## BP (Overweight)

BP enjoyed a period of strong relative performance in 2022 and early 2023, but a large part of this was reversed in 2H23. The company has encouraging long-term financial targets, which have been revised higher several times, but market confidence in those targets is low after a set of quarterly earnings misses and uncertainty over the CEO position.

Nevertheless, this has left BP as a compelling valuation case. BP is the lowest valued of the Energy majors on range of metrics, including P/CF and FCF yield. Also, the company has the second-highest total distribution yield (after Equinor) and the second-lowest payout ratio (after Eni) in the sector. If both its payout ratio and distribution yield were to converge to the sector average, that would generate \~30% upside to BP's share price relative to the sector.

However, this merely highlights the extent of the upside that is possible. Realising this potential probably requires two things: 1) a return to consistent quarterly earnings delivery, and 2) the removal of uncertainty over future strategy via the appointment of a permanent CEO. Although neither of these two factors are in place at the moment, they are still quite likely to emerge in coming quarters. Therefore, we would argue that BP is still one of the most compelling risk/rewards within the European Energy sector.

## Equinor (Downgrade to Underweight)

Equinor remains a high-quality firm with a strong asset base and prudent management.
Nevertheless, we expect the shares to lag the sector this year, driven by three factors.

First, we are relatively bearish on the outlook for European gas prices, which is arguably the main determinant of Equinor's share price performance relative to the sector. European gas demand has remained weak, LNG imports continue to be strong, and inventory levels are (very) high by historical standards. There is some risk premium built into TTF gas prices for the possibility of a cold spell over the remaining part of the winter, but eventually, we suspect this premium to diminish.

Second, we expect negative earnings revisions. This is the case for most stocks in the sector, but we see more downside risk to Equinor's consensus forecasts than for most others. This is partly due to our view on gas prices, but even simply assuming prices in line with the current forward curve, we would argue that consensus net income for 2024 is \~20% too high.

Third, we take a more conservative view on shareholder distributions than consensus. Equinor shares found considerable support from unusually high shareholder distributions of \~\$17bn in 2023. In turn, this was the result of extraordinary FCF in 2022 when European gas prices spiked to unprecedented heights. Now that gas prices are much lower again, we suspect that shareholder distributions will decline faster than consensus estimates.

Based on a European gas price of around €30/MW by 2026 (in-line with current forward curve), we estimate that Equinor's sustainable FCF potential is around \$6.5bn per year from 2026 onwards. Therefore, distributions will likely need to decline from \$17bn last year to \~\$6.5bn per year from 2026.

During 2024 and 2025, Equinor can distribute more as the balance sheet is still in 'net cash' territory. However, there is considerable uncertainty over the outlook for the global and European gas markets after 4Q 2025 when the LNG sector starts a large, multi-year period of capacity additions.

In our experience, Equinor is operationally risk-taking but financially conservative. Given the uncertainty over the second half of the decade, we would expect management to be relatively cautious in making shareholder distributions 'from the balance sheet' in 2024 and 2025. We estimate that dividends + buybacks will amount to \~\$12bn and \~\$8bn in those years respectively, but that is below consensus at \$14bn and \$10bn for these two years.

Finally, we find Equinor's valuation relatively uncompelling. We already assume that the company's ordinary dividends per share will grow from 1.22 in 2023 to 1.71 cents by 2030, after which we assume that steady-state growth of \~4% through 2050 kicks in. That dividend trajectory strikes as not conservative at all. However, discounted back to end-2024 at a 9% cost of equity, the present value of this dividend stream is no more than \~NKr 264, which is our new price target. Hence, following a similar approach as for the rest of the sector, we see \~20% downside to the shares.

## Galp Energia (Downgrade to Underweight)

Following material underperformance over 2022 and 1H23, Galp Energia's shares surged materially in 2H23, primarily reflecting upgrades to 2023 production guidance, significantly better contribution from midstream businesses and an improvement in oil prices, particularly in 3Q23. The upgrades to production guidance followed a material improvement in production efficiency in Brazil, directly related to substantial improvement in decline rates at Tupi in 2H23.

We believe that the operational positives seen in E&P production and midstream businesses are now broadly reflected in consensus. 2024 consensus production currently stands at \~122kboe/d, broadly flat y/y and well above current guidance of >115kboe/d. However, as we mark-to-market to the current forward curve and incorporate a softer macro outlook, we see material \~10% to consensus CFFO estimates over 2024/25. We continue to believe there is limited clarity on capex over 2024-25. We assume net capex to average \~€1.2bn p.a. during 2024/25. Including average annual dividend payments of \~ €180m to minority shareholders of Petrogal Brasil, we forecast Galp's FCF (i.e. after minority dividend payments) to average \~€700m p.a. in 2024-25, leading to a average FCF yield of \~7.0% -amongst the lowest in the sector.

Importantly, this also prevents the company from raising shareholder distributions materially. Galp's distribution policy has dividends increasing by 4.0% annually and total distributions up to a maximum of a third of the operating cash flow. \~30% of our new CFFO forecasts leaves the buyback at €250m to be executed in 2024e and 2025e. We also assume the buybacks to channel into a slightly higher dividend growth rate and forecast \~6.3% and 7.5% y/y dividend growth for 2024 and 2025 respectively, taking DPS to €0.58 in 2024e and €0.62 in 2025e. This also implies an average distribution (dividend +buyback) yield of \~7.0% for 2024/25 - the lowest in the sector ( Exhibit 39 )

We also forecast Galp's gearing (ND/ND+E) to remain high relative to peers at \~31% by end 2024e, broadly in-line with 32% at end 3Q23.

As a result, we expect the company's shareholder distributions to lag peers. Our price target of €12.8 offers \~9% downside.

## OMV (Underweight)

OMV shares have been negatively impacted throughout 2023 by a sustained fall in petchem margins and depressed earnings in the Chemicals and Materials segment. Expectations for a late 2023 recovery have not been realised and have been consistently delayed, impacted by an over-supplied market. We expect a material recovery in margins at least post 1H24, a sentiment echoed by OMV management who, during the 3Q23 earnings call, expressed views that 1Q24 will likely continue to be difficult in the Chemicals space. This lends support for our view of a more drawn-out recovery for margins here. Within our coverage universe, OMV has the highest leverage to the petrochemical business.

From a financial perspective, we continue to forecast material reductions to consensus estimates. This is despite the heavy downgrades to consensus 2024 net income post 3Q23 results ( Exhibit 44 - Exhibit 45 ). At the current forward curve, our 2024-25 CFFO estimates are \~13-14% below consensus, with the difference primarily arising from lower

contribution from both Chemicals and Energy businesses. In addition, our 2024/25 capex estimates are \~5% higher than consensus, with the difference partly explained by the increased costs at the Kallo PDH project. Overall, these two effects lead to our FCF estimates being \~45% lower than consensus in 2024-25.

OMV's valuation also remains rich. On our forecasts, the 2023-24 average FCF yield (including the dividends paid to minorities - which yield \~3.5%) is \~6.0% ( Exhibit 39 ). The shareholder distribution yield for 2024, at \~9%, is also low relative to the sector, and is not covered by FCF - we forecast 2024 FCF yield at \~5.0%. Our price target of €37.9 offers \~5% downside.

Finally, we continue to see strategic uncertainty for OMV. The company's long-term strategy is rooted in expanding further into sustainable fuels and chemicals, primarily through acquisitions and disposing E&P assets. In line with this strategy, so far, the company has announced negotiations with ADNOC on a potential combination of Borealis and Borouge. Whilst we are yet to see any announcement of a deal, we note uncertainty related to valuation and any potential cash payouts from OMV. In addition, OMV has highlighted that it could potentially pursue more acquisitions and has noted the target of growing sustainable chemical products within its product portfolio.

## Sector rating back to In-Line

The relative performance of the Energy sector is typically driven by five factors: 1) oil & gas prices, 2) the direction of inflation and interest rates, 3) geopolitical risk, 4) the outlook for consensus earnings revisions and shareholder distributions, and 5) valuation.

Back in August 2023, we raised our sector rating to 'Attractive' (see here) as we argued that oil & gas prices were well underpinned at the time, earnings upgrades were ahead, the outlook for shareholder distributions looked attractive and valuations were undemanding.

In the months thereafter, many of these factors played out, although a lot of the relative gains that followed were given up in the last two months of the year. Judging the sector against each of these five factors now, we see a mixed scorecard that no longer supports the 'Attractive' stance:

1) Oil and gas markets are well supplied. We would characterise fundamentals in oil & gas markets as 'soft'. As discussed in our latest Oil Manual publication, non-OPEC supply growth is likely to exceed global demand growth in 2024. That reduces the room in the market available to OPEC oil. In response, we expect OPEC to constrain production and possibly extend and/or deepen existing cuts. However, this will likely limit inventory builds but not prevent them. We estimate that Brent prices are supported close to \$80/bbl in 1H, but as inventories rise, we expect Brent prices to trend down slightly towards mid-\$70s as the year unfolds.

Gas market are also well supplied. In Europe, inventories remain elevated by historical standards. Demand has failed to respond to lower prices, any cold spell has remained elusive so far, and LNG supply continues to be forthcoming. After the decline in November and December, a large part of the downside to price has been realised already, but nevertheless, it appears to us that risks remain skewed modestly lower.

2) Inflation is moderating and our house view on interest rates is neutral. Therefore, these factors are unlikely to be tailwinds.

Interest rates and inflation expectations have been key drivers of the sector's relative performance in recent years, and for good reasons. Commodities are 'real' assets so they tend to maintain their value in inflation-adjusted terms. As a result, commodity sectors tend to perform well during inflationary periods.

In addition, the FCF outlook of commodity companies tends to be front-loaded. This means their valuations are less reliant on expectations for FCF many years in the future. Their 'short duration' cash flows make commodity sectors less sensitive to interest rates, which gives the sector a relative tailwind when rates are rising.

When inflation surprised positively and rates were rising over much of the last 2-3 years, this was typically a tailwind for the sector. However, judging by our latest house forecasts for inflation and interest rates, this is unlikely to be repeated in 2024 (see here)

3) Geopolitical risk is already high. In the past, geopolitical risk events have often been bearish for markets but supportive for commodity prices, as they might disruption supply. Therefore, commodity sectors like Energy have often played a useful role in equity portfolios by providing protection against geopolitical risk. For example, owning Equinor in 2022 provided excellent diversification against Ukraine-related risks.

Looking ahead, we do not have strong convictions in this area. However, geopolitical risks are already elevated at the moment, so much of this effect has already played out. Also, with OPEC spare capacity at an historically high \~5 mb/d at the moment, there is a buffer in the system (at least in oil) to absorb supply disruptions. That has blunted the support that geopolitical risk can give to energy prices and the Energy sector.

4) We see modest downside to consensus earnings forecasts and think expectations for shareholder distributions are already 'full'. Earnings and cash flow expectations have come down from their lofty levels in 2022 but have been relatively stable in recent months, despite declines in oil & gas prices.

Assuming oil & gas prices in-line with current forward curves, we see downside to earnings forecasts for all integrated energy companies, except for BP - see Exhibit 15 .

Admittedly, our earnings models have a habit of undershooting consensus most of the time (or said differently: consensus forecasts usually seems high to us). Therefore, we would expect consensus estimates to fall but assuming they stay higher than we model, the declines in coming months are probably limited to single digit percentages rather than double-digit ones.

In the end, however, the sector is mostly driven by shareholder distributions, not earnings or cash flow per se. As shown in Exhibit 13 , market expectations for share buybacks have been surprisingly resilient in recent months. Despite falling oil & gas prices and downwards revisions to FCF expectations, share buyback expectations are down only modestly from their peak.

As a result, the payout ratio implied in consensus estimates has risen, which is shown in Exhibit 17 . This chart shows consensus dividends and share buybacks, taken as percentage of consensus CFFO, all over a rolling 12-months-forward window.

By now, consensus already assumes that the sector distributes \~40% of CFFO to shareholders over the coming year. This is in-line with guidance from some companies, but certainly not all and it is high by historical standards. Even before Covid, when dividend distributions from several companies were significantly higher, the payout ratio of the sector maxed out at \~40%. This suggests limited upside to payout ratios from currently expected levels.

Also, given the 'soft' fundamentals of oil & gas markets, there is some probability that oil & gas prices decline more significantly than we currently forecast. We judge this probability to be relatively low for now, but this is well within the range of possible outcomes. Corrections in oil & gas prices typically generate attractive M&A opportunities. However, to benefit from these, acquirers need strong balance sheets. With that in mid, we suspect that management teams will make more cautious trade offs between strengthening balance sheets and maximizing share buybacks.

5) Finally, the sector's valuation is somewhere between 'supportive' and 'fair'. Different valuation metrics paint slightly different pictures for the sector's valuation. We summarise this in Exhibit 18 and Exhibit 19 , as well as Exhibit 26 to Exhibit 41 .

The sector still appears lowly valued on FCF yield. At \~12%, the FCF yield of the Energy sector is still more than 2x the market yield. However, the sector also trades at 4x CFFO, which is precisely in line with the historical average, and a dividend yield of 5.4% which is actually \~100bp below the historical average. The sector's dividend yield is \~1.5x the market yield, also relatively tight by historical standards.

In the end - and consistent with previous research - we would argue that the sector's dividend outlook anchors its valuation. A company's value equals the present value of the cash flow that investors can extract from it over its lifecycle. The only way minority shareholders can extract cash from large Energy companies is via the dividend. We typically count share buybacks as distributions too, but they impact valuations only via the reduction in the share count, which facilities further growth in dividends-per-share. Hence, the dividend outlook remains the key anchor for valuations of the majors.

Therefore, for the sector to outperform, either dividend yields must compress or dividend per share growth must surprise to the upside.

The former is not so likely at the moment, as the sector's relative dividend yield is already low by historical standards. The second also has limited likelihood at the moment, we would argue, as we see downside to consensus FCF forecasts and implied payout ratios are high already as well.

Dividends-per-share can still accelerate if companies change the distribution mix, with more dividends and less share buybacks. Although that would keep overall distributions the same, it would still send a signal of confidence (dividends are forever; buybacks are for now). However, with some uncertainty ahead over commodity prices, this does not seem

## on the cards for now.

At the moment, the sector's dividend yield stands at 5.4%. We estimate that dividends per share can grow at 4-5% per year in the long term. The sum of dividend yield + dividend growth suggests that investors can still generate an annual return in the high single-digits from the sector in coming years. That is too generous to rate the sector 'Cautious' but given the factors above, the case for 'Attractive' has also weakened. Hence, our return to an 'In-Line' sector stance.
