## Longing for some shorts

## The long-end technical looks more fragile near term; a few trade ideas to get short

• The long-end has been leading the rest of the market tighter since the Fed finished hiking as yields are historically attractive and HG corps are one of the few liquid sources of long-end fixed income product. This should remain the case so long as yields don<sup>’</sup>t drop significantly from current levels.

However, the 10s30s spread curve looks too flat to us at this juncture. It has flattened 9bp YTD to a near record low of 14 bp. Part of what drove this most recent flattening was that there was an unusual imbalance in January between the maturity of demand relative to supply.

We think will correct itself in February as long-end issuance picks up, especially now that rates have started to rally post the most recent more dovish Fed meeting. This long-end issuance pickup is typical in February and is perhaps reflected in it having become a seasonally weak month over the last few years. Investors appear to have started selling down long-end paper in recent days in preparation, with dealer inventories at the long-end significantly higher as a result.

• We believe there is a good rational to be short a basket of long-end paper here in spread terms and we accordingly show a basket of bonds with spread curves at the flatter end of their recent range. A more macro approach to this theme is via curve-specific ETFs which have gained traction more recently in terms of trading liquidity and AUM.

## Go short long-term capital

The drivers of demand at the long-end are fairly well understood by most market participants at this point. To summarize, long-end yields are at historically high levels (currently at the 90th percentile over the past 10 years, per Figure 2 below) and there is relatively low odds of them going significantly higher now that the Fed is debating when to start cutting rates (nearly 150bp of rate cuts by year-end are currently priced in by Fed Funds futures). Furthermore, for any investor desiring or needing to allocate to long-dated fixed income product, there is a dearth of alternatives outside of HG corporates: HG corporates currently make up 23% of the IG-rated USD universe (USTs, Securitized Products, Munis and Corps) but this jumps to 42% when considering just debt with a maturity greater than 25 years. In other words, buying long-dated fixed income leads to a natural overweight of HG Corps.

The above construct is unlikely to change dramatically until yields are materially lower. However, we believe a confluence of factors led long-end spread valuations to an extreme in January that is likely to reverse somewhat in the coming weeks. We go through our rational below:

After the dramatic rate rally in November-December many investors felt that the peak in yields for this cycle was firmly in the rearview mirror and therefore were quite content to see long-end yields rise again in January, offering them another bite at the apple in the new calendar year just as cash started to come in the door at a much higher rate, with fund flows at their highest level in 2.5 years.

This backdrop has started to reverse in recent days - UST 30yr yields are moderately lower post the January FOMC meeting which was relatively dovish (the JPM economics NLP model read this as the least hawkish statement since late-2021 per Figure 6 NLPanlysi coude thwk’ Fm   201 ), though perhaps not as dovish as market expectations. One of the more dovish aspects of Powell<sup>’</sup>s subsequent remarks was the asymmetry on employment: strong employment gains won<sup>’</sup>t necessarily forestall rate cuts, but weak employment gains would “absolutely<sup>”</sup> hasten rate cuts. This likely puts a cap on long-end rates near term, with modestly negative implications for the supply & demand balance at the long-end in HG.

We see strong evidence of this demand for long-end product in January in the combination of issuance distribution and TRACE flows. The proportion of long-end issuance was unusually low at just 8% of total issuance, below even the average for January which is typically lighter given the preponderance of Financials issuers. Not only were overall TRACE volumes at a record high over the course of the month but the proportion directed towards the long-end was close to the highest in 14 months at 32% of total volumes.

In other words, there was no mismatch between the volume of supply and demand (\$194bn of supply yet spreads 1bp tighter in January) but there was a mismatch in the maturity of demand versus the maturity of supply and the secondary market stepped in to bridge this gap. Thus, the 10s30s spread curve flattened to 12bp during the month, a new record flat level outside of crisis periods like 2020 where it briefly inverted (Figure 910s3 preadcuv fltn owi Jy).

Figure 9: 10s30s spread curve flattened to new record lows in January
![](images/42c10334e41020fa21c2fa1030e6f836896d4db08004a27981fb0b853a2d265e.jpg)
Source: J.P. Morgan.

This supply construct should change in February. As noted above in Figure 7 the proportion of 30yr issuance typically jumps in February as the issuance calendar pivots to Non-Financials post earnings and in fact February has seen the highest proportion of 30yr issuance of any month of the year over the past 4 years ex-2020 (so half of this sample size was already in hawkish Fed territory). Moreover, the recent dip in yields and spreads is combining to make for a much more palatable financing environment for issuers considering the long-end. As proxied below by the difference between the long-end HG coupon versus its yield, the <sup>‘</sup>refinancing cost<sup>’</sup> has shrunk from a high of 190bp last fall to 69bp currently, which is near the middle of the range since the Fed started hiking rates. To the extent that hoping for lower rates is no longer a strategy for many issuers humbled by the continued gyrations in the rates market in recent months, the current setup looks quite favorable for long-end issuance in February.

Pivoting to the investor side of the equation, we note that February has been the worst month of the year for spreads over the last 5 years, on average ( Figure 11 ). The February spread weakness in recent years had some idiosyncratic drivers like Credit Suisse last year or the start of Fed hikes the year prior so obviously, none of these idiosyncratic catalysts are likely to repeat themselves in February 2024. That said, with spreads so tight it is easy to see how any negative developments could push spreads wider from here, such that this coming February matches its seasonal pattern. What the combined Jan-Feb seasonal pattern may be suggesting is simply that investors have tended to get long the HG market in January via both the primary and secondary market which has then left them more exposed to the ensuing negative idiosyncratic events in February. The first part of this pattern looks to have been replicated yet again this past January and was likely exacerbated at the long-end by the aforementioned supply/ demand imbalance. We note that investors have started to more actively sell long-end bonds in recent days, bringing long-end dealer inventories to their highest level in many months by our estimation (Figure 12).

## How to express a 10s30s spread curve steepener? ETF and Bond alternatives

At a macro level, we note that maturity specific ETFs have been gaining traction in recent months with VCLT now above \$6bn AUM (other long-end specific ETFs with significant AUM include IGLB and SPLB). Below, we show the spread curve between VCLT and VCIT which is the equivalent ETF for the 10yr segment (similar to IGIB or

SPIB). While this spread curve tracks our JULI 10s30s Non-Financial spread curve, it is currently flatter likely as a result of the Financials component where many spread curves are inverted (17% of VCLT and 37% of VCIT is Financials) as well as other ETF-specific technicals. ETFs can therefore serve as proxies for different parts of the maturity curve.
