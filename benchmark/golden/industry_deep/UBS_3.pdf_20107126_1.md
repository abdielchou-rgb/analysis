## US Inflation Monthly: Where is inflation falling to?

## Despite a couple of strong months 12-month core inflation continues to fall

 In each of the past 11 months, and 15 of the past 16 months, the not-seasonally adjusted core CPI monthly increase has been below the increase from 12 months prior. There is little sign that inflation is becoming stuck at a high level.

 However, where trend or underlying inflation — the level inflation would tend to if all other markets were in equilibrium — is very uncertain. Normal methods of determining the level of the inflation trend have been very poor guides since the onset of the pandemic.

## February inflation data will likely give way to a much weaker March

 We took the February inflation news as a move back to slowing after much of the surprising strength of January — particularly for rents and core non-rent services. We project a 27bp increase in core PCE prices in February with upward revisions to December and January of roughly 5bps total. This would keep 12-month core PCE inflation rounding to 2.8% through February.

 We currently project the headline CPI to rise 24bp in March (seasonally-adjusted; 53bp not-seasonally-adjusted) with core CPI prices rising 23bp. Falling used car prices and a notable slowing in OER should be key features of the March CPI. We will review these projections as additional data becomes available over the next few weeks.

 A detailed forecast spreadsheet is available on our website.

## Core PCE inflation forecast still below consensus at 1.9% for 2024

 We continue to expect that moderating demand, easing supply conditions, slowing rents, and a softening labor market will push down inflation over much of the next year.

With the 12-month change in core PCE prices through February looking to be within 1bp of what we had projected a month ago, we have largely left our forecast unchanged this month, though we edged down our projection of core PCE inflation in 2026 from 2.2% to 2.1%. In the near term we project 12-month core PCE inflation to edge down to 2.7% in March, 2.5% in April, and 2.3% in June as base effects from early last year fall out of the 12-month change.

 Our PCE inflation forecast for this year remains on the low side of consensus, with our core PCE inflation projection 0.2pp to 0.5pp below other consensus measures. In contrast, our CPI and core CPI inflation projections are a touch above consensus (and swaps) as we expect the wedge between CPI and PCE inflation to remain elevated this year.

## Where is inflation headed?

We view the February inflation data as more consistent with slowing inflation than the consensus seems to think. This could be for two reasons. One, we were more concerned coming out of January and had an above-consensus view for the February CPI, meaning the CPI came in a little below our expectations not above them. Two, it could reflect the very wide uncertainty we had for that February print — particularly for core non-rent services prices. The CPI coming in within 3 basis points of our expectations suggests to us that January was an outlier and there is not some fundamental shift occurring that is altering the relationships we use to forecast inflation.

We see the February inflation data as good news

One reason that many observers may be more concerned about inflation is that they are looking at the generally upward trend in seasonally-adjust monthly changes in the core CPI since the low point in June. (Figure 1) We are somewhat less convinced. Most of that upward trend through the end of last year was in vehicle prices, and with motor vehicle inventories increasing (Figure 18Motr vehiclns ag,bu mw) we do not think rising motor vehicle prices are a notable concern. We also think there is a decent argument to be made that seasonal adjustment has become more difficult in the wake of the pandemic, so focusing on the not-seasonally-adjusted data is probably more useful than normal.

## But where is inflation headed to?

Even as inflation is headed down where it is headed down to is less clear. The framework for this type of exercise tries to answer the question: "Where would inflation likely be in 3 to 5 years assuming the economy is roughly in line with its potential and other factors are not pushing up or down inflation?" The inflation rate that comes out of this answer is often referred to as trend, underlying, steady-state inflation.

A few different methods are traditionally used for trying to determine where trend is: estimated trend inflation models that look at common trends in the various inflation components; estimates from models like Phillips curves that include drivers of inflation; and long-run inflation expectations. We are skeptical that any of these alone, or even in combination, are a very good guide of where inflation is actually headed.

Trend inflation models: Models of trend inflation, such as the NY Fed Multivariate Core Trend (MCT) model, are notably higher than were prior to the pandemic. Currently the NY Fed MCT measure is at 3.0%, 1¼pp higher than the 1¾% it averaged in the year prior to the pandemic. However, we put very little weight on these type of trend inflation measures, which are simply filters of past inflation and therefore have tended follow actual inflation on the way up and are following actual inflation on the way down. For example, a year ago the MCT was at 4.9% — more than 2 percentage points off of where actual PCE price inflation is a year later.

Where inflation is headed to is not clear

The intercept or trend from inflation forecasting models: Another approach is to use the trend or intercept from an inflation forecasting model, such as a Phillips curve or general

Trend inflation models suggest notably higher inflation than prepandemic ...but these models just follow a smoothed version of actual inflation

equilibrium model. This is slightly more structural than a trend inflation model in that it attempts to adjust for fundamental forces (such as labor market tightness or supply influences) that might move around actual inflation. Currently our Philips curve models suggest a trend of inflation around 2½% to 2¾% for core PCE prices — about ¾ pp above where they were prior to the pandemic.

While this model-based approach has significant merit we believe the state of the art is lacking. "Triangle" Phillips curves, of the sort used at the Fed and that we use, failed badly to predict the the pickup in inflation or the subsequent slowing (likely because they fail to fully capture many of the potential inflation drivers, such as a changing mix of demand and non-energy and non-import price supply shocks). As a result of the models inability to understand the inflation pickup based on the variables included in the model, the inflation trend implied by these models rose and fell with actual inflation. For example, our non-expectations-based models in April 2022 projected inflation to stay between 4½%and 5% through at least the end of 2024. Even variants introduced during the pandemic (such as the mid-2022 work by Ball, Leigh, and Mishra) suggested that an unemployment rate above 4½% for an extended period of time would be required to get inflation down to the level it has already fallen to.

Inflation expectations: In theory inflation expectations should matter considerably. As Fed Chair Janet Yellen noted it is long-run inflation expectations the Fed is most concerned with. Three of the most prominent long-run inflation expectations measures are, on balance, suggesting that inflation will be hotter in the medium-to-long run than prior to the pandemic:

 The Michigan Survey of Consumers currently shows expectations of inflation over the next 5 to 10 years at 2.9% with those expectations bouncing between 2.8% and 3.2% over the past year. This is roughly ½pp above the 2.3% to 2.6% range that largely persisted over the year prior to the pandemic.

The 5-year, 5-years ahead inflation compensation measure derived from TIPS markets, which should be fairly immune from near-term forces buffeting the economy, is currently around 2¼%, having bounced around between 2.2% and 2.5% in the past year. This is roughly 40bp above where it was in the year prior to the pandemic when it ended 2019 around 1.9% after bouncing around between 1.7% and 2.0%.

The Survey of Professional Forecasters from the Philadelphia Fed is less elevated compared to these other two measures, and in the most recent reading (prior to the January CPI) the measure of expected PCE inflation over the next 10 years had returned to 2.0% measure — exactly where it had been on the eve of the pandemic and for most of the time since the FOMC announced 2.0% as its inflation target in 2012.

Weighing these three measures equally would suggest inflation expectations are around 30bp higher than pre-pandemic, though it is not clear how much those expectations really matter, as a Fed paper by Jeremy Rudd made clear a few years ago. The Survey of Professional Forecasters has been a particularly bad guide for future inflation during the pandemic: it followed actual inflation downward during the first year of the pandemic then upward through 2022. Over the past 5 quarters it has fallen rapidly as actual inflation has fallen and only moved back to 2.0% in the most recent reading. The TIPS measure has fared a little better: while it has a similar downward movement early in the pandemic before rising up through the middle of 2021, it has not seen the overshoot observed in the Survey of Professional Forecasters. Among these expectations measures, only the Michigan Survey of Consumers did not see a drop early in the pandemic or a clear overshoot in 2022. However, in the 20 years prior to the pandemic that survey measure showed more correlation with past inflation over the prior 6 to 9 years than with future inflation, so it is unclear how much weight to put on this measure.

Overall, we have no confidence that any of these methods of determining trend inflation provide a reasonable guide of where inflation will be once the shocks hitting the economy have dissipated and the labor market is roughly in balance. That said, all the of the methods are pointing to trend inflation being higher now than prior to the pandemic.

Additional considerations of where the steady-state level of inflation will likely land are unclear: decarbonization efforts and onshoring or friendshoring could be negative supply shocks pushing up inflation; on the other hand, increased ability to work remotely and rising immigration could be positive supply shocks. There are also considerations of long-run money growth, population aging, and the fiscal stance, which can be a positives or negatives on inflation depending on the specific assumptions used.

Additional considerations could also affect long-run inflation

Overall, we are wary of estimates that trend inflation is above 2¼% — partially because most of the ways that would have estimated that level now would have estimated an even higher level a year ago and partially because we think the Fed will raise interest rates to make sure that inflation does not remain that elevated. That said, we do think the balance of arguments suggest a higher inflation trend than prior to the pandemic, but exactly where is unclear.

Prior to the pandemic we assumed an inflation trend of 1.8%, but with current estimates of actual inflation a bit below that, it is quite possible that the pre-pandemic inflation trend was a little lower than 1.8%. Overall, any point chosen is somewhat arbitrary and there is a big degree of uncertainty, but we believe an inflation trend ranging anywhere from slightly below 2% up to 2¼% is reasonable.

Overall we put the long-run inflation trend a little higher than prior to the pandemic, but with wide uncertainty

## Near-term inflation forecast February PCE inflation

February headline PCE prices, released late next week (Friday, March 29), are projected to have increased 35bp with core PCE prices rising 27bp. Over the past 36 months one standard error around these estimates has been 4bp (with a median absolute error of 2.5bp). With these increases the 12-month change in core PCE prices is projected to hold at 2.8% in January — the smallest since March 2021 and roughly ¾ of the way back to the FOMC's 2.0% inflation target from its 5.6% peak in February 2022.

## Upcoming CPI prints

We are currently forecasting a 24bp increase in the headline CPI in March (seasonallyadjusted; 53bp not-seasonally-adjusted) with 12-month inflation rising from 3.15% to 3.36%. We forecast the core CPI to increase 23bp in March as OER slows notably and used car prices decline solidly. 12-month core CPI inflation is forecast to edge down from 3.86% to 3.75% — continuing its gradual downward trend and it slowest pace since April 2021. As additional data becomes available over the next few weeks, we will refine this projection.

We currently forecast that in March core CPI inflation will just barely round down to 3.7%

Looking further ahead than the March print, over the next six months we forecast seasonally-adjusted core CPI increases will begin to look like more like pre-pandemic monthly increases as rents slow considerably over the next few prints, used car prices generally decline, and ebbing demand and easing supply conditions lead to a slackening in the pace of monthly increases. ## Box: Rules of thumb for impacts of key economic shocks

We usually rely on estimated time-series models to bring in the impact of various factors on our forecast. However, rules of thumb are often useful to give a rough idea of how important a shock is likely to be. Below are a few key rules of thumb that we use:

 1 percentage point increase in the unemployment rate pushes down core inflation by 8bp.

 Permanent \$10 per barrel level shift in the price of Brent crude pushes up the level of CPI by 40bp and core CPI by 4bp.

 A one percent decline in the dollar increases consumer import prices by 0.15 percent.

 A one percent increase in import prices results in a 0.12 percent increase in core consumer prices.

A one percent increase in CRB spot food index equals about 8bp in prices of food at home with about 2/3rds in the first six months and 1/3rd in the six months after that. (CPI food at home is 8% of the CPI basket.)

## Price inflation projection through 2026

We continue to project core PCE inflation will slow over the next year as supply and demand come more into alignment for goods and the considerable easing in market rent increases pass into CPI and PCE rent measures. The rise in unemployment that results from a recession starting in the middle of this year adds additional downward pressure to inflation, particularly for the non-rent core services components.

Given the January PCE price data and CPI/PPI/import price data for February, along with revisions to prior months, cumulative core PCE change in recent months through February looks to be about 1bp higher than forecast in last month's US Inflation Monthly. As a result, our forecasts are little changed for this year and we continue to forecast 1.9% core PCE inflation this year under the assumption that the economy will slow markedly and slip in to a recession around the middle of this year. However, we have made a change to round down our 2026 core PCE projection from 2.2% to 2.1%. The unemployment rate in our forecast (4.2%) averages a couple tenths higher than our presumed natural rate of unemployment (4.0%) and the "speed" effects (from unemployment falling 0.3pp between 2025 and 2026) are assumed to only add a few basis points. (Figure 48)

We project core PCE prices will increase 1.9% this year and 2.1% in 2025E

## Components of core PCE inflation

Figure 51 through Figure 55 show 3- and 12-month changes for the main components of our core PCE projection along with our forecasts and model projections. The models are estimated taking 2023Q4 as data and forecasted starting 2024Q1.

1. Core goods prices (25% of core PCE, Figure 51) have slowed considerably over the past year and are projected to continue to slow over the next handful of months as demand weakens and the effects of easing supply constraints continue to ripple through the production and distribution network (particularly for vehicles). Goods price deflation is projected to hit its lowest point around the middle of the year.

Our Phillips curve model for the category has adjusted down considerably over the past few quarters and now the model is roughly in line with our projection though the middle of this year and below it further out.

2. We forecast monthly housing services increases (essentially owners' equivalent rent plus tenants' rent, 20% of core PCE, Figure 52) will slow over the next few quarters as the easing in rents for new leases filters into CPI/PCE housing rent inflation.

Our model for housing services inflation based on fundamental rent market factors (the unemployment rate, rental vacancy rate, and housing affordability)also shows slowing, but a little bit more gradually.

3. Price inflation for PCE health services (19% of core PCE, Figure 53) is projected to run a little above the pace over the past year as the price reductions from the ending of Medicare Covid reimbursement rate increases is past. However, the upward push is only moderate as wage growth in health care has been slowing for the past year and Medicare adjustment for wage increases (which first appeared in the October PCE prices) was estimated by the Center for Medicare and Medicaid Studies to be a touch lower than last year.

The 12-month change in shelter rent inflation is past its peak, but still very elevated.

4. PCE food services inflation (6% of core PCE, Figure 54) has been strong through most of the pandemic. We have seen slowing in recent months and expect further easing of the pace of increases with moderating wage increases.

5. Inflation for other core PCE services (31% of core PCE, Figure 55) has slowed in recent months and we expect the pace will ebb further as wage growth slowly eases.

## Key risks to our modal forecast

We view our projection as the most likely (modal) scenario and see risks on both sides of the inflation forecast with the overall uncertainty surrounding the inflation projection remaining larger than usual over the past 20 to 30 years (though down from a year or two ago). Over the next few months we see the risks as two-sided around our forecast though skewed somewhat to the upside.

We think that inflation risks are skewed to the upside over the next few months

## Upside risks

1. Continued near-term momentum: As noted in the front section of this report, we think that the strength in January and February price increases was largely consistent with the early year noise and will fade in the next couple of months. However, as discussed in last month's US Inflation Monthly, the composition of the January strength was much different than usual and it is possible that it will lead to somewhat less slowing than usual over the next few months.

2. Continued tight labor market passing into services prices: The labor market remains tight. If the economy does not go into the recession that we have written into our baseline forecast, and wage growth remains elevated, it is likely that nonrent services inflation will be higher than we have forecast over the next year. Our rough estimate based on a fairly steep Phillips curve is that the labor market continuing at close to its present level of tightness could add around 30bp to 40bp to our core PCE inflation projection in 2024.

3. Higher inflation expectations/trend inflation: Estimating trend inflation is extremely imprecise and it is possible that inflation expectations and the inflation trend is notably above where we have it.

4. Rents re-accelerating: While we are not seeing a lot of strength in new tenants rents, the solid housing market and rising existing home prices could start to push up new tenants' rents and, eventually, CPI rents.

## Downside risks

1. A slowing economy: The recession that we have forecast starting this summer could be deeper and have a greater slowing effect on inflation than we have anticipated. In particular, in recessions prices for financial services, non-profit services, and travel-related services generally fall quite notably.

2. Stronger productivity growth: The past couple quarters have seen a pickup in productivity growth. While we see this pickup as a return to the to the long-run productivity trend after some time below that trend, it could be the start of a longer-run productivity boom. If so, such a move could lead to lower price inflation for the same level of wage growth. In the late-1990s, as productivity growth picked up, price inflation fell.

3. Lower natural rate of unemployment: Additionally, it is possible that the ability to work remotely has led to better employment opportunities pushing down the natural rate of unemployment. If so, the natural rate of unemployment could be closer to 3.7% than the 4.0% we assume. If so, this would subtract close to a tenth percentage point off our or inflation forecast.

## Comparison to the forecast models

Our forecast models have revised downward notably since peaking in the second half of 2022, though they revised up this month as we have brought in the strong Q1 data. The models expect core PCE inflation around 2.8% this year (Q4/Q4). Our forecast is notably lower.

As with most Phillips curve style models, they were unable to forecast the inflation as it surged upward and, as expected, they have not been a good guide as inflation has fallen. In particular, they do not include an effect for changes in the mix of demand and the supply constraints that hampered efforts to satisfy this changing mix. We believe the combined effects of the changing demand mix and supply issues were far more important in driving the inflation surge over the past 3 years than were the standard Phillips curve drivers of aggregate resource slack.

Our Phillips curve models have performed poorly during thus inflation experience.

Nonetheless, over the next couple of years we believe those unusual factors in this inflation surge will continue to diminish and and the models will continue to revise down. Our forecast is roughly in line with the lower range of models by 2026.
