# Parallels with the 2015-16 recovery - what can we learn and what to do from here?

## A false start or a sustained recovery?

There are many similarities between the current episode of market recovery and the 2015-2016 episodes. For the three points in history, we have seen significant relaxations on property and monetary policies, restriction from regulators such as on IPOs, and short selling suspensions as well as stock purchases by the national team. There were also notable differences in areas such as company earnings, capital returns and sector valuations. While the 2H15 episode saw a false start with the CSI 300 recovering 27% from trough before declining to a new low, the recovery in 1H16 was a more gradual but sustained process. Where are we this time around? In this report, we compare the telling metrics between these three points in history and use this as a guide for stock / sector selection.

## Macro picture weaker but micro is better

At a macro level, the situation appeared more challenging now than the 2015/16 episodes with a deeper-than-expected property downturn, more modest policy easing, and a more cautious consumer sentiment. At a micro level, the picture is less so. Overseas exposures have risen to c.15% of revenue from 12.5% in 2015, while gearing levels and free cash flow generation of companies have risen to historical highs. This healthy balance sheet is allowing companies to lift their capital returns to shareholders − payout ratio is now at 33% vs 29% in 2015/2016 while trailing 12M buyback amount is also at a historical high at RMB260bn (c.0.3% of market cap but much higher for the internet sector). Meanwhile, IPO levels have also declined significantly from RMB36bn/ mth in 2015/2016 to RMB6bn/mth in the last 12 months, which will ease the liquidity drain on the equity markets. Earnings growth (8% for 2023 based on consensus estimates) also appeared more resilient than in 2H15 and 1H16, which saw earnings decline by 6% and 11%, respectively.

## Comparisons at a sector level

At a sector level, 10 out 13 sectors' valuations are now below that reached in the prior two episodes − the reason as to why the headline MSCI China valuation is on par with these prior episodes was due to a change in constituents mix. Comparing the sector performances in the three episodes, we note that: 1) in all three episodes the share price recovery did not entail positive earnings revision; 2) that said, for the recovery to be sustained like in 2016, earnings revision momentum needed to improve (i.e., either less negative earnings downgrade or earnings upgrade); and 3) 2015 saw a more divergent share price rally led by brokers and renewables while the recoveries this time and in 2016 were broader based and balanced. We believe a broader based rebound likely suggests a more rational improvement in broader investor sentiment from extreme levels.

## Observations from other asset classes

Cross referencing other asset classes, we note: 1) commodity markets currently suggest a stabilisation: in 2015 commodity prices have continued to decline while in 2016 they bottomed out; 2) credit spreads have narrowed in the past 6 months but valuation has yet to catch up; 3) FX market suggests a stabilization as was the case in 2016; however 4) government bond yields suggest that the recent episode is closer to 2015 with yields continuing to fall.

## Conclusion: we are closer to the 2016 recovery than the 2015 peak

On balance, we believe the recent recovery is closer in nature to the 2016 recovery with: 1) similarities in timing, with policy easing having been implemented for some time; 2) some macro indicators such as PPI and exports are closer between now and 2016; 3) behaviour and levels in other asset classes are more similar to 2016; and 4) while the macro picture is generally weaker, listed companies seem more resilient in terms of earnings, capital returns and profitability. If the 2016 experience was to repeat itself, the following quarters could see a slower grind higher, sustained by improving earnings momentum.

## What can we learn from 2016 in terms of sector / stock picking?

 Large cap performed better while defensives did not materially underperform: Post the initial bounce from the trough in 2016, large cap and value marginally outperformed in the ensuing 3-month period. However, in the 2nd 3-month period, growth and large cap outperformed as earnings momentum picked up more materially.

 Follow the earnings revision momentum: Sectors that saw better earnings revision momentum, including internet, auto and tech, had outperformed. In general, it would seem that share prices behaved more rationally with moves more consistent with earnings revision post March 2016.

 Market likely to embrace near-term gainers initially: Outperformers during the initial rally tended to continue their outperformance in the following 3 months as investors focused on those with better near-term momentum and visibility.

## Sector and stock picks

With c.15% of companies having reported their results so far, it is still a little difficult to draw meaningful conclusions at a sector level. However, it would appear that earnings were broadly in-line with expectations which is already a plus for the China market, and at a sector level momentum is improving for the likes of internet, restaurants, sportswear, education and defensives, based on recent earnings revisions. We continue to favour a high dividend yield strategy as a core portfolio for now with beta options in the form of internet, select consumer sectors such as beer, restaurant, sportswear and education as we await clearer signs from the reporting season. ## Valuation Method and Risk Statement

For various stocks across the industries we cover in the Hong Kong and mainland China stock markets, we use a variety of valuation approaches, including DCF models, Gordon growth model analysis and relative valuation analysis using various multiples such as PE, EV/EBITDA and P/BV.

We think the risks facing China's equities include a hard landing for the property market, a capital exodus associated with currency depreciation and slow structural reform progress. In our view, any government policies that do not adequately address these risks could result in a shock to the market. For example, an excess of stimulus policies could pose a risk to the transition from an investment-driven to a consumption-driven economy and increase the debt of government and state-owned enterprises.
