## Soft landings, TouchdoWNs, and Safety in the End Zone

Yields are higher this week on the back of strong data and Fed-speak that continues to signal a desire to see sustained disinflation before eventually cutting rates. But options market implied distributions point to a significant weighting on a tail risk scenario that could bring aggressive cuts

● With such a tail scenario being dramatically different from baseline easing expectations, policy uncertainty remains considerable, and is creating a supportive backdrop for elevated day-to-day volatility. But rates are also rangebound and mean-reverting, because of which realized volatility based on less frequent sampling (such as biweekly) is much lower. While we acknowledge this headwind, we nevertheless remain bullish on volatility on the back of heightened policy uncertainty and lingering tail risk

● With forward OIS rates still below modal expectations for the path of the Fed, carry trades on the swap yield curve remain an attractive proposition, but we prefer implementing them conditionally in a selloff to mitigate the downside in the event that tail risks worsen and trigger a sharp rally …

… buy 3Mx2Y payer swaptions versus selling 3Mx3Y and 3Mx5Y payer swaptions (30% and 75% risk respectively)

Given the potential for tail risks to escalate, and also given our Treasury strategists<sup>’</sup> bullish view on the market, we also find value in asymmetric ways of creating bullish exposure …

… buy H5 and Z5 3M SOFR futures contracts versus selling U4 SOFR futures and paying fixed in 10Y swaps

● Thanks to low coupon issues at the short end of the basket and high coupon issues at the long end, the June Treasury futures ultra-long bond contract is characterized by interesting dynamics. The current CTD, the Aug <sup>‘</sup>49, could switch to the long end of the basket - the Nov <sup>‘</sup>53s - in a rally, which would bring about duration shortening rather than lengthening. We discuss the impact of high coupons / conversion factors at the long end of the basket, versus low coupons / conversion factors at the short end. A CTD switch to the Nov <sup>‘</sup>53s, which is already nearly equi-cheap, would not be material in the traditional sense, but would significantly lower wildcard optionality

Our own view is similar - we too see banking sector concerns as limited in scope to smaller banks, and see this chiefly as a tail risk that needs to be priced into markets. Indeed, a closer look at implied distributions suggests that markets are pricing in such tail risk. As we have often done in recent weeks and months, we look at premia on calls and puts with various strikes on Dec '24 3M SOFR futures, and reverse-engineer a weighted combination of scenario-specific conditional distributions that would match the observed implied distribution. Such a decomposition of the implied distribution allows us to gain more intuition on the far-from-center scenarios that the market is pricing in. As we can see in Figure 4, after this week's moves, the options market's modal scenario is similar to our own modal view for \~5-6 cuts this year. But the market is also pricing in a reasonable weight on a tail risk scenario that would correspond to \~300bp of rate cuts by year end. In other words, it would appear that markets are indeed treating banking sector headlines as a tail risk that is being priced into markets.

What does all this mean going forward? With regards to the swap yield curve, this points us in two directions. One, as we noted last week, yield curve carry trades remain attractive since forward OIS rates are still below expected future policy rates, thanks to the above mentioned tail risks being priced into forwards. But carry trades must be constructed carefully, so as to avoid considerable downside in the event that tail risks materialize. One way to do this is to implement carry trades conditionally in a selloff, using payer swaptions. For instance, consider conditionally underweighting 2s in a selloff versus a 30/75 weighted blend of 3s and 5s. Implied volatility levels in the swaptions market currently allow for this trade to be constructed close to premium neutral at the forwards. The weighted spread corresponding to this trade is largely uncorrelated with rates, but offers 12bp of carry over 3 months. Moreover, constructing this with payer swaptions mitigates the chief risk to this trade, which is an escalation in tail risks that could cause a front loading of Fed easing expectations and trigger a sharp bull-steepening. Therefore, we recommend this trade (see Trade recommendations).

At the same time, it is also worth exploring attractive ways of positioning for a rally in the market. Indeed, our Treasury strategists are now bullish and expect yields to decline in the belly of the curve (see Treasuries). Outright longs are of course a possibility. But we also favor trades that are likely to outperform asymmetrically in a rally. One such example involves buying Reds versus near-fronts and 10s. For instance, as Figure 5 shows, the yield spread corresponding to buying the 4th and 7th SOFR futures contracts (30:100 weighted) versus the 2nd SOFR futures contract has been well correlated to 6M forward 10Y yields with a beta of 0.55, and currently appears too steep. Thus, buying the 4th and 7th SOFR futures contracts (30% and 100% risk weights) versus selling the 2nd SOFR futures contract (100% risk weight) and paying fixed in a 6M forward 10Y swap (40% risk weight, less than the beta by design) is a way to construct a trade that is likely to outperform in a rally, while also offering over 20bp of positive slide over 3 months. The choice of weighting on the 10Y leg relative to the beta imparts a bullish bias to the trade. The statistical mispricing should help to offset some of the downside in a selloff (given the bullish bias), while adding to the trade<sup>’</sup>s attractiveness in a rally. Therefore we recommend this trade (see Trade recommendations).

With regards to swap spreads, our views remain unchanged - we continue to find value in swap spread wideners in the belly, with maturity matched swap spreads remaining \~4bp below fair value and with favorable tailwinds for swapped demand in this sector from investors such as banks. Similarly, swap spreads in the front end continue to appear to wide, and we remain biased towards narrower swap spreads. On the margin, recent banking system headlines are likely to make Reserves even stickier near current levels, which in turn likely biases RRP balances lower going forward. As we have noted often, falling RRP balances are a key driver of our narrowing view on front end swap spreads, and we maintain this view going forward. This week<sup>’</sup>s increase in Reserves on the Fed<sup>’</sup>s balance sheet suggests that this dynamic may be at play.

## The June WN contract

The ultra-long bond contract is well known for being a sector dominated by the wildcard option, which has been a constant feature of that sector in recent years. Traditional CTD switches have thus far been far less probable in the WN contract. That appears set to change as we move forward from the March contract to the June contract in a few weeks. This makes the WNM4 contract quite interesting in somewhat counter-intuitive ways. We discuss this below.

The CTD into WNM4 is currently the 2.25% Aug '49 issue, with the Nov '49 being a close second. But somewhat interestingly, in a relatively modest rally, the CTD is projected to switch to the 4.75% Nov '53s, all the way to the long end of the basket. Even more interestingly, such a lengthening in the CTD maturity would not lead to a lengthening in duration risk, but a shortening.

All this seems counter-intuitive at first glance. To make sense of it all, we begin by noting that futures prices are determined not by the prices of bonds in the basket, but by bond prices divided by their conversion factors. In other words, if we imagine plotting the factor-scaled price (defined as price divided by conversion factor) of all the bonds in the basket versus yields, the futures price-versus-yield profile would track the lowerenvelope of all the bonds' individual profiles. Thus, what matters for futures pricing is not bond prices, but factor-scaled prices. In a similar vein, the futures contract's bpv is determined (to a decent first approximation) not by the CTD's bpv, but by the factor-scaled bpv (i.e., bpv divided by conversion factor). In Figure 6, we include the bonds in the WNM4 basket sorted not by maturity date, but by factor-scaled bpvs. As can be seen, the Aug '49 is no longer at the short end of the basket, but rather in the middle, and the Nov '53s are at the short end of the basket, not the long end. Thus, it is logical that a decline in yields could cause the CTD to shift towards the Nov '53s. In a very similar vein, it is also worth noting that when conversion factors differ widely, comparing BNOCs conveys a misleading picture of the impact of a CTD shift. Instead, a more accurate picture can be obtained by looking at factor-scaled BNOCs - i.e., BNOC divided by conversion factor. Here again, we can see that a switch from the Aug '49s to the Nov '52 would be noticeable but modest in terms of impacting the futures price, given the fact that the two bonds' factor-scaled BNOCs are 3/32nds apart, while the unscaled BNOCs differed greatly.

There is one important difference, however, between having the Aug '49s as CTD versus the Nov '53s, and that has to do with the wildcard option. The Aug '49 has a low coupon and a low conversion factor, which makes the wildcard option quite valuable. In contrast, the Nov '53s have a high coupon and a high conversion factor, which causes the wildcard option to be worth less than a third as much as it is for the Aug '49s in futures price terms (Figure 7). This dynamic makes it much more difficult to monetize the wildcard option, and makes it likely that the WNM basis will trade closer to levels that would be consistent with simply pricing in traditional switch optionality. Currently, the Aug '49's WNM4 net basis is about 4 ticks wider than our estimate of fair value for that basis based on traditional switch optionality. This suggests there is about 4 ticks of wildcard optionality priced into the contract (or 7+/32nds, in futures price terms). This appears fair given current probabilities of CTD shifts. However, given the above-mentioned risks associated with the wildcard option this time, we would expect the basis to converge more than has typically been the case in this sector.

## Options

During a week that brought data that signaled continued strength in the economy and higher yields, longer expiry implieds outperformed shorter expiry implieds. For example, 6Mx2Y was down slightly by 0.1 bp/day over the week and 2Yx2Y was up slightly by 0.2 bp/day (Figure 8).

We have maintained a bullish stance on volatility for several weeks now, mostly on the back of increased policy uncertainty (and therefore the expectation of high delivered volatility) as well as cheap valuations. Our expectations of high delivered volatility are indeed being realized - 2-week realized volatility on 10Y swap yields is now over 10bp/ day, for instance. The same is true in the front end where 2-week realized volatility on 2Y swap yields stands pretty close to 10bp/day. Yet, implieds have been slow to bounce back and as Figure 9 shows, they continue to appear below fair value in almost all sectors. There is some consolation in the fact that implieds outperformed relative to expectations based on moves in the underlying drivers, suggesting that residual convergence is indeed happening even if somewhat slowly.

But, why have implieds been slow to bounce back towards fair value despite high realized volatility? Perhaps the answer lies in the fact that although yield moves have been very choppy and volatile on a day-over-day basis, they have also been ultimately range-bound in recent months. As a result, realized volatility has been much lower when sampled less frequently than daily - for instance, 6-week realized vol is at nearly 9bp/ day in the 2Y sector when sampled daily, but only a touch above 3bp/day if sampled every two weeks. Said differently, while there is a lot of realized volatility to monetize for the active delta hedger, there is carry to be had from selling volatility for the investor who can hold short gamma positions that are delta hedged every two weeks or less often. This phenomenon is true across all tails ranging from the front end to the very long end (Figure 10), and has also been persistently true in recent months. It is likely that this high degree of mean reversion (which produces lower volatility as one widens sampling intervals) has acted as a headwind for long volatility positions.

While we are cognizant of this headwind, we are cautious about assuming continued mean-reversion and rangebound markets. As we noted earlier in this piece, policy uncertainty is now considerably elevated, and options markets are indicating that markets are placing a significant weight on a tail risk scenario that could bring aggressive rate cuts (see Figure 4 and associated discussion above). This is significant because small shifts in the "weights" corresponding to these scenarios can produce large moves in forward yields at the front end. A stylized representation of this is shown in Figure 11, where we examine the impact of shocks to the weights corresponding to each scenario. For instance, a rise of 10% in the weight corresponding to the aggressive cuts scenario would produce a 25bp drop in Dec '24 forward OIS yield levels. Given such potential for realized volatility, we continue to maintain our bullish stance on volatility and recommend delta hedging frequently.
