## United States

Home sales remain tepid to start the spring buying season

Business investment spending still broadly trending sideways

The FOMC minutes indicate disappointment on inflation

• Next week<sup>’</sup>s April core PCE inflation reading expected up 2.8%

The most recent batch of economic indicators suggest the economy is expanding near a trend-like pace. Beneath the surface, however, the more interest-sensitive sectors look sluggish. April home sales continue to look listless, particularly in the resale market where little inventory is coming online. Meanwhile, the latest figures on capital goods orders and shipments indicate that business investment spending growth still looks subdued.

While business investment spending has been soft, business hiring has been buoyant. And the latest weekly figures on jobless claims point to a still-tight labor market. After a concerning bounce higher two weeks ago, new claims have since retreated to a low level of 215,000 in the most recent report. The employment signals from the nationwide PMI report haven<sup>’</sup>t been the most reliable indicators of job growth, but the latest reports suggest slower growth in service hiring though perhaps some pickup in factory employment.

The minutes from the early May FOMC meeting were released this week and confirmed that the Committee intends to maintain the current restrictive stance of policy longer than previously anticipated in response to the disappointing firstquarter inflation readings. There was also some reassessment of just how restrictive policy actually is, mirroring discussions occurring outside the Fed. And in contrast to Chair Powell<sup>’</sup>s more dovish press conference after the meeting, it appears that “various<sup>”</sup> Committee participants were more willing to tighten policy further should inflation risks appear to mount.

Among the data released next week will be the second look at 1Q GDP. The preliminary report printed at 1.6% annualized growth last quarter, and we look for that number to be revised lower to 1.1%. More closely watched will be the April core PCE inflation number, which we expect to be up 0.3% monthon-month (0.26% to two decimals) and 2.8%oya.

## Humdrum home sales

Home sales tanked in 2022 as the Fed began its aggressive tightening cycle. Since then housing demand has been stagnant at low levels. That continued in April, when existing home sales disappointed with a 1.9% decline to a 4.14mn pace (Figure 1).

Figure 1: Existing and pending home sales
![](images/2600a1b57957092a4f3a8428c615be31cd0cca2249cbfcd83827d2fa6b1a0923.jpg)

An annual sales pace around 4mn units is similar to where the market bottomed after the GFC and is likely the level consistent with the bare minimum turnover. New home sales don<sup>’</sup>t face the same constraint of reluctant sellers locked in by low mortgage rates. However, even with the greater availability of inventory (Figure 2), new home sales haven<sup>’</sup>t exactly been boomy. New sales were reported to have declined 4.7% on the month. While the monthly moves in this report can be quite noisy, the trend is still one of a restrained pace of sales growth as borrowing costs remain elevated.

## Cautious capex

The April durable goods report was a mixed bag in what it said about recent capital spending trends. Nominal shipments of core (nondefense, ex-aircraft) capital goods increased 0.4% last month, though the prior month was revised lower to show a 0.3% decline. A similar pattern also occurred for core capital goods orders. Stepping back, the level of such shipments in April was below the 1Q average (Figure 3).

Figure 3: Core capital goods orders and shipments \$billions, sa
![](images/fc6f01f2bfbfa6b23bc0e56277ec595dc5292a3cdcf7122ff6d4597ba5f90701.jpg)

The capital goods figures are an important input into estimating business spending on equipment in the GDP report. Consistent with the sideways pattern seen in Figure 3, the level of real equipment spending in 1Q of this year was almost identical to the level observed back in 2Q22.

## Comforting claims

Two weeks ago the weekly level of initial jobless claims jumped from 209,000 to 232,000. At the time we suspected that move reflected noisy seasonal adjustment, particularly for New York. Claims have slipped lower since then and the most recent reading printed at 215,000, near the springtime lows (Figure 4). Although some other indicators have pointed to a cooling in the demand for workers, the claims data are still suggesting a resilient labor market.

## May flash PMIs rebound

The May flash PMIs came in stronger, with the manufacturing composite rising 0.9 to 50.9 while services business activity rebounded 3.5pts to 54.8. These outcomes followed a few months of declines and meaningfully beat our and consensus expectations for a slight further softening in both. The details were supportive, with gains across nearly all survey components for both manufacturing and services. Accordingly, S&P Global reports that the all-industry composite jumped to a 25- month high.

Figure 5: US all industry output PMI and GDP growth
![](images/0a31856bb651b09ff9e328f76ad65c059abf998d0113497eb86f84b1bed79727.jpg)

To be sure, the PMIs haven<sup>’</sup>t been a great guide to predicting growth in this cycle (Figure 5). In fact, business surveys more generally haven<sup>’</sup>t been great guides. But the recovery in these figures may provide some reassurance that activity isn<sup>’</sup>t hitting an air pocket.

## Inflation expectations hold steady

Through the upside inflation surprise in 1Q, household inflation expectations have been relatively steady. In last week<sup>’</sup>s NY Fed Survey of Consumer Expectations, one-year ahead expected inflation moved up to 3.3% while three-year ahead moved down to 2.8%. In a similar pattern, in the final May reading of the University of Michigan survey, year-ahead inflation expectations ticked up to 3.3% while the five- to 10- year measure was unchanged at 3.0% and down a tick from the preliminary reading (Figure 6).

Figure 6: Michigan survey inflation expectations
![](images/dafabf81c2985d3cd13c200b17a48738605770b44999d56d84cd34fc3a8bcb29.jpg)

The inflation story will remain front and center in next week<sup>’</sup>s holiday-shortened calendar, with the April core PCE number due up Friday. Anecdotal information from Wednesday<sup>’</sup>s Beige Book may also give some insight into how businesses see their cost structures and pricing power evolving.

The path forward for inflation remains a key focus for markets and policymakers, and with the April CPI and PPI data already in hand, we anticipate both the headline and core PCE price indexes will have advanced 0.3% in April (0.26% to two decimals for the core reading). Continued solid monthly gains imply that the year-ago inflation rate on headline should have held at 2.7% while core remained at 2.8% last month. We look for further disinflation as the year progresses but, as various Fed officials have noted, progress has been slower than previously expected. The core services excluding housing (“supercore<sup>”</sup>) price index appears likely to have risen 0.2% (0.24% unrounded) in April, with the year-ago inflation rate seen having cooled modestly to a still-elevated 3.4%oya last month.

Retail sales disappointed in April with a flat headline and a 0.3% drop in the oft-cited control measure that excludes gasoline, autos, building materials, and food. The former two categories posted solid nominal gains for April, and unit auto sales were strong as well. While the goods side of the consumption picture nonetheless looks to have softened some, we still look for relatively robust spending on services in April, continuing its elevated pace over the past several months as household fundamentals (including job growth and asset appreciation) remain supportive. Given the expected change in prices, we look for a 0.3% rise in real consumer spending for April.

The prior few months of income gains also have been strong, but we think those growth rates will have cooled in April. We look for nominal income to have increased 0.2% even as wage and salary growth was likely flat on the month. Thus we expect that real disposable income slipped 0.1% in April. Given our expectations for disposable income and spending, we think the saving rate slid 0.4%-pt to 2.8%, which if realized would mark the lowest reading since mid-2022.

In manufacturing, the output PMI jumped 1.3-pts to 52.4, while other key components also improved on the month (including a small decline in supplier deliver times, suggesting less potential supply chain dislocations). Our tracking estimate for the ISM-weighted composite recovered 1.0-pt to 50.7 for May, back above the 50-breakeven threshold. Future output also improved modestly in the May flash. That said, the ratio of new orders to inventories (the stocks of finished goods) declined modestly further below one in May, which points to less momentum for production relative to other aspects in this report.

In addition to the jump in the headline business activity measure, the flash services PMI report showed a partial recovery in the employment component to 49.4, but the level remains at its lowest point since the pandemic-induced recession (other than April). Yet the new business and future activity components of the services PMI improved and paint a more constructive picture of the services sector.

Turning to indicators of cost and price pressures for the May flash, input prices continue to climb for manufacturing but largely continue to trend sideways for services. Meanwhile, output prices were mixed across sectors but not significantly changed over the past several months.

New orders for durable goods surprised notably to the upside for April, with the headline rising 0.7% against our and consensus estimates for a decline of a roughly similar size. However, the March reading was revised down a tenth to 0.8%. (The initial reading last month was a much stronger 2.6% for March, but that was revised lower after the factory orders report.) Much of the upside surprise to the headline number was in defense, which jumped 13.2% on the month. Meanwhile, civilian aircraft orders fell -8% last month; a somewhat larger decline was anticipated. But the closely watched core capital goods orders were also firmer than expected, rising 0.3% in April. Overall, however, core capital goods orders have been trending flat to very slightly higher over the past year. Shipments of durable goods surprised to the upside as well, rising 1.2% in April. Both defense and non-defense aircraft shipments were also firm last month, while core capital goods shipments rose 0.4%—stronger than the flat reading we had expected, although again March was revised modestly lower. Inventory accumulation also appeared slightly stronger in the report, albeit with small downside revisions to March as well. All told, the revisions did not move 1Q tracking from 1.1% q/q saar, versus the initial release at 1.6%ar. While this report points to some slight upward risk to our 2Q GDP

growth forecast, it is still very early in the current quarter in terms of tracking the hard data.

The University of Michigan consumer sentiment index declined 8.1 points to 69.1 in the final May reading after several months of relatively flat prints. Both the current conditions and expectations components of the headline index had significant declines, down 9.4 and 7.2 from April, respectively. Even though rising stock prices and a fall in retail gasoline prices for the month to date likely supported consumer sentiment, it seems like the prospects of continued high interest rates and downbeat expectations about the labor market and income growth weighed on sentiment.
