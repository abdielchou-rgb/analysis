# Crowded trades: The next beneficiaries—the what, the where and the why

## Crowded trades: To be, or not to be?

A new industry wave always brings good thematic opportunities to A-shares. As investors fear missing out on investment opportunities, the market can sometimes view short-term growth as persistent and overprice long-term growth potential, stretching valuations and crowding positions. However, once the industry boom is subject to some headwinds or the liquidity environment tightens, the unwinding of crowded trades can lead to a long-lasting downward trend for related stocks. In our view, crowded positions do not necessarily lead to a reversal in the near term if the sector's earnings growth prospects remain strong. Sectors with record-low positioning are unlikely to see a strong rally until fundamentals turn around. We think understanding the rationale behind the crowding and tracking it can be a useful tool in risk management and alpha generation.

## Three categories of crowding, with case studies

(1) For new industry waves, earnings upgrades can drive strong share price performances, and impressive returns attract more fund inflows. The unwinding happens when sector growth slows (see the cases of electronics and electrical equipment). (2) A persistent market style leads to crowded positions while a style shift breaks the trend (see the cases of food & beverage (F&B) and home appliances). (3) Extreme risk appetite reshapes portfolio strategy, and the changing market direction can naturally lead to a collapse of the old favourites (i.e., the previously crowded trades − see the cases of computers and healthcare).

## How long does it generally take to unwind crowded positions?

We show the unwinding of crowded trades generally lasts for an average of about two years, meaning that once the process starts, an immediate recovery is unlikely. We think a second growth curve, market style shift and market direction changes can act as a catalyst and help previously crowded names attract investor attention again.

## What might be next beneficiaries?

We think the following sectors and themes have the potential to become institutional investors’ key holdings this year. (1) High dividends and quality SOEs: After the swift rebound since early February, the market may not have a new wave of momentum until clearer signs of an earnings recovery. Investors may choose to add defensive stocks in the near term. (2) High beta TMT (technology, media & telecoms): Investors with high levels of risk appetite may ride on the tailwinds from the AI theme in the near term. (3) Old favourites with solid growth: The bright medium-term market outlook may bring more fund inflows into some pro-cyclical crowded trades, including F&B.

## Performance of crowded trades seems to follow similar pattern

A new industry wave always brings good thematic opportunities to the A-share market. Earnings upgrades can drive strong share price performances while impressive returns attract more fund inflows to a certain theme, often leading to a positive feedback loop. Meanwhile, investors can fear missing out on investment opportunities as the common time period for a mutual fund's (MF) performance review is only one to six months. Some of the funding sources are from retail investors, which are more biased toward short-term relative returns. As a result, the market can sometimes view short-term growth as persistent and overprice long-term growth potential, stretching valuations.

However, once the industry boom is subject to some headwinds or the liquidity environment tightens, the unwinding of crowded trades leads to a long-lasting downward trend for related stocks. From TMT and consumers to new-energy-related sectors, history tends to repeat itself.

## Crowded trades in hindsight − why and where?

We have scrutinised the rise and fall of major 'crowded trades' since 2010, where in each case there is a material change in A-share MFs' positions. In general, we can put all the crowding (and subsequent unwinding) stories into three categories: (1) new industry waves bringing new investment opportunities; (2) the dominance of a certain market style; and (3) extreme risk appetite reshaping portfolio strategy. Here, we take a closer look at each category with case studies.

## Category No.1: New industry waves bringing new investment opportunities

For new industry waves, new technology and business models bring new investment opportunities. Earnings upgrades can drive strong share price performances and impressive returns attract more fund inflows, leading to crowding.

The unwinding of the positioning happens with the slowdown in sector growth. The process usually lasts until the second growth curve is found. Investors who may have lost out in the previous correction often need time to regather their confidence.

## Case: Electronics (Q112-Q414 and Q414-Q219)

Q112-Q414: Crowding in the electronics sector began in Q112 after Apple unveiled its iconic iPhone 4. Many listed companies along its supply chain became the major beneficiaries of this groundbreaking product, and the electronics sector subsequently experienced a material re-rating during the 2013-14 bull run. But institutional investors' positions were quickly reduced to barely overweight the sector from the previous record high. With the penetration rate of smartphones rising to about 80% in 2013 and users gradually switching from 3G to 4G, the growth of the tech hardware plateaued.

Although MFs' positions in the sector reduced substantially as of the end of Q414, it was immediately followed by the second round of crowding, which likely explains why sector share prices were so resilient.

Q414-Q219: The introduction of 'Internet+' in the CCP's 'Two Sessions' meetings of 2015 most benefited TMT sectors. Likely fuelled by six benchmark interest rate cuts and five reserve ratio requirement (RRR) cuts starting at the end of 2014, TMT sectors quickly became a site for extreme speculative frenzy with the electronics index more than doubling in H115. However, the bubble turned out to be short-lived − the crowded positions soon withdrew alongside the collapse of share prices.

The sector soon recovered during 2016-17, riding on the trend of industry consolidation and consumption upgrades. A-share listed companies along the Apple supply chain grabbed more market share within their niche subsectors. Profit growth rebounded to triple digits as MFs once again rushed to this sector.

Unfortunately, this trend was interrupted by US-China trade tensions in 2018, which was not only reflected in the sector's operating results but also in stock performance. Electronics is one of the major export-oriented sectors and took the biggest hit from multiple tariff hikes. Both the sector's stock price and profit growth went through a material correction. Institutional investors' positions collapsed as they opted instead for defensives throughout 2018.

## Case: Electrical equipment (Q318-Q323)

Most of the listed participants in the electrical equipment sector previously specialised in the electric grid business. Hence, when profit growth for this old business began to decelerate and stagnate in 2015, so did MFs' positions. However, the sector found a new growth trajectory in recent years as its business mix gradually shifted towards solar equipment and EV batteries. Amid government support for carbon neutrality, the sector reported a few quarters of exponential earnings growth on the back of a favourable business environment and accommodative policies. MFs rushed into the electrical equipment sector, pushing stock prices to an all-time high in November 2021. However, the sector soon found itself mired in the aftermath of excess capacity and a long-lasting price war. Stock prices began an extended decline in August 2022 as institutional investors left the sector in droves.

## Category No.2: Dominance of a certain style

We note that a shift in 'market style' has a huge impact on share price performances. The most well-known example is the outperformance of high-quality industry leaders from consumers over 2017-20. With solid earnings growth due to rising industry consolidation and continued foreign inflows on MSCI A-share inclusion, the 'value investing dominated the market. The outperformance of 'quality' names led to a substantial amount of MF products being issued. The funds raised pushed up share prices, but this positive feedback loop was disrupted by a shift in market style brought by changes in macro conditions starting in 2020.

For 'growth' versus 'value', our research shows growth's outperformance is correlated with market beta. In other words, when the market trends up, growth generally outperforms value. This observation reflects a common belief that investors typically chase growth with higher beta in a market rally and accumulate defensive positions within value during a downturn.

For large caps versus small caps, there seems no clear connection between earnings growth and share price returns for large- and small-cap style rotations. Historically, the outperformance of small caps has been somewhat driven by improved liquidity and lower risk-free rates. Although investors may not necessarily allocate more assets to equities when interest rates fall, years of experience and an ingrained mindset that ample liquidity benefits small caps likely encourages investors to consider the risk/reward profile attractive and increase their allocations for small caps.

## Case: Food & beverage (Q415-Q122)

China was successful over the deflationary pressure via the creation of PSL (pledged supplementary lending), shantytown renovation and supply-side reform in 2014-16. Quality blue-chip companies with economies of scale and brand premiums usually posted high profitability and strong earnings growth momentum owing to the recovery in demand. As F&B industry leaders are typically blue-chip earnings compounders with resilient growth prospects, institutional investors started building up their positions.

The sector's growth trajectory was derailed in 2018, due to a deteriorating macroeconomic backdrop, rising selling expenses and unsustainable inventory levels. However, the defensive nature of the sector kept MFs' positions elevated. The sector's stock prices started an even steeper rally as a considerable amount of newly issued funds were searching for quality blue-chip names amid macro uncertainty. However, with valuations ever more stretched, the macro environment remaining challenging and consumption recovery weaker than expected post 2020, MFs began unwinding their positions within the F&B sector's crowded trades.

## Case: Home appliances (Q315-Q321)

The creation of PSL and, subsequently, the monetisation of shantytown renovations to counter the downturn in the property market in 2015 gave the home appliances sector a strong boost. Historically, home appliance sales have tended to have a strong positive correlation with residential property floor space sold. In addition, onshore investors have long considered the equity of home appliance leaders as core assets, particularly in the risk-off and risk-aversion stage of a market cycle. 2017 has been marked by the outperformance of large caps over small caps. As the sector went through the first round of a price war, a more consolidated industry with clear leaders riding on the back of the property market boom attracted institutional investors to rush in.

The second round of the industry's price war began in 2019-20 when one of the leaders completed channel reforms so it could lower retail prices without affecting margins. Though industry leaders emerged relatively unscathed, US-China trade tensions in 2018, COVID from 2020 onwards and a prolonged property slump all exerted pressure on the sector's growth prospects and profitability − as profit growth moderated, institutional investors began to cut their positions.

## Category No.3: Extreme risk appetite reshaping portfolio strategy

Market direction changes naturally lead to a collapse of the previous crowded trades. In our report (see Frame your thinking on A-share sector allocations(Part II)), we pointed out that market direction usually has a significant impact on investors' sector allocations − e.g., in a risk-on market, investors generally prefer sectors with higher beta while the market may focus on defensiveness during a risk-aversion stage.

Having calculated sectors' sensitivity to market beta in risk-on, risk-off and risk-aversion stages, we note that once the market beta enters another stage, investors' sector allocation strategy rapidly changes − e.g., F&B and healthcare (historically the two best performing sectors in the risk-off periods) displayed relative defensiveness during the struggling market over 2011-13 and thus attracted MF managers’ attention. Their crowded positions unwound in 2014 as the broader market picked up on liquidity easing. In addition, the computer sector − with its high beta − outperformed the broader market significantly during the 2015 bull market. Its strong outperformance naturally came to an end after the A-share market peaked.

## Case: Computers (Q412-Q317)

The year 2015 was a year of abundant liquidity following six benchmark interest rate cuts and five RRR cuts by the PBoC, starting at the end of 2014. Industry wise, the widespread rollout of 4G gave the sector a strong boost and the sector's high-beta nature resulted in the computer (software) sector subject to the heaviest speculation. The risk-on sentiment, abundant liquidity and industry-wide prosperity all created one of the most extreme examples of sector boom-and-bust in recent memory, as the index almost quadrupled between June 2014 and June 2015.

However, the clearing of non-compliant OTC margin financing in June 2015 triggered extreme stock market deleveraging and sent the sector's share prices into a downward spiral. Even though the sector experienced supportive policies on the back of national security and the import substitution theme, and did experience a rebound in profit growth, it never managed to fully recover in terms of MFs' position or overweight ratio since the collapse. The prevalent pessimism and investors' switch to quality were the main reasons behind this extreme unwinding.

## Case: F&B (Q111-Q115)

Although the A-share market emerged relatively unscathed from the aftermath of the Great Financial Crisis in 2009, 2010-11 turned out to be a prolonged slump. Investors fled to defensive sectors in search of certainty. Traditionally a defensive sector, F&B attracted institutional investors, with premium baijiu becoming one of the major beneficiaries during the risk-off stage (see Figure 24). ## Case: Healthcare (Q112-Q414)

Healthcare over Q112-Q414 resembles the previous example − in addition to its defensive nature (see Figure 24), healthcare also fitted perfectly into the larger story of China's demographic change. With an aging population as the backdrop, high-growth healthcare companies with innovative new drug pipelines caught the capital market's attention. However, with investors growing concerns over the rollout of the group purchasing organisation (GPO) programme and its potential impact on drug prices, MFs quickly cut their positions. The sector's overweight ratio at one point reached almost 12% but then fell to below 4% within a year. Although the sector's stock prices rose during Q114-Q4, we think this was due to the fact that 2014 was a bull market when no single sector registered a loss in share price performance for the year. However, healthcare still ranked bottom of the returns table.

# How long does it generally take to unwind crowded positions?

Our study shows it takes time for investors to unwind crowded positions. The share prices of crowded trades may end up declining over long periods. Empirically, the unwinding of crowded trades last about two years on average. In fact, it took F&B/ healthcare/electronics about seven/six/four years to climb out of their previous troughs and reach the next peak of crowding. This means that once the crowded trades start to unwind, an immediate recovery is unlikely.

(1) For new industry waves bringing new investment opportunities, a second growth curve helps both drive another material valuation re-rating and investors who suffered in the previous correction to regain confidence.

(2) For sectors benefiting from a certain style's dominance, a shift may lead to positions unwinding. Crowding may not return until the overall market style swings back.

(3) For sectors with tailwinds from extreme market risk appetite, the market direction change naturally leads to a collapse of the previous crowded trades.

## What might be next beneficiaries? High-dividend names and quality SOEs

Since mid-January, a number of government agencies − including the CSRC, Central Huijin, SASAC, State Council and those behind the working report from the Two Sessions meetings − have made frequent public announcements in support of China's capital markets. These coordinated market-supporting policies came into play and relieved the liquidity risk; A-share indices have quickly rallied since 6 February.

With the CSI 300 Index up 4% YTD, investors may turn back to the inflation and property market outlooks for this year while watching how the company earnings recovery plays out; as a result the A-share market's rise may become less steep towards late April.

The market may not have a new wave of momentum until there are clearer signs of earnings recovery. Under these market conditions, investors may switch to value, highdividend names and quality SOEs with defensiveness. MFs were still significantly underweight upstream sectors and SOEs as of end-2023, suggesting plenty of room for investors to add positions.

## High beta for high-risk-appetite investors in the near term

As we are currently waiting for clearer signs of earnings recovery, investors may either switch to defensives (high-dividend names or quality SOEs) or speculate on a few thematic names without near-term earnings.

For investors with a high-risk appetite, we expect the computer software sector to be the biggest short-term beneficiary of the global artificial intelligence (AI) theme, while media is benefiting from an improving regulatory environment, especially for the online gaming industry. We note MFs' positioning was still light as of end-2023.

## Investors' old favourites with solid earnings growth

For the whole of 2024, we think the bullish A-share thesis remains intact. (1) Any rebound in nominal GDP growth could drive a rapid rally in companies' revenues, sending EPS growth for the CSI 300 Index up from 3% in 2023 to 8% in 2024E. Industrial profit was up by 7.7%/16.2%/10.2% Q323/Q423/2M24, indicating the earnings recovery may be kicking off. (2) With policy easing gathering strength, wrongly priced stocks could re-rate from a cross-asset perspective. (3) The entry of long-term investors, coupled with the return of northbound investors (net inflow of Rmb18.1bn since March) could drive up valuations at the mid-point.

Risk-on sentiment should bring more fund inflows into A-shares, leading to some procyclical crowded trades outperforming. We note MFs' positions in traditional favourites such as F&B and electrical equipment have been less crowded after the market corrections over 2022-23. Among them, we like electronics and F&B from a bottom-up perspective. We believe electronics should be able to benefit from an upswing in the global tech cycle. Accelerated semis localisation would enable capable domestic vendors to unlock revenue potential in high-end segments, such as autos and industrials. We believe the recovery of disposable income growth and the release of excess savings should drive consumption recovery, hence boosting the F&B sector's profit growth.

## Valuation Method and Risk Statement

For various stocks across the industries we cover in the Hong Kong and mainland China stock markets, we use a variety of valuation approaches, including DCF models, Gordon growth model analysis and relative valuation analysis using various multiples such as PE, EV/EBITDA and P/BV.
