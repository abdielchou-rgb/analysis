# Opportunities in QIS After Recent Market Volatility

## Backdrop

A confluence of factors contributed to the volatility we have seen in markets in the last 6- 7 trading sessions. Last Friday’s US employment report came in soft across the board and raised questions about the soft-landing thesis that had been baked into market prices, where valuations were already stretched. This has raised market concerns about economic growth heading towards a hard landing or even recession. The latest ISM Manufacturing PMI data at 46.8 has also significantly missed the market estimate of 48.8 and was below the threshold level of 50. Uncertainty about the economic outlook spiked. The market now expects about a 70% chance of the Fed delivering a 50bp cut in September, significantly higher than the 10% chance at the end of July.

This came after a hawkish hike by the Bank of Japan – while Morgan Stanley economists were expecting it, it was far from the consensus going in. This could lead to greater divergence of monetary policy between the Fed and the BoJ, that is with investors perceiving that the Fed may need to cut more and sooner and that the BoJ may hike more – in both cases, more than expected. The hawkish stance has resulted in JPY appreciation. Compared to end-July, JPY has appreciated by 10%, with the latest weekly movement at the 96th percentile since 2000. The JPY appreciation has triggered unwinding of carry trades, with a widespread correction in equity markets globally and significant factor movements.

Based on the soft economic data and a tightening rates path in Japan, we saw global equity markets lose 8.2% from their peak in July with regional differences (Japan Topix down 24% from its peak in July) by August 5. Since then equity markets have generally recovered. Thus, we are in a period of heightened uncertainty about the economic outlook while markets are relatively robust, eyeing an environment where rates are expected to be substantially lower in 2025.

## Fundamental outlook

Fundamentally, Morgan Stanley's Chief Global Economist Seth Carpenter continues to expect a soft landing for the US economy as our base case (see The Global 360, August 6, 2024). This leaves our expectations for policy paths intact, ending 2024 at policy rates of 4.625% in the US, 3.25% in Europe and 0.25% in Japan. This path also goes along with an expected steeper yield curve in US and Europe by the middle of next year. While our expected path has remained unchanged, there is more uncertainty around this path, particularly with regards to whether rates go lower faster than expected.

The substantial equity market movements over the recent past are an indication of the market pricing in more uncertainty around the soft-landing scenario. The movements in implied volatilities – such as the VIX – were even more substantial. Outsized moves in implied volatilities compared to the pricing of the underlying markets are a hallmark of investors pricing uncertainty into markets.

We need more data points to clarify if soft data are just a blip towards a soft landing or we are really heading into a more challenging economic environment. Only once the data provide a clearer path should uncertainty be priced out of the market. Thus, investors need to carefully evaluate their expected probabilities of a soft landing versus a deeper contraction of the economy while at the same time expecting an increased level of volatility and volatility spikes as we get there.

## How did Quantitative Investment Strategies (QIS) fare over the last few weeks?

Overall QIS have fared well over the year so far ( Exhibit 3 ). If we take a simple average of the performance of key QIS for the year to date, we arrive at +2.2% annualized (+1.4% non annualized), which is above the long-term average of +1.8%. Thus, QIS strategies and portfolios as a whole delivered attractive performance. If we zoom into the more recent market volatility, we find on average still positive returns. However, when we adjust strategy returns by long-term volatility, we find a marginally negative performance. That said, overall QIS performed rather neutrally over the course of the past few weeks, and a well-diversified portfolio should have been rather unaffected by the general risk-off moves.

Trend following and FX DM Carry were among the more disappointing strategies. On the Trend side, we expected heightened volatility in our mid-year outlook in anticipation of the moves we have seen over the past weeks. In the equity and volatility space, there is a range of strategies with rather strong performance year to date. In the recent market volatility, we find that VIX-based convex strategies – such as strategies with signal-based long positions – did rather well and performed consistently with return expectations. Furthermore, Low Risk in equities performed in line with its long-term defensive profile.

## Rates Trend

Our stance towards Trend over the past few months was careful, with us preferring defensive versions of Trend with a defensive bias towards equity positioning and positive bias towards rates. Compared to the broader Trend strategy universe, these types of strategies held up well and are actually marginally positive over the past three months. With the moves we observed in rates, we are now constructive on the return outlook for a medium-term rates Trend-Following strategy. Using medium-term Trend signals between 3 and 12 months, the signals have generally turned flat to positive for US and European rates, driving positions to be on average long. Compare Exhibit 6 and Exhibit 7 for an overview of the cumulative returns of rates futures used to calculate the trend signals. Thus, medium-term rates Trend following has abandoned its short bias for the time being.

In a world with a steeper curve, long rates is a carry-generating position. The additional bonus of long rates is the expected positive duration effect from rates moving lower in the medium term. While volatility may remain high in the shorter term, we think that it’s a good time to start building up positions now. ## Long Straddle Replication

The MOVE index shot up by more than 25 points since the lows in mid-July, reflecting uncertainty around the rates outlook. This elevated level indicates uncertainty on the rates path. However, the directionality is less clear as we have seen strong moves in rates recently and we may see intermittent spikes upwards on the medium-term path lower. Rates straddles are great ways to benefit from this market volatility and we would expect them to perform well while at the same time providing convexity to portfolio returns.

Given the high levels of implied volatility, running these strategies with outright options is expensive, and we therefore would favour delta replication. That said, such replication strategies need to be carefully crafted and executed to reap the benefits of rates volatility.

## Equity Value and Quality Investing

As already outlined in our mid-year outlook, our factor investment preferences in equities are Value and Quality (compare here). This view has not changed. We like Quality for its defensive characteristics in deeper market corrections. This may not have come to fruition so much this year yet but, if the economy should slow down more than our base case, Quality would likely provide for an effective backstop. Value investing on the other hand would generally speaking give up returns in market distress due to the value trap (i.e., it leans into the companies that are exposed to catalysts driving markets lower). Absent sector-specific catalysts, the slowdown is linked to policy tightening to bring inflation down, which had the predictable by-product of slowing growth (soft landing). In such an environment, Value should be defensive. Thus, we would favour a combination of both factors.

While we are generally more cautious on Momentum after its strong performance since the start of the year (see Quant Matters – Reversal on the Momentum Highway, March 11, 2024), Low Risk has had a rather strong performance over the recent market volatility spike and may do well in further spurts of distress. Facing these return dynamics, a global multi-factor portfolio in equities might also be well suited to invest over the coming months.

## Equity Volatility Carry

Implied volatility is, at 27.85, above its 10-year average of 18.17, indicating a substantial volatility risk premium. Furthermore, we saw a movement in implied volatilities that was outsized compared to spot moves. With a market move of -6.7% in the S&P 500 over a 21- day period we saw the VIX increase by 15.34 points (which is already reversing some of the maximum gains we saw earlier this week). If historical relationships between spot and VIX moves were to hold ( Exhibit 8 ), one would have expected an increase of 7.12 points in the VIX instead. While the base VIX level was depressed from the start, this large discrepancy hints at scope for further mean reversion in the VIX.

This may generally be a good entry point into European and US equity volatility given attractive implieds as well as a relative resilience of the respective underlying cash equity markets, but there may be more volatility and gap risk ahead. Thus, we actually prefer relative value volatility where we are short volatility at the front end and long at the back end of the term structure. These strategies would usually short options with maturities between 1w and 1m and go long options with maturities between 3 and 12 months. Those strategies benefit from the fact that the curve is currently inverted considerably in the US and Europe ( Exhibit 9 ) while in the long run it is flatter (or in some instances upwards sloping).

# Which QIS are expected to be challenged in the Morgan Stanley base case outlook

## FX DM Carry

FX DM Carry was one of the weakest performers since July. Not surprisingly this performance was mainly driven by a short position in JPY versus USD. Given that we still see a large spread in rates between USD and JPY, it is not likely for this position to turn positive. However, the general direction we see for JPY is for it to strengthen further, with our outlook for 2Q25 at 140. Combining our negative position with a forecast for a stronger JPY implies a negative return expectation for FX Carry. At the same time, volatility in the currency market is linked to the rates market and the economic outlook of the different countries, leading to higher expected volatility and volatility spikes over the coming months. Thus, the risk/reward profile for FX DM Carry appears unattractive. The EM side looks a bit more favourable when we concentrate on expected carry, valuation and fundamental forecasts, but we would also expect increased volatility.

## Equity Momentum

Equity Momentum had a good start to the year and delivered very strong performance in 1Q 2024. With the recent market contraction some of those gains reverted. Furthermore, the signal intensity in the strategy has decayed, indicating that there is less dispersion in the momentum signals of equities. Such a scenario is usually associated with below average returns. Furthermore, heightened market volatility due to uncertainty is generally providing an environment that is not supportive of Equity Momentum. Thus, we expect the strategy to deliver below average performance while market uncertainty remains elevated. One way to stabilize Momentum returns in this environment is by combining the strategy with mean reversal signals.

• Uncovered option writing is thus suitable only for the knowledgeable investor who understands the risks, has the financial capacity and willingness to incur potentially substantial losses, and has sufficient liquid assets to meet applicable margin requirements. In this regard, if the value of the underlying instrument moves against an uncovered writer’s options position, the investor’s broker may request significant additional margin payments. If an investor does not make such margin payments, the broker may liquidate stock or options positions in the investor’s account, with little or no prior notice in accordance with the investor’s margin agreement.

• For combination writing, where the investor writes both a put and a call on the same underlying instrument, the potential risk is

unlimited.

• If a secondary market in options were to become unavailable, investors could not engage in closing transactions, and an option writer would remain obligated until expiration or assignment.

• The writer of an American-style option is subject to being assigned an exercise at any time after he has written the option until the option expires. By contrast, the writer of a European-style option is subject to exercise assignment only during the exercise period.

## Appendix: Strategy Risk Factors

Buying calls or call spreads: Investors who buy call options risk loss of the entire premium paid if the underlying security finishes below the strike price at expiration. Investors who buy call spreads (buy a call and sell a further OTM call) also have a maximum loss of the entire up-front premium paid. The maximum gain from buying call spreads is the difference between the strike prices, less the upfront premium paid.

Buying puts or put spreads: Investors who buy put options risk loss of the entire premium paid if the underlying security finishes above the strike price at expiration. Investors who buy put spreads (buy a put and sell a further OTM put) also have a maximum loss of the upfront premium paid. The maximum gain from buying put spreads is the difference between the strike prices, less the upfront premium paid.

Selling calls: Investors who sell covered calls (own the underlying security and sell a call) risk limiting their upside to the strike price plus the upfront premium received and may have their security called away if the security price exceeds the strike price of the short call. Additionally, the investor has full downside exposure that is only partially offset by the upfront premium taken in. Investors short naked calls (i.e. sold calls but don’t hold underlying security) risk unlimited losses of security price less strike price. Investors who sell naked call spreads (i.e. sell a call and buy a farther out-of-the-money call with no underlying security position) have a maximum loss of the difference between the long call strike and the short call strike, less the upfront premium taken in, if the underlying security finishes above the long call strike at expiration. The maximum gain is the upfront premium taken in, if the security finishes below the short call strike at expiration.

Selling puts: Put sellers commit to buying the underlying security at the strike price in the event the security falls below the strike price. The maximum loss is the full strike price less the premium received for selling the put. Put sellers who are also long a lower dollar-strike put face a maximum loss of the difference between the long and short put strikes less the options premium received.

Buying strangles: The maximum loss is the entire premium paid (put + call), if the security finishes between the put strike and the call strike at expiration.

Selling strangles or straddles: Investors who are long a security and short a strangle or straddle risk capping their upside in the security to the strike price of the call that is sold plus the upfront premium received. Additionally, if the security trades below the strike price of the short put, the investor risks losing the difference between the strike price and the security price (less the value of the premium received) on the short put and will also experience losses in the security position if he owns shares. The maximum potential loss is the full value of the strike price (less the value of the premium received) plus losses on the long security position. Investors who are short naked strangles or straddles have unlimited potential loss since if the security trades above the call strike price, the investor risks losing the difference between the strike price and the security price (less the value of the premium received) on the short call. Additionally, they are obligated to buy the security at the put strike price (less upfront premium received) if the security finishes below the put strike price at expiration. Strangle/straddle sellers risk assignment on short put positions that become in the money. Additionally, they risk having stock called away from short call positions that become in the money.
