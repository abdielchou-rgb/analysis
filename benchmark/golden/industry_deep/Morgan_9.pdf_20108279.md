With the data generally surprising to the downside across labor, growth, and inflation over recent weeks, 10-year yields are trading roughly 30bp below their local peak and are coming more in line with model-implied levels (Figure 2). OIS forwards continue to price in the first full cut in November and 41bp of easing over the remainder of the year, only 3bp more than last week. Though recent data leave us comfortable with our call that inflation and growth will step down over coming months, we continue to see the risk that the first cut comes later than our baseline forecast. In turn, this suggests it is still too early to add duration, as yields have tended to decline in the 3 to 5 months ahead of the first ease (see Treasuries 2024 Outlook, 11/21/23). Moreover, we think it<sup>’</sup>s unlikely monetary policy expectations will shift materially before the May employment report in three week<sup>’</sup>s time, and given that it is a negative carry proposition to extend duration, we remain neutral for now. On the curve, we continue to hold 5s/30s steepeners as a medium term low-beta long expression, but we unwind 2s/5s flatteners (see Treasuries).

We remain neutral on TIPS in the current environment, as breakevens are also likely to remain rangebound in coming weeks. We think it will be difficult for breakevens to widen materially against a backdrop of gradually softening inflation and activity data, especially as the Fed is unlikely to signal imminent rate cuts, the carry profile is turning less attractive, and TIPS issuance is increasing. That said, we also see limited room for near-term narrowing, given that valuations remain somewhat cheap, and we think next week<sup>’</sup>s supply will be digested with little incident. We continue to hold forward breakeven wideners via Jul-24/

Jul25 TIPS for relative value (see TIPS).

While the lack of an upside surprise in CPI was sufficient to pressure volatility lower alongside yields, the backdrop of policy uncertainty remains and a rangebound yield environment is supportive for resuming a bearish stance on volatility. However, implieds in the intermediate tails appear too cheap to warrant initiating a short gamma exposure and we resume our bearish stance in the wings of the curve. Swap yield curves have remained surprisingly rangebound over recent months despite rising Fed expectations and we think this narrowing pressure has been offset by rising long term inflation expectations. However given our expectations for moderate declines in inflation expectations going forward, we think swap yield curve, hedged for Fed expectations, are likely biased flatter and we prefer to express this through 12Mx3M/3Mx10Y swap curve flatteners, hedged with 33% risk weighted receive fixed position in 3Mx2Y swaps (see Interest Rate Derivatives).

In money markets, Dreyfus became the next MMF sponsor to liquidate its institutional prime fund as a result of MMF reforms. To date, six fund sponsors have chosen to liquidate their institutional prime funds or convert them to government. While they collectively held about \$220bn in AUM as of April-end, their total CP/CD exposure was under \$20bn, which is de minimis in context of the \$1.5tn market. To that end, we have seen little impact to short-term markets thus far, with CP/CD spreads actually narrowing over the past week. While it is possible spreads could widen as remaining institutional fund managers shore up liquidity in anticipation of potential outflows ahead of the October implementation date, we think the impact will be much more muted than what we observed in 2016. Indeed, the size of the institutional prime fund market has declined and its share of the CP market has shrunk as these funds gradually diversified away from CP/CD. Moreover, the technical backdrop remains supportive for spreads, with \~\$450bn in RRP balances offering ample liquidity to the front end (see Short-Term Fixed Income).

With Chair Powell<sup>’</sup>s comments and the April CPI report assuaging fears of further hikes and vol moving lower alongside rates, the macro backdrop has been supportive for mortgages. Most of the stack tightened 5-10bp over the week, led by the belly, before giving up 3-5bp of OAS on Thursday afternoon amid a bout of HF and money manager selling. However spreads are now at the tighter end of the range and we think it will be challenging for spreads to tighten further given the technical backdrop. In particular, the upper belly (4.5s and 5s) look tight, likely as investors express a preference for slightly out-of-the-money collateral as a form of call protection. Deep discounts still offer 30-35bp of T-OAS in our model, while higher coupons (5.5s-7s) offer more spread after adjusting for locally flat s-curves. We would prefer to be modestly underweight 5s at these tight absolute OAS levels, and prefer higher coupons for spread pickup (see Agency MBS).

Likewise, mortgage credit has remained tight as growing optimism around earlier Fed cuts has the market pricing in flatter credit curves. Using the CHASE shelf as an example, Sr AAA is \$0-20 tighter with AAA Mezz \$0-22 tighter from Sep<sup>’</sup>23. Lower rates mean faster credit deleveraging. The AAA/BBB basis is similarly tight in non-QM, tightening from 130bp to 85bp since January on the VERUS shelf. Markets are priced firmly, but are unlikely to shift much tighter unless we see data that continues to confirm that rate cuts are coming. Separately, our mortgage credit colleagues analyze delinquency data in GSE CRT pools. Delinquencies have been increasing among more recent <sup>‘</sup>22 and <sup>‘</sup>23 vintages. A rising share of these loans is also entering trial mods. However, declining credit performance has yet to translate into losses and we are not particularly concerned yet as collateral is still fundamentally supported by a resilient housing market, but will keep an eye on its evolution (see RMBS Credit).

This week, the New York Fed released its Quarterly Report on Household Debt and Credit for 1Q24, reporting that credit card delinquencies are rising fastest among those with higher utilization rates (i.e. those who are “maxed-out borrowers<sup>”</sup>). These borrowers are disproportionately younger and are within lower income quartiles, while they comprise roughly 16% of credit card balances and represent roughly 10% of borrowers. That being said, we still think up in quality credit card ABS remains attractive across bank (US and Canadian) and retail programs. While the NY Fed shows rising delinquency rates across total credit card debt outstanding, bankcard ABS trusts account for \~14% of total credit card debt (\$151 billion of \$1.12 trillion) and are comprised of very high quality (about 90% > 660 FICO) and seasoned accounts. Bankcard ABS fundamentals remain robust with the payment rate rising to 38.7% last month, versus 25.8% five years prior. Moreover, consumer credits are significantly enhanced, with first loss cushion on credit card ABS at 18.98%, while chargeoffs rose modestly to 2.33% in April. Meanwhile delinquencies remain near the middle of their historical ranges, with 90+ delinquencies at 0.69% in April, up from 0.56% a year ago but still below 0.76% five years ago, whereas 30+delinquencies stood at 1.38% last month, compared to 1.62% five years prior (Figure 3, see ABS).

HG credit spreads lagged other risk markets, closing roughly unchanged on the week despite the rally in equities and CDX.IG, and this underperformance makes sense in the context of lower yields and still heavy issuance leading to higher dealer inventories. Issuance has continued at a fast clip, with \$87bn of supply MTD through Wednesday and issuance in May likely to exceed its \$120bn historical monthly average (Figure 4). At the same time, JULI yields peaked in May at 5.85% and have rallied to 5.55%. With MTD returns on the index at +2.3%, leaving YTD total return just -0.3%, it makes sense some investors will have taken profits, causing some weakness in credit versus other markets. That being said, the strong total return performance will likely feed through into better fund inflows. We think we are a long way from a level of yields at which demand would slow, which we see around \~5.0%. Indeed, while spreads are at very tight levels, we think there still exists a supply/demand imbalance which is likely to push spreads moderately tighter over coming months. Moreover liquidity conditions have been improving for the HG market, compared to peers. Turnover in the HG market is at its highest levels in over a decade, while liquidity has dried up in other markets, likely due to the ratings and carry advantages IG offers (see Flows & Liquidity: Are investors churning their bond portfolios more frequently?, Nikolaos Panigirtzoglou, 5/16/24). At these tight levels, we have seen spread compression between Financials and Non-Financials, and we think Yankee banks in particular have room for further tightening from here (see Corporates).

High-yield spreads tightened modestly this week and are hovering only 5bp above their post-GFC lows amid steady retail inflows, lighter capital market activity, and strong earnings. Single-B spreads outperformed their peers, tightening by 12bp, while BB spreads tightened 1bp and CCC spreads widened by 2bp. HY/IG spreads of 250bp are now only 5bp above their low since the beginning of 2022 (see High Yield). Turning to fundamentals, we note that a considerable \$260bn of bonds/loans have been refinanced YTD, with the average monthly pace of \$53bn nearly double 2023<sup>’</sup>s \$28bn pace. Maturities through 2026 have been reduced by \$585bn, or 55%, to \$471bn since YE22, with access broadening into B3 or lower-rated maturities (Figure 5). Meanwhile the percentage of leveraged credit maturing over the next three years has only reduced from a record high of 22.1% at YE23 to 20.7% currently, as considerably more credit risk is priced into maturing loans than bonds. Indeed 62% of loans maturing by YE26 are rated B- or lower, which compares to 34% for bonds, and these loans are trading at wider spreads than the index. Thus, there is more room for issuers to “chop wood<sup>”</sup> in capital markets over the next few years as opportunities arise. Inevitably, primary markets for loans, bonds, and private credit will play a pivotal role (see High-Yield Bond and Institutional Loan Maturity Schedule, Nelson Jantzen, 5/13/24)

CLOs have been outperforming, but spreads have compressed, bonds are trading above par, and relative value is starting to look stretched in certain scenarios. Demand for floating-rate CLOs can remain strong until there is a materially dovish pivot by the Fed but as these spreads tighten it<sup>’</sup>s possible market conditions becomes less-supportive depending on the disinflation process. While robust liquidity conditions continue, our strategists recommend selectively paring back exposure in some high-priced bonds and re-allocate into new issue for risk management purposes (see CLOs).

Similar to their mortgage and corporate comparables, a softer macro backdrop together with lower rates has catalyzed a technical tailwind for CMBS markets, with 5yr and 10yr conduit LCF AAAs tightening by 6bp and 4bp, respectively, to J+96. Moreover the spread curve continued to bullishly flatten, with IG bonds outperforming at the top of the stack. While we can continue to see spread curves flatten on macro sentiment and technicals, the risk/ reward looks less attractive going forward. Indeed the fundamental backdrop continues to look strained, but we see opportunities for relative value. The serious delinquency rate for FRESB loans has been steadily increasing for the past 18 months and reached a new record high of 2.4% during the April remit period, with New York properties contributing the most to the overall rise (Figure 6). However we have similarly seen a rise in modifications in the

FRESB market, again concentrated in loans backed by New York properties, as a way to ensure continued loan performance. Meanwhile prepayment speeds have tumbled given high rates and slow rent growth dis-incentivize refinancing. Despite slow speeds, we think new issue FRESB A10Fs offer pretty good relative value, looking cheap to Freddie K A2s in our view, but caution they provide limited liquidity relative to the benchmark GSE CMBS bonds (see CMBS).
