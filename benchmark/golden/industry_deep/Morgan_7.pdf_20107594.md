## Strong USD versus low yielders

In macro markets, the year has been dominated by two themes: 1) The outperformance of the US economy; and 2) Low volatility, leading to carry trades ( Exhibit 1 ). This has led to a split performance of USD – outperformance versus low yielders but total return losses versus high yielders and some commodity FX. Carry trades continue to perform well, recent price action not withstanding ( Exhibit 2 ).

Recent conversations with clients have revealed some scepticism about the durability of these themes, particularly following recent US CPI data which point to the risks of a hot US economy rather than the Goldilocks supply side-driven expansion our US economics team is calling for. Given the latter, we remain in the camp that volatility should stay muted, though clearly geopolitical risks pose a threat to this view. Divergence and carry should remain key themes in FX markets.

Carry to outperform: A key reason why USD is likely to stay strong versus low yielders is simply that volatility should stay low, keeping the high carry of USD attractive. One of the most significant drivers of low volatility and strong risk appetite is the growing evidence of a supply side-driven expansion of the US economy that is simultaneously keeping growth robust and wages muted. While recent CPI data may cause some to start questioning this benign narrative, we believe that the drivers of it are powerful.

In any case, the combined CPI and PPI, when translated to PCE, does not look overly worrying for the Fed (see here). Provided growth remains robust, we doubt that risk appetite will be affected significantly, even if US rate rise further.

Indeed, UST yields have been on an upward trend all year and risk appetite and carry currencies have outperformed. Carry trades would be undermined by a pick-up in volatility. Also, the level of FX volatility is more dependent on the level of rate volatility ( Exhibit 3 ) rather than the level of US rates themselves ( Exhibit 4 ). In other words, a gradual rise in UST yields is more likely to be absorbed by FX markets than a sharp one, even if 10-year UST yields were to move back up to 5%.

Divergence exists: One of the more puzzling trends in markets recently has been the strong co-movement of European and US front-end interest rates, despite the clear divergence in the recent cycles of the eurozone and US economies, particularly when it comes to inflation. This co-movement led market participants to question whether the ECB can or is willing to start an easing cycle ahead of the Fed.

This debate has always been a bit odd as there are numerous examples of central banks starting their easing cycles ahead of the Fed this year, so it shouldn't be a great surprise to learn that the ECB is willing to do the same. As always, it simply depends on the data. There might be a case to argue that any US data which suggest a delay could imply similar data awaiting the eurozone and thus a delay for the ECB.

But growth and inflation data have clearly diverged and, as we heard from the ECB last week, June cuts are the base case, with several more likely in 2H.

Divergence between the US and European economic and monetary policy cycles is now starting to be reflected in markets ( Exhibit 5 and Exhibit 6 ), quite rightly, and points to continued EUR/USD downside. Our year-end target remains 1.04 though recent momentum has put this target in play considerably sooner. We would also argue that GBP fits into the same category, where the risks of a more substantial easing cycle are higher versus the US.

Asia ex Japan is another region where desired monetary policy divergence is likely to lead to currency weakness (see Asia Macro Strategy: Three Themes in Asia Local Markets ). Regional inflation is falling and the bias of monetary policy for many central banks in Asia is for easing, with any risks of a delayed cycle largely in response to currency weakness. In other words, as regional inflation stays under control, it is no surprise that regional currencies are weakening versus USD as rate differentials widen further in favour of USD.

## Implications of higher-for-longer oil prices

Our commodity analysts recently revised their oil price expectations up, peaking at US \$94/bbl in 3Q24, versus US\$90/bbl prior. While not a large revision, the move is significant in the sense that it reflects the combination of a supportive balance in the oil market – buffeted by OPEC's and especially Saudi Arabia's supply cuts – and the pricing of a geopolitical tail risk scenario where prices spike to \~US\$140/bbl.

EM investors received a taste of the external imbalances an oil spike can spur two years ago. Even now, much of the EM importer space still has not fully recovered as rising core yields extended the hit to externals. Against this, EM exporters continue to benefit from revenue strength, though this has only translated into upgrades for IG and BB exporters, given structural constraints for much of the single B/CCC exporter space.

Outright, neither IG nor HY oil importers' spreads have fully recovered since Russia's invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, though both are coming close. However, lasting divergence is much more apparent on the IG side.

Notably, IG importers are still trading around 40bp wider to exporters than before the invasion, mostly due to continued risk premia in CEE and LatAm curves. Much of this segment has lagged in implementing fiscal consolidation after 2022. For most, this was a result of an acceleration in government spending to moderate the impact of higher interest rates and energy prices on consumer balance sheets, exacerbated by a decrease in revenue growth rates.

Meanwhile, IG exporter index spreads have compressed from around 80bp to below 50bp, making this the only space that saw aggregate spread improvement in the 2022 oil price spike. IG exporters mainly include the IG Gulf sovereigns, which have for the most part – Saudi Arabia aside – maintained dual fiscal/external surpluses since 2022.

Ecuador the key beneficiary, followed by Pemex and then Colombia: Ecuador is the key beneficiary given that oil is still the largest export, even though shrimp has done well to catch up. ## CEE importers

The CEE region was the hardest-hit region in the 2022 oil price spike. Hitting both externals and fiscals, regional credits have spent the past two years on bond issuance sprees to make up shortfalls. Coming from a period of exceptionally low import bills – subdued by the drop in activity during Covid – and already in a difficult fiscal state, the Russia/Ukraine conflict caused clear scarring to CEE credit quality. Where the prospect of EU RRF flows did not offset some of the hit, this translated to negative outlooks and downgrades.

CEE sovereigns have to deal with the impact of the energy spike even now. The import bill is still elevated in the context of the past decade. Debt levels and costs have also risen. For credit, we think that Romania is the most vulnerable given its inability to consolidate externals, which stands in contrast to trade surpluses for other CEE nations. ## MENA importers

For MENA importers, rises in oil prices have an even larger effect than for CEE credits for three reasons: 1) Subsidies are persistent and generally not targeted; 2) Economies generally rely more on oil (and gas) than in Europe; and 3) External buffers have not fully recovered across MENA.

Having said that, there is an argument to be made that most MENA importers are in a better place to absorb a moderate further rise in oil prices than they were in 2022. Starting with Egypt, we expect FX reserves to rise considerably to US\$58.2 billion in 2024 from US\$34.8 billion on the back of large FDI investment ( Exhibit 27 ). This should allow it to weather wider current account deficits partly brought on by high energy costs. Jordan has also signed up to a new EFF and the regional conflict increases the chance of further concessional support, if an external shock were to require it. Morocco has sustainable market access, access to a new US\$5 billion Stand-by Arrangement and strong tourism inflows, which for now do not appear to have been impacted much by the regional conflict.

## Remittances as a key backstop

For full details, see EM Fixed Income Strategy, Economics & US Public Policy: Immigration Driving Remittances, April 12, 2024.

Remittances grew at exceptionally high rates during the pandemic: They provided crucial support for many LatAm countries' current account balances facing stress from lower tourism and higher oil prices, which heightened market concerns around their external positions.

Remittance flows have continued to rise in absolute terms, although they are growing more slowly than before the pandemic: Relative to GDP, flows have been declining since 2022 as economic growth has recovered in LatAm. We think their trajectory is still impressive, as remittances continue to increase from exceptionally high levels in absolute terms. Some market participants even thought they would decline after the pandemic spike.

A key question over the past year is why remittances haven't declined even though pandemic-related drivers have largely faded: For answers, we look to the drivers of remittances to LatAm. Since the US is the largest source of these remittances, flows are highly dependent on US economic conditions. This past year, we have attributed a lot of the resilience in remittances to the strength of the US economy, and the US labor market in particular (see Labor Supporting Remittances, July 12, 2023). However, our US economists' recent work on the impact of immigration on the US economy, especially the labor market, points to immigration as a critical factor that we have overlooked.

To identify the key drivers of remittances, we model flows out of the US and find that US growth, employment and inflation have been the primary factors (see US Driving Remittances to LatAm). Based on our US economists' expectations of a soft landing in the US, our model suggests that on aggregate, remittance growth should stay near current levels and then slow further, though remaining positive in 2025. However, with immigration providing upside risks to US growth and labor markets (see US Economics: Immigration Focus), we see risks to remittances also skewing to the upside.

The next question becomes – what would it take for growth to turn negative? We see US growth and US immigration policies as the two key risks: While we assign a low probability to this scenario, we would expect remittances to decline if US growth and the labor force both contract. In our economists' bear case, US growth would be hit, although continued strength in immigration flows and the larger number of migrant workers in the US should help reduce the impact of higher unemployment on remittances. We also see a shift to more stringent immigration policies as a key risk here, especially if it results in an outflow of migrants from the US (see US Election-Related Risks to Immigration).

## Do large policy pivots lead to a rotation out of credit into rates?

Idiosyncratic positive developments have underpinned a lot of the outperformance of EM HY versus IG recently. The current wave of reforms (particularly Egypt, Turkey and Nigeria) has to a large extent been about monetary policy adjustments and FX adjustments – ingredients that often make rates an attractive investment proposition.

With this in mind, investors have been asking us two questions: First, do previous episodes of carry trade attractiveness often result in credit underperformance (asset class rotation)? Second, what offers better carry – rates (FX-hedged) or credit?

We focus our attention on Egypt, Nigeria and Turkey as these have seen a significant rise in interest in their local markets given the recent policy pivots. Specifically, we look for conditions where there was a large FX adjustment and/or substantial monetary policy hikes.

Exhibit 36: Periods of FX and policy rate adjustments
![](images/2b04a3ce6054a2ced0a5faef22c6fa0c22f9a2b9654f0ac67810bbb2853c58d8.jpg)

Egypt and Nigeria are similar in that they have coincidentally adjusted at similar times. In 2016 Nigeria went through its policy pivot in 1H16 while Egypt moved later in the year. Again in 2024, Nigeria (after the current government got in power) has seen massive changes in both FX and monetary policy rates complemented by fiscal policy changes like subsidy removals. Egypt followed suit in March with similar but better-sequenced FX and monetary policy adjustments following sizeable FDI investment pledges.

For Turkey, while we look at two other episodes of adjustments, June 2023 is the most consequential as this comes after a period of substantial unconventional monetary policy which resulted in foreign investor participation rates at 0.5%. We don’t consider FX moves in this case as the lira (unlike the pound and the naira) is more free-floating.

On the first question, history shows no evidence of cannibalisation: After the start of a hiking cycle and/or FX adjustment, we notice that in the following six months, credit on average tends to outperform rates (unhedged). As we have written before, periods of FX adjustment tend to be credit positive as investors see this rebalancing as reducing external vulnerabilities and often are a prerequisite to an IMF programme (for instance in Egypt) ( Exhibit 37 ).

There is no direct evidence of causality as regressing rates against credit shows no statistically significant relationship. Ultimately, investors who can own rates and credit likely end up holding both as opposed to selling out of one into the other.

As such, the ongoing reforms in these countries should not be seen a threat to credit performance but rather complementary of it. In fact, the portfolio inflows to rates that are now evident in these markets actually improve the hard currency technical picture as this lowers the risk of a larger-than-expected issuance. While we expect issuances in both Turkey and Nigeria, we think that the risk of them issuing more than we expect is low. In Egypt we do not expect any issuance as the FDI and portfolio inflows are sufficient to meet financing needs in the near term.

## New trades and stances

Turning to a dislike stance on the Dominican Republic amid upcoming risks: The credit has outperformed over the course of recent months and is now pricing in nearly one upgrade. While we do see some of its outperformance as justified due to its improving fundamental backdrop expected in the second half of this year, we do see: 1) Risks associated with higher oil prices as not sufficiently priced; 2) The May 19 election to cause some underperformance despite relatively low risks; and 3) Its high beta leaving it vulnerable to any broader risk-off moves in the BB space.

Similar to most oil importers, the Dominican Republic feels higher oil prices negatively both in its current account balance and in its fiscals ( Exhibit 29 ). We see risks that fuel imports will rise this year, although external risks overall have come down compared to 2022. There have been improvements in the Dominican Republic's current account balance over the past year, with continued strength in remittances and tourism. Notably, FDI was able to fully cover this deficit last year, which has helped the country to accumulate substantial reserves in recent years.

However, we do note that reserves registered the largest decline in the first quarter of this year since 2Q20, both outright and as a share of GDP. They are now at 11% of GDP, only 1pp of GDP above the IMF's recommendation of 10% of GDP. With high GDP growth expected this year, nominal reserve build would have to pick up again in order to remain above this recommended level.

We see bigger risks to fiscals. Subsidy expenditure in the Dominican Republic peaked in 2022 at 2.3% of GDP and remained high versus history in 2023 at 1.6% of GDP ( Exhibit 44 ). In the first two months of this year, high subsidy expenditure has continued, outpacing recent years. The administration recently froze energy prices again and the election in May should limit the the chances of subsidies being reduced before then, in our view (see Energy Driving Expenditure, August 23, 2023). Although it is unlikely that subsidy expenditure reaches 2022 levels, we do see risks of it increasing versus last year. Both the administration and the IMF are expecting subsidy expenditure to decline, with the IMF forecasting it to reach 0.9% of GDP in 2024.

Furthermore, we could see higher oil prices causing issues to arise with progress on its fiscal reform and energy pact. We see low election risks, with polling indicating that the incumbent has a high probability of winning. This administration has been adamant about discussing a fiscal rule and getting back on track with its fiscal pact once economic conditions allow. While we still expect discussions of a fiscal reform in the second half of this year, we do see risks of higher pressure on energy prices causing more hesitation (see Key Fiscal Reform, August 23, 2023).

## Three themes in Asia local markets

Two weeks ago, we took a more neutral stance on Asia FX by suggesting carry trades and positioning for USD downside via options. In rates we closed our receiving trade in China and recommended paying the front end in Thailand (see EM Strategy: Asia Macro Strategy: Portfolio Recalibration, April 3, 2024).

In hindsight we should have brought forward our medium-term bullish view on USD. As discussed in EM Strategy: Asia Macro Strategy: Stay Long Carry, March 4, 2024, we saw USD appreciating in 3Q due to improving demographics and better productivity.

We believe that there are three themes in Asia local markets:

## 1. Outperformance in the US economy still weighs on Asia markets

In the past, a strong USD economy has been a necessary but not sufficient condition for the risk rally in EM local markets, and it depends on whether the rest of the world is narrowing or widening the gap versus US growth.

This time we see the latter. We discussed in EM Strategy: Asia Macro Strategy: Stay Long Carry that investors still underappreciated the importance of improving demographics due to a rise in immigrants.

Immigrants could contribute to the economy via two channels: First, the increasing supply of labour could bring down wage inflation. We started to see it in recent average hourly earnings.

Second, these immigrants will contribute to both NFP and growth. This has again been shown in the latest NFP and growth data.

Such an impact could be long lasting as these people gradually enter the job market. The impact could be even more material because the number of immigrants could be frontloaded in 2024 given a potential immigration policy change following the US election.

Lower wage inflation and better growth is indeed a Goldilocks scenario for the US: To be clear, it is Goldilocks for the US but not for the rest of the world as other economies including Europe and China are facing worsening demographics. In other words, the growth gap between the US and the rest of the world could widen.

What bothered the market even more was this week's upside surprise in CPI (see US Economics: CPI Data Imply 0.31% Core PCE in March, April 10, 2024). With three months of upside surprises in a row, it is difficult to argue that the overshoots in January or February were one-off or seasonality related.

It shows that inflation in the US remains sticky and might not come down as quick as the market and the Fed are expecting. As a result, the market is now debating whether it is a question of if and not when the Fed will cut rates in 2024.

There are two possible results: The Fed would have to turn more hawkish or the Fed would have to let the market tighten financial conditions itself via higher USTs, a stronger USD, wider credit spreads or lower equity prices.

Such an outperformance in the US economy could continue to weigh on Asia FX and rates over the medium term, in our view.

## 2. China to keep CNY stable until 3Q24

China is exporting deflation pressure to the rest of the world via export prices and not via CNY. In other words, the stability of CNY has been very important in Asia, anchoring most of the currencies in the region.

## The sentiment on China has improved in recent weeks. What are our latest thoughts?

The structural problems including demographics, debt and deflation haven't been addressed. The slump in property prices is creating a wealth effect, which reduces consumers' appetite. The weak private sector confidence also led to a higher unemployment rate and wage cuts.

The solution to that in many investors' view would be a demand-side stimulus, while the government seems only willing to do supply-side stimulus via doubling down industrial policy including three new sectors: EV, battery and green energy. The gap between what the market is looking for and what the government is willing to do remains wide.

This is now well known by the market, which suggests that the market is pricing in this structural story to some extent in the absence of any new internal or external shock: This also suggests that any move now could be driven by the data or events.

Looking at the data, our economists have recently upgraded their China growth forecast from 4.2%Y to 4.8%Y for 2024 on the back of better exports (see China Trendspotting: Raising Our 2024 GDP Forecast on Export Strength, April 10, 2024).

We should point out that we saw a similar pattern last year. Entering 2023, most investors were optimistic about China's reopening, and better data in January and February prompted many to update their GDP forecast to 5.5-6%Y, but that optimism reversed quickly in 2Q.

Into 2024, most investors were pessimistic, but January and February data again prompted the Street to lift its GDP forecast again. Hence, how the market moves would depend on the data in 2Q.

The key to watch would be whether better exports would be able to offset the ongoing decline in property prices and weak consumer confidence. We don’t have high confidence in calling that.

But from policymakers' perspective, they will focus on year-on-year GDP, which should easily achieve 5%+ growth in 2Q due to a low base. This would reduce the chance of fiscal easing at the both April and July Politburo meetings.

With limited catalysts from data or Politburo meetings, we should be watching a few things in 2Q and 3Q: 1) Whether Vanke will be saved or allowed to default; 2) Whether there are any negative headlines around some regional banks or local governments; and 3) When the government will hold the Third Plenum and whether there is any personnel change in the Politburo.

On CNY, we elaborated on our view in Our Reading of the Recent Move in USD/CNY, April 3, 2024. The fixing below 7.10 following the US CPI print despite the broad rally in DXY is another strong signal that the PBOC would like to keep CNY stable in the next few weeks. The spike of CNH and equity sell-off on March 22 reduced the chance of short-term CNY weakness but increased our confidence that the PBOC could let CNY move in the future.

Why 3Q24? There are three reasons: 1) USD could still be strong due to a better US economy; 2) The market could start to price in increasing tariff risk should polls continue to show Trump leading versus Biden; and 3) China would need a weaker CNY to sustain the export contribution to GDP in order to meet the 5%Y growth target in 2024.

Particularly on the last point, given the recent soft peg against USD, the broad USD appreciation has pushed CNY CFETS to reach 98.4. Its level isn't too expensive given the historical range of 92 to 102 ( Exhibit 65 ) but such a valuation is opposite to the marginal change in the balance of payments, which started to worsen from 2H23 ( Exhibit 66 ).

Admittedly, such an equilibrium might not be broken soon given stable FX reserves and strong exports. But we see a good chance for the PBOC to tolerate USD-led CNY weakness some time in 3Q24. This would be earlier than what most investors think, based on our conversations. Again a weaker CNY could weigh on the rest of Asia then.

Overall, we believe that hawkish central banks in Asia support paying trades.
