A Yen rally for the ages. FX markets are reeling from the 5%+ JPY appreciation this month, which ranks among the fastest two-week rallies in the currency this millennium (Figure 1The pastwok vind rc Yly). The grim mood music among investors is distinctly different to the euphoria of the Nov/Dec’23 period when the JPY TWI had registered a very similar trough-to-peak gain: the pace of the move was less breathless on that occassion, but more crucially, the cross-asset backdrop was best characterized as an ‘everything rally’ powered by frenzied expectations of disinflationary Fed easing. However simultaneous implosions in tech stocks, industrial metals and commodity/ EM FX alongside JPY strength this time around are reviving dark memories of 2001/’07 when the Yen had wielded a fearsome reputation as a safe haven beneficiary of carry trade crashes. Plenty has changed since then in Japan’s current account (trade deficit) and inflation (deeply negative real rates) backdrop to blunt the Yen’s crisis hedge characteristics, but macro-fundamentals tend to take a backseat when deleveraging fever grips crowded positions.

History suggests that acute Yen strength tends to peter out, though old lows are not easily revisited. The good news for structural Yen bears, or those unfortunately caught up in this carry unwind maelstrom, is that intense Yen strength of the kind seen over the past two weeks tends not to sustain. Figure 2 shows that the typical historical pattern following 5%+ fortnightly JPY TWI rallies is for the currency to begin to flatline / sidewind almost immediately thereafter. The bad news is that: (a) secondary tremors of a similar magnitude are par for the course over the next 1-2 months, even absent new JPY highs; and (b) more damagingly, reversal back to business-as-usual Yen weakness is very much the exception rather than the norm; even in instances where Yen declines have eventually resumed, cooling off periods of 2-3 months have been necessary to undo the technical damage caused . The caveat to these empirical patterns is that none of the post-GFC episodes of abrupt Yen strength featured the current scale of negative JPY real yield penalty and associated domestic Japanese outflows, hence our bias at this stage is that the 2010 or 16 style extended Yen appreciation that unfolded amid low/negative US - JN real yield gaps will likely not repeat this time around.

August seasonality come early. At one level, these midsummer convulsions can be straightforwardly rationalized as typical risk bearish August seasonality pulled forward by a couple of weeks. Seasonality is a somewhat flaky pillar on which to rest macro views, yet has proven to be surprisingly consistent in characterizing risk-off in August; the last two years for instance have featured an epic Powell takedown of financial conditions loosening amid the Fed’s inflation fight at the Jackson Hole symposium in 2022, while fall’23 saw bond markets grapple with the reality of the Treasury’s muchabove-average financing needs after the Q3 refunding announcement. This early fall pattern in FX has typically manifested in a stronger USD TWI and to a lesser extent JPY, and pronounced weakness in high-beta FX (Figure 4). While the intensity of the USD’s LHS-of-the-smile / safe haven credentials can be questioned in this cycle given its already broad ownership on carry and US election hedging grounds, weakness in well-subscribed to asset currencies (AUD, LatAm etc) is more straightforward to explain based on exante positioning. With markets still someways off from resurfacing on the other side of this seasonal tunnel, we continue to run with a bullish USD / bearish risk tilt to our trades portfolio (see Macro Trade Recommendations).

How much more runway is there for the carry trade unwind? There is no hard science to answering this most frequently asked question this week, but the following observations inform our judgment that the correction is in its late stages but is still potentially incomplete:

Our Yen strategists note that the current level of USD/JPY has fallen below below short-term fair value consistent with three Fed rate hikes this year, after having overshot to the tune of 8-10 Yen earlier in the year. While shortterm undershoots cannot be ruled if equity volatility continues, the bulk of the overshoot is now behind us (see Background of recent plunge in USD/JPY and outlook)

By the standards of broader G10 FX carry trade unwinds since the GFC, the current episode ranks as a relatively minor ruction (Figure 5). There is a loose correlation between the ex-ante yield of high-minus-low carry baskets as well as overall global yields, and the ex-post magnitudes of carry trade drawdowns (higher yields = smaller drawdowns), based on which our prior is that the double digit collapses of the past decade are unlikely in the current context.

On positioning, our cumulative assessment from parsing CFTC and DTCC option data is that \~40% of the overall FX carry trade buildup has been unwound in recent weeks, but that there still remains a solid stock of Yen shorts left to rinse (link). In other words, positioning does not stand in the way of additional Yen gains if de-risking continues, though we do note that Yen shorts exist for a reason and are unlikely to be cut all the way back to zero.

On the whole, our best guess is that the Yen rally is likely to take a breather in the lead-up to / following next week’s BoJ in line with the well-worn pattern of USD/JPY rebounding after MPMs, even after directionally hawkish delivery that is in line with expectations. While JPM has an out-of-consensus call for both QT and a 15bp policy rate hike at next week’s BoJ meeting, the fact that i) the JPY OIS curve is at least partially priced for this outcome, ii) that the hiking cycle is likely to remain slow (\~25bp / 6-mo) such that already shallow terminal rate pricing may remain subdued-for-long; and iii) the continued drag of a substantial US - JN real policy rate gap and mature USD OIS pricing of forthcoming Fed easing suggests that the Yen may not be a classical beneficiary of central bank hawkishness as in other DMs (see link).

## Assessing contagion potential from JPY strength to CNY, CHF and antipodean FX

The Yuan is not the Yen. USD/CNH broke below 7.25 this week, aided by the sharp pullback in USD/JPY and the broad unwind of carry trades. While the downdraft in USD/CNH superficially resembles that in USD/JPY and both qualify as popular funding currencies, their differences are vast in our view.

First, bearish CNY positions were less crowded unlike yen shorts which sent USD/JPY above 160 in late 2Q. Granted that in our EM Client Survey, CNY FX has been the most UW currency among EM investors in the past year. However, the real money dominated nature of the survey means that part of the bearish CNY positions could have stemmed from natural hedging demand for fixed income positions, and that the build-up of speculative CNY shorts may have been more contained than is given credit for in light of heavy PBoC intervention since late last year. This is evidenced by USD/CNH tracking broadly below rather than above rate differentials in the past few months (Figure 6), marking a sharp contrast to yen’s 5-6% overshoot relative to rate fundamentals in 2Q.

Second, domestic fundamentals of China vis-à-vis Japan are structurally different, with persistent deflation pressures and a dovish PBoC weighing on CNY FX. In contrast, risks around BoJ rate hikes and its path towards durable monetary policy normalization are directionally yen supportive, even though USD/JPY will remain hostage to a large degree to Fed policy.

Third, CNY FX clearly faces a much more hostile external environment with US election and tariff risks looming. Growth and rates aside, tariff risks could bring direct depreciation pressure for the yuan in a way that does not affect the yen. While we see broadly tariffs being less punitive for CNY FX compared to 2018/19, some risk premium is still warranted for the extreme case for 60% tariffs that can lift USD/CNY into the 7.60 - 7.70 zone (link).

## Do we need to re-think US election trades in light of the Harris surge?

Is the ‘Trump Trade’ kaput? In a word, no. Since VP Kamala Harris replaced Pres. Biden atop the Democrat ticket, betting markets have repriced the likelihood of Democrats winning the presidency back to near-debate levels. To be sure, that is still a decent gap vs Republicans, but it is substantially narrower (Figure 11Beting markods hvc l,u p w). Relatedly, some early swing state polling suggests Trump leads in a majority of the pivotal battlegrounds, though margins look relatively narrow (accounting as well for margins of error).

We don’t think markets should be overly concerned with unwinds of so-called ‘Trump trades’ – in part because there was only modest evidence of any real risk premium of such an outcome in the first place (at least in FX).Moreover, at least some ‘Trump trades’ have performed this week, even if patchily, even as betting market odds moved in the opposite direction. Two cases in point: first, USD has broadly outperformed the last two weeks (particularly vs high-beta), but has underperformed JPY & CHF. This largely mirrors the trade-war-era playbook of 2018-2019, despite betting market margins moving in a direction that would imply higher probability of a less-confrontational trade policy stance. It is true that the most well-subscribed to tariff hedge in FX — long USD/CNH — did wobble at various points this week; but on the other hand, USD/MXN is sharply higher this month, even though a liquidation of ‘Trump hedges’ would have suggested exactly the opposite. Meanwhile, the US 2s10s curve made two-year highs this week against a backdrop of Republican odds receding (although it has to be acknowledged that this was not the bear steepening that ‘red sweep’ hedgers were playing for).

## Explanation of Emerging Markets Sovereign Research Ratings System and Valuation & Methodology:

Ratings System: J.P. Morgan uses the following issuer portfolio weightings for Emerging Markets Sovereign Research: Overweight (over the next three months, the recommended risk position is expected to outperform the relevant index, sector, or benchmark credit returns); Marketweight (over the next three months, the recommended risk position is expected to perform in line with the relevant index, sector, or benchmark credit returns); and Underweight (over the next three months, the recommended risk position is expected to underperform the relevant index, sector, or benchmark credit returns). NR is Not Rated. In this case, J.P. Morgan has removed the rating for this security because of either legal, regulatory or policy reasons or because of lack of a sufficient fundamental basis. The previous rating no longer should be relied upon. An NR designation is not a recommendation or a rating. NC is Not Covered. An NC designation is not a rating or a recommendation. Recommendations will be at the issuer level, and an issuer recommendation applies to all of the index-eligible bonds at the same level for the issuer. When we change the issuer-level rating, we are changing the rating for all of the issues covered, unless otherwise specified. Ratings for quasi-sovereign issuers in the EMBIG may differ from the ratings provided in EM corporate coverage.

Valuation & Methodology: For J.P. Morgan's Emerging Markets Sovereign Research, we assign a rating to each sovereign issuer (Overweight, Marketweight or Underweight) based on our view of whether the combination of the issuer’s fundamentals, market technicals, and the relative value of its securities will cause it to outperform, perform in line with, or underperform the credit returns of the EMBIGD index over the next three months. Our view of an issuer’s fundamentals includes our opinion of whether the issuer is becoming more or less able to service its deb obligations when they become due and payable, as well as whether its willingness to service debt obligations is increasing or decreasing.
