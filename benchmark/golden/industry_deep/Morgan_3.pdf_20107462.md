# EM Asia Strategy: Good (global growth) isn’t good enough

A rising global growth tide... For growth assets, the pleasant surprise of 1Q24 was that consensus continues to be surprised on the upside by stronger-than-anticipated activity data. Greenshoots in growth have broadened out beyond the U.S. to the rest of the world, and traditional leading indicators suggest that there remains significant runway for the upturn in the global manufacturing cycle to run (Figure 1Leading ctorsu blwy fhp m ). Morgan economics team<sup>’</sup>s flagging broadening improvement in the global manufacturing cycle (link), driven in part by inventory re-stocking (new orders, inventories and orders/inventory ratios are all rising simultaneously in recent PMI reports).

…need not lift all boats. A hallmark of post-COVID-19 financial markets is that many traditional correlations have tended to break down more frequently, and the ongoing upcycle in global growth is proving no different. Some traditionally growth-sensitive assets, such as equities and, increasingly, commodities, have played to script, at least in direction, if not entirely in magnitude, but others, such as the USD and EM FX, have not (Figure 2). Complications in the current instance stem from two sources:

First, despite the broadening out of the global cycle recently, this remains at its core a U.S.-led upturn (Figure 3) powered by structural forces not at play elsewhere, at least to the same extent (immigration-driven labor supply surge, productivity upturn/ AI optimism, a cyclically wide fiscal deficit, etc.). Chinese growth is stabilizing based on the latest set of PMIs, but it is a far cry from the 2008/12/16-style cyclical reflation that carried the RoW ex-U.S. in its wake. In addition, a bottoming out of Chinese growth does not necessarily equate to CNY strength, since there remains a policy imperative to maintain currency competitiveness at a time when the Yen is under depreciation pressure. U.S. exceptionalism and CNY mediocrity are both salient for the broad dollar and USD/Asia and, by extension, Asian rates, given the role FX (in)stability considerations have played in handicapping expectations of central bank easing in the region.

Second, the impact of any growth lift on financial asset prices is being blunted by the re-assessment of Fed easing prospects and the associated rise in U.S. real yields, with the feedback loop from higher oil/commodity prices to inflation expectations being a key concern. The jury remains out on the relative importance of growth vs. financing conditions for different markets, but given the extent to which the all assets had rallied in 4Q23 on expectations of Fed easing even amid anemic growth at the time, it stands to reason that a paring back of easing expectations would have a symmetrical dampening impact.

EM Asia labors disproportionately more under higher global yields. For Asian local markets, there is little doubt in our mind that U.S. real yields — and, by extension, the broad USD — matter considerably more, given the low-beta nature of the region. This is straightforward to demonstrate in at least two ways. First, bivariate models of GBI EM Asia returns vs. global growth and UST yields show the latter to be substantially more statistically significant, with understandable variation between rates and FX. Second, cumulative returns from GBI EM Asia in rising global PMI and real yield environments are unimpressive, and significantly worse than for EM as a whole (Figure 4). On an outof-sample basis, we suspect that Asia LM — especially FX — will underperform even these mediocre historical norms, owing to: (1) the absence of China ballast in the current cycle; and (2) the positive correlation between oil and DXY, which disproportionately impacts an energy-importing bloc like Asia via inflation, trade balance/BOP and fiscal channels, albeit with some mitigating impact from administrative tools.

Asian rates vs. FX performance wedge to sustain on carry grounds. One bright spot in Asia LM so far this year has been the resilience of EM Asia rates amid the volatile repricing elsewhere (see Rates section). We have turned MW (from OW) Asia duration this month, given the backup in core yields and rise in oil prices, but the asset class RV between rates (better) and FX (worse) is likely to stay unchanged for the foreseeable future. As the cone of potential outcomes for Fed easing in 2024 has narrowed and prospects of capital gains from rapid central bank easing have dimmed, carry/slide has come to the fore as a determinant of yield moves: the greater the degree of ex-ante anticipation of future rate cuts, the larger the backup in yields when USTs re-price unfavorably (Figure 5). Asian rates have been relatively shielded from this phenomenon, given the shallow inversion of forward IRS curves (except the odd market like TWD, which saw an unexpected rate hike delivery), and could continue to outperform other EM regions as a defensive asset should core yields continue to sell off.

Hedging long Asian rates — pay USD rates or own USD? Client conversations suggest that fixed income portfolio construction in the region has typically taken on a receive Asia vs. pay U.S. flavor this year, with some long USDs also thrown in for good measure; a frequently asked question in this context is the optimal relative weighting of the various legs. One way to approach this question is on an in-sample basis: Figure 6Ownig USDsrathe py. d A lo  mu v shows that an empirical attempt to recreate a utopian upward-sloping straight-line return stream requires combining unit length in Asian bonds with a 1.7x UW on Asian FX; the FX hedge has turned out to be statistically more significant than paying U.S. rates, presumably because of the large interim drawdowns associated with below-consensus U.S. CPI prints, dovish Fedspeak/Fed pivots, etc., whose bearish influence on the USD is dampened by anemic RoW growth. The bottom line of this analysis — preferentially owning Asian rates over FX — is exactly the relative value stance that we have been advocating in these pages for a while, and represented our GBI EM beta allaocation before we switched MW duration this month.

## EM Asia rates: Resilience can only last for so long

EM Asia duration has remained remarkably resilient amid a confluence of external shocks. The first quarter of 2024 saw EM Asia duration well and truly get put to the test, having had to grapple with a mix of higher external rates, a meaningful Fed repricing, FX pressures and now materially higher oil prices. Through all of this, EM Asia rates remained remarkably resilient, outperforming most EM peers (Figure 7EM Asiadurton help vyw f ). We put this down largely to the fact that, coming into the year, the pricing of rates cuts was broadly shallow, which meant the region has acted as somewhat of a “safe harbor<sup>”</sup> amid the global volatility.

## EM Asia FX: More headwinds than tailwinds right now

Late cycle reflation not a tailwind for Asia FX. With global macro markets trading toward a cyclical reflation, we make the distinction between early cycle reflation and late cycle extension. Empirically, both regimes exhibit a tightening-expansion dynamic, with U.S. real rates and breakevens rising, as proxies for the policy response and the business cycle. Asian currencies perform best in the early cycle, when energy prices and interest rates are low, in turn driving a strong investment cycle that feeds into external demand that suits their manufacturing-oriented economic structures. These factors reverse for both the commercial and financial channels though the business cycle, with soft exports, firm energy imports and carry disadvantages as key headwinds for Asia FX.

The resultant lack of inflows - compounded by poor FX conversion in many markets this cycle - has left Asia FX more reliant on global financial conditions, increasing the dominance of global factors. More concerning, a firmer global business cycle has begun to hurt Asia FX, with their betas to U.S. breakevens flipping negative for both commodity importers and exporters alike. This makes regional currencies ideal funders in the late cycle, led by the JPY, feeding though to the rest of Asia.

Volatility rises in the east. With low-yielding funders driving FX weakness in EM Asia FX, we consider NEER basket weights and historical betas to the JPY and CNH to assess relative vulnerabilities within the region. Combining the two approaches suggests that JPY weakness is likely to hurt EM Asia FX in any scenario, but softness is likely to be much larger if the RMB also weakens in tandem. Currencies can generally be grouped into three clusters – KRW, IDR and THB (with the highest exposure and betas), INR (with the lowest weights and betas), and TWD, MYR, SGD and PHP (with high weights, but lower betas).

Weighing out the impact. Asian central banks manage their currencies relative to NEER baskets, with the J.P. Morgan effective exchange rate indices<sup>’</sup> weights presenting a reasonable proxy. Considering several deepening layers of these weights, we find that EM Asian currencies exposure is relatively limited if the JPY moves in isolation – on the order of 10-20% of the NEER basket. Greater weakness can arise, however, if the CNY is also allowed to weaken in tandem with the JPY – rather than just enough to keep the RMB TWI constant – raising EM Asia<sup>’</sup>s exposure into the 35-55% range. The one exception to this is the Indian rupee, where the combined exposure of direct and indirect effects sums to only 10%. Vulnerability naturally scales with proximity to such an explosion, with TWD and KRW most heavily exposed in the region. While it is unsurprising that the other low-yielding trade-reliant economies of THB, MYR and SGD are also heavily exposed, it<sup>’</sup>s interesting that IDR and PHP are somewhat middle of the pack in exposure – contrasting with INR despite their similarly high-yielding domestically oriented economic structures.

The yuan-yen beta battle. Considering historical sensitivities to the RMB and JPY via regression coefficients on a 35w rolling basis, exponentially smoothed, it is rapidly apparent that the CNH matters a lot more than the JPY for EM Asia. On average, beta to CNH is 3x that of JPY, with the exception of INR, which has almost no beta to JPY and only moderate sensitivity to CNH. The results suggest a similar batting order, with the low-yielding trade-reliant cluster most exposed, though the overall order is re-arranged into KRW, THB, SGD/MYR, followed by TWD. On the opposite end of the spectrum, the INR remains the most resilient amid the current environment. Curiously, the IDR is just as vulnerable as MYR and THB, despite their divergent economic structures. The other notable difference is the TWD – which ranks third from the bottom in terms of JPY sensitivity, though this has been rising of late.

## China: Growth is not the only game in town

Growth in China is turning better, but it is not the only game in town for local markets. Having been a growth underperformer for the better part of last year, China finally sees some light at the end of the tunnel, being no exception in the latest wave of upward growth revisions globally. J.P. Morgan economists already upgraded full-year growth from 4.9% to 5.2% on the back of stronger-than-expected activity data around LNY (note) and continue to see upside risks to near-term growth forecasts, with recent data showing no signs of moderating in robust growth momentum. Financial markets were initially unconvinced in early 1Q with the data optimism, lagging the bottoming in economic activities, but they have caught up with data surprises in recent weeks (Figure 22). The cyclical tailwind for the yuan has been largely limited, with external headwinds lingering and the ongoing repricing of the Fed weighing particularly on low yielders. A little more patience in CNY rates. The bullish momentum in CNY rates since 4Q23 has run out of steam, as recent upside data surprises reduce hopes of an imminent rate cut from the PBoC. On one side, policy makers continue to signal a structural dovish bias, keeping rate cut expectations alive over the medium term. Despite disappointments around a partial MLF rollover last month, the PBoC has continue to supply liquidity via other structural instruments, with the latest being the addition of a CNY500bn relending facility to support technological innovation. Such easing steps, though primarily through quantity, rather than price-based tools, still reinforce PBoC<sup>’</sup>s dovish stance and help to cap yields on the upside. However, meanwhile, 10y CGB yields already track more than 15bp below economic fundamentals/PMIs (Figure 26), underscoring an unfavorable valuation. This will likely limit room for a further rally in duration, absent an immediate policy tailwind. With money market rates failing to break lower (DR007 broadly on par with OMO rate) and front-end payers being carry-positive, the curve has flattened notably since the start of the year. To some extent, the swap curve even looks overly flat relative to the carry profile, pointing to risks of re-steepening from here (Figure 27). The expected mean reversion, however, is more likely if/when repo rates drift lower, prospects around which remain uncertain, taking into account accelerating issuance in the coming months. Sandwiched in between technical headwinds and structural dovishness from the PBoC, CNY rates could continue to consolidate over the short run. We stay neutral, waiting for better levels to add duration.

## India

Outright trades: Long INR (vs. CNH)

FX: A haven of stability far from the east. The rupee remains the most insulated currency from the late cycle dynamic plaguing Asia, with a more domestic economic orientation and strong defensive characteristics. The top-down macro tailwinds are boosted by bottom-up support from the boom in services exports, keeping the external balance strong despite softer FDI inflows. Elevated vol-adjusted carry makes risk premia in the INR very attractive, particularly when funded vs. North Asian low yielders, with the CNH our preferred choice. That said, tailwinds are fading on the margin, with the turn of the fiscal year likely to lift activity and imports as government spending ramps up. Energy prices creeping higher amid the late cycle extension are likely to drag the trade balance lower, with core biased weaker, as well. Positioning has pared back on the margin, but remains significant, suggesting complacency induced by low market volatility. While the INR has been the trade of the year in Asia, and the RBI is likely to continue anchoring its stability into the elections, the coming months may see a marginal deterioration in the risk-reward, though it remains elevated and one of the best opportunities in Asia, in our view.

Rates: Limited scope for near-term capital gains; we remain neutral on local duration here. India local markets have seen considerable interest and foreign inflows over recent months as we head towards index inclusion later this quarter (Figure 29, see here). Despite the increased interest, we have preferred to retain a neutral duration stance. The RBI kept rates on hold at last week<sup>’</sup>s MPC meeting, as largely expected, in a 5-1 vote (same as the last meeting). Importantly, there was no sign of any shift in tone to a more neutral stance, with the focus still very much on the withdrawal of accommodation and a “fuller<sup>”</sup> policy transmission. Given the robust growth outlook, it is providing authorities ample space (and time) to look to bring inflation back to the 4% target, with the central bank keeping an “unwavering focus on price stability.<sup>”</sup> While there are reasons to be bullish (much better fiscal outlook, scope for sizeable index related inflows), without any shift from the central bank, we don<sup>’</sup>t see a strong case for a meaningful duration rally at this point in time.

## Indonesia

GBI-EM Model Portfolio: OW duration, MW FX

Outright trades: Long 3y INDOGB

FX: Cyclical tailwinds meet seasonal headwinds. The rupiah is Asia<sup>’</sup>s traditional cyclical reflation beneficiary with Indonesia a key commodity exporter of energy, food and metals. This top-down tailwind is compounded by the bottom-up gains from the downstreaming of metals exports, but offset by a lack of risk premia this cycle. Poor FX conversion has thus plagued the IDR, with the build-up of FX a potential buffer against further weakness, should conversion rates rise. That said, growing trade links to China have resulted in a higher beta to the RMB in recent years, with policymaker intervention patterns beginning to mirror that of their Chinese counterparts and translating into recent rupiah weakness. The ongoing dividend season for JCI-listed firms is also a key headwind, with potential outflows likely to pace 1-2% GDP annualized till July. The near-term picture remains quite mixed as a result, but the tactical bias skews to the long side, in our view, at least in relative terms to other Asian currencies.

Rates: Remain OW INDOGBs in the GBI-EM Model Portfolio and hold a long 3y INDOGB in outright trades. Despite the more volatile external rates backdrop, INDOGBs have remained relatively resilient of late. Much of this has been down to increased central bank bond purchases. YTD BI gross holdings of local currency bonds have increased from IDR1,363tn to IDR1,405tn (Figure 31). We retain a relatively constructive outlook on local duration at this point. While headline inflation has showed some signs of stickness, it has been much to do with volatile food prices, as core remains relatively benign (see here). At the same time, there is an onging easing in domestic liquidity conditions playing out that has reduced pressure for banks to trim their bond holdings (see here). One thing that bears watching in coming months is the direction of FX reserves, the rebuilding of which is something the central banks have stated will be a precondition to easing. Net FX reserves have fallen to their lowest level since Nov-23. While the scale of the decline in reserves over the past six months is certainly not on par with what we saw in Sep/Oct-23 (which saw the BI react with an out-of-consensus rate hike), it is the risk to bullish rates trades.

## Philippines

Outright trades: Close long PHP vs. basket

FX: Post-Easter seasonals patchy, with deteriorating terms of trade. Remittances remain a major driver of the peso<sup>’</sup>s seasonal variations, with Christmas and Easter the top two periods of strength. A third, less obvious season for inbound payments, however, has been the school year, with the global diaspora sending funds home for school fees, textbooks and other back-to-school purchases. This has historically occurred in the March-May months, with the school year typically starting in June. The post-pandemic shift to a late August start has thus left a vacuum of payments in the post Easter-period, likely translating into FX weakness amid a strong period, historically. While this pattern is gradually mean-reverting, with the academic year starting in late July and a month earlier than in 2023-24, April-May could still see a weaker-than-usual peso as a result. This shifting seasonality is likely to be compounded by rising energy prices, which have been a key drag on the peso amid global reflationary impulses. With these dynamics in mind, we tactically look to close out our PHP longs.

Rates: Neutral duration, given risks to inflation outlook. After bottoming in Jan-23, headline inflation has started to pick up again in recent months, printing at 3.7yoy% in Mar-23 - placing it back near the top of the central bank target range (2-4%). While much has been due to rice prices, core inflation momentum has also shown signs of picking up (Figure 33Inflatio meuhs rdpck ). Inflation may exceed the upper bound of the target range between 2Q24 and early 3Q24 (see here). While real policy rates still screen elevated, this may delay the BSP cuts to later in the year, especially if the first cut from the Fed continues to get pushed back. Further out the curve, it should be helped by a more constructive supply outlook for the rest of the year. Authorities are well ahead of regional peers, with c45% of the calendar 2024 plan already complete following the large RTB issuance during 1Q24. Authorities do tend to front-load issuance quite notably during 1Q, with the 2024 pace broadly in line with that seen in 2023 (see here).
