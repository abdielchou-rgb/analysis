## Five Questions We're Hearing, Vol. 2

Venu Krishna, CFA, BCI, US | Japinder Chawla, CFA, BCI, US | Rex Feng, BCI, US | Madhuresh Rai, CFA, BCI, US | Young Chen, BCI, US

Excerpted from U.S. Equity Insights: Five Questions We're Hearing, Vol. 2, published on May 16, 2024

We address a few key issues that have been top of mind for investors, including our updated views on Big Tech post-1Q earnings, potential for the rally to establish a broader base, a higher for longer rate environment, what motivated the April sellof, and macro risks that could catch equity markets unprepared.

1. Are you still constructive on Big Tech post-1Q earnings? Big Tech fundamentals still look good here and we think there's room to run over the next couple of quarters, even though the bar for the group to deliver has been set very high (as shown by the negative reaction to META results). Big Tech revisions have strengthened further post-Q1 earnings, bifurcating even more from the rest of the S&P 500. Big Tech's defensible margins have scarcity value in an environment characterized by slowing growth and sticky inflation, and the group is exposed to Growth, Quality, and Size, factors on which we have a Positive view.

2. What is the nearest and most likely catalyst for the rally to establish a broader base, and which segments of US equities are most likely to benefit? We see nascent interest in upside other than in Big Tech. That said, broadening has been modest and we think there's room for a lot more. Earnings growth is the most likely catalyst, in our view. A great deal of optimism has been baked into Big Tech earnings estimates, and comps going forward are likely to become more challenging, potentially as early as 2H24. We think this creates an opportunity for the rest of the S&P 500 to contribute more meaningfully to YoY earnings growth in 2H24 and into 2025, provided the macro backdrop remains supportive. In an encouraging sign, revisions for the rest of the SPX have also improved following the Q1 earnings season.

3. Rates are now assumed higher through year-end with the risk of no cuts in 2024; can equities continue to work? The UST 10Y yield is critical. Over the last 45 years, the correlation between change in nominal yields and equity returns skewed negative when the benchmark 10Y rose above \~5%, and the current yield-equity correlation has already become quite negative — by one measure, the most negative it has been since 1997. Still, we have some yield headroom after the May FOMC and core CPI, so we think equities can still work in this environment even if we get no cuts through YE24, as long as inflation continues to moderate.

4. Was recent volatility purely systematic/technical and, if so, where does that leave positioning? We think there was a major technical and systematic element to the April sellof. The S&P 500 established local lows around key technical support levels, and long equity positioning among CTAs came down sharply over the first half of April. Against the backdrop of 1Q24 earnings coming in much better than expected, we think the sellof was a clear reflection of macro jitters tied to stickier-than-expected inflation, amplified to the downside by asymmetric positioning among systematic strategies. Now that the S&P 500 has clawed back all of its April losses with an improved earnings outlook in the back pocket, we think equities are likely in a better position from a valuation standpoint.

5. Which macro/tail risks are equity markets ignoring right now? Negative rates-equity correlation and outperformance of our Early Recession basket suggest that investors are keenly attuned to both "too hot" and recessionary economic outcomes. Signs of consumer stress appeared in 1Q24 reporting season, but share price weakness suggests this is well understood. Instead, we think stagflation (while unlikely) has the potential to catch equity markets unaware because some traditionally defensive plays would be exactly what you don't want to own in such a scenario. Tight risk premiums also suggest that little, if any, geopolitical risks are being priced in right now.

## What to buy in the UK?

Emmanuel Cau, CFA, Barclays, UK | Matthew Joyce, CFA, BBI, Paris | Magesh Kumar Chandrasekaran, CFA, Barclays, UK | Emmanuel Makonga, Barclays, UK | Arihanth Bohra Jain, Barclays, UK

Excerpted from European Equity Strategy: What to buy in the UK?, published on May 15, 2024

UK equities are starting to catch a bid, but valuation/positioning disconnect vs. fundamentals remains. We find FTSE100 and 250 both attractive, but see the latter benefiting more from lower rates and improving domestic demand. Our analysts particularly like Banks, Housebuilding, Retail, Real Estate, Leisure and Utilities.

More interest in the UK, but significant mispricing remains. UK equities have caught up with other global indexes after a strong April/May, benefiting from rotation and broadening of the rally. We note somewhat increased exposure being taken in UK equity market derivatives like futures and call options, which may be investors dipping their toes in before buying outright. Longer-term flows are still depressed, though, as is UK market cap share of MSCI World, currently at a record low of 4%. Both look disconnected from earnings fundamentals, which have not fallen as much as prices, while UK ROE has rebounded. Inbound M&A on cheap valuations (screen in the report), and a pick-up in buybacks boosting total yield, are two potential further tailwinds for UK equities, in our view.

UK macro outlook is brightening. Our economists' out-of-consensus call for the BOE to start cutting rates in June is on track given slowing inflation, on the wages front in particular. This should provide much-needed support to the anaemic and highly rate-sensitive UK economy. We note, however, that activity momentum has already started to rebound, even before rate cuts started. Our economists' recently upgraded UK GDP growth forecast for 2024 of 0.8% is double the consensus estimate of 0.4%. We expect both consumer demand and corporate margins will potentially benefit from this improving growth-policy trade-of.

FTSE100 and FTSE250 equally attractive, but 250 more exposed to rates cuts. Lower UK rates vs. higher-for-longer rates in the US should continue to cap material GBP upside. With 70% of revenues coming from abroad, the FTSE100 is highly sensitive to FX, so is likely helped by a weaker GBP. Meanwhile, the more domestic-oriented FTSE250 could benefit from stronger activity and rate cuts. Looking at valuations of UK sectors vs. global peers, FTSE250 looks very cheap, as do Energy, Telecoms, Utilities, Banks, Div Fins, Real Estate, Leisure, Media and Healthcare Services.

Which sectors do our analysts like in the UK? Our analysts particularly like Banks, Housebuilding & Construction, Retail, Real Estate, Leisure, Business Services, Diversified Financials, and Utilities. And in Mid & Small Cap, domestic Consumer and Cap Goods companies. All should benefit from improving growth and falling interest rates. We provide a screen of our analysts' Overweight-rated UK stocks with at least 10% upside to their price target, and rebalance our domestic (BCEUUKDM) and exporters (BCEUUKEX) baskets.

## New tarifs: More bark, less bite

Roanna Chau, Barclays Bank, Hong Kong | Jit Ming Tan, CFA, Barclays Bank, Singapore | Avant Save, CFA, Barclays Bank, Singapore

Excerpted from US-China: New tarifs: More bark, less bite, published on May 14, 2024

US set to impose higher tarifs on both products made in China and those that are exported to the US from third countries. The impact on Chinese firms is likely to be minor given limited US exposure, whereas lackadaisical demand for EVs is a bigger problem for Korean battery-makers.

Tarif decision. President Joe Biden’s administration is reportedly set to announce new China tarifs as soon as this week. According to Bloomberg, the new tarifs will focus on various industries including electric vehicles (EVs), batteries and solar cells, with existing levies largely being maintained<sup>1</sup> . The tarifs are expected to cover strategic sectors where China has cost advantages and excess capacity. Apart from these strategic sectors, Chinese-made medical supplies and equipment, along with steel and aluminum, might face additional tarifs as well, according to CNBC and Reuters<sup>2</sup> .

See Washington Research: US trade policies towards China, 18 April 2024 for our Washington Research on what could happen to US trade policy towards China if President Biden or former President Trump is elected?

Eyeing impact through supply-chain channels: The direct impact from EV-specific tarifs would likely be limited for Chinese corporates because their direct US exposure is limited. But higher tarifs and/or tighter trade restrictions (specifically trade diversion, IRA) against the backdrop of EV demand constraints could have a material impact on companies in the EV/ battery ecosystems.

• US Inflation Reduction Act: Under the US Treasury's proposed guidance3 , to qualify for the IRA tax credit, clean vehicles must not use battery components or critical materials from specific countries (including China) starting in 2024/2025 respectively. The prospects of increased scrutiny implies incrementally tougher raw material restrictions. This would likely have spillover efects for Chinese/Korean companies that are part of the EV/battery ecosystem.

• EV winter: Our US equity colleagues recently reduced their global 2024 EV penetration estimates (as % of new car sales) for 2024. These revisions largely reflect pressures in Europe and the US, albeit partly ofset from an improved China outlook. Globally, they cut their 2024 forecast for EV sales to 19% of total (from 21%), and for 2030 to 43% (from 48%). The EV narrative has pivoted sharply to demand constraints from supply constraints.

## Cooling down

Emmanuel Cau, CFA, Barclays, UK | Matthew Joyce, CFA, BBI, Paris | Magesh Kumar Chandrasekaran, CFA, Barclays, UK | Emmanuel Makonga, Barclays, UK | Arihanth Bohra Jain, Barclays, UK

Excerpted from Equity Market Review - Cooling down, published on May 17, 2024

Falling rates on cooling US growth/CPI data has lifted equities to new highs. The rally amid a dovish tape has come with a notable rotation under the hood, consistent with our view to trim reflation tilt and add to rate cut winners. But we still like Financials and some Cyclicals, while dollar pause ofers some respite to EM.

Cooling down. Global equities have quickly recovered their April losses, rallying to fresh highs this week. In-line US CPI brought relief, but yields had already rolled over for a couple of weeks. Cooling US growth/labour market data, but also Powell's afirmation that the next move was more likely a cut than a hike, had indeed challenged the hawkish repricing seen early spring. So 'bad' news on the economy has become 'good' for markets again - at least for now. Rate market expectation ytd has swung from being wildly optimistic coming into the year (6-7 cuts priced), to being so pessimistic that by end of April, only \~1 cut was priced for 2024. While Fed's message has been fairly steady throughout these market swings (and now looks broadly in line with softening data), being knowingly 'data dependent' in their reaction function is arguably adding to the volatility of market pricing. No doubt these swings will continue if we see either a reacceleration or further slowdown from here. Only if the data comes in just right may market pricing of cuts calm down a bit. I.e. the path to a soft landing is narrow, and the not-too-hot nottoo-cold economy is a fragile balance. ## Economic Outlook

## Dancing to the data

Christian Keller, Barclays, UK | Mark Cus Babic, Barclays, UK | Akash Utsav, Barclays, UK

Excerpted from Global Economics Weekly, published on May 17, 2024

This week's data provided some soft landing comfort to markets, which have become highly sensitive to labour market news and inflation prints, in the absence of central bank forward guidance. Next week's FOMC minutes may matter less, but Europe's wage data and PMIs and CPI for the UK and Japan will have the attention.

## Data are the captain now

## Every print matters ...

After the post-pandemic surge in inflation wrong-footed central banks' forecast-based policy frameworks, they abandoned forward guidance. In its place, policymakers have adopted an approach that prioritises contemporaneous data, allowing for a more nimble response to rapid changes in the economic environment and acknowledging the limitations of even state-of-theart macro forecasting technology. However, this shift has also introduced more unpredictability in rate expectations and market volatility, and transmission lags complicate policy-making based on spot data.

That said, as important as it is to understand the implications of renouncing forward guidance, the reality is that for market participants, weighing the merits and drawbacks of this change may be inconsequential. What matters more is that spot data dependence appears to be here to stay, and has even intensified recently after the inflation spike in Q1 caught the Fed by surprise just after it turned dovish in H2 23, despite strong activity data. Therefore, our only course of action is to keep calm and carry on, diligently assessing and interpreting the data as they flow in.

## ...as CPI and PPI may provide some comfort to the FOMC...

In this context, the data released this week are likely to give the FOMC slightly more comfort, as core CPI surprised to the downside and decelerated modestly to 0.29% m/m from recent elevated prints. This was driven by another month of deflation in core goods and, importantly, an easing in core services inflation across housing and supercore. Combined with the PPI, which was stronger than expected in the headline but weaker in the components that feed into PCE, this implies core PCE is likely to ease: we estimate to 0.24% m/m in April, from 0.32% m/m in March, with the bulk of the deceleration materialising in the core services category.

At the same time, the week brought further signs of a benign normalisation in growth. April headline retail sales were flat and the control group contracted 0.3% m/m, both surprising to the downside, as they pulled back after March's strong print. Similarly, industrial production printed weaker than forecast, as it moved sideways and manufacturing production contracted. Housing data — starts and building permits — also were somewhat softer than expected.

To be sure, neither of these dynamics should be over-interpreted. Even at 0.24% m/m, core PCE would still be elevated. It will take a series of softer prints to bring down the 3m and 6m runrates, and firmness in this week's import prices could partly pass through to consumer prices down the line. Meanwhile, we think that the fundamentals for consumer spending, such as income growth and a substantial reservoir of excess savings, remain supportive, and the trajectory of industrial output is sideways, not down. Thus, we see the activity data as consistent with the soft landing we are pencilling in our baseline.

All told, these data are moving in the right direction and are in line with the FOMC's intention to keep rates unchanged until it gains greater confidence that inflation is on a sustainable path to target. Thus, the recent data flow somewhat bolsters our conviction that the FOMC will deliver one 25bp rate cut this year, which we think will take place at the earliest in September.

## China announces the right policy measures, but size matters

## Lopsidedness and weakness in economic data

In China, the April data combined with an upside surprise in IP and improving exports, which contrasted with very weak domestic demand indicators, such as a miss in retail sales, a deeper housing slump and softer infrastructure investment. The disappointing April retail sales reinforced the picture of weak consumption, held back by leveraged household balance sheets and a deteriorating labour market. In addition, the April housing data show that the real estate sector is yet to bottom, as the contraction in property investment deepened further and home prices continued to fall in all but one of the 70 cities that we track.

Earlier inflation and credit data also give little cause for optimism. April headline CPI remained very close to deflation, even as it edged up to 0.3% y/y amid rising energy prices, and core CPI also stayed soft at 0.7% y/y (compared with 0.6% y/y the previous month). PPI recorded the 19th consecutive month of deflation. Meanwhile, total social financing growth eased more than expected, with long-term credit demand falling y/y for households and firms, while government bond issuance remained slow.

Industrial production, which beat expectations, and robust exports are likely to provide some ofset to the domestic demand weakness as far as GDP growth is concerned. However, such lopsided growth looks increasingly unsustainable. Unable to revive domestic

consumption, China seems to have resorted to ramping up production capacity in manufacturing, reflected in robust IP, which relies on being able to export this increased production. However, even if this may have been a successful strategy in the past, it might no longer be in a world where China is already a manufacturing giant and the US and Europe seem no longer willing to absorb its goods out of increased concerns for their domestic industries and heightened geopolitical rivalry (see the comment on trade tensions below).

## New policy steps in the right direction, but too small

In response to such bleak fundamentals, the government has pledged to roll out bolder measures to support the housing market and at the 30 April Politburo meeting called for "coordinating and studying policies to reduce housing inventory." This week, a number of policy announcements and press reports suggest steps are being taken to translate this into action. While all the details are not yet clear, the most feasible and efective approach to clear the excess housing inventory will be for local governments directly to purchase unsold properties at a discount and then convert them into afordable housing. So far, individual cities have started to communicate such plans, with six tier 2-3 cities having revealed quantitative purchase targets and three additional cities announcing plans to reduce housing inventory, but without giving quantitative targets. The government has also announced lower down-payment requirements for home buyers and scrapped the national mortgage rate floor.

However, the financial backing for this housing inventory destocking is limited. In particular, the PBoC announced this week that it would establish a nationwide programne to unleash CNY300bn of re-lending funds to be extended to 21 banks, which should translate into an estimated CNY500bn (0.4% of GDP) of credit for housing purchases. Such a scale is, in our view, too small in the context of perhaps CNY28trn worth of unsold properties (estimated using the extremely elevated housing inventory to sales ratio); ie, the CNY500bn funding accounts for less than 2% of unsold inventory.

## Rising trade tensions

## New US tarifs on China are not irrelevant

Finally, the Biden administration proposed retaining the tarifs President Trump imposed on China and significantly increasing them in certain "strategic industries." This includes quadrupling the tarif on electric vehicle (EV) imports to 100%, but extends it to other clean tech manufactured goods, such as solar panels and EV batteries, and also covers semiconductors, lithium batteries, steel and aluminium. This made the announcement bigger than just a tarif increase on Chinese EVs, which had already made it into the news last weekend.

It is true that the direct efects of these tarifs are likely to be small on both sides. For example, in the US, the total value of imports at stake is only \$18bn and China accounts for only 2% of EV imports into the US. Similarly, the direct exposure of Chinese EV producers to the US is limited, as only 16% of Chinese-made EVs are exported, and the US is not a major destination.<sup>5</sup>

However, the focus on the direct efects risks misses the broader trend of trade fragmentation due to geopolitical decoupling. Data from the Global Trade Alert database show that the number of new trade policies since 2008 that are trade restricting far outstrips the number of trade liberalising ones. Similarly, while the global trade to GDP ratio rose sharply in the 1990s and 2000s, it has been gradually declining since the Global Financial Crisis. Large trade realignments rarely happen overnight, but we see the administration's moves as an acceleration of the secular trend away from globalisation, with possibly significant long-term implications for global growth and inflation.

## What will Europe do?

It remains to be seen to what extent the EU will follow suit and how China will respond to both. The results of the EU's investigation into China's EVs are due by 4 July at the latest. The tarif by the US on Chinese EVs will only have increased the pressure on the EU, as an efectively closed US market implies even more pressure for Chinese EV exports to be diverted to Europe. Within the EU, the countries that produce predominantly inexpensive smaller cars – France and Italy – fear Chinese imports the most, pressing for high EU tarifs. In contrast, Germany's higher-end car producers oppose any such tarifs, as they fear the potential retaliation for their exports into China more than the competition from cheaper low-end Chinese EVs at home. Most observers expect some compromise, whereby the EU imposes some smaller tarifs (15-30% according to one think tank),<sup>6</sup>  but the stakes for Europe are high and the lobbying in Brussels is likely to be intense.

Moreover, Europe will have to consider the threat of potential tarifs against European cars – EVs or others – in case of a new Trump administration in 2025. It is too early and the uncertainties too high for markets to respond at this point, but it is not impossible that an escalating tit-fortat tarif war could become a relevant driver for markets in the not-so-distant future.

## Thematic

## U.S. Energy Services & Technology (Pos)

## U.S. Ofshore Energy Services: Upgrade XPRO to OW, Downgrade OII to UW

Expro is uniquely positioned vs. the other ofshore SMID caps with moats around its two core business lines – tubular running services (TRS) and subsea well testing. We believe there is upside to '25 estimates as higher TRS revenue from deepwater rig activity and technology upsell drives margin expansion. Well testing (used in all phases of the well lifecycle including exploration, development, production) should also benefit on higher exploration activity by the Majors, a ramp-up in ofshore FIDs, and operators looking to extend the life of existing assets/ production. We raise our PT to \$27 (from \$18) based on 7.0x our '25 EBITDA estimate (from 5.0x previously) and upgrade our rating to OW. OII's mid-single-digit growth guidance this year ("more than 5%") is a function of the defensive nature of its portfolio – something we expect will be dificult to break out of even as ofshore upstream spending is poised to increase double digits over the next several years. A number of disparate businesses comprise the Energy segments (\~80% of revenue), all with low growth profiles with the exception of the ROVs business (\~25% of revenue), which has seen pricing increase to multi-year highs. With limited earnings visibility and no medium-term targets from management, we believe FCF generation will remain limited going forward. We lower our PT to \$21 (from \$22) based on 6.0x our '25 EBITDA estimate (multiple unchanged) and downgrade our rating to UW.
