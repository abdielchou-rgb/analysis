# Global Portfolio Manager's Digest Who Has the Power?

We provide context and perspective on research across regions and asset classes, this week highlighting: our extensive analysis of the imminent AI energy needs as adoption increases; our dive into China's cost advantage for producing EVs; and our mid-year thoughts on European interest rates.

• Powering AI Demand: We attempted to sanity check potential U.S. data center power needs based on 1) what is implied by NVIDIA consensus revenue, and 2) disclosure on utility contractual visibility for Northern Virginia. Taking a step back, following decades of no-to-low U.S. electricity demand growth, AI adoption is leading to increasing U.S. electricity demand forecasts. While a number of top-down forecasts suggest that data centers could reach up to 9% of U.S. electricity demand by 2030, these forecasts have raised more questions than answers due to a lack of underlying assumptions. Specifically, while many investors cite the IEA's estimate that DCs already account for \~4% of U.S. electricity demand, we believe the IEA overstates current U.S. demand at \~200 TWh vs. our \~150 TWh estimate. Further, based on implied GPU chip sales from NVIDIA consensus revenue and power required per GPU derived from NVIDIA disclosure, we calculate \~12 GW / 90 TWh from AI alone in the U.S. by the end of 2027. NVIDIA's market cap suggests this is just the start. And lastly, we note that Northern Virginia is by far the largest U.S. data center market and has disclosed "high confidence" contractual visibility for >15 GW of regional data capacity by 2030. Assuming it holds its 20-30% share, this implies total U.S. data center capacity of \~50-75 GW / 380-560 TWh by 2030, or 9-13% of current U.S. electricity demand.

• Mid-Year Thoughts on European Interest Rates: As we approach mid-year, we summarized our key views for European interest rates. EUR yields have spent the first half of the year drifting higher as rate expectations were repriced. On an absolute basis, given the ECB's policy narrative and the inflation dynamics despite mixed growth, we think EUR duration is unlikely to be a source of bullish impulses for global duration. On EGBs, French politics should drive near-term price action, but rate cuts and relatively light peripheral net supply could provide support in 2H. Medium-term fiscal risks argue for caution toward French, Italian, and Belgian bonds, but we see pockets of value in the Spanish, Portuguese, Finnish, and Austrian markets. Specific to the UK, Gilts may prove an unexpected source of stability in 2H24. The combination of central bank easing, stable politics, and very cautious fiscal policy should systematically reduce term premium and support cross-market outperformance. And lastly, European inflation markets have been range-bound in 1H24. To that end, without a major input cost shock, a slow grind lower in HICPx forwards could continue, with RPI risk premium compression potentially more vigorous. With this in mind, we are cautiously long UK real yields and neutral on the EA given a better balance of political and fiscal risks.

## China NEV Cost Advantage Appears Structural and Sustainable

Jiong Shao, CFA, BCI, US | Lian Xiu (Roger) Duan, BCI, US | Xinyao Song, BCI, US | Dan Levy, BCI, US | Trevor "TL" Young, BCI, US | Josh Cho, BCI, US | Daniel Lai, BCI, US | Henning Cosman, Barclays, UK | Erwann Dagorne, BBI, Paris | Arya Ghassemieh, Barclays, UK

Excerpted from China Technology: China NEV Cost Advantage Appears Structural and Sustainable, published on June 26, 2024

In this report, we have estimated BOM for several leading Chinese NEV models and concluded that there appear to be some structural reasons behind lower costs for Chinese NEVs.

Structural reasons behind lower costs for Chinese NEVs include wider adoption of LFP batteries and China's dominance in making LFP batteries; tarifs imposed on these batteries by the West make NEV manufacturing costs higher for Western auto makers. We generally believe Chinese NEV makers have roughly about a 40% cost advantage over their Western peers.

Other reasons for lower manufacturing costs for NEVs in China include a well-developed domestic supply chain and willingness for domestic suppliers to accept lower prices given the imbalance of power between NEV makers, especially the larger ones, and parts suppliers. As we have been predicting a rapid consolidation among Chinese NEV makers in the next 3-5 years, we consider similar consolidation should happen in the supply chain, although not to the same degree as OEMs would likely prefer multiple suppliers to keep component costs competitive among suppliers.

In addition to industry-wide structural reasons for lower costs, BYD also enjoys the benefits of vertical integration by making its own batteries and a majority of components & semis used in its cars, as well as enormous economies of scale (by selling over 300,000 units per month). As a result, BYD has the highest NEV vehicle gross margins in China if not in the world, and the company continues to plow its profits back into R&D to extend its lead over peers. For example, its soon-to-be-launched LFP batteries should have improved performance and its recently launched DM 5.0 plug-in hybrid technologies should extend the driving range to at least 1,300 miles even when batteries are not charged ahead of time.

In terms of the threat posed by Chinese NEV makers to their Western peers, we believe it is clear and present, and hard to alleviate. As diferent countries have their own objectives and agendas (Italy versus Germany, for example), it is hard for them to come up with an EU bloc-wide solution. And most economists don't believe tarifs or regular government intervention is a solution except in rare cases. But given the significant cost advantages highlighted earlier, even imposing a 20-40% tarif may not be enough to ofset those cost advantages, and in the meantime consumers in those countries would be forced by their own governments to pay much higher prices for NEVs.

## Three Ps in a pod

Rohan Khanna, Barclays, UK | Max Kitson, Barclays, UK | Alessandro Di Spirito, Barclays, UK | Moyeen Islam, Barclays, UK | Charley Chau, Barclays, UK | Maria Chiara Russo, Barclays, UK | Iaroslav Shelepko, Barclays, UK

Excerpted from European Interest Rates: Mid-year thoughts: Three Ps in a pod, published on June 26, 2024

We summarise our key views across EUR/UK front-end and duration, EGB spreads, curves, swap spreads, inflation, and volatility markets.

The outlook for 2024 was promising at the start of the year, as the sharp fixed income rally in the last two months of 2023 salvaged positive total returns for DM bonds after the annus horribilis that was 2022. Much to the market's disappointment, however, the first half of this year has been spent agonising over the start, cadence and destination of policy normalisation cycles of the various DM central banks and to what extent the Fed's policy choices would influence these decisions. Central banks, supported to some extent by macro data, have worked hard to keep a lid on the easing narrative in a bid to stop the market running away with pricing a protracted easing cycle – that is, despite the SNB, Riksbank, the ECB and the BoC having dialled back policy restrictiveness ahead of the Fed. Simply put, there are still more questions than answers around how far central bank policy normalisation can extend, with political uncertainty a new variable in the mix.

In the latest Global Outlook, our economists' baseline is for steady global growth, uneven but continued progress on inflation, and a gradual easing cycle, despite rising political risks. They expect the world economy to stay resilient and grow 3.1%, just below last year’s 3.2%. The 'last mile' on inflation has been a bumpy process across the West. Services inflation has been sticky on both sides of the Atlantic. The uneven progress on inflation means central bank easing is likely to be slow this year, with the Fed cutting just once, the ECB two more times and the BoE three times, on our estimates. Fiscal risks remain in focus, with France in the spotlight as a new parliament takes shape, and Italy risks resurfacing as the 2025 budget plans are due to be discussed in the autumn, with the shadow of the US presidential election outcome also looming large.

The equity risk premium (ERP) is lower than ever, but risk premia have been falling across the rates and credit landscape as well. French elections: Round 1

Emmanuel Cau, CFA, Barclays, UK | Matthew Joyce, CFA, BBI, Paris | Magesh Kumar Chandrasekaran, CFA, Barclays, UK | Emmanuel Makonga, Barclays, UK | Arihanth Bohra Jain, Barclays, UK

Excerpted from Equity Market Review - French elections: Round 1, published on June 28, 2024

Markets are likely pricing in a hung parliament in France already, consistent with the polls. So the possibility of an absolute RN majority, or a better showing for the far left, would likely be risk of. A stronger-than-expected showing for Macron's party would be risk-on, if it raises the chances of a centrist coalition.

France goes to the polls and into the unknown. The first round of the French legislative election will take place this Sunday, 30 June. Much has been written about it since President Macron called the snap election three weeks ago, but little has changed. Polls have consistently been putting the far right Rassemblement National party ahead, with \~34% of the votes, followed by the left Front Populaire coalition (\~27%) and then by Macron's centrist Ensemble ruling coalition, at \~20% (Figure 2). However, given France's unique two-vote, but first-past-thepost voting system, there is still considerable room for the actual number of Parliament seats per party to difer from a simple translation from poll percentages, due to two main factors: 1) Turnout will be an important factor to watch on Sunday - the higher the turnout the more moderate the outcome, in our view. There is some evidence that turnout will be high, and we note that the polls have slightly improved for Macron's party in the last few days; 2) Tactical voting after constituencies know which candidates have advanced to the second round could further muddy the process of translating polls into Parliament seats. If three candidates are through the 1st round (only 12.5% of the vote is required to to get to 2nd round), one hard-left, one hard-right and one centrist, we would consider the outcome highly uncertain. In some cases voters may welcome having a centrist candidate, as they may prefer to cast their vote for a centrist rather than for either extreme. In other cases a three-horse race may simply end up splitting the anti-RN vote and making the outcome even less clear. If, on the other hand, each seat is only a two-horse race between hard-left and hard-right, with no centrist candidate available, it will force many voters to choose one or the other. With so many variables to contemplate, it is very dificult to accurately predict the likely breakdown of actual seats in Parliament. But as it stands, either a hung parliament with no party having an absolute majority, or a cohabitation, appear to be the most likely outcomes.

Five main potential scenarios. 1) If the results of the first round confirm current polls, we would expect a fairly muted market reaction on Monday. A hung parliament is likely priced in already, so may not necessarily bring much more near-term downside for risk markets, although it may not bring much upside either. Our assumption is that it would most likely result in a technocrat government that would try to maintain fiscal discipline (but it would be suboptimal for political stability compared to the current situation). 2) A higher score for the RN that increases the chances of the party getting an absolute majority at the 2nd round would likely be risk-of initially (equities and eur/usd down, OAT spreads up) in our view, given concerns about fiscal credibility, EU fragmentation and a potentially confrontational cohabitation with President Macron. However, it is possible that RN may seek to reassure markets by backtracking on some of their most controversial economic policies in order to avoid a crisis. We think it is likely that they would aim to stay in power for the long run and build credibility to win the Presidential election in 2027. 3) A more favourable outcome for markets would be a relative RN majority that gives them a chance to form a coalition with some LR dissidents. Investors may see it as similar to the situation in Italy, which has been pretty stable since Meloni became PM. 4) The worst scenario from a market standpoint, in our view, would be if the far left scores much better than expected, given their anti-business and fiscally loose agenda. We would expect a sharp widening in spread, as well as much lower eur/usd and equity markets. 5) A higher turnout at the first round could bode well for Macron and give him a chance to form a coalition with centrists from the left and the right. This would maintain the status quo and be the most positive outcome for markets, likely to retrace most of their losses of the past three weeks. With many socialist MPs unwilling to endorse policies from the far left, and a majority of LR candidates against a coalition with RN, alliances could shift towards the centre into the second round.

## Investment grade lags

Bradley Rogof, BCI, US | Dominique Toublan, BCI, US

Excerpted from US Credit Alpha, published on June 28, 2024

Spreads have been soft, but higher quality is underperforming. Single-A industrials are lagging the most, and BBs are faring the best. We recommend various reversal trades in cash and CDX. We also revise our issuance forecasts across the credit markets. We expect issuance to slow but remain sizable in 2H.

US investment grade cash has underperformed almost everything, even Europe. IG has underperformed HY in cash. The IG index is close to its widest level since March, at 94bp. The index is 9bp wider on the month and is trading 2bp wide to its YTD average. At the same time, the HY cash index is unchanged MTD at 308bp and is trading 5bp below its YTD average.

IG cash has also underperformed CDX IG and versus Europe. The basis between the IG 1-10y cash index has widened by 6bp MTD and is now at 30bp. That corresponds to a 3bp underperformance, adjusting for beta. Furthermore, US IG is at YTD wides versus EUR (hedged). In swapped terms, USD bonds are trading 8bp cheap to EUR bonds (+4bp w/w). This diferential is using issuer-matched pairs.

Finally, IG has also massively underperformed equities. CDX IG has gone from trading in line with SPX to trading more than 3x standard deviations wide. Equally, CDX IG implied volatility has risen significantly versus SPX implied volatility.

At a more granular level, single-A industrials are lagging the most and BBs are faring the best. Compression has been a major theme in the past couple of weeks. Higher-rated industrials are sufering the most. The BBB/A basis is at 40bp, 9bp tighter YTD and the narrowest since 2007. The spread ratio is also quite compressed, at 1.57x, from 1.75x at YE23. This is the lowest BBB/A ratio in 10 years outside of a short period in 2022.

BBs are doing even better than BBBs. We find that basis is too compressed and recommend a basket trade to express decompression in BBs tight to BBBs once again. The duration-adjusted basis is at 73bp, -7bp on the month, and the spread ratio is at 1.73x (-0.24x MTD). Both are at or close to their lowest levels since early 2020.

However, CCCs have decompressed meaningfully versus BBs and Bs, approaching their widest level since February, near 800bp. At 2.87x, this is the highest the CCC/B ratio has ever been (it reached 2.85x in March 2002 and 2.83x in January 2020).

Altogether, this implies that investors are feeling comfortable, but selective, about credit risk. Although recent economic data have surprised to the downside, the secondary market action indicates that investors are not worried about a severe negative turn in the credit cycle. This is corroborated by conversations with clients. However, many did turn marginally more cautious in the past couple of weeks, as indicated our Capitulation and Complacency Signals. We believe investors have been reacting more to the lower Treasury yields than to the underwhelming recent economic data, which came in the weakest versus expectations in about two years.

We update our issuance forecasts across credit markets. We expect supply to remain sizable in 2H24. Issuance has surprised to the upside this year. We expect it to continue to be on the heavy side in IG, HY, and LL. For IG, we forecast \$495bn of issuance in 2H, compared with \$805bn YTD, for a total of \$1.3trn in 2024. In HY, we increase our FY24 issuance forecast to \$280-300bn, up from our prior forecast of \$200-230bn, but a slowdown from the YTD pace (\$160bn YTD). In loans, we project FY24 supply at \$380-400bn (ex-repricings), driven by continued refinancing needs. YTD, \$300bn of par has repriced, and 2H24 could see an incremental \$250bn of volume.

The near-term events calendar is heavy and might be a source of volatility. The French elections this weekend and next are probably the event with the most disruption potential (see RN consolidates its lead for our European economists' most recent discussion of the French elections). But that's not all. PCE and consumer spending will be out this Friday, and ISM manufacturing and services, together with the jobs report, will be published next week.

We still expect spreads to be range-bound around the current levels. We expect economic growth to be steady, inflation to be sticky, and Treasury yields to increase. This implies a favorable backdrop for credit fundamentals. At the same time, we expect global demand for credit to remain robust in these conditions and easily absorb the issuance we expect for the rest of the year.

## Europe

## Looking towards end-2024

Søren Willemann, Barclays, UK

Excerpted from European Credit Alpha, published on June 27, 2024

We see markets performing strongly for the rest of 2024, aided by improving economic growth, steady easing by the ECB and still-strong demand. We discuss our H2 views across credit – IG, covereds, hybrids, CDS.

It was all going so well. For the first five months of the year European IG credit generally traded well (Figure 11), and justifiably so: risks of a recession had fallen as the economic situation continued to improve, while ECB easing remained on the table. High all-in yields continued to drive demand. Barring a sudden pick-up in idiosyncratic risk, credit markets have been, for the most part, frustratingly dull.

The unexpected announcement of snap French elections shook this up with markets focussed on the implications of either a tail-outcome of the far-left gaining power or how well RN could 'cohabitate' with Macron ahead of the first round on Sunday, 29 June (see here for our more detailed views on France and European credit). Polls continue to point to an uneasy 'cohabitation' between Macron and RN which could cause a short-term relief rally in credit and refocus attention on improving growth, ECB on a gentle easing path, and demand remaining strong. We think any tangible economic downside from the French election in this scenario is likely to come to fruition only much later, arguably not in 2024.

In this week's Alpha, we refresh our views across IG credit, hybrids, covered bonds and CDS. Summarizing our spread views for the base case and for a French tail-outcome in Figure 12, our key views are:

• In IG, we see spreads tighter still – at 95bp by end-2024, vs 121bp currently, aided by the improving macroeconomic backdrop and continued demand strength, helped by a slow easing cycle keeping medium-term all-in yields high. We retain our Overweight rating on Banks, Underweight rating on Real Estate and favour the 5yr point.

• In covered bonds, H2 supply should be slower and combined with likely rising NICs should support performance in the secondary market. We like covered bonds up to 7yr maturity.

• Within hybrids, our simplistic view is 'anything but LT2s': within banks, we prefer AT1s given valuations and low extension risk. But looking across hybrids, LT2s screen tight also to insurance T2s and to corporate hybrids. This makes us see LT2s as a carry trade, at best. Not least given the spectre of French political risk spilling into a confrontation with the EU, this makes us prefer insurance LT2s and corporate hybrids. AT1s remain wide to Bs, and absent a 'French tail', increasing idiosyncratic risk in HY should allow AT1s to compress to B-rated credit, and also to LT2s.

• In CDS, we see some tightening potential in a 'cohabitation' scenario, but Main could underperform cash. SenFin-Main could compress in that case but it currently has positive convexity for a French tail-outcome. We would expect Xover to re-de-compress relative to Main.

We see three principal sources of risk to European credit markets:

1. US rate volatility. From a European perspective, US rates could go higher for a variety of reasons: a Republican sweep allowing for ever-more expansive fiscal policy, inflation surprising to the upside again, or simply UST supply upside surprises. Regardless of the source, this could hurt European credit, both in terms of IG credit's empirically strong links to rate vol, but also to an increase in refinancing risk for more firms in HY. With demand for IG likely to improve with (somewhat) higher all-in yields, we see higher rates as a decompression event between HY and IG.

2. China. We see China as a driver of European risk both in terms of economic downside (with Europe as an exporter to China), and in terms of a risk of EV competition and tarif escalation. Both drivers support our Underweight ratings for both IG and HY Autos.

3. France. Polls indicate the two most probable outcomes as either a hung parliament or a 'cohabitation' scenario, with the tail risk being a left-wing majority which could lead to concerns around fiscal discipline (see Between a rock and a hard place). In any outcome, we think risks of renewed political downside could linger: either in the shape of Macron resigning as President, or in terms of heightened friction with the EC. This risk is arguably a challenge to the consensus overweight on banks, and is also reflected in our preference for corporate and insurance hybrids over bank LT2s.

## It's the elections, stupid

Christian Keller, Barclays, UK | Akash Utsav, Barclays, UK

Excerpted from Global Economics Weekly, published on June 28, 2024

Election uncertainty is weighing on the economic outlook, as recent data show signs of softness. Elections in France and the UK, as well as developments in Biden's candidacy, will be a market focus. US labour market, final PMIs, and euro area inflation should provide more clues about growth and prospects for rate cuts.

## Elections dominate

"It's the economy, stupid" is a famous phrase coined by Bill Clinton's advisor James Carville during Clinton's successful presidential campaign in 1992. However, a generation later, this saying no longer rings true. If economic variables such as growth and employment mattered most for voters, the incumbent US president should have a comfortable lead in the polls, Brexit should have been rejected, and the ongoing shift of European voters away from the political centre would be at odds with Europe's record-low unemployment. Whatever the reasons, the dominance of economics as the decisive factor in elections seems to have given way to the opposite: in a relative benign economic environment of low unemployment, decent wage growth and slowing inflation, it is elections that dominate the economic outlook rather than the other way around. James Carville's advice to today's economic forecasters could well be: It's the elections, stupid!

## France: Faites votre choix!

The first round of the French legislative elections will take place this Sunday (30 June). The latest opinion polls continue to show Mrs Le Pen's RN and its allies in the lead with c.36% of the voting intentions and being the main winners of the first round. However, owing mainly to the specific features of the French two-round voting system, the uncertainties about seat projections for the new National Assembly remain elevated.

Recent polls indicate that turnout will be higher than in previous legislative elections, and we expect the number of potential three-way races to increase. Much will depend on the approach adopted by President Macron's coalition (Ensemble) in the constituencies where it comes in third place but qualifies for the second round. The majority of left-wing parties are calling for the renewal of the Republican Front and the withdrawal of the third-placed candidate (who, in view of the polls, is likely to be centrist). But Ensemble has yet to take a clear stance on the question, particularly in the case where a possible withdrawal could benefit a candidate from Melenchon's LFI.

That said, while three-way races would likely end up favouring the RN, it is not clear at this stage whether the withdrawal of a centrist candidate would be suficient to prevent the election of the RN candidate in a contest against the left. As the upper bound of the latest projections already suggests, we believe that an absolute majority for the RN and its allies cannot be ruled out and continue to see the two most likely outcomes as a hung parliament with no immediate absolute majority and a National Rally cohabitation government.

## UK: Not a close call

The UK general election takes place next Thursday (4 July), with votes counted overnight into Friday. In contrast to France, the outcome seems much less uncertain: polls and seat projections suggest that Labour are comfortably on course to win a majority and form a government. Most recent seat projections put the majority at between 90 and 200 seats.

Our base case is for around a 100-seat majority, which would allow the party to deliver on its policy agenda as currently stated, albeit in a relatively cautious way. We would expect a focus on continued fiscal prudence and gradual movement towards closer alignment with the EU. We believe this would be positive for UK growth, consistent with our outlook for a continued gradual recovery this year accelerating into next, continued disinflation and a cumulative c.150bp of rate cuts by the BoE between August 2024 and August 2025.

We also consider how the scenarios of i) a stronger Labour majority, ii) a larger presence of the right wing Reform party, or iii) a hung parliament, might afect key policy areas (read Election night: What you need to know, 27 June 2024). However, with Labour still likely to have significant influence, we see a broadly similar direction of economic travel across all three.

## China: No election, but a 'Plenum

China does not do elections, but rather Politburo meetings and Plena. At this week's Politburo meeting, China announced that the much-awaited Third Plenum will be held 15-18 July. ## US data mixed, but resilience remains broadly intact

Incoming economic data have been mixed but overall still portray a resilient economy. The third estimate for Q1 24 GDP was revised up 0.1pp, to 1.4%q/q saar in line with our expectations. Upgrades to business fixed investment, inventories and net exports partly ofset the larger-thanexpected downward revisions to real personal consumption spending (PCE) estimates (to 1.5%q./q saar from 2% earlier).

Incoming monthly estimates for May suggest that activity likely accelerated somewhat in the current quarter. Real PCE rebounded 0.3% m/m, with goods spending bouncing back following a classic payback efect from Amazon's "Big Spring Sale" event in March. Smoothing through distortions from monthly fluctuations in the goods category, PCE was up somewhat faster than earlier this year, and in line with the widely anticipated slowing from the unsustainable pace in late 2023. Meanwhile, other components appear to have picked up the slack, with equipment investment poised for a double-digit gain in Q2 even after accounting for this week's soft May estimates of capital goods shipments and May estimates pointing to an upsurge in inventory investment. All told, we are tracking a 2.1% q/q saar increase in GDP in the current quarter.

We expect next week's dataflow to continue to downplay risks of a significant deterioration in activity. In the June employment release, we expect nonfarm payroll employment to moderate from 272k to 200k, with the workweek holding steady at 34.3 hours and average hourly earnings returning to their recent trend of 0.3% m/m (3.9% y/y) following a May increase. This should imply an increase in payroll income in the vicinity of 0.4-0.5% m/m, providing another round of support to consumers. On the household side, we expect the unemployment rate to continue to round up to 4.0%, at the upper end of the 3.9-4.0% range that has prevailed since February. Advance indicators from indeed.com suggest that job openings likely ticked up to 8.3mn in May. Finally, we look for little meaningful change to the ISM readings for manufacturing (49.0) and services (53.1), with both remaining at the upper end of ranges that have prevailed since late 2022.

## China's fiscal data reveal weak fundamentals

Revenue estimates in China's latest fiscal data (reported by MoF) provide indirect evidence that demand conditions remain on weak footing. Revenues from individual income tax remained in contraction territory for the fifteenth consecutive month, pointing to a challenging labour market. Similarly, tax revenue on corporate income in May also contracted for the first time this year. With subdued domestic demand exacerbating overcapacity issues, we think corporates are finding it dificult to pass on their costs to consumers and raise retail prices.

Overall, we think the contracting fiscal revenue data reflect the slowing economy, with a deteriorated labour market, a lackluster consumption recovery, a prolonged housing market contraction, and prolonged PPI deflationary pressure.

Next week, we expect the June NBS manufacturing PMI to remain in contraction, at 49.4 versus 49.5 in May. We note that more than half of the industries we track posted a drop in operation rates in June, including textiles, autos, chemical PVC, and petroleum/asphalt equipment, while rates in the mining & smelting sectors improved.

## Central banks to the rescue?

## US inflation dynamics allow for September cut

PCE price inflation was modest in May, in line with earlier indications from the CPI and PPI. The headline deflator was flat, and the core rose just 0.08% m/m (2.6% y/y) on the heels of an upwardly revised 0.26% m/m in April. The downswing may exaggerate the disinflationary dynamics a bit, as the moderation was driven by non-housing core services ("supercore"), which in turn reflect declines in volatile components (eg, transportation and financial services). These are unlikely to be sustained, and we expect core PCE to accelerate to about 0.2% m/m in June as supercore firms. That said, this would still be suficiently soft to sustain our baseline of a September Fed cut, even with FOMC communications providing little reason to think that it might ease on the grounds of sustaining full employment.

## Euro area inflation's path towards 2% continues...

In Europe, the June preliminary inflation data released for France, Spain, and Italy were in line with our and consensus expectations. While national data showed that core inflation was just a touch weaker in France and a touch stronger in Spain than we expected, overall we do not

detect major surprises. Based on these releases and our forecasts for German inflation (to be released on 1 July), our trackers for euro area headline and core inflation did not change from our forecasts at the first decimal place. However, we see risk that headline inflation could be rounded to 2.4% y/y.

## ...but the ECB will not bail out fiscal adventurers

Next week, the ECB Forum on Central Banking will be held in Sintra (1-3 July). While this will not bring any pre-announcement for the July or September meeting, Sintra would be the appropriate venue for providing clarity on the interest rate path and the potential response of the ECB if there were to be any turmoil in the bond market related to the outcome of the French elections. On the latter, we think the ECB Governing Council will continue to ask governments to put in place policies in compliance with the European fiscal rules. The TPI will be activated only if countries are not in the excessive deficit procedure or, if they are, they take on board the European Commission's recommendations.
