## Market commentary

Since we last published a fortnight ago, the rates curve bear flattened, with 2yr yields up by 20bp, in response to the latest round of economic data and Fedspeak. On data, the January inflation report came in hotter than anticipated, with core CPI up by 0.4% (consensus 0.2%), marking the largest monthly gain since April 2023. Similarly, the PPI reading for January also witnessed firming, with the PPI ex-food and energy moving higher by 0.5% (see US Weekly Prospects, M. Feroli, 2/16/24). With respect to employment, the labor markets continue to demonstrate resiliency with initial claims surprising favorably, dropping from 213k to 201k during the week ending February 17, which is on the lower end of the spectrum when compared to recent months (see US: Initial claims beat expectations, D. Silver, 2/22/24). The strong reading on inflation and sustained labor strength have resultingly contributed to a significant decline in market rate expectations. Currently, OIS forwards are pricing in 82bp worth of total cuts for 2024, compared to 112bp two weeks ago and 169bp in mid-January, with the first potential cut now being pushed back to June (Figure 1).

Additionally, the minutes to the January FOMC meeting skewed hawkish as most participants noted the risks of easing too quickly. While the risks to achieving their employment and inflation goals are coming into better balance, they are still highly attentive to inflation risks, such that the Fed will be patient on cutting rates. Overall, the minutes did not reveal any more information than what we already knew, repeating themes already mentioned in the statement and press conference (see Just a little patience, M. Feroli, 2/21/24). This week<sup>’</sup>s Fedspeak echoed a similar sentiment, pushing back against rate cuts in the immediate future (Figure 2).

Against this backdrop, markets are now pricing in a less dovish path than our forecast—that is, a total of 125bp this year, with the first cut in June. At the same time, our Treasury strategists note that positions are no longer skewed to the long side, which makes the case that yields have room to decline from current levels in the coming months. That being said, it<sup>’</sup>s worth noting that the latest round of data, given its strength, has introduced uncertainty not only with respect to the timing of rate cuts but also their magnitude. As such, it<sup>’</sup>s likely Treasury yields will remain elevated with a higher risk premium, and it will take a few months of data in order for current elevated uncertainty to disappear. Moreover, our expectations for the timing of the start to QT tapering have been pushed to June (see below). As a result, our Treasuries strategists have revised their year-end 2-year Treasury forecast to 3.80% (versus 3.25% previously), and year-end 10-year target to 3.80% (from 3.65%), implying less steepening than previously expected (see Revising our interest rate forecasts, J. Barry, 2/22/24) (Figure 3).

## Pushing back on timing of QT’s taper and end; ON RRP balance to stall out in coming weeks

As noted earlier, the discussion on QT in the January FOMC minutes was brief. This seems to suggest that the Fed is in no hurry to start the QT taper process anytime soon, even if it<sup>’</sup>s prudent on the part of the Fed to begin discussions about its eventuality. To be sure, as the Fed noted, conditions in the short-term funding markets remained stable over the past few weeks and continue to remain so. The modest upward pressure SOFR experienced over the November and December month-ends did not resurface at the end of January. In fact, SOFR has trended lower this month as increased specialness in front-end Treasuries likely dragged the benchmark rate lower, similar to what occurred in 2022 (Figure 4The incrasd pl fot- u kyg SOFRw m,   20).

Furthermore, it<sup>’</sup>s likely that the rapid decline in ON RRP balances will stall out over the coming weeks in response to net T-bill supply turning negative in late March/early April and MMF AUMs remaining elevated (Figure 5). Both factors bias the need for increased ON RRP usage, which suggests balances might linger around \$500bn in the coming months. And while a more steady ON RRP balance would suggest that QT will predominantly be coming out of reserves, there is still \$3.5tn of reserves in the banking system, which we think is currently more than ample.

As such, we now expect the Fed to announce a reduction in the monthly caps at the June meeting, instead of March. Reflecting the later QT taper start date, Figure 6 shows our projections for the Fed<sup>’</sup>s balance sheet. In particular, we estimate ON RRP balances will hit \$300bn in December, a threshold we believe the Fed wants to maintain to effect smooth functioning in the money markets. If we<sup>’</sup>re right, this means QT will continue through 2024, leaving \$3tn of reserves in the banking system by year-end (see Interest Rate Derivatives).

One risk to the above outcome is the April tax date. Given tight labor markets and strong asset performance in 2023, those dynamics foreshadow another large TGA surge, similar to what we saw in 2022, when TGA balances declined \~\$150bn heading into April 18 and then surged \~\$400bn after the filing date, and took about a month to normalize (Figure 7). Given a relatively elevated ON RRP balance in the near-term and the possibility that TGA balances might surge again this year, this implies a larger drain in reserves during this time period. Indeed, we<sup>’</sup>re forecasting reserves to fall from \~\$3.5tn to \~\$3.3tn in April, with the decline likely isolated to the week of April 15. The availability of the SRF and a positive balance at the ON RRP should mitigate any fallout, but it bears watching just how close we<sup>’</sup>re getting to the lowest comfortable level of reserves.
