A second consecutive federal surplus looks likely. The Federal Government’s 2023-24 Budget update revised the underlying cash deficit estimate to just \$1.1bn, from a deficit of \$13.9bn estimated in the Budget. Prior to 2022-23, Australia’s budget had been in deficit for 14 consecutive years. The government has indicated that recently announced changes to ‘Stage Three’ tax cuts scheduled for July “will have a net cost of \$1.3 billion over the forward estimates”, or less than \$400mn per annum. As such, the changes have no material bearing on our assessment of the budget position or AOFM borrowing programme.

Operating deficits persist in four of six states in 2023-24. NSW and VIC are on track to record five consecutive years of general government operating deficits. Each had recorded just two general government operating deficits across the 21 years prior to covid.

▪ AOFM issuance estimated at \$50bn for 2023-24. Revised down in early January from an initial estimate of \$75bn. Semi government issuance is set to outstrip AOFM issuance this year, we projected a total of \$85bn in 2023-24.

▪ All Australian states now have a stable rating outlook from S&P, and Australia remains one of only 9 countries with a AAA federal credit rating across all three major ratings agencies. Victoria’s credit rating is lowest among the state governments at AA / Stable, but we remain constructive on Victoria’s fiscal health. We would become less so if future budgets show a material upside blowout in debt from current projections. That would see Victoria's debt levels enter unprecedented territory under the prevailing fiscal framework.

The Federal Government could deliver another cash surplus in 2023-24 after the Budget update revised the deficit estimate to just \$1.1bn, from a deficit of \$13.9bn estimated in the Budget. Prior to 2022-23, Australia’s budget had been in deficit for 14 consecutive years.

Macroeconomic fluctuations often account for most of the risks associated with exposures to instruments that promise to pay fixed or variable interest rates. For an investor who is long fixed-rate instruments (thus receiving these cash flows), increases in interest rates naturally lift the discount factors applied to the expected cash flows and thus cause a loss The longer the maturity of a certair cash flow and the higher the move in the discount factor, the higher will be the loss. Upside surprises in inflation, fiscal funding needs, and FX depreciation rates are among the most common adverse macroeconomic shocks to receivers. But counterparty exposure, issuer creditworthiness, client segmentation, requlation (including changes in assets holding limits for different types of investors) changes in tax policies, currency convertibility (which may constrain currency conversion, repatriation of profits and/or liquidation of positions), and settlement issues related to local clearing houses are also important risk factors. The sensitivity of fixed-income instruments to macroeconomic shocks may be mitigated by indexing the contracted cash flows to inflation, to EX depreciation or to specifieo interest rates – these are common in emerging markets. The index fixings may – by construction – lag or mis-measure the actual move in the underlying variables they are intended to track. The choice of the proper fixing (or metric) is particularly important in swaps markets, where floating coupon rates (i.e., coupons indexed to a typically short-dated interest rate reference index) are exchanged for fixed coupons. Funding in a currency that differs from the currency in which coupons are denominated carries FX risk.
