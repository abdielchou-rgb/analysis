# China: A strong start in Jan-Feb activity

# Revising up current quarter growth to 6.6%q/q saar

Jan-Feb activity data came in stronger than expected, especially in production and manufacturing along with strong exports released earlier.

• On industrial production, we estimate average IP in Jan-Feb was 1.6% m/m sa higher than the December level

Regarding domestic demand, retail sales rose 5.5%oya, and on average were 2.4% m/m sa higher than the December level. Catering, telecommunication, sports and entertainment outperformed.

Fixed investment came in above expectations, rising 4.2%oya in Jan-Feb. Despite continuous weakness in real estate investment (which fell 9.0%oya), strong manufacturing investment (+9.4%oya) and steady infrastructure investment (+6.3%oya) jointly supported FAI activity.

Regarding labor market conditions, the nation-wide urban survey-based unemployment rate moved up to 5.3% in February (vs. 5.1% in December). Out-performance in the manufacturing sector does not seem to have generated the same paced increase in employment due to labor demand differences between manufacturing and service sectors.

In response to stronger-than-expected data in Jan-Feb, we revise up current quarter GDP growth to 6.6%q/q saar (previously: 5.5%q/q saar), or in yoy terms, at 4.6% (previously: 4.3%). However, continuous deflation pressure implies that nominal GDP growth will soften to 3.1%oya in the current quarter.

China<sup>’</sup>s Jan-Feb activity generally beat expectations. Industrial production rose 7.0%oya, much stronger than market expectations (consensus: 5.2%; J.P.Morgan: 4.8%). Based on our seasonal-adjustment process, in sequential terms, average IP in Jan-Feb was 1.6%m/m sa higher than in December. This is consistent with stronger-than-expected export data released earlier, and solid manufacturing activity released today (manufacturing investment rose 9.4%oya and industry-use electricity production rose 8.3%oya). Despite continuous weakness in real estate investment (-9.0%oya), strong manufacturing investment (9.4%) and steady infrastructure investment (6.3%oya) jointly contributed to better-than-expected FAI growth at 4.2%oya (consensus and J.P. Morgan: 3.2%). On the consumption side, retail sales rose 5.5%oya (consensus: 5.6%; J.P. Morgan: 4.5%), implying that average Jan-Feb retail sales were 2.4%m/m sa higher than the December level (based on our seasonal adjustment).

Overall, Jan-Feb activity data points at a strong start to the year. We revise up current quarter GDP growth to 6.6%q/q saar (previously: 5.5%), or in yoy term, to 4.6% (previously: 4.3%). Nonetheless, due to persistent deflation pressure (J.P.Morgan forecast of GDP deflator: -1.5%oya in 1Q24), nominal GDP growth will slow down to 3.1%oya in our revised forecast.

The direction of upside surprise for IP is not surprising, given strong export data released earlier (Jan-Feb average exports rose 7.5%m/m sa compared to the December level). Yet the magnitude of the upside surprise is still significant. It is further supported by strong manufacturing activity. Manufacturing investment rose 9.4%oya (vs. 6.5% in 2023), and industrial-use electricity production rose 8.3%oya. This was related to the recent policy priority to promote innovation and industry upgrade, especially in the manufacturing sector (developing advanced manufacturing, promoting upgrade and transformation of traditional manufacturing).

On the consumption side, the service production index rose 5.8%oya and retail sales rose 5.5%oya in Jan-Feb. Our estimates show that average Jan-Feb retail sales were 2.4%m/m sa higher than the December level. In breakdown, retail sales growth was led by catering (12.5%oya), telecommunications (16.2%oya), sports and entertainment (11.3%oya) and auto sales (8.7%oya). Online-retail sales rose 15.3%oya, and its share in total retail sales slipped to 22.4% (vs. 27.6% in December).

Consumption and service activity growth is in part due to LNY seasonality, and in part due to lingering reopening dividend as consumption recovery is still incomplete. For instance, average Jan-Feb retail sales in 2023 were 5.4%m/m sa higher than the December 2022 level, when the reopening effect was stronger. While household income expectations have stayed soft, consumption recovery has benefited from less spending on home purchase (new residential home sales declined 32.7%oya in value terms in Jan-Feb) and a smaller increase in household deposits (which rose 5.7 trillion yuan in Jan-Feb this year, compared to an increase of 7 trillion yuan during the same period last year), although the “value-for-money<sup>”</sup> consumption feature remains.

A cautious reading is the modest increase in unemployment rate. Survey-based unemployment rate rose to 5.3% in February (vs. 5.1% in December) and ticked up to 5.1% in 31 cities (vs. 5.0% in December). This is perhaps due to uneven policy support and industry developments. Manufacturing sector has out-performed, but does not seem to create new jobs at the same pace (due to differences in labor demand between manufacturing and service sectors). By contrast, some service sectors (e.g. education, internet platforms, financial services, etc.) have been affected by regulatory headwinds in recent years, and employment outlook is still soft in these sectors although the worst period of regulatory headwinds might be behind us.

Not surprisingly, the weakest link in the economy remains the housing sector. Real estate investment declined 9.0%oya in Jan-Feb (vs. -9.6% in 2023). New residential home sales declined 24.8% in volume term and fell 32.7%oya in value term, and house price decline widened in Jan-Feb. Housing-related consumption items also stayed weak, e.g. construction and decoration (2.1%oya) and furniture (4.6%oya) sales.

## Some data issues in NBS statistics

While the strong activity in Jan-Feb is encouraging, there are some data issues in NBS statistics.

To start with, there are inconsistencies between aggregate and breakdown for some data series. For instance, the NBS reported that IP rose 7.0%oya in Jan-Feb, and in breakdown by ownership, IP for SOEs rose 5.8%oya, for joint-venture rose 7.3%, for foreign companies rose 6.2% and for private companies rose 6.5%. Despite of no weight information by ownership breakdown, the share of joint-venture companies is relatively small. Hence, aggregate IP growth seems higher than implied by breakdown data points.

Another example is the breakdown in FAI growth. The NBS reported aggregate FAI growth was 4.2%, real estate investment growth was -9.0%, and FAI excluding real estate rose 8.9% in Jan-Feb. This implied that the weight of real estate investment is 26.3%, which is too high given declining share of real estate investment in recent years. In other words, if the first two numbers are correct, it seems that FAI excluding real estate growth should be lower than 8.9% (though still decent growth at around 7% based on our estimates). Similarly, the NBS reported that private investment only rose 0.4%oya in Jan-Feb, but private investment excluding real estate rose 7.6%oya. While the latter data point (only released in recent months) wants to send a valid message that weak private investment is largely due to the collapse in housing market activity, we have problems to derive reasonable estimates of underlying assumption on private real estate investment (growth and its share in private investment).

But among various data issues, the more important issue is seasonally adjusted growth, which is critical to gauge the dynamics of key economic indicators. We have used TRAMO-SEATS seasonal adjustment incorporating China<sup>’</sup>s public holiday schedule (link). Prior to 2021, our SA outcome was largely consistent with the NBS SA results. But since 2022, our SA results have been very different from NBS SA results (for IP and retail sales series). While we admit our SA approach also has caveats, the NBS SA outcome looks fishy for two reasons.

First, the NBS SA series looks too smooth to be true, especially in 2022. China faced the shock from lockdown policy in 2022, first in Shanghai and later spread to most cities until a big turn in COVID-policy in December 2022. One would expect economic activity (IP and retail sales) was volatile (like as reflected in our SA results), instead, NBS SA series shows very stable growth since 2022.

Second, a statistics fact is that seasonal adjustment will affect monthly dynamics, but will not affect through-the-cycle change (i.e. 12-month change should roughly be the same for SA and NSA series). This is not the case for NBS statistics. For instance, retail sale level in December 2023 is 18.5% higher than December 2019 in NBS SA term (vs. 12.2% higher in NSA term), and IP level in December 2023 is 33.1% higher than December 2019 in NBS SA term (vs. 21.1% higher in NSA term). The NBS does not explain why the discrepancy will arise between NSA and SA series (maybe because of revision of history data but not reported).

## Revising up current quarter growth forecast

Stronger-than-expected activity in Jan-Feb led us to revise up current quarter growth forecast to 6.6%q/q saar (previously: 5.5%q/q saar). In yoy terms, current quarter real GDP growth will be 4.6% (previously: 4.3%oya). We maintain our growth forecast in the coming quarters unchanged (4.7%q/q saar in 2Q, 4.5% in 3Q and 4.1% in 4Q0. Our full-year GDP growth forecast now stands at 5.2% (previously: 4.9%).

Despite a strong start in real activity, deflator pressure persists. We expect GDP deflator will continue to be negative at -1.5%oya in the current quarter. Hence, nominal GDP growth will soften further to 3.1%oya (vs. 3.7%oya in 4Q23 and 3.5%oya in 3Q23). In other words, the pass-through from improved real activity to improved corporate earnings will still take time.

## Further details of Jan-Feb activity data

Industrial production came in stronger than expected, growing 7.0%oya in January - February (J.P. Morgan: 4.8%, consensus: 5.2%), compared to 6.8%oya growth in December. In sequential terms, we estimate that its Jan-Feb average is 1.6%m/m sa higher than December, more than offsetting the 0.8% m/m sa fall in December. Meanwhile, the NBS<sup>’</sup> SA estimates of average IP in Jan-Feb is 1.44% m/m sa higher than December level (vs. +0.52% m/m sa in December).

In terms of output of major products: 1) Electricity production rose 8.3%oya in Jan-Feb, compared to 8.0%oya in December. 2) Auto production unit rose 4.4%oya in Jan-Feb (vs. 24.5%oya in December). 3) Regarding machinery and equipment production, electric machinery equipment production grew 4.6%oya in Jan-Feb in value-added terms (vs. 10.1%oya in December), general machinery equipment production rose 4.1%oya (vs. +4.6%oya in December) and special machinery equipment production rose 2.0%oya (vs. +3.6%oya in December). 4) Regarding tech-related activity, production of communication, computer and other electronic equipment grew 14.6%oya in Jan-Feb (vs. 9.6%oya in December). 5) Regarding production of construction-related sectors, output of crude steel ticked up by 1.6%oya (vs. -14.9%oya in December), while cement production fell 1.6%oya (vs. -0.9%oya in December).

• Regarding service sector activity, the service production index rose 5.8%oya in Jan-Feb (vs. 8.5%oya in December), led by catering and accommodation (12.1%oya), as well as information transmission, software and IT (10.4%oya).

Regarding domestic demand conditions, nominal retail sales rose 5.5%oya in Jan-Feb (consensus: 5.6%; J.P. Morgan: 4.5%), compared to growth of 7.4 %oya in December. In sequential terms, we estimate retail sales Jan-Feb average was 2.4%m/m sa higher than December, following the 0.4% average monthly gain in 4Q23. For comparison, the NBS estimates retail sales were 0.19%m/m sa higher on average in Jan-Feb than the December level, following the 0.25% gain in December.

In the breakdown, auto sales (in nominal terms) rose 8.7%oya in Jan-Feb (vs. +4.0%oya in December), while retail sales ex-auto rose 5.2%oya (vs. +7.9%oya in December). In further details, catering expenses/restaurant spending grew 12.5%oya in Jan-Feb (vs. 30.0%oya in December), sales of petrol rose 5.0%oya (vs. 8.6%oya in December), sales of clothing rose 1.9%oya (vs. 26.0%oya in December). Besides, sales of jewelry grew 5.0%oya in Jan-Feb (vs. 29.4%oya in December), and sales of medicines rose 2.0%oya (vs. -18.0%oya in December). In addition, sales of household electronic appliances rose 5.7%oya in Jan-Feb (vs. -0.1%oya in December), and sales of information and communication equipment continued to show solid growth at 16.2%oya (vs. 11.0%oya in December).

Fixed asset investment also came in above expectations, growing 4.2%oya for Jan-Feb (J.P. Morgan and consensus: 3.2%), compared to growth of 3.0%oya growth in full-year 2023. In further breakdown: (1) manufacturing investment grew 9.4%oya in Jan-Feb (vs. 8.2%oya in December); (2) infrastructure investment grew 6.3%oya (vs. 6.8%oya in December); and (3) real estate investment remained sluggish 9.0%oya in Jan-Feb (vs. -12.4%oya in December). Besides, private investment inched up 0.4%oya in Jan-Feb (vs. +0.6%oya in December).

With regards to the housing market, residential floor space sold fell 28.6%oya in December (vs. -25.8%oya in December). In value terms, residential home sales fell 34.7%oya (vs. -25.6%oya in December). Residential floor space started fell 31.3%oya (vs. -13.3%oya in December).

Real estate developers raised 1.6 trillion yuan in Jan-Feb (down 24.1%oya), compared to 12.7 trillion yuan in full-year 2023 (down 14.4%oya). Among major funding sources, advanced payments from pre-sales fell 34.8% oya and accounted for 28.6% of total funds for Jan-Feb, mortgage payments fell 36.6%oya and accounted for 13.7% of total funds, self-raised funds fell 15.2%oya and accounted for 33.2% of total funds, while bank loans fell 10.3%oya and accounted for 19.4% of total fund.
