## United States

• Core CPI prices increased 0.3% in December, about in line with the average over the last year

• 4Q real GDP still tracking around a 2.0% annualized increase

• The FOMC may slow balance sheet normalization soon, but we still look for the first cut in June

• We expect next week<sup>’</sup>s December retail sales report to come in on the soft side

The highly-anticipated December CPI report didn<sup>’</sup>t deliver any big surprises. While the headline measure increased 0.3%, a tick firmer than expected, the ex-food and energy core category also increased 0.3%, which was in line with expectations. The core measure is now up 3.9% over a year ago, and is running up at a 3.3% annual rate over the past three months. Given this week<sup>’</sup>s CPI and PPI reports, we expect the December core PCE figure, reported in two weeks, will increase 0.21%, bringing the year-ago increase down to 3.0% from 3.2% in November. Core inflation continues to moderate, but its year-ago run rate is still well above the Fed<sup>’</sup>s 2% goal for PCE inflation.

In this instance we don<sup>’</sup>t see the case for the Fed to rush into rate cuts. Certainly as it heads into the late January meeting there is little suggesting the economy is weakening in a way that it should be biased to ease soon. If it doesn<sup>’</sup>t signal that bias in January then, barring some major shock, it<sup>’</sup>s very unlikely to cut in March. We are still comfortable looking for a first cut in June.

There was less news on growth this week and what little news we did receive didn<sup>’</sup>t change the picture. The trade and inventory data for November probably took out the modest downside risk we had around our 2.0% tracking of 4Q GDP. The initial claims data for the first week of the year were also favorable, coming in at a quite low 202,000. Next week brings a richer set of activity releases, including the December retail sales report.

## Better than 0.4%, not as good as 0.2%

As mentioned above, the December CPI increased 0.3%, a tick firmer than expected, lifting the year-ago increase to 3.4% from 3.1% in November. The ex-food and energy core CPI was also up 0.3% (Figure 1), in line with expectations, and the year-ago gain in that measure ticked down to 3.9% and on a six-month annualized basis the core CPI is up 3.2%.

Relative to our expectations, the upside surprise in the headline measure was driven by a firmer-than-expected 0.4% increase in energy prices, lifted by a strong 1.3% increase in electricity prices. ## No break in activity indicators

The nominal trade balance moved from -\$64.5bn in October to -\$63.2bn in November. Trade flows weakened in November, as nominal exports and imports (of both goods and services) both fell 1.9% that month; exports and imports also fell in volume terms for goods in November. In terms of the volume data in hand (which are available for goods only), the trade deficit for the first two months of 4Q is narrower than the 3Q average, and we think net exports are on track to provide a modest lift to GDP growth in 4Q.

Initial claims edged down from 203,000 to 202,000 during the week ending January 6. Continuing claims also moved down in the latest report, declining from 1.868mn to 1.834mn during the week ending December 30. The recent levels of filings have been low and signal continued strength in the labor market.

## Fed balance sheet back in focus

Last weekend Dallas Fed president Logan gave a speech backing up the mention in the most recent FOMC minutes indicating that the Committee will soon discuss when to slow the pace of balance sheet reduction. In a research note this week we discuss our new assumptions for balance sheet reduction plans, and believe they will announce a slower pace of balance sheet reduction at the March FOMC meeting.

The Fed will have another decision to make at the March meeting. The Bank Term Funding Program (BTFP) set up in the midst of last year<sup>’</sup>s regional banking crisis is set to expire in March absent a decision to extend the life of the facility. In recent weeks banks take-up of the program has increased (Figure 5). It<sup>’</sup>s not clear if the increase is due to renewed stress or banks taking advantage of the program<sup>’</sup>s favorable terms before it is shut down.

The December consumer price index (CPI) increased 0.3%, a tick firmer than expected, lifting the year-ago increase to 3.4% from 3.1% in November. The ex-food and energy core CPI was also up 0.3% (0.309%), in line with expectations, and the year-ago gain in that measure ticked down to 3.9%.

On a three- and six-month annualized basis the core CPI is up 3.3% and 3.2%, respectively. So the broad trend is one of core inflation continuing to gradually moderate from a very strong pace. While core inflation is continuing to trend in the right direction, we don<sup>’</sup>t see the Fed in a rush to cut rates. With all the key data now in hand, it<sup>’</sup>s hard to see how the January FOMC meeting would result in guidance to ease at the subsequent meeting in March (as markets apparently still expect). Perhaps the Committee could soften the guidance toward a neutral bias, but that would be more consistent with easing further on in the year. We continue to look for a first cut at the June meeting.

Relative to our expectations, the upside surprise in the headline measure was driven by a firmer-than-expected 0.4% increase in energy prices, lifted by a strong 1.3% increase in electricity prices. The food CPI was up 0.2%, in line with recent increases. Food away from home, a good measure of broad inflationary pressures, was up a tolerable 0.3%. Within the core measure, prices for core goods were flat last month. Both new (0.3%) and used (0.5%) vehicle prices were up last month, though we expect used vehicle prices should moderate in coming months. Core service prices were up 0.4% last month. Increases in the heavily weighted rent measures continue to moderate only very gradually. Tenants<sup>’</sup> rent was up 0.4% last month, a tick below the November gain, and owners<sup>’</sup> equivalent rent increased 0.5%, the same as the prior month. Super-core prices, core services ex-rents, were up 0.4% in December. Medical care service prices were up a firm 0.7% last month, supported by another gain in the CPI health insurance measure.
