# Morgan Stanley ReseARCH May 19, 2024 09:49 PM GMT China Economics Mid-Year Outlook | Asia Pacific Steady Yet Subdued

Real GDP growth has stabilized thanks to exports and manufacturing capex, but nominal GDP growth is likely to stay subdued at 4.5%Y in 2024 and 4.9%Y in 2025. The current policy approach of pushing manufacturing capex rather than boosting consumption is likely to mean deflationary pressure lingers.

Stabilizing real GDP growth from exports and manufacturing capex: We expect real GDP growth to reach 4.8% in 2024, before moderating to 4.5% in 2025. Resilient exports and manufacturing capex are likely to be the key growth drivers this year, backed by a global trade recovery and a policy push to upgrade supply chains, but both are likely to moderate next year amid rising trade tension and overcapacity issues. That said, gradual housing market stabilization in response to modest policy support may provide some cushion. Given still slow economic rebalancing efforts and fading "revenge spending," private consumption growth is likely to soften in 2024-25.

Modest policy easing underway: Beijing has made incremental progress in containing the risk of a debt-deflation loop, including a push for a faster budget rollout (urging an acceleration in government bond issuance, launch of the auto trade-in program), initial moves towards reining in overcapacity in the lithium battery market, and increasing efforts to support housing inventory digestion. We expect the augmented fiscal deficit to widen by 0.5ppt of GDP in both 2024 and 2025, though the focus may gradually shift from manufacturing upgrades this year to housing stabilization next year. Meanwhile, there could be a 25-50bps RRR cut in the next couple of months to facilitate government bond issuance, followed by a 20bps policy rate cut in 2H24.

But reflation has a long way to go: Despite the recent policy positives, only 20- 25% of desired reflation efforts have been fulfilled, in our view. Given that Beijing's focus has remained on pushing investment in manufacturing rather than boosting consumption, deflationary pressures would still linger. We thus expect the GDP deflator to stay subdued at -0.3% for 2024 (-0.5% in 2023) and rebound only modestly in 2025, to 0.4%, keeping nominal GDP growth subpar at <5%.

Risks to watch: In our downside scenario, the dual headwinds of faster LGFV and housing deleveraging and broad-based trade tension escalation would lead to an entrenched debt-deflation loop. In turn, real GDP growth would slow to 4.0%Y in 2024 and 3.2%Y in 2025, while the GDP deflator would slip to -1.2% and -2%, respectively. In our upside scenario, we assume more forceful policy efforts to rebalance the economy and digest housing inventories, bringing about more decisive reflation. This could lift real GDP growth to 5.3%Y in 2024 and 5.5%Y in 2025, and bring nominal growth back to desirable levels of 6-7%.

# Growth Stabilizing, Decisive Reflation Yet to Come

We expect China's real GDP growth to stabilize at 4.8% in 2024 from exports and manufacturing capex strength (vs. the 2022-23 average of 4.1%): Export volume growth is likely to stay robust at 8%Y in 2024 (versus 2%Y in 2023 and 10%Y in 1Q24), benefiting from global trade recovery. Domestically, manufacturing and green capex should remain strong, outweighing continued softness in construction activities – this means a modest acceleration in growth of fixed capital formation to 4.0%Y (versus 3.8%Y in 2023). However, private consumption growth is likely to slow to 4.8%Y (versus a 5.1% two-year CAGR in 2022-23) amid fading "revenge spending" and a soft job market.

Real growth could moderate to 4.5% in 2025: We cite a modest rise in trade tensions with the US and EU and a moderation in manufacturing investment amid overcapacity and geopolitical pushback. However, gradual stabilization in the housing market in response to incrementally stronger policy support could mitigate the headwinds.

## Exports a key support to growth in 2024; rising trade tensions a concern

We expect volume growth is likely to remain robust at 8%Y in 2024 (versus 2%Y in 2023 and 10%Y in 1Q24), benefiting from the global trade recovery and lower export prices: Our global economics team forecasts resilience in global real GDP growth at 3.1%Y in 2024/25 (3.2% in 2023), while global trade would accelerate slightly, with real export (goods and services) growth at 3.5%Y and 4.1%Y in 2024/25 (1.7% in 2023), likely reflecting less drag from inventory, a global tech up-cycle, and supply-chain de-risking. Meanwhile, we believe the latest US tariff hike is more symbolic and is likely to have limited economic impact because the affected products only account for 0.5% of total exports. We therefore continue to expect strong exports this year (8% volume growth) – a key driver of our 4.8% real GDP growth forecast this year.

For 2025, we assume a modest rise in trade tensions, but a favorable global trade backdrop means that real export growth could hold up at 6%. In particular, we think China's export market share could be sustained despite supply chain de-risking, which should benefit China's capital goods exports (part of it may have been driven by China’s own outbound investment).

The key risk is a continued rise and broadening in trade tensions: Fresh US tariffs might encourage Europe to follow suit with more assertive action, as an anti-subsidy probe into China's green exports has been ongoing. Europe accounts for more than a third of China's exports of green products and older-generation semiconductors – both of which have overcapacity issues. Another risk is that the US could take further action to curb exports made by China (such as on Chinese auto components imported through Mexico, for example), on top of those "Made in China".

China to remain pragmatic to mitigate trade tensions: Responding to tariff escalations, we think China may adopt commensurate and symbolic tariff hikes. With regard to a structural rise in trade tensions, we believe China will take a pragmatic approach. In particular, Chinese manufacturers may actively seek outbound investment opportunities to avoid tariffs and secure/expand end-markets. The more subtle yet far-reaching impact of this trend is that it not only creates jobs in recipient countries but also facilitates technology and skill transfers. This could enhance and deepen global collaboration by better leveraging comparative advantages and improving foreign relations. That said, the realization of these positive global spillovers requires pragmatism in managing trade and investment relations in the first place.

## RMB outlook in the cross-currents of current account strength and trade tensions

## Near-term appreciation in tandem with dollar, followed by modest

depreciation: We expect the CFETS RMB index to appreciate modestly in 2Q-3Q, largely continuing with the trend in 1Q24 amid a strong trade surplus, de facto pegged USDCNY and stronger dollar. We expect the RMB basket to slightly weaken from 4Q24 (USDCNY reaching 7.35 by end-2024) amid a continued rise in trade tension and narrowing trade surplus. As the dollar index may start to decline from 2Q25, according to our global FX team, we think USDCNY may slightly decline from a peak level of 7.38 in 1Q25 to 7.30 by end-2025.

Throughout our forecast horizon, we estimate the RMB basket will remain largely stable at 99-101 and higher than the post-2015 average of 96: We believe this reflects:

• Policymakers showing rising preference for a stable RMB: The PBoC has tightened its grip on RMB since 3Q23 in order to fend off a potential downward trend of currency depreciation and capital outflows. This reflects the lesson from 2015-16 that if economic fundamentals are less robust, weak expectations for currency tend to be self-fulfilling. Meanwhile, policymakers also likely think that if the RMB outperforms other EM currencies in times of USD strength, it would help anchor expectations on the macro-economy and financial market, thus promoting the currency's internationalization.

• Supply chain competitiveness: We think China's rising competitiveness in emerging sectors, in particular the "new trio" (EVs, batteries, and solar panels) and capital goods, will support a resilient trade surplus and thus the RMB.

## Beijing is thus making incremental efforts to stabilize domestic demand

In our view, another way to mitigate trade tension impact is to boost domestic demand, particularly consumption: As it is, concerns of a "China Shock 2.0" have stemmed from China's elevated trade surplus amid low export prices, which have led to large distributional effects and disadvantaged local manufacturers in other countries. Therefore, a stronger boost to consumption could help reduce the imbalance by digesting overcapacity domestically (and thus bringing up export prices) and transforming income from exports into imports, giving trading partners more room to redistribute the gains from trade.

To this end, we have seen some incremental progress in implementation of the "5R" policy framework (Reflation, Rebalance, Restructuring, Reform, and Rekindle): In our view, this is the key strategy for China to revive domestic demand amid the prevailing challenges of debt, demographics and deflation ("3D" challenges). These include Beijing's push for a faster budget rollout (urging an acceleration in government bond issuance, launch of the auto trade-in program), initial moves to rein in overcapacity in the lithium battery industry, increasing support for housing inventory digestion, a more balanced tone on LGFV deleveraging, and the start of equity market reforms to promote investment returns. In our view, these efforts combined have fulfilled 20-25% of the desired "5R" strategy, which has effectively reduced the risk of debt-deflation downward spiral, though still insufficient to form a decisive reflation path.

## Modest fiscal and monetary easing to continue

We expect the augmented fiscal deficit to widen slightly, by 0.5ppt of GDP this year, in line with the NPC budget: The fiscal rollout has been back-loaded this year given the >5% real GDP growth in 1Q24 led by exports, but the pace is likely to accelerate in 2Q-3Q, evidenced by the start of an Rmb1trn ultra-long STB issuance from May 17 and the planned issuance of Rmb1.1-1.2trn in LGSB for May-Jun (vs. \~Rmb700bn in January-April). The key policy focus will be on tech localization, energy safety, and urbanization.

There could be another 0.5ppt of GDP fiscal expansion in 2025: We see the focus gradually pivoting from manufacturing upgrading towards domestic housing inventory digestion amid overcapacity and geopolitical pushback.

Meanwhile, the PBoC is likely to conduct further modest monetary easing in view of elevated real interest rates: We expect a 25-50bps RRR cut in the coming couple of months to accommodate faster government bond issuance, followed by a 20bps policy rate cut in 2H24, facilitated by a Fed pivot this September. In 2025, we see the PBOC cutting the seven-day OMO rate to 1.45% (1.6% in 2024) and the one-year MLF rate to 2.0% (versus 2.3% in 2024) – slight asymmetry to strike a balance between managing capital market leverage and promoting a lower lending rate (LPR is priced based on MLF).

To monitor the policy direction, we will closely watch three key catalysts for the rest of this year:

• The Third Plenum (early/mid July): The April Politburo meeting said the key theme of the meeting would be further deepening reforms and promoting the modernisation of China. We believe the policy focus will remain on enhancing technology innovation and supply chain self-sufficiency, in terms of a push for economic reforms and a concerted focus on key strategic sectors.

• Quarterly Politburo meeting (late July): The likely >5% target real GDP growth in 1H24 is likely to reduce the possibility of additional fiscal stimulus beyond the NPC budget. Instead, we expect the meeting will focus on the implementation of announced policies, including issuance and deployment of ultra-long STB and LGSB, and a modest expansion of the consumer trade-in program beyond autos under the fixed budget.

• Central Economic Work Conference (mid-December): The meeting will set the 2025 GDP growth target (to be disclosed at the NPC next March) and the general policy tone. Given the possibility of increased trade tension in the US presidential election year, we believe the policy focus next year will gradually gear economic rebalancing towards consumption.

## Property sector to be less of a drag in 2025 amid supporting measures

A new round of policy easing measures recently, with potential impact: This was triggered by the worsening housing market downturn year-to-date, with the persistent deflationary pressure it imposes on the economy. One of the major positives of the package is a clear focus on supporting inventory digestion rather than just completing ongoing projects. The PBoC announced a Rmb300bn "Public Housing" relending program to help state-owned companies buy unsold homes for conversion into social housing – modest in size but earlier than we expected. The demand boost also came as a surprise, with a nationwide cut in the down payment ratios to their lowest-ever rates.

Housing support measures may continue to evolve in the coming quarters: We see more urgency to execute home completion with better utilization of policy banking tools. The uncertainty surround housing inventory digestion is still visible in two ways:

• On one hand, the pre-approved relending quota still fell short of the Rmb3-4trn needed to lower primary inventory to desirable level, and implementation is still likely to be complicated amid moral hazard concerns.

• On the other hand, if local government pilot programs in the coming months give rise to an actionable and replicable mechanism, we think there could be a largerscale nationwide implementation with more quasi-fiscal backing.

In our base case, successful implementation of ongoing policies gradually reins in the ongoing decline in the housing market: The announced policies, combined with potential further home purchase relaxations, should facilitate faster market clearing, support liquidity among developers, restore home buyer confidence, and (with a lag) improve housing investment. That said, we still expect the housing market to contract in 2024 amid continued price headwinds, followed by a milder decline in 2025. Indeed, the size of stimulus (cumulatively \~Rmb1trn if fully implemented) is still relatively modest compared with heavy deleveraging pressures (a housing sales shortfall of \~Rmb6trn vs. peak level).

We thus expect developers' housing investment growth to remain subdued at -9.3% in 2024 (vs. -9.6% in 2023), followed by a narrowed decline in 2025, to -5% to -6%. Meanwhile, housing sales volume growth is likely to decline a further 14% this year (vs. - 8.5% last year), before improving to -3% to -5% in 2025.

## However, reflation has a long way to go

Despite our expectation of growth stabilization this year, the key drivers are likely to be exports and public capex strength, while efforts towards economic rebalancing have remained limited given the continued supply-centric policy mindset. This means overcapacity issues could still linger in the coming quarters, keeping deflationary pressures alive.

In view of this, we expect China's GDP deflator to remain subdued at -0.3% for 2024 (- 0.5% in 2023), before rebounding modestly to 0.4% in 2025: This means nominal GDP growth will stay at a subpar level of 4.5% in 2024 and 4.9% in 2025, firmly below consensus. Within the underlying details, we see core CPI remaining largely stable at 0.7%Y in 2024 and 0.8%Y in 2025: services prices are likely to remain resilient considering relatively tight supply amid population aging, offsetting subdued core goods prices with pass-through from PPI. Meanwhile, though PPI YoY may gradually narrow the decline with a lower base kicking in, it would only exit deflation from 2Q25, as manufacturing capex moderates while lower prices are likely to have squeezed out some inefficient supply.

Risks to Watch

Domestic policy direction and geopolitics are the key swing factors in our outlook.

## Downside scenario: faster deleveraging meets trade tension escalation

In this scenario, we assume twin headwinds from a faster pace of housing/LGFV deleveraging under a more hawkish policy stance, and more broad-based US and EU tariff hikes on Chinese exports. These would bring about further deterioration in aggregate demand, exacerbating the overcapacity problem and becoming a drag on price growth. Moreover, the continued decline in industrial profit margins would force companies to cut wage growth, triggering household deleveraging and thus an entrenched debt-deflation loop.

In turn, real GDP growth would slow to 4.0%Y in 2024 and 3.2%Y in 2025, while the GDP deflator would slip to -1.2%Y and -2%. This would send the nominal GDP growth to a mere 2.8% in 2024 and 1.2% in 2025, pushing up the debt/GDP ratio, even with a slowdown in credit growth.

## Upside scenario: more forceful adoption of the "5R" strategy

Here we assume more aggressive fiscal stimulus to rebalance the economy towards consumption and resolve the elevated housing inventories under this scenario. This could be through a decisive rise in social welfare spending to unwind the elevated precautionary savings, as well as a massive housing purchase plan to convert developers' unsold units into social housing, with faster implementation. We believe these measures, if implemented, would effectively increase the disposable income of low-income families for consumption, and help improve homebuyer sentiment. Meanwhile, export strength could be sustained by better-than-expected global demand and stable trade relations with the West. These combined would bring the economy towards a more decisive reflation path.

In this context, we'd expect China's real GDP growth to accelerate to 5.3%Y in 2024 and 5.5%Y in 2025, with a turnaround in the GDP deflator to 0.5% and 2.5%, respectively, bringing nominal GDP growth back to desirable levels of 6-7%.
