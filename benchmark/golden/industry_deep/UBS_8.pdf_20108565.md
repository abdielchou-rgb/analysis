# Global Economics & Strategy UBS World at a Glance

## A monthly synthesis of all our views in a single document

We summarise our views on the main economies and investment strategy on the following pages. The aim is not to be comprehensive, but rather to provide a succinct summary of the main changes in our views and recommendations over the past month. The contributing analysts are listed in the relevant sections of the report—if you have any questions on any particular area, please reach out to them directly.

## It's complicated

The US appears to be slowing (from a high level) while growth in the rest of the world is accelerating (from a low level). At the same time, we've seen a noticeable uptick in the run-rate of global service sector inflation the last 2m, which we suspect is a head-fake (due to distorted seasonality), and we believe we're about to go into a period of softer inflation prints. China has announced what looks like a convincing plan to put a floor under property demand, but funding details are still lacking.

Against this backdrop, we still see upside for equity markets (6% for MSCI AC World by year-end) but note a growing disconnect between fundamentals and performance. We like China outright, and prefer Europe over the US, and remain biased to defensive growth and banks. In fixed income, we believe we’re going into a period where softer inflation data will put more cuts back into the US curve; and we also like front end rates in the UK, Switzerland, Korea, India and, increasingly, Mexico. In credit, we assume the US slowdown is moderate enough not to increase credit risk, and prefer IG 3-5y, IG Banks and Telecoms, BBBs over BBs and HY non-cyclicals; in Europe we still like Financials (AT1s). In FX, we still expect moderate USD strength, especially against low-yielding FX, and we favour pro-carry strategies, with USD and AUD as favoured longs and CHF as a funder. We also remain bullish gold and have just significantly lifted our forecasts.

## Economics Summary

## Key Messages

• US growth is slowing while RoW growth is accelerating, led by consumption.

• There is a global simultaneous uptick in services inflation which we believe is due to distorted seasonal factors.

US consumption appears to be slowing (from a high level). The stall in public infrastructure investment and private manufacturing plant construction suggests fiscal support is finally fading. Growth in the first half of ’24 is running below 2% annualized compared to the 3% pace in 2023. We expect some further slowdown in the 2nd half of this year as investment pauses on US election related tax and trade policy uncertainty. Within consumption it looks as if the expansion is increasingly reliant on higher income households, with signs of distress in parts of the income distribution (delinquency on auto loans and credit cards is back up to 2010 levels) but still very high levels of liquidity and balance sheet strength at the top end. By contrast, our nowcast models suggest consumption in the rest of the world is improving, as inflation is receding, albeit from low levels (Figure 1).

The impact of new US tariffs on China should be small as the tariffs (mainly EV’s, batteries, metals and semiconductors) affect only 5% of US imports (and only 1% of China’s exports), and the phase in of the tariffs is slow: 24% of the tariffs are effective September 1 this year, 13% on Jan 1, 2025 and 63% from Jan 1, 2026. The battery tariffs (where China has a US import share of 65-72%) will likely hurt the most, and increase the cost of cars, while others (EV’s where China only has a 1% market share) seem more aimed at deterrence.

There appears to be a pick-up in global services inflation the last two months. The fact that it is happening globally (the co-movement in inflation of even underlying CPI components remains at all-time highs), without any obvious cause (nominal wages are trending lower and labour markets are easing) point to a potential global seasonal adjustment problem, similar to what occurred post-Lehman. For the US, for instance, the April core CPI print would have been 8bp lower (100bp annualized) using pre-pandemic seasonals, and the seasonal distortion is also obscuring the improvement in the rent data the Fed has been waiting for. We expect disinflation momentum to resume in the next few prints.

We still expect the Fed to deliver two cuts this year (Sep/Dec), though Fedspeak makes it clear we need a sequence of softer inflation/employment data for 3 months or so to put the cuts back on the table. The ECB is locked in for a June cut, but there seems insufficient support for a quick second cut in July. A quarterly pace of 25 cuts looks most likely and we believe the first few cuts will go ahead regardless of what the Fed does. The BoJ we think may curtail JGB purchases from June in an attempt to send a somewhat more hawkish signal to contain JPY weakness.

Economic momentum in China moderated in April, with the property sector still in deep contraction. However, the government announced a coordinated package of policy easing to help the property market, including a reduction in the national mortgage down-payment ratio and a removal of mortgage interest floors. Most importantly, an effort seems underway to allow local governments to purchase existing homes (either unsold from property developers or in the secondary market) for social housing, in an attempt to put a floor under prices and help boost demand. Although the government does not look likely to purchase properties itself, the central bank could provide funding to local governments via the banking system. The scale of the funding remains unclear, however. The PBC set up a RMB300bn facility (which banks can leverage up) whereas our property team believes around RMB2.5trn would be necessary for tier 1 and 2 cities alone. We expect growth to sequentially slow in Q2 but then rebound in the 2nd half of the year.

Prime Minister Sunak announced a UK general election for July 4. Parliament will be dissolved May 30 and the new parliament will not reconvene until July 17. Historically, manifestos are published 18-29 days before the election (June 5-16) but labour has previously said its manifesto will include a focus on constructing new homes, green energy, modernising the NHS, reforming childcare/education, and addressing crime. The lack of fiscal space will constrain how much they can actually do. We don’t expect the election to impact near term monetary policy. Labour has been widely expected to win (ahead by 20pts in the polls), BoE conditions projections only on announced policies, and most measures won’t kick in until next year. That said, the BoE has cancelled all speeches and public statements until after the election which will add uncertainty about the timing of cuts (we have pencilled in June but it hinges on the next CPI print).

## Equity Derivatives Strategy

## Key Messages

 The narrative on US growth/inflation has shifted materially in recent years. We highlight what could work if the next narrative is in the direction of 'stagflation'

Growth data continues to be critical for the volatility regime. If unemployment rises, volatility would historically be closer to 20%. If a slowdown without rising unemployment is possible, recent 10% volatility could continue.

Dividend options are now available for the S&P 500. Large companies are paying and growing their dividends.

Narrative shifts: Over the last two years, US growth has been remarkably resilient and many of the covid related inflationary pressures have abated. As a result, the market has moved from a narrative of potential 'hard landing' to 'soft landing'. Then onwards to 'Goldilocks'. The recent resurgence in monthly core inflation has taken this now toward 'no landing' - strong growth and elevated inflation. Through these progressions, we have used our innovative 'Market Cookbook' to assess the potential returns across sectors and for where volatility may be most mispriced.

What if the stagflation narrative is next?: We think an underappreciated risk in the coming months is that the growth data deteriorates before the inflation data. Our monthly Nowcasts for key US data releases highlight the potential for May Core CPI to be 0.38% - an acceleration from April. The growth data is not forecast to disappoint but there are growing concerns that we may see retail sales and employment weakness in the months ahead. We modelled a scenario for a stagflation narrative. We assumed that US inflation breakevens could continue a little higher but that growth disappointments could lead to lower real yields and wider credit spreads. In this scenario, the US market could fall around 7% with cyclical sectors underperforming. Volatility could rise to 15-20%. Gold miners would be the only sector that would rise.

Growth data to drive volatility: Our Macro Cookbook can help calibrate expectations for the volatility regime based on economic forecasts. We use growth, CPI, unemployment and interest rate data back to the 1960s to find periods that are most similar to what we expected in the coming quarters. For our central scenario of a soft landing, volatility could range between 10% and 20%. Employment data like nonfarm payrolls may play an important role in defining the volatility regime. Our work highlights that if we get a soft landing with no rise in unemployment, volatility can stay low around 10%. If unemployment rises, volatility could be closer to 20%.

Options for dividend momentum: Previously limited to European indices, options on dividend futures are now available for the S&P 500. We have highlighted that these may be compelling for investors. Dividends can exhibit downside tail risks and so call options can be helpful in risk management. Additionally, many large US tech companies are paying and growing their dividends and higher yielding sectors like Utilities are now performing very strongly too. With futures trading with a material discount to our expectation of index dividend payments and the growth potential for dividends, these new dividend options present an appealing risk/reward in our view.

## EM Strategy

## Key Messages

With US nominal growth likely slowing, one headwind for EM FX is set to fade. But weak exports, tight risk premia and declining carry limit the upside. Hold hedges here.

 China is one of our core equity OWs; S Africa stands at an inflection point.

 Front end rates in Korea, India and, increasingly Mexico, likely offer value as the Fed cuts in Sept.

FX: We have highlighted weak risk/reward in EM FX this year. In the next 1-2m headwinds from rising US yields could abate amid UBS' expectations for slowing US nominal GDP growth, providing tactical opportunities in EM FX. We are not confident, however, that this will provide sustained support to EM FX. 1. Carry is in the bottom decile of its post-GFC distribution and could weaken ahead - different output gaps, margins, exposures to global food inflation, and proximity to weak export prices from China arguably suggest even greater pressure on EM central banks to ease than the Fed, barring strong FX depreciation). 2. Implied FX vol sits at just the 8th percentile of its historic distribution; from these tight levels of risk premia EM FX historically struggled. 3. China's subdued credit flows, capacity utilisation and export prices pose challenges to trade growth in the rest of EM, while the risk of additional US tariffs on Chinese imports looks under-priced for the CNH, in our view. 4. Imbalanced trade growth - dominated by a) China b) tech (Korea and Taiwan exports are up 8-10% y/y on a headline basis but are flat/negative outside of tech). Favoured EM longs: TRY, EGP, PLN, INR. Preferred shorts: CNH, THB, COP, CZK.

Equities: Strength in DM economies relative to EM, delayed Fed rate cuts and strong USD make it a tough setup for EM equities to outperform DM, in our view. Our preference is for markets that have attractive fundamentals and being favourable exposed to these themes: 1) strong domestic economic support to index EPS, 2) higher for longer oil prices, 3) higher for longer US rates and 4) exposure to the Asia tech cycle. We are overweight China on better earnings expectations on consumer-related sectors (55% of MSCI China), underpinned by inexpensive valuations. We are also overweight Brazil, Malaysia and Philippines. We are neutral Korea and Taiwan as relative valuations are now at decade high premiums on the tech upcycle. We are underweight India as we believe valuations are extraordinarily expensive for companies’ ordinary fundamentals.

FI: Median EM inflation is only 40bp above its pre-pandemic (Q4-19) pace compared to 125bp for DM. Contained global food and oil prices, higher exposure to weak China export prices and our below-consensus views on inflation in much of EM (Latam, Korea, India, Czech/Hungary) and the US suggest to us that pockets of EM local debt offer value. While resilient growth and services inflation in much of EM today is a risk, we note progress here in economies such as Korea and Europe. We like front end local debt (FX hedged) in India, Korea, Thailand and Brazil, against being paid in the front end of Poland and Malaysia. In credit, spreads remain priced for a US soft-landing scenario led by an expensive IG segment. Stay selective.
