# Circling the Airport or Landing?

Waiting for the US economy to land in any manner has felt like circling the airport without hearing the pilot ask the flight attendants to prepare for landing. The wait has made investors and market prices increasingly cast doubt on an eventual hard landing – just at the wrong time, we think.

## Global Macro Strategy

We review our fiscal and liquidity framework for the US economy. In classic fashion, markets may have priced out a US recession just at the moment when it should think about pricing one in again. We discuss why a negative output gap in Europe, a significant headwind to inflation, argues to stay short EUR/USD via long-dated options.

The dramatic increase in narrow liquidity (banking reserves) over the past year surely supported the financing of riskier investments. And with the dramatic injection of fiscal support, it's no wonder the prices of risky assets rose last year and so far YTD.

The easing of financial conditions that came with the performance of risky assets probably played a role in keeping the labor market from weakening more than it did. The confidence of CEOs and other small business owners remained stable through 2023, likely buoyed by strong equity and credit complex performance.

• The feedback loop between risky asset performance and the confidence of business leaders should resonate without much explanation. So should the feedback loop between business leader confidence and their decisions about hiring and firing.

It's becoming increasingly difficult to answer the question with "low labor supply" of "fear of the inability to rehire". Elevated CEO confidence provides a more durable rationale, in our view.

As such, it may take a decline in equity prices or widening in credit spreads to kick off the feedback loop between lower CEO confidence and higher unemployment claims. But if the liquidity backdrop remains as supportive as it has been YTD, this type of decline in risky asset prices looks unlikely.

Still, if we're right, the supportive liquidity impulse will fade this year alongside the fiscal impulse already fading away. That environment may lead investors to expect less growth, which would show up in asset prices more easily, given a thinner liquidity cushion. In classic fashion, markets may have priced out a US recession just at the moment when it should think about pricing one in again.

## Curb EUR Enthusiasm

We estimate that Europe is entering a negative output gap this year, which is a significant inflation and EUR headwind. Stay short EUR/USD via long-dated options. See more here: EUR/USD | Curb EUR enthusiasm

## Trade idea: Maintain long 1-year EUR/USD 1.07 put at 1.38% (expiry February 18, 2025)

A common pushback we get on our EUR-bearish thesis is that eurozone data are poised to beat expectations, thanks in part to a very low bar.

We agree that eurozone data could beat market expectations. In fact, this has already happened (see Exhibit 7 ). But it hasn't been impacting EUR/USD; its correlation to local economic data surprises has fallen to effectively zero (see Exhibit 8 ).

Even so, by focusing on data surprises we think that investors are missing the point: the level of data, specifically growth, matters. Persistently weak growth could generate a negative output gap, which would amplify disinflation and raise the probability that the ECB would have to cut rates not just to neutral but even below.

Indeed, we calculate that Europe will be facing a negative output gap, using both our own economists' growth forecasts and the ECB's more optimistic growth figures (see Exhibit 9 ). The longer that GDP growth remains below trend, even if beating expectations, the more the negative output gap widens, weighing on inflation and policy expectations.

At the moment EUR/USD isn't trading on this theme, in part because local rates aren't either – they're chiefly driven by US rates (see Exhibit 10 ). This is likely because markets are perhaps correctly assuming that the first cut from central banks are highly correlated to the Fed's.

This makes sense to us. Inflation remains noticeably above target and a return to the 2% goal is far from guaranteed – a concern central bankers widely seem to share. Cutting rates well before the Fed to solve for growth weakness risks currency depreciation, in turn raising import prices and backtracking on the disinflationary progress made so far.

## The supply to focus on: Labor supply

A few months back, we flagged that rising labor supply was going to be a crucial determinant of wage inflation, as well as GDP growth (see The supply that the Treasury investors should think about, and Lower Duration Supply + Higher Labor Supply = Long 3m30y Receivers). Earlier in the year, we noted that investors were once again focusing on Treasury supply instead of labor supply (see Rates Market Focused On The Wrong Supply...Again).

Our suggestion has been that as labor supply rises, investors should contextualize high growth with the growth in population. This increase in labor supply should also help in cooling wages, and increase the chance that high growth and low inflation can co-exist.

Yet, markets continued to focus on growth for the most part of the last six months and ignored the downside surprises in inflation (see Exhibit 15 ). varies, as well as the magnitude. For instance in the 2000 cycle, the flattening is rather insignificant, while in 2008 the GFC led to a 22bp flattening. Markets are currently pricing a re-steepening of 1s2s of 9bp between now and the first cut.

EUR 2s5s: 2s5s steepened in 75% of the time in the six months preceding the first cut, with most of the steepening occurring between three months before and one month before (except in 2008 where the slope steepened 18bp in the month ahead of the cut).

In the current cycle, 2s5s was rather unchanged between six months and three months before the first cut, in line with previous cycles. We note little steepening is priced in forward swaps for the coming three months (cumulative of 11bp) compared to a median steepening of 23bp and despite being more inverted. The 1m correlation between 1s2s and 2s5s being currently negative, the 2s5s moderate implied pricing could be partly a reflection of the 1s2s implied steepening.

The 2011 cycle is the outlier, with 2s5s flattening 21bp in the six months ahead of the first cut. This makes sense, in our opinion, given the ECB only hiked twice and had to stop its normalization cycle amid the sovereign crisis.

EUR 5s10s: 5s10s steepened in 100% of cases in the six months ahead of the first cut, by a median of 23bp, while in the current cycle a 4bp flattening is implied in forwards. This is mainly due to the 8bp flattening between December and now. Noticeably, most of the steepening tended to happen in the three months ahead of the first cut. In this cycle, only 4bp of steepening are priced in the three months to the cut.

The largest steepening occurred post 1991 cycle in a context where curves were still extremely inverted three months before the first cut and even more so than currently (5s10s at -46bp at the time vs. -5bp currently).

EUR 2s5s10s: The price action on 2s5s10s varied between cycles, which is consistent with findings on the 1s2s slope. However, in the month ahead of the first cut the fly richened 100% of the time, and by an average of 11bp. Markets imply a 3bp cheapening in that period for the current cycle, again in line with the implied 1s2s steepening (positive beta).

EUR 10s30s: 10s30s behaved differently in all previous cycles. In the 2000 cycle, 10s30s steepened 22bp in the six months to the first cut, with the bulk of the move being between six months before and three months before.

In 2008, 10s30s flattened initially before the first cut as the GFC was unfolding and stocks were plunging (see Exhibit 40 ). However, before the risk-off and after the last hike, 10s30s had steepened as noted here. In 2011, 10s30s flattened by 23bp in the first six months of the plateau amid the sovereign crisis, as highlighted in the previous analysis. Thereafter it recovered slightly as Exhibit 39 shows. Markets are now expecting 3bp of steepening before the first cut.

EUR 5s10s30s: Similar observations can be made on 5s10s30s. The 10y sector cheapened in the 2008 plateau given the poor performance of risky assets, while in the other instances the fly didn’t move materially on aggregate in the six months to the first hike.

EUR 30s50s: For ultras, data is available for only two cycles. The slope was flattened in 2007 in the first place amid the risk-off while in 2011, the curve steepened especially between six months and three months before the first cut.

## QT considerations into the next FY

While the past week was relatively quiet on the data front, with euro area HICP releases the main events and month-end buying flows dominating the price action, we found Ramsden's speech particularly important in setting QT expectations, and on balance it further raises the bar for a stop in active QT sales at least during the current period (October'23 - September'24).

It is worth noting that previous communications from the BoE already made the bar high for a stop in active sales, especially in the short term, as they continued to stress the limited estimated impact on policy stance and the bond market. In terms of gilt yields, a study from the BoE suggested that the impact has been marginal (as the parameters of the programme were well communicated), despite some market participants thinking that active QT has had a meaningful impact on long-end yield valuations. As per the BoE, the impact on activity and inflation has been estimated as minimal – less than 0.2% and 0.1pp, respectively. The other important point stressed many times is that QT should operate in the background, with Bank Rate as the active monetary policy tool, and that sales should be gradual and predictable. Predictability also makes us think that active QT sales might continue in order to reach the APF reduction plan of \~£100bn (including passive roll-off).

In his last speech, Ramsden outlined how the QT programme has been a key element in the balance sheet normalisation process, together with the start of the unwinding of the TFSME. In addition, it has been highlighted that the BoE's QT "differs from other central banks, notably the Federal Reserve, which aims to maintain its QE portfolio at a level that will back an ‘ample’ level of reserves while the composition of Bank’s balance sheet is likely to seem less familiar in terms of its size and composition". The composition will also be relevant as the Preferred Minimum Range of Reserves (PMRR) approaches, which sits between £335bn and £495bn, while the decision on the future size and composition of the balance sheet is instead a separate issue, implying that the APF could be fully unwound if it is judged necessary for policy reasons, and the level of the PMRR should not affect this judgement as the BoE's facilities for supplying reserves, including STR, are designed to ensure that the MPC can remain focused on monetary policy when deciding on the pace of QT.

These considerations further raise the bar for a stop in active QT even if Bank Rate is cut, while after September 2024, when the BoE should set a new target in terms of APF reduction, active QT might stop given the scale of liquidity that will be removed through TFSME repayments and considering that passive sales will amount to \~£90bn for the October 2024 - September 2025 period.

## Why have ASW been cheapening?

Factors behind such significant ASW cheapening over the past few weeks—amid expectations that a NIRP exit is now just a matter of time—would appear to include (1) a lack of domestic investors, (2) speculation that the inflation-overshooting commitment is set to be scrapped, and (3) a perceived possibility that the BoJ will return to a single excess reserve remuneration rate after ending NIRP.

With regard to (1), JSDA trading flows for January were suggestive of domestic investors remaining reluctant to buy JGBs (see Exhibit 58 ), with dealers perhaps seeing their inventories swell as a consequence of other players holding off on adding to their exposure until normalization actually commences.

With regard to (2), as Takata himself hinted, there is of course a possibility that the inflation-overshooting commitment will be deemed unnecessary once the +2% inflation target is “in sight”. It will be difficult for the BoJ to cut back its JGB purchases much further so long as the commitment remains in place (see Japan | Lower BoJ Expectations Meet Positive Carry Trades), which basically means that it will need to be scrapped in order for quantitative tightening (QT) to commence. Our economists thus expect the commitment to be brought to an end simultaneously with NIRP and YCC.

On the third point, we have recently been fielding more and more questions about how the BoJ might go about managing its policy rate in a post-NIRP world. In particular, many clients appear concerned that returning to a single-tiered structure for BoJ current account balances—with a uniform remuneration rate applicable to excess reserves— might diminish any incentive for banks to participate in the BoJ’s Loan Support Program and Climate Change operations (which are currently used as a means of increasing Macro Add-on Balance headroom).

This could reduce demand for (predominantly shorter-dated) JGBs as collateral and generating upward pressure on the short end of the curve (see Exhibit 59 ).

## G10

G10 | Policy gaps vs. the Fed: Levels G10 cannot withstand?
Inspired by our EM strategists, we look into how many rate cuts G10 currencies can withstand compared to the Fed. Similar to LatAm FX, our G10 results do not show a regional performance pattern for the nominal differential versus the US.
However, our EM strategists did find that LatAm currencies have underperformed consistently if their real policy rate differential versus the US reached a certain threshold, which we do not find for G10.

Instead of the realized nominal or real policy rate differential, we find that G10 currencies have historically underperformed versus the USD if the real 10y yield spread relative to the US was below the \~30th percentile.

However, the results differ quite substantially between 2000-2008 and 2012- 2019, suggesting wider long-end real yield spreads matter more for G10 performance in an environment of higher rates and no deflation concerns, as before the Global Financial Crisis. Put differently, changes in real yield spreads arguably matter more than spread levels, with 2000-2008 being a period of greater variation than the 2010s.

G10 | Policy gaps vs. the Fed: Levels G10 cannot withstand? We review central bank pricing correlations across G10. The ECB is increasingly perceived as following Fed policy. Risks to this perception are clearly EUR/USD negative. And the Riksbank is increasingly perceived to be following ECB policy.

Given that markets are already pricing ECB-Fed policy convergence, we see risks to pricing as increasingly skewed towards EUR-negative policy divergence. Our economists think that the ECB’s first cut would still take place in June even if the Fed were to cut later than June or not at all in 2024. The elevated Riksbank-ECB policy pricing correlation likely reflects the Riksbank’s stated concern about the level of EUR/SEK.

## Europe

## EUR/USD | Curb EUR enthusiasm

European data have been beating expectations, thanks to a very low bar, but are a far cry from 'good'. Incoming data continue to suggest that European growth data are tracking well below potential (1.3%Y or so).

The longer below-potential growth persists, the more likely we are to see a negative output gap. Using our economics team's forecasts, we estimate that the eurozone will have a -1.0% output gap by end-2024 and -1.3% by end-2025. Even using the ECB's more optimistic growth figures, we still calculate a -0.7% output gap for 2024.

A negative output gap implies reduced demand-driven inflation and creates risks that the market will price in not just a terminal rate at neutral but perhaps even below. Such a rapid cutting cycle suggests a lower EUR/USD spot, higher volatility and lower EUR carry, all of which render EUR a more attractive funding currency.

EUR/USD downside remains an attractive, positive carry hedge for a variety of macro scenarios, including a more dovish pricing for the ECB's cutting path. We continue to recommend buying 1-year EUR/USD puts at 1.07.

CHF | Why the inflation malaise of the 2010s?

The 2010s was a decade subject to low inflation in Switzerland. This was not the result of a singular overarching reason as some may expect, but a combination of factors. Three main contributors being: a slack labour market, a strong currency, and indexed housing rents.

The slack labour market put pressure on wages, in turn feeding through to services. A strengthening Swiss franc meant that both imported goods and services, a quarter of the CPI basket, were made relatively cheaper. Finally, indexed housing rents anchored a significant portion of inflation as the rental reference rate fell throughout the decade.

What happens when the current inflationary cycle comes to an end is yet to be defined. Switzerland may find itself once again in an inflation malaise, it may find itself in a sweet spot (0-2%), or it could even have an inflation problem depending on where global inflationary pressures settle.

But can we conclude that G10 currencies can never tolerate 10y real yield differentials below a specific percentile? That depends on the kind of macro environment currencies are trading in.

Even so, by focusing on data surprises we think that investors are missing the point: the level of data, specifically growth, matters. Persistently weak growth could generate a negative output gap, which would amplify disinflation and raise the probability that the ECB would have to cut rates not just to neutral but even below.

Using the potential growth forecasts from the European Commission, we estimate what the output gap could look like using realised 2023 growth figures and both our own economists' growth forecasts and the ECB's forecasts from the December meeting.

The Commission estimates that potential growth in the euro area is roughly 1.3-1.4%Y. The near-recessionary growth rates in 2023 turned the output gap from positive to negative in 2023. Continued below-trend growth rates in 2024 and 2025 lead, based on our economics team's numbers, the output gap to reach -1.3% of GDP by end-2025.

This would be a negative output gap close to levels last seen in the aftermath of the eurozone debt crisis from 2010-15 if we exclude the Covid period.

Even using the ECB's arguably optimistic growth forecasts, we still estimate a modestly negative output gap of -0.5% of GDP.

We think that this is an important theme, but it may not be tradeable right away. This is because, as noted above, EUR/USD is being chiefly driven by US rates. But also US rates are driving European rates, which should more cleanly reflect a change in policy expectations. The recent rise in the correlation between Fed and ECB policy expectations stands out relative to other central banks' relationship to the Fed, as we discuss in EUR/USD | Curb EUR enthusiasm .

There is an economic rationale to the high correlation. We think that markets will continue to price a strong relationship between the timing of the first cut across central banks, but may be more willing to price in differentiation in the pace of cuts and terminal rate down the line.

Why? Two reasons. First, central bankers are still far from sold that inflation will reach their 2%Y goals. CPI has been moving in the right direction but is still above target, with core CPI on a three-month moving-average basis, GDP-weighted, still about 70bp above the 2%Y inflation goal (see Exhibit 82 ).

Second, cutting rates well ahead of the Fed risks currency depreciation, which could reignite inflation via import prices. Economies outside the US tend to be more sensitive to exchange rate pass-through, and we think that central bankers are cognizant that currency weakness risks undoing the welcome progress on inflation.

However, we're not sold that this same logic applies later this year to the depth and speed of the cutting cycle. This is because more progress will have been made on inflation.

In the case of Europe, as inflation continues to decelerate (perhaps quicker than anticipated), we think that markets and the ECB alike will increasingly pivot their focus away from inflation and towards growth. Continued weakness in growth data raises the risk of entrenched disinflation and the focus pivots towards defending the 2%Y goal from the downside, not upside.

In short, we may more clearly see policy divergence visible in market pricing when the market changes its focus from 'when's the first cut' to the cutting cycle itself. As we discuss here, the pace of the cutting cycle and terminal rate matter more for both rates and FX than the timing of the first cut itself.

That pivot could come as early as the March Fed and ECB meetings. But the forecast revisions, such as the 'dots' from the Fed and the growth and inflation projections from the ECB, could make it clear to the market that the first cut is likely coming soon. Markets are anticipatory and thus, once the first cut is clear, the debate becomes about the pace and timing.

While the Fed's dots may show a gradual pace of cutting over multiple years, the ECB's growth and inflation projections might imply more urgency to bring policy not just back to neutral quickly, but perhaps even below. Markets beginning to price in this divergence could be an important EUR/USD-negative catalyst.

## A slack labour market, a strong currency, and indexed housing rents

The 2010s was a decade subject to very low inflation in Switzerland. This low inflation regime was not the result of a singular overarching reason as some may expect, but a combination of the three aforementioned factors.

The combination of these three factors contributed significantly to keeping headline CPI subdued both in goods ( Exhibit 85 ) and in services ( Exhibit 86 ), relative to G10 peers.

Exhibit 85: Switzerland has quite consistently occupied the bottom spot across G10 regarding goods inflation
![](images/5bc5232f8a0dd9413155fc62930195c9607375d9d25b186058765b7ca06ae3f3.jpg)
Source: Macrobond, Morgan Stanley Research

Exhibit 86: The difference is even more stark with regards to services CPI despite Japan keeping Switzerland company
![](images/d438cb5d01911fed2485866223bb4b179f50a8d73333e94a31e481753faf3363.jpg)

Given the proportional size of services in the CPI basket (60%) and gap that Switzerland exhibits relative to the rest of G10 (ex Japan) we take a particular interest in services, starting with the labour market.

## 1) Low wage growth and a slack labour market

Despite being known for high salaries, the balance of labour in Switzerland favours employers over employees.

Historical data suggests the structural ratio of job vacancies per unemployed person is roughly 1:10 ( Exhibit 87 ). There have been a couple disruptions of late, causing the ratio to spike. However, as the effect of these shocks abate, the ratio corrects back down toward the long-term average.

Exhibit 87: The Swiss labour market appears to be structurally loose...
![](images/52c990185825e7882e41bde43d1086cc05bdcaa52ab24fdb412fd0202cfac423.jpg)
Source: FSO, Bloomberg, Morgan Stanley Research

Exhibit 88: In turn leading to weak wage growth as employees struggle to bargain
![](images/bafd0842f328abbcd7f5f5b8936e37f2dd3f6d2a4d0aebb8c3ea7a3046fecc5a.jpg)
Source: FSO, Morgan Stanley Research

On its own, the excess supply of labour relative to demand already complicates wage negotiations for employees given the lack of credible alternatives.

However, when considering the sparse union presence in Switzerland, with only 14.4% union membership at the latest reading according to the OECD, the wage-bargaining ability of employees worsens.

This low wage-setting power from both individual and collective bargaining stand points then feeds through to lower wage growth ( Exhibit 88 ), and in turn soft services inflation ( Exhibit 86 ).

## 2) Switzerland, imports, and a strong CHF

24% of the goods and services that make up the Swiss CPI basket are imported. The roughly 60% appreciation of CHF on a trade-weighted basis since 2010 ( Exhibit 89 ) has put significant downward pressure on the cost of these imported goods and services and by extension therefore on the CPI basket as a whole.

In fact, in the 10 years pre-COVID, imported goods and services on average exhibited 0.8%Y deflation ( Exhibit 90 ) – producing a significant drag on the CPI basket.

Currency appreciation has also played an important role in the current inflationary cycle. The SNB explicitly targeted a stronger franc (in nominal terms) and was thus, to a large part, able to shield Switzerland from foreign inflation.

Exhibit 89: The Swiss franc has appreciated 60% since 2010 on a trade-weighted basis despite being subject to a peg against the EUR from 2011-2015
![](images/aa94b99714f48caef3cfec32f18ef3674f6880d156411cf6e64014c2513a054d.jpg)
Source: Bloomberg, Morgan Stanley Research

Exhibit 90: The strong franc weighed heavily on the cost of imported goods and services

Source: FSO, Morgan Stanley Research

## 3) Low rates hold back housing

The biggest individual item in the CPI basket is found in housing rent, with a weight of 18.4%.

In Switzerland, housing rental contracts are often open-ended with the rental price indexed against a national “reference rate”.

This “reference rate” is calculated quarterly based on the average mortgage rates offered by Swiss Banks. As a result of the low rates environment of the 2010s, mortgage rates continuously decreased as the banks competed for business ( Exhibit 92 ).

The falling reference rate therefore put significant downward pressure on rents ( Exhibit 91 ), in turn anchoring a significant part of the inflation basket.

Exhibit 91: Inflation in housing rents has remained relatively subdued despite house prices soaring globally
![](images/bf5e3be8d8e324612ac94c3f68a36f34fd997d336b9d388b2d4035d9f3801bda.jpg)
Source: FSO, Morgan Stanley Research

Exhibit 92: The rental reference is based on the average mortgage rate in Switzerland rounded to a quarter percent
![](images/813c641828c74f938956cdb285200445792c9be0c911b5f31ef56f5a24606505.jpg)
Source: OLF, Morgan Stanley Research

In conclusion , Switzerland has been experiencing an inflation malaise not due to a singular over-arching theme but due to an “anti-goldilocks” combination of, labour, FX, and housing effects.

This combination, however, now potentially looks more like a blessing than a curse given how the SNB and the Swiss economy have navigated the recent inflationary shock.

While rents have not been particularly helpful in keeping inflation low throughout this cycle, CHF appreciation and a slack labour market have.

What happens when this inflationary cycle comes to an end is yet to be defined. Switzerland may find itself once again in an inflation malaise, it may find itself in a sweet spot (0-2%), or it could even have an inflation problem depending on where global inflationary pressures settle.

## Closing our short EUR/JPY position

The FX market has been in somewhat of a lull of late as it awaits new catalysts. Moreover, allowance is also being made for the risk of median dots for 2026 onwards given that economic indicators continue to surprise on the upside and financial conditions are as yet on the loose side.

The jury is still out as to whether the strength of January labor market and services inflation data will prove to be a temporary aberration or a portent of inflation picking up speed once again. We thus see little prospect of markets pricing in a deeper Fed rate cut trajectory before they have had a chance to see the next jobs and CPI prints in early March.

As we discussed in JPY | Is There A Risk Of Potential MoF Intervention? February 17, 2024, with USD/JPY still offering such attractive positive carry, it is difficult to envisage the greenback plummeting against JPY under either a 'soft landing' scenario characterized by gradual rate cuts or a 'higher for longer' scenario whereby US-Japan interest rate differentials would remain wide for quite some time to come (see Exhibit 104 ).

## Contemplating JPY flows: A better explanation than “New NISA”?

In Macro Strategy: Impact Of The “New NISA” Tax-Free Investment Scheme, February 6, 2024, we explained why we expected recent enhancements of Japan’s Nippon Individual Savings Account (NISA) tax-free investment scheme to have only a limited FX market impact despite helping to catalyze increased outflows into overseas assets via investment trusts.

But it does still need to be acknowledged that the continuing strength of USD/JPY has gone beyond what can be explained solely in terms of US-Japan interest rate differentials (see Exhibit 105 ).

So, which other factors might be at play? One possibility is that there has been some sort of interaction between the USD/JPY rebound since the start of 2024 and the continuing boom in Japanese equities (see Exhibit 106 ). Macroeconomists would tend to view causation as running from JPY to equities given that a weaker currency has historically been mostly positive for exporter earnings (see Exhibit 107 ).

![](images/b19989e27564f02665de662a43661eb15ab65d24af5972ff1d1b250c1488e10b.jpg)
Source: Bloomberg, Morgan Stanley Research

![](images/348f6692af3999f011fec47cfb372a726f696dafba05351da618deb7436c051f.jpg)
Source: Bloomberg, Morgan Stanley Research

But might there also be a channel whereby higher stock prices generate downward pressure on JPY? It is not inconceivable that a rally in Japanese equities could cause overseas investors to 'repatriate funds back into their home currencies for profit-taking purposes.

That said, positioning in Japanese equities by overseas investors has historically exhibited positive correlation in the sense that USD/JPY instead tends to fall when foreigners are selling off Japanese stocks (see Exhibit 108 ).

Exhibit 107:Higher USD/JPY typically leads to higher manufacturing corporate earnings
![](images/62221ced5b4bc83708884e15b4c048f3be613f6634e6219a0b68337de42bf286.jpg)
Source: Japan MoF, Morgan Stanley Research
Exhibit 108:Overseas investors' equity positioning tends to have a positive correlation with Japan

![](images/8845daf66c97ebae3ca0caa53621c6da07b56f80a7aa141988edb75500f8cd6c.jpg)
Source: Japan MoF, Morgan Stanley Research

We thus find it hard to conclude that direct flows into Japanese equities have been having a major impact on USD/JPY. Our impression is that many overseas investors tend to buy Japanese equities on an FX-hedged basis, particularly at times such as now when expected returns can actually be boosted by hedging (with the premium that can be generated from a one-month USD/JPY hedge currently running at around 5.6% annualized).

Consider an overseas investor with an FX-hedged position in Japanese equities. A rally in Japanese stock prices will obviously increase the market value of that position in JPY terms and thereby introduce new exposure to FX risk. However, this can be hedged away via an additional purchase of USD/JPY corresponding to the increase in JPY-denominated market value.

The Nikkei 225 has of course risen by around 17% over the first two months of 2024, meaning that overseas investors are likely to have seen very significant increases in the market value of their Japanese equity positions. Such positions will likely have been around JPY 275 trillion as of end-2023 (see Exhibit 109 ), meaning that additional demand for USD/JPY might have totalled almost JPY 9.4 trillion for January-February alone even if only around 20% of positions were hedged against FX risk.

It is unfortunately impossible to obtain data on FX hedge ratios for foreign investors exposure to Japanese equities. That said, our own discussions with investors indicate that while some Japan-focused hedge funds (for example) may simply hold JPY funds directly on behalf of Japanese institutional investors, there are others that use FX swaps to meet their various currency funding needs.

Meanwhile, big global asset managers will typically have 'fixed' allocations for each currency and invest in Japanese equities to the extent that their permitted JPY exposure allows.

By way of a yardstick, it is perhaps worth noting that FX-hedged products currently account for around 15% (by AUM) of all Japanese equity ETFs that are traded in foreign currencies.

There is obviously no guarantee that a similar percentage would be seen for the stock market as a whole, but it does at least seem plausible that FX-hedging flows could have quite a significant FX market impact during periods such as January-February 2024 when Japanese equities have been rallying particularly strongly.

As we discussed in Macro Strategy: Impact Of The “New NISA” Tax-Free Investment Scheme, NISA-related demand appeared to drive a roughly JPY 783 billion outflow into overseas investment trusts in the second week of January alone, but even that would pale by comparison to the roughly JPY 9.4 trillion JPY-selling inflow consistent with our assumed average hedge ratio.

Plotting the residual for our USD/JPY fair value model (with the US-Japan interest rate differential as an explanatory variable) against the percentage change in stock prices shows quite a close correspondence since overseas investors first started to buy up Japanese equities in earnest from May 2023 (see Exhibit 110 ).

![](images/cdbc23a495b8a4709c424a1e74f936a767e5a0fb8ca91b9b0fe7f2a185203fe5.jpg)
Source: BoJ, Japan MoF, Morgan Stanley Research
Exhibit 110:Residual of USD/JPY fair value model seemingly has been explained by the JPY equity return recently

![](images/b55f0c9fb20132751d04ffb3f64ffdd9cbb8149a05148d6c4e295c732e291c06.jpg)
Source: Bloomberg, Morgan Stanley Research

It would thus appear that weakening of JPY beyond what can be explained by US-Japan differences in monetary policy stances might owe at least something to FX-hedging flows for overseas-held positions in Japanese equities.

This in turn suggests that JPY might currently be facing downward pressure via both fundamental and technical channels as a consequence of Japanese stock prices having continued to surge higher despite apparent concerns about a 'higher for longer' scenario being necessitated by strong US labor market and inflation data.

The flip-side, then, could be a combination of a stock market correction and significant JPY-buying flows in the event of a hard landing scenario starting to be viewed as more likely.

## Trading Strategy 1 – "Trade Longs/Fade Shorts"

When the 5-day moving average crosses above the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract (long duration) and hold for a 25-business-day period. When the 5-day moving average crosses below the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract and hold for a 25-business-day period. In short, this strategy buys futures when the Simple Moving Average Crossover (SMAX) generates both a long and a short signal, given the historical outperformance of long signals traded long and underperformance of short signals traded short. Given that the SMAX could generate both a long and a short signal within the predefined holding period, an investor may have a 200% long position since each of the two signals would be traded in separate portfolio sleeves.
