# US Economics & Global Macro Strategy | North America

## US Inflation: After the Upswing

Reflation in 1Q24 was substantial, but weaker prints are coming. Rents, goods, and financial services will decelerate at the margin. Underappreciated is that 1Q strength was in part driven by residual seasonality, pointing to payback in 2H24. The downswing is coming.

## Key Takeaways

Reflation in 1Q24 was mostly explained by an acceleration in goods and financial services prices. We expect both components to decelerate from here.

Leading indicators point to even weaker rents inflation in the second half of the year than we previously anticipated.

## Deceleration Ahead

Inflation surprised substantially to the upside in 1Q24. We were early to call for a temporary reacceleration in 1Q, which helped us stay firmly planted in the view that the Fed would not cut before the middle of the year even as market pricing piled into rate cuts starting in March. Even so, the strength of the upswing was even more than we had anticipated. The 3-month annualized pace of core PCE inflation rate soared from 1.6% in Dec-23 (SAAR) to 4.4% in Mar-24.

The upward surprises triggered concerns of a sustained acceleration in core inflation, but we think markets are overreacting by drawing a trend out of the last three inflation prints. Chair Powell agrees, during the Q&A following the May FOMC meeting he noted "my expectation is that we will, over the course of this year, see inflation move back down". We expect deceleration ahead too, with slowdown in the monthly pace of core PCE inflation, especially in 2H24. We see sequential rates broadly aligned with the Fed's 2% inflation target by the end of the year, bringing annual core PCE inflation to 2.7% 4Q/4Q in 2024. ## Two key assumptions underpin our call:

(1) We expect deceleration in rents inflation in 2H24. The last two prints of the quarterly new tenant rent index (4Q23 and 1Q24) point to a faster pace of deceleration in rent CPI/PCE inflation in 2H24. The index keeps track of rents on new leases, which have performed well in our models as leading indicator rents inflation.

(2) 1Q24 reacceleration was mostly concentrated in two components (core goods and financial services) which are likely to decelerate ahead. Taken together these two

components represent two-thirds of the upswing in the 3-month pace from Dec-23 ( Exhibit 3 ). Moreover, only a few items within core goods and financial services explain the strength in both components (more on this in the next section). Hence, the upswing does not look like a broad-based acceleration pointing to a sustained upward trend. Moreover, Chinese deflation and lower stock returns suggest lower inflation in these components.

(3) Residual seasonality played a role in the recent acceleration. Our analysis suggests that seasonal factors are now worse at capturing seasonal patterns than before COVID. As a result, core PCE tends to be stronger during the first quarter of the year and weaker in the second half.

We explain these key points in the next section as we get Into the Weeds.

## Into the Weeds

## Rents

## Effective Rents Point to Deceleration

The BLS New Tenants Rent Index (NTRR) came in weak in 4Q23 and 1Q24 – both prints below 1%Y. These data are in line with our view that rents should decelerate faster in 2H24. The NTRR leads rents CPI by about 3 quarters and is a useful explanatory variable in our models ( Exhibit 4 ).

Exhibit 4: Effective rents point to lower rents inflation in 2H24
![](images/6c18deef2e4e551c09b29690d72f0146793e46c411fda04e76b86fb90bc0819e.jpg)
Source: BLS, CoStar, Morgan Stanley Research

A strong advantage of the NTRR is that it uses data from the same survey the BLS employs to calculate rents/OER CPI, and includes both multi and single-family units, making it a comprehensive indicator.

As always, there are shortcomings to note: (i) the index can be revised meaningfully, especially the latest prints, (ii) it is not a sufficient statistic to predict rents CPI because it omits rent renewals. We tend to discount NTRR prints when they deviate significantly from other data on effective rents. But these last two prints seem to be aligned with other data sources like CoStar.

Even though there is no nationally representative data, the information we get from our REITs analysts indicates that renewals are running below the current pace of rents CPI/ PCE – renewals inflation is currently about 5%Y whereas rents inflation is currently running at about 6%Y. The fact that both new-lease and renewals inflation rates are below current rent inflation is a clear sign that rents CPI/PCE should step down in 2H24.

## OER vs Rents Wedge

In previous research, we noted that OER is likely to remain stickier than rent of primary residence. Our forecasts assume that OER MoM inflation prints will remain 3bp above Rents of primary residence the rest of 2024 – we expect OER at 0.35%M on average the

rest of 2024, and Rents at 0.32% for Rents.

The recent methodology change in OER, which puts more weight on single-family rental units (SFR), might add strength to OER. The BLS increased the weight on SFR in OER calculation from 35% to 45% in Jan-23, and from 45% to 50% in Jan-24. The change in weights implies higher OER because the single-family segment of the market is expected to remain stronger compared to multifamily.

Data from private sources on new leases point to more strength in SFR, reflecting different supply and demand dynamics compared to multifamily ( Exhibit 5 ). Demand tends to be more stable for single-family units due to demographics – renters of singlefamily homes tend to be wealthier and have children. Also, our REITs analysts note there is now an over-supply of multifamily units approaching historically high vacancy rates, whereas SFR supply has not increased as much recently ( Exhibit 6 ).

Exhibit 6: Historically high multifamily vacancy rates

Even though the direction of travel is clear, how much the wedge between OER and Rents will increase is subject of debate. We think it is unlikely we see a sharp rise in the wedge ahead because of three reasons:

1. The methodology change started in Jan-23 and yet we have not seen a significant widening of the wedge. OER's single-family weight increased 10pp in January last year, but the average monthly wedge was just 1bp in 2023. Single-family weights increased further in Jan-24, but the increase was just half of last year's (5pp).

2. In a past webinar on shelter inflation, the BLS noted that the methodology change played only a minor role explaining the upswing in OER in Jan-24 (see slide 11 here).

3. Perhaps more importantly, we found contradicting evidence regarding SFR strength which adds uncertainty ahead. Even though private sector data show that SFR rents are increasing faster than multi-family ( Exhibit 5 ), information shown by the BLS seems to contradict that view. During the webinar on shelter inflation the BLS showed a slide comparing single-family rents vs overall rents in the CPI housing survey and, to our surprise, the data showed that typically SFR rents were below average (see slide 31 here). Hence, private sources show a different picture compared to the BLS. Why the difference? It is unclear, but it might be related with the fact that private sources put more focus on institutional investors with newer units which might show higher rent growth. Hence, even though SFR rents might show a stronger picture in the rest of 2024 in both data sources (as it happened in Jan-24), we take this information as warning that private sources might

be overstating the relative strength of SFR.

## Residual Seasonality

Finally, residual seasonality may be the dark horse behind the upswing in 1Q24 inflation. Seasonal factors did not pick up typical price increases during the first quarter of the year – especially in January.

Seasonal factors can be wrong because the magnitude of price increases at the beginning of the year are a function of the current demand elasticity for specific goods, which can change over time especially at the micro level.

Measuring residual seasonality is tricky because we don't observe the true seasonal factors, only estimates of them. However, we do know one fact about correct seasonal factors: they should eliminate all seasonal patterns in the data, Hence, one way to assess how BEA's seasonal factors have performed recently is to check if published core PCE SA series still show any seasonal patterns. We do that by double seasonally adjusting core PCE series, that is, seasonally adjusting the published SA core PCE series and checking whether the series changes materially. Any important modification in the series is a sign of residual seasonality.

Exhibit 14 shows the median difference between published and double-SA series for every quarter (in QoQ SAAR). Three important conclusions emerge:

(1) There is evidence of residual seasonality 1Q24, with payback especially in the second half of the year especially in 3Q

(2) Residual seasonality has become more important after COVID. Our estimates indicate that residual seasonality increased annualized core PCE inflation (QoQ) in 1Q by 40bp on average in 2021-2023.

(3) There is of course noise at the end points of a data series, but our results show that residual seasonality might have been even more important in 1Q24 compared to the last few years. Given that seasonals just reshuffle inflation within a given year, residual seasonality this year suggests more and perhaps earlier payback.

What components show residual seasonality? Our analysis indicate that there is residual seasonality in both core goods and services, with a similar pattern: stronger 1Q residual seasonality post-COVID and payback in the second half of the year.

## Risks to Our Forecast Are to the Upside

Inflation forecasting is a humbling process and we could be wrong for several reasons, but there are two factors that we think are more important given their potential impact to the inflation path.

(1) Stronger rents inflation driven by migration flows (key indicator to watch: effective rents). One key risk to our forecast is a potential reacceleration of rents inflation should there be a boost in demand for rentals associated with recent immigration flows. Recent upswings of immigration flows have caused acceleration in rents in other countries (see Exhibit 15 showing the case of Canada). Canada's immigrant population holds a higher share of skilled labor with higher incomes, and higher household formation rates. In contrast, US immigrants tend to be lower-skilled, are paid lower wages, and we have not seen a pickup in household formation rates.

# Will Rates Get a Downswing with Inflation?

## Guneet Dhingra

Inflation has been pivotal to the rise in yields in the last three months. In fact, of the last 80bp rise in yields since the start of 2024, nearly 100% has come from the three days around the CPI prints (see Exhibit 16 ). Even the core PCE prints elicited a rise in yields last week, when the 1Q24 number came in higher than consensus. US rates markets have been hypersensitive to upside inflation surprises.

What is notable, in our view, is that markets were not as sensitive to downside surprises in 2H23. Thus, while markets did not react to cooling inflation (see Exhibit 17 ), investors looked at the cooling data and took that for granted, which might explain the current hypersensitive reaction in yields to the inflation surprises. This suggests that Treasury yields might be more vulnerable to cooling inflation than at any time in the last year.

As it turns out, our economists see deceleration ahead, with support from cooling rents, continued cooling in goods, and a reversal of residual seasonality. We think that Treasury yields could fall meaningfully if this forecast materializes.

## The residual seasonality risk

While many investors expect cooling rents, and cool goods prices, we think that the residual seasonality dynamic is the least appreciated among investors.

In the recent sharp reaction to higher inflation prints, the overwhelming narrative adopted by investors has been that inflation is proving "sticky". In essence markets have extrapolated the recent uptick in inflation strength to the future. But residual seasonality suggests that 1Q strength may remain confined to 1Q, and subsequent inflation readings have a downward slope.

Exhibit 18: Core PCE Q/Q trends in 2023
![](images/52dbaa81418c36f5b03e30e0c636a565231454fbe6fff9de5447c01f3a96aa00.jpg)
Source: BEA, Morgan Stanley Researc

![](images/b2c2d22e8c2ac82526661502a177241dad340906635d49f70395f631c525384d.jpg)

As our US economists detail above, 1Q numbers for core PCE have been stronger than average inflation for the year, with this year's numbers getting a boost of 0.6pp on the SAAR level. This means instead of \~3.7% SAAR, the 1Q number could have been 3.1% SAAR.

And this has been a known phenomenon – and there have been attempts to address this by the Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) in the pre-pandemic years, with the third and last phase of this having been implemented in mid-2018. Removing the 2019 numbers, and the 2020/21 numbers given the pandemic distortions, nine of the last 12 years have seen this residual seasonality trend.

Most importantly, given that many investors prefer looking at recent trends, it is notable that this residual seasonality trend was very visible in 2023 (see Exhibit 18 ). Q/Q Core PCE in 1Q23 was \~5.0% annualized, versus merely \~2.0% annualized in 4Q23. That is some drop in inflation. Also, 1Q24 at \~3.7% is significantly lower than 1Q23 at 5%.

And this residual seasonality in inflation numbers has created some seasonality in the Treasury market itself. Treasuries have been seeing upside surprises in February (when January core CPI is released). Given that nine of the last ten years have seen upside core CPI surprises, Treasury yields have risen in February. And given that 1Q core PCE comes out in April, even April has seen an uptick in seasonal rise in yields.

In the last five years, February and April have a 99% and 93% statistically significant seasonality for higher yields, respectively (see Exhibit 21 ), which lines up well with residual upside seasonality in inflation data for 1Q – released in February through April.
