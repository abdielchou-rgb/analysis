# Could Fed funds bottom out above 4%?

Could Fed funds bottom out above 4%? USA: Dallas Fed mfg activity (Feb): Up 16.1pts to -11.3, above mkt. USA: New home sales (Jan): Up 1.5% mom to 661k saar, below mkt. UK: CBI retail sales index (Feb): Up 43pts to -7, above mkt. JPN: PPI services (Jan): Up 2.1% yoy, below mkt

PODCAST: Listen to Matt Luzzetti, Matt Raskin and Matt Barnard discuss the outlook for the Fed yield curve after hot inflation prints. 20 minutes, here.

US equities were little changed Monday, S&P down 0.1%. US10yr yields up 4bps to 4.28%.

A beat on Dallas Fed manufacturing, up 16pts to -11.3 in February.

ECB's Lagarde: Europe's economy should "pick up" later this year, wage pressures "remain strong".

The S&P 500 has now advanced for 15 of the last 17 weeks. Henry Allen notes this has only happened one other time in the last 50 years, back in 1989. AI excitement, rate cut hopes and better macro data help explain why. But the rally is also very narrow, and inflation persistence could become problematic.

There are decent prospects for the fed funds rate to “bottom out” during this cycle above 4%. Matt Luzzetti draws this conclusion by arguing that it is useful to frame the Fed outlook as a two-stage process. In stage 1, the Fed delivers a mid-cycle adjustment with a few 25bp rate cuts. Stage 2 is a potentially lengthy pause, with further rate reductions requiring evidence of weakening growth or adverse developments in financial conditions.

Europe CPI preview. Marc de-Muizon expects this Friday's euro area headline inflation for February to come in at 2.6% yoy and core inflation at 3.0% yoy, slightly above consensus and market pricing. The risks to the forecast are overall balanced, although the risks are slightly on the upside for services and slightly on the downside for goods.

Current \$ & € spec-grade defaults are modestly above historical averages and Steve Caprtio's forecasts suggest they will plateau to rise mildly in the US and potentially retreat in the EU from here. More details in Steve's latest Global Default Monitor, here.

What to expect from China's national people's congress. Yi Xiong expects the congress to occur in early March and will deliver a 2024 growth target likely be set at "around 5%", above market's current consensus growth forecast of 4.6%.

Mingyue Xin previews the upcoming Eurex futures rolls and is neutral the RX, OAT, IK and BTS rolls, but modestly bullish on the OE, DU and UB rolls.

Alan Ruskin thinks CAD & JPY have more in common than meets the eye, especially on long-term considerations. Both are extremely cheap on a PPP basis, but long-term managers should not assume a gravitational pull back to PPP on a long-term basis.

Connectivity between FX and US/European equities has picked up. Rohini Grover shows that both have been key drivers of high-frequency FX over the past week.

Australia's yield curve is far steeper than peers, likely reflecting a market view that the RBA has been less aggressive than peers, meaning less in the way of cuts and weak growth. Tim Baker thinks this view is wrong - policy has tightened about as much as the economy can take, which should see the curve flatten in time driven by the long-end. Tim previously saw AUD 10y yields 5-10bps above US10y but now sees them 20bps below – 4% at mid-24, 3.95% at end-24.

Macroeconomic fluctuations often account for most of the risks associated with exposures to instruments that promise to pay fixed or variable interest rates. For an investor who is long fixed-rate instruments (thus receiving these cash flows), increases in interest rates naturally lift the discount factors applied to the expected cash flows and thus cause a loss. The longer the maturity of a certain cash flow and the higher the move in the discount factor, the higher will be the loss. Upside surprises in inflation, fiscal funding needs, and FX depreciation rates are among the most common adverse macroeconomic shocks to receivers. But counterparty exposure, issuer creditworthiness, client segmentation, regulation (including changes in assets holding limits for different types of investors), changes in tax policies, currency convertibility (which may constrain currency conversion, repatriation of profits and/or liquidation of positions), and settlement issues related to local clearing houses are also important risk factors. The sensitivity of fixed-income instruments to macroeconomic shocks may be mitigated by indexing the contracted cash flows to inflation, to FX depreciation, or to specified interest rates - these are common in emerging markets. The index fixings may - by construction - lag or mis-measure the actual move in the underlying variables they are intended to track. The choice of the proper fixing (or metric) is particularly important in swaps markets, where floating coupon rates (i.e., coupons indexed to a typically short-dated interest rate reference index) are exchanged for fixed coupons. Funding in a currency that differs from the currency in which coupons are denominated carries FX risk.
