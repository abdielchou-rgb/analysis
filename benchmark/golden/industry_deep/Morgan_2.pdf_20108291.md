## China Property

A new chapter in policy support

On 17 May, China rolled out the strongest policy support package for the property sector since the turmoil started in 3Q21. While we think its effectiveness may not be enough to trigger a big recovery soon, we believe it shows authorities have become more determined to support the downside, thus mitigating the risk of property sales further worsening and supporting our base case that stabilization may take place in 1H25 (or 4Q24 the earliest). Until then, home prices may still be under pressure. Versus investor expectations, the mortgagerelated measures are a positive surprise (and have come in earlier than expected), while the purchase of unsold units has already been widely expected due to earlier market speculation. Their size of Rmb0.5 trillion (in bank loans) is in line with our expectation, but disappoint some investors who were expecting >Rmb1 trillion of PSL. After the recent rally, while we think there might still be short-term excitement, the sector may soon take a breather (more) as (1) expectations for policy support have been priced in mostly; (2) valuations of some POEs are becoming rich. To ride on the theme of property stabilization, SOE property managers may generally be better plays for their earnings defensiveness and potential payout increases. Here, we like Poly Property Services the most, followed by CR Mixc and Greentown Service. Among the developers, we like CR Land. Share placement by POEs is a near-term downside risk, in our view.

What was announced? (1) The State Council called for local governments to purchase unsold completed properties for conversion into affordable housing in cities with high inventory (more), based on demand (以需定购)and reasonable prices (酌情以合理价格. Accordingly, Rmb500 billion of loans (with 60%, or Rmb300 billion, to be funded by PBOC relending facilities) will be provided through 21 national banks; (2) the minimum down payment ratio was lowered to 15% for first homes and 25% for second homes (more); (3) the mortgage rate floor was lifted (previously LPR-20bps for first homes and LPR+20bps for second homes) (more); (4) lowering of HPF mortgage rates by 25bps (more).

Are these measures effective? We are encouraged by the direction to purchase unsold units, as this directly creates demand. However, scale matters. Assuming 80% LTV, the loans will support Rmb625 billion of purchases, representing 6- 7% of our annual sales value forecast (or 8-9% of volume, assuming a 20% discount). In theory, if the government purchases all unsold completed units (391mn sqm GFA) at a 20% discount, this will require Rmb3.1 trillion. As for other mortgage-related measures, which are mainly aimed at improving affordability, we think they will help but are hardly a cure-all as confidence in home prices and income/employment are more important considerations.

Is it different this time? Since the industry turmoil started in 3Q21, we categorize the support measures into six stages (Figure 1). Most measures (e.g., the 16-point notice and urban village renovation) have been ineffective, and we were never excited about them. While we do not expect SOEs’ property purchases to be sizeable enough to drive a big jump in sales, this approach should at least be able to support the downside. As property sales year-to-date have been worse than expected, we believe authorities are finally keen to support. That said, while buying unsold properties may remind investors of the cash compensation in shantytown redevelopment in 2015-18, we reiterate our view that the government has no intention to revive a property boom.

 Is it all-in already? Not yet. If property sales remain weak in the next few months, we believe additional measures could still be rolled out gradually, e.g. (1) expansion of bank lending for property acquisitions (e.g., another Rmb300- 500 billion); (2) removal of all home purchase restrictions in non-core districts in tier-1 cities; (3) lowering of transaction tax rates; and (4) relaxation of price control in core areas of top-tier cities.

## What are investors saying?

“Underwhelming” is the most common feedback we heard, as many investors we spoke with were expecting new PSL of Rmb1-2 trillion. We do not rule out the possibility of additional funding through PSL, but we do not think the government wants to announce a concrete target or plan per se. Also, as we believe the government’s intention is only to support the downside, funding which may boost sales by a mid-single-digit (i.e. Rmb500 billion) sounds like an appropriate scale to us.

Mortgage easing is a positive surprise: Since this was not mentioned in earlier market speculation, the three major mortgage easing measures from the PBOC came in earlier than most investors’ expectations. Investors seem encouraged by this gesture as this shows authorities appear to be determined to support the sector. That said, most investors believe the impact of boosting sales will not be too big.

“Anything but developers”: Some investors are turning more hopeful that the property market will stabilize in the next 1-2 years (the consensus view is that an immediate recovery is unlikely). For those who are looking beyond the next few months and hope to ride on the investment theme of “property stabilization”, interestingly, most are not eyeing developers, but other property-related sectors, such as property management (e.g., CR Mixc, Poly Property Services), property brokers (e.g., Beike (Not Covered)), China banks and even Ping An. The major concern about developers is a potential multi-year decline in earnings (and thus DPS downside) dragged down by a margin squeeze. Among developers, the most enquired name remains CR Land, although a minority of investors also asked us about small-cap SOE developers.

Purchase scale is uncertain: The most frequently asked question was the purchase scale. While many investors believe Rmb500 billion of funding support will not be sufficient, many are also worried that even Rmb500 billion cannot be used up, as in its latest notice, the State Council emphasizes that purchase programs will be carried out by local SOEs (instead of a central government-led platform). Investors worry local governments may not have sufficient incentives to implement this on a large scale as public rental housing may not be profit-making (rental yield of 2-2.5% may be barely enough to cover financing costs). In fact, for the original bank loan trial program of rental housing (Rmb100 billion), so far only \~Rmb12 billion has been drawn. We recognize these hurdles, but we believe the momentum will accelerate this time, because the central government has called for “digesting inventory” and thus it will likely be more proactive in checking on the progress with local governments.

 Placement risk is on the rise: Many hedge fund investors we spoke to believe there may soon be equity raising activity by non-distressed POE developers given the positive market sentiment.

## Measure #1: Purchase of unsold homes

Directionally in line with the earlier market speculation: This newly officially announced supportive measure is directionally in line with the earlier market speculation (for more, please see our note A potential game changer? It’s all about the scale). However, the following highly-anticipated elements are not yet specified: (1) PSL funding of Rmb1-2 trillion; (2) central government forming a nationwide platform (instead of asking local governments) to acquire unsold properties.

This is not an entirely new initiative: Since 2022, at the local level, >20 cities have announced initiatives to purchase properties (either from primary or secondary market) for conversion into public rental housing or affordable housing (Table 3). However, the total scale (30-40K units) has not been sufficient to move the needle. For detail on the different approaches, please refer to our earlier note A potential game changer? It’s all about the scale.

Bank support for purchase of unsold homes is also not entirely new: Back in January 2023, the PBOC already rolled out a pilot program to encourage banks to provide rental housing loans (Rmb100 billion) to 8 cities (Tianjin, Chengdu, Qingdao, Chongqing, Fuzhou, Changchun, Jinan and Zhengzhou), allowing them to use the proceeds to acquire properties for conversion into public rental housing (interest rate <3%). The latest supportive measure essentially expands the scale from Rmb100 billion to Rmb500 billion.

What’s the impact? In the pilot program, the LTV is a max. of 80%. Using the same ratio, bank loans of Rmb500 billion can support total funding for purchases up to Rmb625 billion (with Rmb125 billion equity to be funded by local governments). Rmb625 billion represents 6-7% of our full-year sales forecast. If we assume local SOEs will buy at a 20% discount, this means a total of 72 million sqm GFA could be purchased (8-9% of annual sales volume). Assuming an average size of 70 sqm GFA (the usual size of affordable housing), this represents \~1 million units. Alternatively, if we assume the average size will be bigger (say 100 sqm GFA as purchase targets may not always offer unit sizes of <70 sqm; Zhengzhou, for example, set a criterion for purchases from the secondary market to be <120 sqm), then the implied number of units is \~0.7 million.

What’s the potential maximum size of purchase? We can gauge this from both supply and demand perspectives. For supply, the State Council stated that the definition of unsold properties refers to “completed unsold units” (i.e. 391 million sqm GFA as of April 2024). Assuming these would be acquired at a 20% discount implies Rmb3.1 trillion. This also does not include the potential purchase from the secondary market. For demand, as local SOEs require a mandate for acquisitions (usually for conversion into public rental housing), we can gauge this by looking at the target supply of public rental housing, which is 1.5 million units per year in 2024 and 2025. If 100% of new public rental housing will be supplied through acquisitions, assuming an ASP of Rmb8K psm (20% discount to national ASP), this will require Rmb1.2 trillion p.a., or Rmb2.4 trillion, in two years.

What are the hurdles? The State Council specified that local government participation in purchases of unsold inventories is on a voluntary basis, and the purchases should be “by need” and “transacted at reasonable prices”. However, since this is a call from the central government, we believe local governments will scale up the purchases. That said, there are still various hurdles: (1) local governments may not have strong incentives as the business itself may not be profit-making; the average rental yield is 2.0% in China (with tier-1 cities even lower at 1.5%); even if local SOEs acquire a property at a 20% discount, the rental yield is 2.5%, which may barely cover financing costs; (2) purchase of properties theoretically sacrifices new construction activities (at least temporarily); (3) the incentive to buy unsold properties from distressed developers may not be high because of concerns on construction quality and potential debt issues (e.g., whether the property might have been pledged for financing). We believe this is why, in the case of Jinan, which has acquired 15 projects from developers, most acquisition targets are SOEs (Table 2).

## Measure #2: Lowering of down payment ratios

The second nationwide easing since August 2023: PBOC announced that the minimum down payment ratio would be lowered from 20% to 15% for first homes (the lowest ever); and from 30% to 25% for second homes. This was the second nationwide easing since the industry turmoil began in 3Q21 (the first was in August 2023 when the minimum down payment ratio was lowered to 20% for first homes and 30% for second homes).

Down payment ratios may still vary among cities: The latest measure only sets the floor for the down payment ratio, but cities still get to decide which minimum down payment ratio to enact. That said, most cities have already lowered the first-home down payment ratio to 20% since August 2023, except tier-1 cities where first-home down payment ratios remain at 30% (Table 4). With the latest policy, we think most cities will lower the minimum down payment ratio to 15%, while for tier-1 cities, it may be lowered to 20-25%.

Will this boost sales? If we refer to the easing of down payment ratios back in August 2023, while we note a marginal improvement in property sales in September and October 2023 (sales vs. the four-year average marginally improved from -61% to -58%), property sales turned worse again in the subsequent months (Figure 2).

## Measure #3: Lifting of the mortgage rate floor

No more minimum mortgage rate: Before the latest move, cities and banks got to determine the mortgage rate, but the PBOC set a floor, which is the five-year LPR-20bps for first homes (i.e. 3.75%) and five-year LPR+20bps for second homes (i.e., 4.15%). The latest PBOC announcement removes this requirement.

However, in fact many cities are already not subject to this requirement: Since last year, the PBOC has already been allowing cities with three consecutive months of home price drops (both Y/Y and M/M) to set mortgage rates freely. Using the 70-city price index of the NBS as reference, 45 out of 70 cities are not subject to the minimum mortgage rate already. As such, the average first-home mortgage rate nationwide as of April 2024 (3.59%) is already lower than the theoretical minimum mortgage rate of 3.75%. In fact, the mortgage rate in some cities is already as low as 3.2 to 3.5%, while tier-1 cities still see a higher average mortgage rate of 3.85-4.05% (Figure 5).

How much can the mortgage rate fall? Our China Banks analyst Katherine Lei estimates that the lowest the mortgage rate may theoretically drop to is \~3.2% if we assume banks do not make any money on new mortgages (more).

How much of monthly savings? Assuming a mortgage size of Rmb1 million (30 years), at the latest first-home average mortgage rate of 3.59%, the monthly repayment is Rmb4,541. If the mortgage rate drops to 3.2%, the monthly repayment would be Rmb4,325, or a saving of Rmb216 per month.

Historically, a drop in mortgage rates did not drive better sales: This is because affordability is not the biggest reason for the market weakness over the past few years. Rather, it is the confidence in home price growth and income/employment. Also, even if the mortgage rate drops to 3.2%, it will still be above the average rental yield of \~2%.

## Measure #4: Lowering of the mortgage rate for HPF by 25bps

 Further lowering of the mortgage rate for HPF: The mortgage rate for home provident fund is now lower by 25bps. For a first-home mortgage with a tenure of >5 years, it has been lowered from 3.10% to 2.85% (Table 5).

This improves affordability for first-home buyers of properties with a lower lump sum: There is a cap on the HPF mortgage amount (typically Rmb500K to Rmb1 million). Therefore, this may mostly benefit smaller-sized transactions. Nine per cent of housing transactions are funded purely by HPF loans, while 37% are funded through a combination of HPF loans and commercial mortgages.

## Other minor measures

Project whitelists: Authorities reported that, by 16 May, 297 cities had already set up a real estate financing coordination mechanism, and commercial banks granted loans of Rmb935 billion to projects under the whitelist. We believe this mainly involved granting of new loans to SOEs/defensive developers, or extending existing loans of distressed developers. This is more a continuation of the existing practice, and thus we are not particularly excited by the whitelists. For more of our views, please refer to our earlier note.

Buying back idle land: The State Council encouraged local governments to consider repurchasing sold but idle residential land plots in order to help ease the liquidity of distressed developers (相关地方政府应从实际出发， 酌情以收回、收购等方式妥善处置已出让的闲置存量住宅用地，以帮助资金困难房企解困). Generally, we think local governments have a low incentive to do so amid tight financial situations, unless this is for land plots in good locations where a distressed developer is willing to offer an aggressive discount. Purchasing completed properties is a much more straightforward approach, in our view.

## Thoughts on the current rally

On a relative basis, this is the strongest rally so far: Of the past 12 rallies since the industry turmoil began in 2021, on an absolute basis, the current rally is already tracking a 45% return (while the strongest return was the 77% jump from November 2022 to January 2023). However, on a relative basis, the current rally (+26%) is already the strongest (Figure 6). The MSCI China Real Estate Index has already exceeded its last peak in November 2023. If we simply benchmark it to the earlier peak (August 2023) as a reference, there is potentially another 15% of upside.

However, valuations are becoming rich: We believe the risk reward to chase now is not high, given valuations, especially of POEs, are getting rich. For example, Vanke-H’s P/E is already >10x vs. the 3-year average of 4-5x. Although some may argue that its 0.3x P/B is still acceptable, we think the P/E cannot be totally ignored given the company is still making a profit (just much lower than before). Also, Longfor’s P/B of 0.6x is already higher than SOE COLI (0.4x), and its P/E of 8.6x is already higher than SOEs such as CR Land (7.8x). We think this is an overshoot.

Policy drives short-term share prices, but property sales are the long-term drivers: Despite the recent rally, MSCI China Real Estate is still \~70% below its all-time peak. While policy expectations always drive short-term excitement (e.g., the 16-point notice), fundamentally share prices are mostly correlated to the trend in property sales (and earnings) on a longer-term basis (Figure 8). Therefore, as we expect property sales to only stabilize at the current level (or at best some mild growth), it would be hard to argue for the share prices to be back to the previous level, unless property sales see a very big boost, which is unlikely in the near term. When there is more proof of sales stabilization, we believe SOEs could see a more sustainable rerating, but not so for distressed developers.

Look for SOEs that still have attractive valuations: Out of all the SOE names that we cover, we think Poly Property Services (6049.HK) stands out with a 12x P/E. For SOE property managers, we believe a fair P/E is \~15x. We like the name for its (1) solid management guidance and track record; (2) strong SOE parent; (3) no questionable corporate governance issues in the past two years; and (4) potential upside to the dividend payout ratio (currently at 40%).

What about the recent rally of certain distressed names? To us, this was not necessarily fundamental-driven. Rather, this might have been just due to short-term buying by speculators. This was also not the first time we saw a similar rally (more discussion in The rally of distressed names is a déjà vu). Back in September 2023, even after SOEs started to fade in share price strength, there were 1-2 weeks of rallies among distressed developers, which were rotated for speculation. Notably, Sunac was the outperformer back then with >200% share price return. This time, Shimao is the speculation target with >300% return so far. Nonetheless, we believe the share price excitement will be short-lived. Even if authorities acquire properties from distressed developers, we think it would only be from the perspective of resolving the issue of unfinished buildings, and the use of the proceeds of this will very likely be restricted to loan repayments or funding of other unfinished buildings. Thus, even if sales of distressed developers improve, the cash proceeds will not be sufficient to revive growth, in our view.
