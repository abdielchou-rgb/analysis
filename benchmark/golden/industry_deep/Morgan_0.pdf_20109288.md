# India Equity Strategy

A budget for all?

The Union Budget for fiscal year 2024-25 will be presented by Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman in the Lok Sabha on 23 July. We believe the first budget of the NDA government’s third term should lay out the vision for the next five years, as it did in the previous two terms, and be a stepping stone toward a “Viksit Bharat” (Developed India) by 2047, as presented in the interim budget in February (Revisiting the Interim Budget 2024-25). As in previous years and in the interim budget, the upcoming budget will be focused on infrastructure and public investment, in our view. This should continue to support local manufacturing, defense, railways, waterways and the power sector. The extra RBI dividend allows authorities to increase targeted welfare spending and still stick to the fiscal consolidation path (Consolidation without compression). On the other hand, any change in capital gains tax rates or holding periods will likely be adversely viewed by the markets, in our view. Going into the budget, our sector allocation remains aligned largely with domestic cyclical plays amid positive earnings momentum, superior economic growth and policy continuity. We remain Overweight on Industrials, Financials, Autos, Real Estate and Healthcare.

Capex thrust to sustain. Post-election-results, the government has hit the ground running by announcing infrastructure projects and higher spending on government programs (see pp.7-8 for details). The Nifty 50 has recovered by a strong 12.5% and India equities have witnessed US\$6.5bn of net inflows from foreign investors since vote-counting day on 4 June. With no change in the portfolios of major ministries and strong support from the coalition, the government is likely to maintain its capex outlay of INR11.11tn for FY25, in our view. With private capex yet to take off meaningfully, a continued thrust on government capex is key for the long-term agenda of the government for a “Viksit Bharat” by 2047. The PLI scheme’s expansion to additional sectors and the introduction of a dedicated scheme for SMEs are keenly watched by investors. Five long-term themes to focus on: (1) indigenous defense; (2) waterways; (3) manufacturing renaissance; (4) affordable housing; and (5) savers to investors (Long-term themes to focus on).

Fiscal freedom to focus on rural. The higher-than-expected RBI dividend of INR2.1tn gives the government a fiscal cushion of 35-40 basis points as a ratio to gross domestic product (GDP). The government can continue its fiscal consolidation path without expenditure compression. This excess windfall along with strong tax collections even allows some room for boosting consumption. A few government welfare schemes (PMAY, PM Kissan Samman Nidhi, recent MSP hikes) along with controlled inflation will help revive the rural economy, in our view. A marginal cut in income tax is also expected, which should lend some support to the lower/middle class. The NIFTY FMCG Index has thus narrowed its relative underperformance following national election results, helped additionally by a narrowing monsoon deficit in recent weeks. That said, we remain selective in the consumer staples sector and note that extreme heatwave conditions adversely affect some consumption segments.

# Consolidation without compression

There is growing clamor for the budget to deliver a “stimulus” to the economy in the wake of tepid private consumption (link). However, it’s important to recognize that the deficit is reducing, so the fiscal impulse is, in fact, tightening. Therefore, the first-order impact is for the fiscal to be a drag on activity, and the real question is how the contractionary impulse can be minimized. To be sure, there will be stimulative secondorder effects from consolidation: (1) lower borrowing and yields, and therefore easier monetary conditions; as well as (2) medium-term benefits from the significant improvement in the quality of spending in recent years because of the higher multipliers associated with capital expenditures. In sum, this year’s budget, and the future fiscal path, will have to be a careful balance that preserves macroeconomic stability while avoiding an excessive growth drag.

## Increased degrees of freedom

The good news is that authorities enter this budget cycle with greater degrees of freedom than in the interim budget in February. Back then, the 2023-24 deficit was pegged at 5.8% of GDP, and the target for 2024-25 was set at 5.1% of GDP. But several events have transpired in the interim:

First, the actual 2023-24 fiscal outturn was 5.6% of GDP – tighter than the 5.8% the interim budged had envisaged. Consequently, the central consolidation last year was meaningful, at 0.8% of GDP. To be sure, state deficits widened by 0.3% of GDP last year, so the combined deficit reduced by 0.5% of GDP. But the Center’s ask for this year has now been reduced to a consolidation of 0.5% of GDP, from 5.6% to 5.1% of GDP. Furthermore, a lower-than-planned central deficit last year meant authorities did not spend money they had already borrowed. So the government’s cash balances coming into this fiscal were commensurately higher and can be used to cut this year’s borrowing (either dated securities or T-Bills).

Second, tax collections also surprised to the upside in the final accounting for 2023- 24. Gross taxes grew 13.5% on a nominal GDP of 9.5% – led largely by personal income taxes. On that higher base, even a more conservative assumption of 11.5% growth this fiscal would result in higher tax revenues than envisioned in the interim budget.

Third, and perhaps more significant, the RBI dividend that was transferred to the government, at almost 0.7% of GDP, was more than twice the level that had been budgeted in February.

The implications of these events are that there is less distance to traverse to reach the announced fiscal target (from 5.6% to 5.1%) and there is an extra 0.4-0.5% of GDP that had not been anticipated in the February budget. These extra resources can be used in some combination of the following:

• They could be saved, such that the fiscal deficit is meaningfully lower than the 5.1% of GDP envisaged.

They could be spent on revenue expenditures (welfare programs and rural spending) since that sector was originally bearing the brunt of the consolidation. If the February budget expenditure assumptions are unchanged, revenue expenditure (exinterest and subsidies) is pegged to grow just 3.3% oya vs. capex that is designated to grow 17%. So this appears a natural place to deploy the extra resources.

They could be used to push even more capex, which increased from 1.7% of GDP in FY20 to 3.2% of GDP in FY24 and is pegged to rise further, to 3.4% of GDP, in FY25.

• They could be used to deliver tax cuts on the revenue side.

In a sense, authorities now have the opportunity to achieve all these objectives simultaneously: increase spending and cut taxes, while still reducing the deficit and borrowing compared to the interim budget. But what authorities choose to prioritize – (1) more consolidation vs. more spending; and, if the latter, (2) tax cuts vs. rural spending vs. more capex – will reflect both how authorities assess the current economic situation and what they seek to prioritize.

## Expecting a fiscal deficit very close to the interim budget, but lower borrowing

Our baseline assumption is that the fiscal target for 2024-25 remains very close to that of the interim budget, and that authorities will target a deficit in the 5.0-5.1% range. We would expect the bulk of the extra fiscal space to be used for increasing revenue expenditures, with potentially some of it used for relief on the tax side. However, even if the deficit stays identical to the interim budget, borrowing is expected to decline by INR0.4tn-0.5tn – either dated securities or T-Bills – compared to the interim budget, because of the extra cash balances inherited from the previous year.

## Consolidation without compression

Avoiding excessive consolidation this year stems from the need to prevent a large fisca drag on the economy. This is where the RBI dividend, apart from its quantum, matters, because of the manner in which it was generated. A large fraction of the dividend comes from interest on the RBI’s foreign currency reserves (reflecting higher global interest rates) and from foreign currency revaluation gains. Therefore, unlike domestic taxes and duties, these proceeds are generated without exerting a “contractionary” impulse on the economy. We estimate that about 0.45% of GDP of the 0.65% of GDP of the RBI dividend this year is not contractionary in nature and, therefore, similar to an asset sale in the fiscal accounts.

The implication is that the increase in asset sales and the non-contractionary component of the RBI dividend across 2023-24 and 2024-25 is about 0.4%. This is significant because the quantum of consolidation will be about 0.5% of GDP if policymakers stick to the 5.1% of GDP fiscal target this year. However, the bulk of this (0.4% of GDP) will be achieved by the increase in (the non-contractionary element of) the RBI dividend and asset sales. So the reduction in the deficit will not impose a commensurate negative fiscal impulse on the economy. In other words, the budget can achieve (fiscal) consolidation without (economic) compression (link).

# Key announcements by NDA 3.0 postelections

Development of a major port at Vadhavan in Maharashtra: On 19 June, aligning with the PM Gati Sahakti program, the Union Cabinet approved the development of an all-weather greenfield deepdraft major port with a project cost of more than INR762bn (\~US\$9.2bn). The project will create a cumulative capacity of 298 MMT per annum, which will also aid EXIM trade flow through the India-Middle East-Europe Economic Corridor. Upon completion, it will be one of the top 10 ports of the world and will create \~1.2mn employment opportunities.

Minimum support price (MSP) hike for Kharif crops: On 19 June, the Union Cabinet increased the MSP for all mandated Kharif crops for the marketing season 2024-25. The government stated that this is in line with the Union Budget 2018-19 announcement of fixing the MSP at a level of at least 1.5x the All-India weightedaverage cost of production. The highest absolute increases have been for oilseeds and pulses, while the hikes range from 1.4% to 12.5%. The government stated that it is committed to providing price to the farmers at 50% more than input cost, and this price hike will result in farmers gaining INR350bn (\~US\$4.2bn) more than in the previous year.

Viability gap funding (VGF) scheme for offshore wind energy projects: The Union Cabinet approved VGF for the installation and commissioning of 1 GW of the first-ever offshore wind energy projects in India (500 MW each off the coast of Gujarat and Tamil Nadu). The approval also outlays a part of the cost for the upgrade of the two ports to meet the logistics of the offshore wind energy projects. The total outlay for the scheme will be more than INR74.5bn (\~US\$890mn). The successful commissioning of 1 GW offshore wind projects will produce renewable electricity of about 3.72bn units annually, which will result in an annual reduction of 2.98mn tons of CO2-equivalent emissions for a period of 25 years. With these two projects, the government aims to create an ecosystem to supplement its ocean-based economic activities, to support the development of an initial 37 GW of offshore wind energy at an investment of INR4.5tn (\~US\$53.9bn).

Expansion of the Varanasi airport: The Union Cabinet approved the proposal of AAI for the expansion of the Varanasi airport and make it a green airport, with an estimated cost of \~INR287bn (\~US\$3.4bn). AAI seeks to significantly improve the airport’s passenger handling capacity from the current 3.9mn passengers per annum (MMPA) to 9.9 MMPA.

Sanctioning of National Forensic Infrastructure Enhancement Scheme (NFIES): The Central Sector Scheme titled NFIES was sanctioned by the Union Cabinet, with an aim to bolster India’s forensic capabilities through strategic investments in infrastructure and manpower. A financial allocation of more than INR22.5bn (\~US\$2.7bn) is made till 2028-29 for the scheme. The government stated that, with the enactment of the New Criminal Laws, which mandate forensic investigation (for offenses involving punishment of seven years or more), a significant increase in the workload of forensic science laboratories is expected, while there is already a significant shortage of trained forensic manpower.

• Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana (PMAY): Eligible rural and urban households were provided assistance for the construction of \~42mn houses (with basic amenities)

since the scheme’s launch in 2015-16. On 10 June, the government announced assistance for an additional 30mn houses under the scheme.

PM Kisan Samman Nidhi: Under the initiative, the government transfers INR6,000 (\~US\$72) every year as minimum income support for land-holding farmers. This happens in three installments every year through Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT). The government has transferred more than INR3tn (\~US\$36bn) to more than 110mn farmers across the nation since launch in 2019. On 18 June, the government released the 17th installment of the scheme, amounting to over INR200bn (\~US\$2.4bn), to over 92mn farmers.

## Revisiting the Interim Budget 2024-25

Capex rises: FY25 capital expenditure outlay of INR11.1tn is concentrated in four areas: (1) INR2.5tn for railways (+5% YoY); (2) INR2.7tn for roads and highways (+3% YoY); (3) INR1.7tn for defense (+9% YoY); and (4) INR1.4tn in transfer to states (+23% YoY). This indicates the government’s clear focus on giving more impetus to infrastructure spending, with a focus on railways, aviation, green energy and electric vehicles - all vital for India to become a \$5tn economy by 2027. However, the pace of the increase in capex is lower than it was in the previous year.

Fiscal prudence: The FY24 fiscal deficit was revised lower by 10bps, to 5.8% of GDP, due to buoyant tax collection and higher dividends from public sector companies, not to expenditure compression. India’s strong digital infrastructure has helped in more formalization of tax collection and also reduced subsidy leakages. The FY25 fiscal deficit was pegged at 5.1% compared to our expectation of 5.2%. This is along the lines of the medium-term target to achieve the fiscal deficit target of 4.5% by FY26. The government has budgeted net tax revenue of +12% YoY for FY25 vs. nominal GDP growth of 10.5%.

Divestment: The government significantly revised its disinvestment goal for FY24 to INR300bn from the earlier INR501bn; this is the fifth consecutive year of the government’s missing its divestment targets. Only \~42% of the revised target was achieved till 31 January 2024. However, the government has set an ambitious target to divest INR500bn in FY25 (+67% yoy).

Refraining from populist measures: The government desisted from increases in social schemes, barring a program to provide free electricity to 10mn households through rooftop solar and extra money for excess electricity output. There were no changes in tax rates, an 8% cut in major subsidies, and an unchanged allocation to the rural employment scheme in the budget. In our view, this is unlikely to revive consumption in the near term. We note that lackluster growth in wages and high inflation have hurt the blue-collar/middle class, particularly in rural areas.
