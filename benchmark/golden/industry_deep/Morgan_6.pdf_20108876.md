# Checking In On The Global Housing Cycle

With central bank easing broadening over 2H24, a clearer shift in the global housing cycle looks likely with affordability and supply-demand dynamics both incrementally improving from extreme levels. Lower rates are likely to benefit volumes more than prices.

A slower turn in the cycle: A year ago we wrote a deep dive on the global housing cycle and its implications for economies, policy and markets: Global Economics & Equity Research: Navigating a Different Housing Cycle (17 Jul 2023). Trends since that point have been broadly consistent with our expectations, although a bit slower than forecast. In our view, this has been driven by a more gradual than expected pivot towards central bank easing creating more persistent housing market headwinds. However, with DM central bank easing now started and expected to broaden over 2H24 we remain confident in a shift in conditions - and continue to see the next housing cycle as being one that is volume-led but price constrained.

Prices and sales stabilised, construction lagging: Conditions remain mixed across the various aspects of the global housing market. Across DM economies, housing sales troughed early last year and have generally tracked sideways at low levels since that point. This was followed by housing prices, which broadly stabilised late last year, with some markets (US, Australia) seeing prices recover back to all-time highs. The lag to date has been in construction, with housing permits remaining very weak across economies and showing limited signs of turning higher at this stage.

Affordability, supply-demand dynamics and mortgage pass-through key: We see three key drivers of housing dynamics from here: 1) affordability generally remains at the worst levels in over two decades - but the start of policy easing should see this improve incrementally from here; 2) supply-demand balances remain very tight, with lagging construction and surging housing demand via migration; 3) mortgage pass-through is influencing policy rate sensitivity and therefore conditions across markets.

US & China markets remain most topical: In the US, we expect easing mortgage rates to see affordability improve and inventories rise, driving continued increases in housing activity and decelerating home price appreciation (but not price declines). In China, we see increased policy support as slowing (but not reversing) the weakness in the housing market, and forecast a contraction in 2024 amid continued price headwinds, followed by a milder decline in 2025.

## Navigating A Different Housing Cycle

Navigating the global housing cycle: In July last year we wrote a global report examining conditions in housing markets across the world: Global Economics & Equity Research: Navigating a Different Housing Cycle (17 Jul 2023). The report was a collaboration including contributions from 10 economics teams, 5 strategy teams, 17 equity analyst teams and the global sustainability team. While emphasising the divergence in sequencing and sensitivity across sectors and economies, the broad conclusion of the report was that the net effect of cyclical and structural drivers meant that 2024 was likely to be a recovery year for housing, with more upside to volume metrics over prices through the next cycle.

Rate persistence has meant a slower-than-expected evolution: Broadly, things have played out as we had suggested in our report last year, albeit at a somewhat slower-thanforecast pace. Prices and sales volumes have both generally stabilised across economies, while new construction continues to lag. The key driver of this is the interest rate path - central bank policy rates are generally higher than what was expected 12 months ago, and as such the improvement in mortgage rates and affordability has been more gradual. Policy rates are now starting to be eased globally, which we think will help the housing cycle evolve.

Equity performance - following the cycle: Wider dispersion across economies and relative to historical outcomes increases the importance of picking trends across stocks, sectors and geographies. The unique cycle means it is harder to estimate what is in the price. We continue to think that a housing recovery that is driven by activity more than prices suggests a more favorable outlook for activity-linked sectors (Homebuilders, Developers) over price-exposed (Banks, REITs). Equity performance over the past year to the broad housing theme has been relatively strong - US Homebuilders have outperformed the S&P500 by \~10% over this time, while an equal-weighted screen of our 20 Global Housing Focus List names have underperformed MSCI World in the last month or so.

## The Five Themes Of This Housing Cycle

A different housing cycle: While the cyclical timing for housing markets is important, we think the current structural backdrop is just as vital. In this section, we highlight the key areas that we think will make this housing cycle unique — covering affordability, supply, mortgage structure, and policy interaction. While there remains significant variation in housing conditions across the economies we cover, the commonality across the themes mentioned in this section allow us to make some broad observations and expectations around how the next housing cycle is likely to evolve, particularly for DM economies:

#1: Affordability improvement delayed, now starting to come through: Affordability remains a key headwind to broader housing conditions, and in most economies is close to its worst levels in the past two decades. There has been little improvement over the past 12 months - a key reason for the reduced cyclicality relative to our forecasts last year. However, with central banks now starting to ease policy rates we have more confidence that affordability is likely to improve gradually from here, while still remaining poor in absolute terms.

#2: Tight supply-demand dynamics even more prevalent: Despite elevated house prices, construction activity across most economies remains subdued and this looks likely to continue near-term: housing permits have notably lagged the stabilisation in both prices and sales of houses. At the same time, housing demand has continued to surge over the past year. Accelerating migration across a broad cohort of economies, including the US, has likely contributed to this increase in demand. While this migration pulse is likely to slow in 2024, it still suggests a tight supply-demand dynamic in the housing market - something that will support rents and prices near-term but should also induce supply over the cycle.

#3: Mortgage structure remains a key source of differentiation across economies: There continues to be a wide dispersion in the pass-through of monetary tightening to mortgage rates - reflecting the vastly different structure of mortgages across economies (from mostly 30Y fixed rates in the US to mostly adjustable rates in Australia). This has implications for the pass-through to outstanding mortgage rates (where Australia still has had one of the largest increases, despite the RBA tightening policy by less than most other central banks). It also continues to provide a "lock-in" effect for several economies (most notably the US) given the large gap between new and outstanding mortgage rates, a meaningful headwind to sales activity.

#4: Housing and rate interaction supports tighter for longer monetary policy: Housing remains a key channel of monetary policy for all economies and a focus for central banks. Housing influences the inflation trajectory both indirectly through its impact on activity and employment, but also directly through housing components of the CPI (see more about housing inflation measures here). The lack of price movement means inflation has been stickier than expected in many economies, despite a contraction of activity. The net effect is likely to be central bank policy remains in restrictive territory for longer.

#5: ESG and government focus to increase: The affordability deterioration for both new purchases and rentals over the past few years has significantly raised the focus of housing for policy makers, from an efficiency and equity point of view. Several governments have already announced measures to improve housing affordability, and we expect this attention to only increase. The announced policies to date have been a mixture of demand and supply-side assistance — given the affordability and construction backdrop we expect a relatively greater skew towards encouraging supply going forward. Additionally, as countries focus on the energy transition, we expect to see more rules and regulations around green buildings and overall sustainable construction.

In Focus: US Housing

## Affordability Improving, But Still Challenged

While US mortgage rates have been volatile over the past six months, they are decidedly lower than cycle peaks of 8% from late October/early November 2023. Given our Interest Rate Strategy Team's forecast for the 10yr Treasury to rally to 3.75% by the middle of next year and our Agency MBS Strategy team's overweight on the mortgage basis, we expect mortgage rates to continue to fall over the forecast horizon. When we combine that with our economists' expectations for nominal income growth of 4.5-5%, we have an environment where affordability is improving.

But even then, affordability is still very challenged relative to history. In Exhibit 14 , we show the monthly mortgage payment as a share of median income. Suffice to say, affordability is going to remain challenged in most conceivable rate scenarios within the forecast horizon. As we stated coming into the year, one of the key questions for the evolution of housing activity and home prices is going to be the level of affordability vs. the direction and rate of change. While the absolute level of affordability will remain challenged, we expect a rate of improvement we have only experienced a handful of times over the past \~30 years.

## Supply Growing, But Still Tight

From the supply front, we think that inventories will increase on the margins, however, we don't think the increase is going to be substantial. As has been the case for quite some time, even this decrease in mortgage rates is not going to be enough to unlock the "locked-in" homeowner. On the demand front, sharp improvements in affordability have not always led to immediate increases in sales volumes. However, the improvements we have seen the past two years have appeared to be a little quicker to stir animal spirits. When we combine that with housing turnover that we think is at or even below the noneconomic volume of transactions ( Exhibit 15 ), the path of least resistance is for sales volumes to increase. The increase in existing home sales will be curtailed by affordability/ tight supply. New home sales have more room to run given the amount of inventory out there.

Growing new home sales volumes and an improving affordability environment should provide home builders with the confidence they need to continue growing single-unit housing starts. Single-unit and multi-unit housing starts in the US have been on very different trajectories over the past two years, and we do not expect the next 12 months to be any different. After decreasing 23% from April 2022 to June 2023, single-unit starts are up 15% since last June ( Exhibit 16 ). On the other hand, multi-unit starts remained at multidecade highs into 2Q 2023. In the \~three quarters since, they are down 30%. We expect single-unit starts to continue to increase over the next 12 months, while 5+ unit starts continue to decrease.

## Pull Through To Prices

As supply increases, the pace of home price growth should slow, but we no longer think that YoY home price changes will turn negative in our base case. The combination of a rapid and robust demand response to recent decreases in mortgage rates with a supply environment that remains historically tight - even if it is growing - will keep home prices appreciating. We estimate the US market to have 4.2 months of supply. Any reading below six months has historically been associated with positive HPA six months later. Ultimately, we think YoY HPA will slow from its current level of 6.4% to 2% by the end of 2024 before accelerating slightly to 3% in 2025 ( Exhibit 17 ).

## In Focus: China Housing

## Property Sector To Be Less Of A Drag In 2025 Amid Supportive Measures

The post-bubble adjustment is seeing light at the end of the tunnel. International experience suggests that the post-bubble adjustment for housing tends to last more than 4 years, with 50% declines in sales volume and 30% price declines. For China, the contraction in housing new starts has been significant as policymakers accommodated leveraged suppliers to exit the market. For housing prices, although the initial decline was slow, the continued relaxation of transaction restrictions has sped up the process in recent quarters, pushing housing prices closer to fundamentals as measured by rental yield or affordability.

Exhibit 18: The bulk of housing supply adjustment in China may have been completed
![](images/41cabf40252e9d590915d5d3c3a5961967d929d1c67446f9d2f1cba0fc17e88b.jpg)
Source: Haver, Morgan Stanley Research

Exhibit 19: Housing prices in China may continue to decline in coming quarters
![](images/8f0dd619fe8b2167db6b5d006d3f5233b9870a32fa5952481d5d0f78cabff55d.jpg)
Source: BIS, Centaline Property, Morgan Stanley Research

A new round of policy easing measures - the right prescription…: The May 17 housing easing package was better than the market expected, triggered by the worsening housing market downturn, with persistent deflationary pressure being imposed on the economy. One of the major positives of the package was a new initiative supporting inventory digestion in addition to the prevailing emphasis on completing ongoing projects. The PBoC announced a Rmb300bn "Public Housing" relending program to help state-owned companies buy unsold homes for conversion into social housing – modest in size but earlier than we expected. This may help shore up liquidity among developers, and the resulting reduction in inventory may also mitigate price headwinds. Meanwhile, the demand boost also came as a surprise, with a nationwide cut in down-payment ratios to their lowest-ever rates.

… but difficult implementation: Policymakers continue to promote and expand the 'whitelist mechanism' – which provides project-based support for home completions, essentially assuming some losses currently borne by previous homebuyers. However, the inventory buyback program may face chokepoints in implementation that might take time to overcome, even though it might be part of the right 'prescription'.

The two major chokepoints are:

1. Adherence to a "market-oriented approach": As only quasi-public funds (relending needs to be paid back and is not interest free) are being deployed, low rental yield from social housing (\~2% at reasonable prices) will likely fall short of the 2.5-3% funding cost, making many projects economically unviable. In turn, local government may need to purchase inventory at a large discount, but that will discourage developer participation and exacerbate pressure on home prices.

2. Aversion to moral hazard: Without clear endorsement from policymakers, local government decisions on "buy from whom" and "at what price" may be subject to anti-corruption investigations in future - we note the ongoing anticorruption campaign in the financial sector is examining some cases that date back more than a decade.

In other words, housing easing remains insufficient not because the pre-approved relending quota fell short of the Rmb3-4trn needed to lower primary inventory to a desirable level - as the quota can be expanded with progress on implementation. Rather, adherence to a market-oriented approach (i.e. no formal public funds) and lack of clear policymaker endorsement would make implementation difficult.

In our base case, successful implementation of ongoing policies gradually reins in the ongoing decline in China's housing market: The announced policies should facilitate faster market clearing, support liquidity among developers, restore home buyer confidence, and (with a lag) improve housing investment - all in a relative and incremental sense. Our property analysts expect the current inventory buying program should be able to reduce viable inventory in major cities to a desirable level by 2H25. We thus expect developers' housing investment growth to remain subdued at -9.3% in 2024 (vs. -9.6% in 2023), followed by a narrowed decline in 2025, to -5% to -6%. Meanwhile, housing sales volume growth is likely to decline a further 14% this year (vs. -8.5% last year), before improving to -3% to -5% in 2025. This would see the housing market have a smaller drag on China's economy in 2025.

## Disclosure Section

Mortgage Backed Securities (MBS) and Collateralized Mortgage Obligations (CMO)

Principal is returned on a monthly basis over the life of the security. Principal prepayment can significantly affect the monthly income stream and the maturity of any type of MBS, including standard MBS, CMOs and Lottery Bonds. Yields and average lives are estimated based on prepayment assumptions and are subject to change based on actual prepayment of the mortgages in the underlying pools. The level of predictability of an MBS/CMO's average life, and its market price, depends on the type of MBS/CMO class purchased and interest rate movements. In general, as interest rates fall, prepayment speeds are likely to increase, thus shortening the MBS/CMO's average life and likely causing its market price to rise. Conversely, as interest rates rise, prepayment speeds are likely to decrease, thus lengthening average life and likely causing the MBS/CMO's market price to fall. Some MBS/CMOs may have "original issue discount" (OID). OID occurs if the MBS/CMO’s original issue price is below its stated redemption price at maturity, and results in "imputed interest" that must be reported annually for tax purposes, resulting in a tax liability even though interest was not received. Investors are urged to consult their tax advisors for more information. Government agency backing applies only to the face value of the CMO and not to any premium paid.
