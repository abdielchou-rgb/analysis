# How Fiscal Reforms Could Affect the Economy

Beijing pledges to ease local government funding pressures with fiscal reforms. However, the proposed tax hikes could make reflation more challenging. We analyze the economic impact of different reform paths.

The rise of 3D challenges has eroded local governments' ability to support growth... In China, local govt shoulders >85% of fiscal spending. However, resurgent secular 3D challenges (debt, demographics, deflation) from 2021 have constrained funding capability, with the structural housing downturn triggering a 40% slump in land sales and massive LGFV deleveraging. Room for on-budget spending is also narrowing, as policymakers have been using supply-side stimulus to address demand weakness amid housing and LGFV deleveraging, rendering increased deflationary pressures and thus softer nominal GDP growth. In turn, augmented fiscal deficit has narrowed by 6ppt to 11.1% of GDP over the past two years.

...necessitating fiscal reforms: The plenum unveiled a 5-year reform plan. Key measures include: (1) increasing central government spending responsibilities; (2) allowing more flexible use of local govt special bonds (LGSB); (3) raising local governments' share in prevailing central-local shared taxes; and (4) expanding the tax source at local levels.

Beijing may accelerate reactive easing, yet with limited economic rebalancing: In our base case, policymakers would allow for more flexible use of long-term CGB and LGSB to accelerate fiscal rollout, and hold off tax hikes in the next 1-2 years. That said, policy support on social welfare reforms and housing inventory digestion would remain modest, given policymakers' aversion to large stimulus and tendency of path dependency, while the status quo of a supply-centric policy framework would keep deflationary pressures lingering. In turn, real GDP growth will likely stay subdued at 4.8% in 2024 and 4.5% in 2025, and GDP deflator would only edge up from -0.5% this year to 0.2% in 2025, and stay <1% in 2026-27.

Risks to watch: Our bull case assumes a Rmb10trn stimulus over the next two years to support social welfare spending and housing inventory digestion, giving a boost to consumption demand by reducing precautionary savings and negative wealth effect. In turn, GDP deflator could quickly turn around from -0.3%Y this year to 1% next year and 1-1.5% in 2026-27, bringing nominal GDP growth back to desirable levels of 6-7%. This would also translate into a more sustained boost to local government's fiscal revenues. In our bear case, a 5-10% premature consumption and local govt. surtax hikes could further erode domestic demand and increase deflationary pressures. This, combined with policy inaction in stemming housing/LGFV deleveraging, would send real GDP growth to 4.3% in 2024 and 3.2% in 2025, with the GDP deflator falling to -1% and -2% and staying <0% in 2026-27.

## Why China needs a fiscal reform

China's fiscal system had heavily relied on a land-financing model: Over the past decades, China has used fiscal policy as the major counter-cyclical tool to weather economic shocks. The heavy-lifting has fallen on local governments' shoulders – under a narrow definition, the burden for 85% of spending has fallen on local governments while they only receive 55% of fiscal revenue in the general budget, and their share in govt expenditure would be even higher if spending in govt funds and LGFVs is included. Before 2021, over one-third of local govt revenue and funding are from robust land sales and excessive LGFV debt buildup, which was based on assumption of a continued housing boom, despite the pace of urban household increases having already peaked in 2016.

But this model has collapsed with resurgent 3D challenges, leading to de facto fiscal austerity over the past two years: China's continued overinvestment defying weakening demographics has led to debt and deflation problems (see China's 3D Journey: A Narrower Path to High Income Status, Sept. 6, 2023). The turning point is marked by the structural housing downturn starting from 2H2021, which has resulted in a 40% slump in land sales and massive LGFV deleveraging, constraining local govt funding capability. As it is, the combined share of land sales and LGFV debt in local govt funding has slipped from the peak level of 36% in 2020 to 27% in 2023 (down from 11% of GDP to 7%). Room for onbudget spending is also narrowing amid declining tax revenue, which has been mainly due to weakening corporate and household income amid persistent deflation, and partly on VAT reform and rate cuts over the past decade. In turn, our estimated augmented fiscal deficit has narrowed by 6ppt to 11.1% of GDP over the past two years.

Local governments' "revenue creation" efforts may also exacerbate deflationary pressures: To increase fiscal revenue, some local authorities have strengthened the collection of non-tax revenues (e.g. penalty, administrative charge), intensified crackdown on company tax evasion (some could be traced back to 1990s), and scaled back some of tax incentives. All of these are contractionary policies in nature and could add to deflationary pressures. This would further constrain corporate and household income and thus tax revenue, forming a vicious cycle.

Relatively limited growth supports from central govt fiscal transfer and rise in LGSB quota: To make up for the local govt funding gap due to land sales shortfall, the central government has increased the size of fiscal transfer, and risen the annual LGSB quota. However, the policy effectiveness is in question, given that: (1) approvals and allocation of fiscal transfers could take time; (2) pace of LGSB issuance has slowed over the past two years, given tighter supervision on the usage of bond proceeds and lack of good-quality projects.

## What Is Beijing's Plan?

To revitalize the ability of local governments to support the economy, the 3rd plenum laid out a fiscal reform plan to rebalance central-local fiscal relations and increase revenue resources for local governments. This should be a five-year plan, given that the plenum vowed to complete the bulk of reform tasks by 2029 (the 80th anniversary of PRC establishment). Specifically, this includes four key measures:

(1) To appropriately increase central government's spending responsibilities. This means room for further central government leveraging up and more flexible usage of treasury bonds. Indeed, soon after the Plenum, Beijing has announced to allocate Rmb300bn ultra-long special treasury bonds (out of the Rmb1trn annual quota) to support equipment upgrading and consumer goods trade-in programs. This makes a small but encouraging step to widen the usage of long-term treasury bonds beyond infrastructure projects, which are having diminishing return amid overinvestment and aging population.

(2) To allow more flexible use of local govt special bonds (LGSB) as seed capital in more areas. The ceiling of LGSB allowed to be used as seed capital has been set at 25% of the annual quota since 2020, but the actual share was only 8-9% over the past four years. This has been due to high criteria on projects, which need to be major infrastructure projects with good cash returns. Thus far the qualified projects have mainly concentrated in traditional infrastructure, such as railways, highways, and airport construction. In this context, should Beijing widen the scope of LGSB usage as seed capital in more areas, it could reduce pressure from local governments to fund seed capital with taxes or land revenues and help accelerate LGSB issuance to support the economy (only 46% of LGSB quota has been used YTD vs. 66% in 2023 and 95% in 2022).

(3) To "optimize" the sharing of prevailing central-local shared taxes: Currently, twothirds of tax revenues are shared by central and local governments, including VAT, corporate income tax, individual income tax, and stamp duty, with local government share at 40-50%. The meeting suggests room for upward adjustments on local govt share in coming years.

(4) To expand local governments' sources of tax revenue: This includes: (1) reforms on consumption tax (the fourth-largest tax in China, with 100% tax income currently attributed to the central govt), by moving the point of tax collection from production to retail stage, gradually reallocating the revenue to local governments, and/or adjusting consumption tax rates and the scope of taxable goods; (2) greater autonomy by the local governments to adjust the city maintenance and construction tax and educational surtaxes – this part of taxes and fees accounted for \~8% of local govt. general budget revenue in 2023.

## How Would Fiscal Reform Affect the Economy?

In our view, the ultimate goal of Beijing's fiscal reforms should be restoring the effectiveness of fiscal policy in reflating the economy. To achieve this, policymakers should: (1) prioritize expansionary policies (e.g. central govt leveraging up, more efficient LGSB issuance) over contractionary policies (e.g. tax hikes), to avoid a vicious cycle of tax hikes – softer demand and more entrenched deflation – weaker corporate & household income – reduced tax revenue; and (2) allow more aggressive central government leveraging up to support social welfare spending and housing inventory digestion – the key to revive consumption appetite and rebalance the economy. However, uncertainty has remained high given policymakers' aversion to large stimulus and tendency of path dependency. We thus lay out three scenarios on the possible reform paths and analyze their respective economic impacts.

## Base case: Continued reactive easing, very modest rebalancing efforts

We believe policy easing will likely accelerate in the near term, though remain reactive... Both the 3rd plenum and July politburo vowed to reach a full-year GDP target of 5%. The pace of incremental easing has also accelerated over the past two weeks, with 10-20bps interest rate cuts, allocation of Rmb300bn long-term treasury bonds to fund equipment upgrading and consumer goods trade-in, and policy guidance to support service sectors (including those subject to regulatory tightening since 2H2021, such as ecommerce, education, and gaming). We view these as small, positive policy steps, and believe policymakers will accelerate the govt. bond issuance by allowing for more flexible usage of bond proceeds from 2H24. That said, these are still moves within this year's budget and thus remain reactive in nature.

...and tax hikes will likely take a backseat: While the Plenum mentioned reforms on consumption tax and city maintenance & education surtaxes to expand local governments' sources of tax revenue, our base case does not assume forming of an imminent action plan. This is because tax hikes in the economic downturn would only add to deflationary pressures and eventually trim the tax revenue, and our study finds that expanding consumption tax to more luxury goods would only bring small increase in tax revenue. Indeed, it is worth noting that the Plenum dropped the wordings on "adjust and optimize the consumption tax rate and the scope of taxable goods" as mentioned in the 14th Five-Year Plan (2021), suggesting to us that a tax hike might not be a near-term priority (see Box: Understanding China's consumption tax reform).

That said, the pace of social welfare reform and housing inventory digestion will likely remain modest in coming years: We have long argued that policy efforts to boost social welfare spending and accelerate SOEs' housing buyback for conversion into social housing would be key for economic rebalancing and thus reflation, as the former could help lower China's abnormally high household saving rate (36.5% in 2023) for consumption, while the latter could help stabilize the housing market and reduce the negative wealth effect on consumption.

However, even though long-term government bonds are being used to support consumption, their potential role in shoring up the social welfare system and housing inventory digestion is still being debated among policymakers. In our view, there remains three policy myths, which would take time to change: (1) concerns about reigniting misallocation in view of the overhang post the "four trillion stimulus" in 2008-09; (2) a tendency of path dependence, in the sense that public spending has been and should continue to be associated with formation of tangible assets (i.e. capital stock) rather than consumption boost; (3) a much stronger aversion to runaway inflation than deflation. But what is concerning to us is that the longer it drags on, the more stimulus would eventually be needed to break the debt-deflation challenge.

We thus maintain our view that reflation remains a long, drawn-out battle: In the near term, while we expect a modest fiscal impulse within the budget to reverse the 1.5-2ppt tightening in augmented fiscal deficit in 1H, our conviction of a mid-year budget expansion remains low given that policymakers will likely prioritize implementation of announced measures given the well backloaded budget rollout YTD. Meanwhile, the status quo of stimulating the supply side with investment may seem to be helpful in defending the real GDP growth trend for now, but it comes with a cost of entrenching deflationary pressures. The slow pace of housing inventory digestion also means that the housing market might only stabilize in 2H2025 or even later. We thus maintain our view of subdued real GDP growth at 4.8%Y for full-year 2024 and 4.5%Y for 2025, with GDP deflator tracking at -0.5% and 0.2%, and inflation over the medium term (2026-27) may stay below 1%.

## Bull Case: Aggressive stimulus to rebalance the economy

We assume Beijing will carry out Rmb10trn stimulus to boost social welfare spending and accelerate housing inventory digestion in the next two years... In our view, to effectively enhance social welfare protection and reduce precautionary savings for consumption, policymakers will need to both increase social welfare provision to 250mn migrant workers without urban Hukou and unify the benefits across social groups, particularly urban vs. rural. This would mean at least a Rmb6-7trn funding gap. Meanwhile, our China property team estimates that Rmb3-4trn is needed to lower primary housing inventory to a desirable level. In this scenario, we assume policymakers will meet both of these funding needs with a Rmb10trn stimulus in the next two years, by issuing long-term treasury bonds and expanding quasi-fiscal easing tools.

...bringing the economy back to a healthier reflation path: Should both of these measures be quickly rolled out from 2H24, it would help restore consumption appetite and stabilize the housing market by late 2024/early 2025, accelerating reflation. We thus expect real GDP growth to reach 5%Y in full-year 2024 and 5.2% in 2025, and the GDP deflator could quickly turn around from -0.3%Y this year to 1% next year and 1-1.5% in 2026-27, bringing nominal GDP growth back to a desirable level of 6-7%. This would also translate into a more sustained boost to local government's fiscal revenue.

## Bear Case: A hawkish policy stance with premature tax hikes

Here we assume Beijing would push out consumption tax /local surtax hikes in next 1- 2 years… Given Beijing's concerns about massive stimulus reigniting asset misallocation, the key downside risk would come from a premature withdrawal of policy stimulus and prioritization of tax hike over meaningful central govt leveraging up in near term, to ease local govt fiscal stress. As it is, the consumption tax revenue was at Rmb1.6trn in 2023 (9% of total tax revenue), and Beijing pledged to attribute the incremental tax income through consumption reform to local governments. Meanwhile, city maintenance and education surtaxes stood at Rmb0.9trn in 2023 (\~8% of local govt general budget revenue), and local governments will have more autonomy in adjusting the tax rates.

...leading to accelerated fiscal tightening and keeping deflationary pressures alive: If we assume 5-10% tax hikes in these two categories over next 1-2 years, it might seemingly boost on-budget fiscal revenue by 1-2ppt, but at the cost of demand destruction given high price elasticity of discretionary consumption demand amid deflation, which could eventually drag on tax revenue. Indeed, we estimate the 5-10% tax hikes could translate into 1-2ppt drag on consumption growth (and thus a 0.4-0.8ppt drag on real GDP growth). This, combined with policy inaction in stemming housing/LGFV deleveraging, would exacerbate the overcapacity problem and thus deflation. In turn, firms could be forced to cut wages growth (particularly for the bottom 20%), triggering household deleveraging and thus an entrenched debt-deflation loop.

As a result, China's real GDP growth could slip to 4.3% in full-year 2024 and 3.2% in 2025, while the GDP deflator could fall to -1% and -2%, and stay in deflationary territory over the medium term.

## Understanding China's consumption tax reform

Consumption tax reform has been a key market focus. While it is mentioned again by the 3rd plenum, we think the possibility of an imminent action plan is low, given that it neither stimulates the economy nor reforms local government's revenue disparity. Its ability to boost local govt fiscal revenue is also in question in the near term, given high price elasticity of consumption in a deflationary environment.

What is consumption tax in China: Different from consumption taxes in other major economies, which apply to most goods and services, consumption tax in China is more of a "punitive tax" on goods discouraged by policymakers, such as luxury items (e.g. high-end watches, jewelry, yachts) and goods considered harmful to health/environment (e.g. tobacco, alcohol, gasoline). The bulk of tax collection is at the production stage, with manufacturers responsible for tax payment. It has been ranked as the fourth-largest tax in China (Rmb1.6trn in 2023, 9% of total tax income), with 100% of tax revenue currently attributed to the central government. Tobacco, fuel, autos, and liquor account for >95% of consumption tax revenue, while the share of luxury goods is merely at 1%.

What would be included in the consumption tax reform? Beijing has been mulling a reform since 2019 to move the point of tax collection from the production to retail stage, reallocate the revenue to local governments, and increase the tax rates and/or coverage. The government said in 2019 that the reform should start with a pilot program on luxury goods (high-end watches, jewelry), before expanding to other categories. It is worth noting that while the plenum this July re-mentioned consumption tax reform, it dropped the wording on "adjust and optimize the consumption tax rate and the scope of taxable goods" as mentioned in the 14th Five-Year Plan (2021), suggesting to us that a tax hike might not be a near-term priority given the prevailing deflationary pressures.

Could consumption tax reform improve local government fiscal conditions?

According to the consumption tax reform plan in 2019, the central government will keep a base level of tax revenue, and the incremental amount above the base through the reform can be attributed to local governments. This means the incremental tax amount to be obtained by local govts will hinge on three factors: (1) amount of tax revenue to be kept by the central govt; (2) magnitude of tax revenue increase with shift of collection point to consumption stage (retail prices>factory-gate prices); and (3) potential of increase in tax rates and/or coverage. In our view, the supporting effect to local government fiscal condition is debateable, considering that:

• Moving the tax collection point to the retail stage/tax rate hike might not give a big boost to tax revenue… Some empirical studies (e.g. Cui and Wang, 2022) suggests that a shift of consumption tax collection from the production stage to retail stage could theoretically rise the tax revenue by \~7%. This, combined with our assumption that the base amount kept by central govt is equivalent to 80%-100% of 2023's total consumption tax, would mean a 1-4ppt boost to local govt on-budget fiscal revenue (or 0.1- 0.3ppt of GDP). That said, a caveat is needed for this estimate: given the relative rigidity of factory-gate prices (i.e. manufacturers would not necessarily cut prices to 100% reflect the tax shift), retail prices will likely increase, which could further discourage consumption and thus drag on tax revenue growth. A hike in the consumption tax rate could have a similar problem.

## • ...and cannot resolve the local government revenue disparity:

Geographically, currently close to 50% of consumption tax revenue comes from central and west China (the relatively poorer regions). A shift of consumption tax collection from production to retail stage would mean more tax revenue to be allocated to more prosperous regions with better consumption, which would eventually increase the central government's burden to provide more fiscal transfers to cash-strapped regions.

Expanding consumption tax to more luxury goods would only bring a small increase in tax revenue: One argument for expanding the consumption tax coverage is that various luxury spending (such as premium clothing brands, luxury bags, private aviation, exclusive club memberships) has not been included. That said, our estimate suggests that a 10-20% consumption tax on these spending might only lift local govt on-budget revenue by 0.3-0.6ppt, while the actual boost could be much less given that the price elasticity of consumption demand on luxury goods would be even higher than basic goods. Meanwhile, the imposition of consumption tax on imported luxury goods would be also in conflict to the VAT cuts on consumer goods imports in 2018-19, the purpose of which was to retain more consumption in the domestic market.
