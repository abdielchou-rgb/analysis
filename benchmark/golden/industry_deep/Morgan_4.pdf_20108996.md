# China: Back to trend-growth in 2H 2024 mid-year outlook

• China’s economic activity started strong in 1Q but softened in 2Q. The volatility was driven by LNY seasonality and changes in policy dynamics.

Policy adjustment since the April Politburo meeting can help avoid a collapse in economic activity in 2Q as observed one year ago. We expect growth will fall below trend in 2Q (3.5%q/q saar) but return to trend-growth in 2H (4.5%q/q saar). Full-year GDP growth forecast is 5.2% in real terms, but just 4.7% in nominal terms.

The lifting factors include investment on manufacturing upgrade, green sectors and exports. While manufacturing investment will likely continue to outperform, exports may face uncertainty amid increasing trade tension and use of tariffs, sanctions and other restrictive measures.

Housing remains the biggest drag. The latest round of housing policy relaxation is insufficient to stabilize housing activity soon, and we have revised down our full-year forecast for housing activity and real estate investment. The new policy initiative to reduce housing inventory is in the right direction, but the pace of implementation is unclear.

Imbalances between domestic demand and supply linger and create deflationary pressure for the economy. We expect the GDP deflator may turn positive in 4Q due to a turnaround in global commodity prices and pork prices, but low inflation pressure will not easily go away.

Policy implementation also face uncertainties. While the April politburo meeting is clear about continuous policy support, the PBOC appears to be cautious on rate and RRR cuts due to concerns about currency and banking stability. We expect a 10bp rate and a 25bp RRR cut for the rest of the year.

The 3<sup>rd</sup> Plenary Session of the 20<sup>th</sup> Party Congress will take place in July. We do not expect major changes in broad approach from the Plenum, and specific policy actions matter more. Still, we are hopeful for positive surprises from fiscal reform, rural land reform and a balanced strategy in promoting productivity growth.

China is facing challenges from geopolitical risk, trade tension and strategic competition with the US, especially entering the US election in November. Trump’s threat to hike tariffs on Chinese goods to 60% poses a major risk for China into 2025.

## A solid start in 1H

China saw a bumpy post-reopening recovery in 2023, with real GDP growth at 5.2%. We had expected the economy to track a recovery path going into 1H24 (5.1% q/q saar) as macro policy had turned growth-friendly since 2H23, before moderating to trend growth in 2H24 (4.3% q/q saar) (link). We also expected the government to set the annual growth target at “around 5%”, which was perceived as somewhat ambitious. As it turned out, economic indicators for January-February beat expectations notably, leading to solid 1Q GDP growth (7.4%q/q saar, or 5.3%oya). The economy’s 1Q strength was led by strong IP, with notable export sector growth , solid manufacturing investment (amid policy support for high-tech, IT and green economy). On the other hand, housing activity and house prices continued to weaken, dragging housing-related consumer spending (Figure 1). In between has been steady, moderate infra-FAI growth, while consumption and service activity also recovered steadily, with support from Lunar New Year-related travel and festive spending.

![](images/da1baed51f0a8dcfa298389b66e8f5ed96ce8e08bbad83555510f1f97a182910.jpg)
Source: NBS, China Customs, J.P. Morgan

Nonetheless, the economy’s solid momentum did not last, and domestic demand started to cool off in March and April. In part that reflected the fading of LNY effects, the reversal of policy support (highlighted by the significant slowing in credit growth in April, along with a rather rare decline in government bond issuance), and possibly policymaker complacency given the upside surprise in 1Q GDP. This has raised the concern of a repeat of the policy mistake last year (too early an exit from policy support), which was only corrected at the Politburo meeting in late July 2023.

The encouraging news is that policymakers seem to have recognized the problem earlier this time, as the Politburo meeting in late April emphasized the importance of policy continuity, calling for an accelerated pace of government bond issuance on the fiscal side (with government bond issuance accelerating to 1.225 trillion yuan in May), and use of rate cuts and RRR cuts by the PBOC on the monetary policy side. To the extent that the housing market continued to be a key drag on the economy, a new round of housing policy relax-

ation was announced on May 17<sup>th</sup> – this not only included additional demand-side easing (relaxation in mortgage downpayment and mortgage rates) but also included a new initiative to reduce housing inventory (300bn yuan re-lending by the PBOC to support government purchase of unsold homes). In response, economic activity seems to have stabilized somewhat in May (link), consistent with our forecast for 2Q GDP growth to moderate to 3.5%q/q saar.

## Economy will return to trend growth in 2H

Looking into 2H24, our forecast is that quarterly growth will return to about 4.5% q/q saar, which is around the economy’s trend-growth pace in our view (link). That would imply fullyear GDP growth at 5.2%. On the one hand, the return of a growth-supportive policy stance since the April politburo meeting could somewhat reduce downside risks in the near term. On the other hand, we do not expect a significant step up in policy support, including meaningful additional fiscal stimulus, beyond what has been approved at the March NPC.

Importantly, regarding top-down policy objectives, the top leadership continues to focus on medium term policy goals of prioritizing the economy’s structural transformation (quality of growth), with ongoing support for strategic new industries including emerging and advanced manufacturing. Meanwhile, the economy’s near-term growth rate is somewhat de-emphasized, provided further downside risks (including the housing sector and local government financing stress) are under control. Indeed, the government takes pride in the success in several new economy sectors (new energy, battery and NEV), encouraging steady progress in green/low-carbon development, and hoping to replicate the success in other new economy sectors. Meanwhile, there remains a debate about the room for further policy stimulus. On the fiscal side, taking together central and local government debts (including local government hidden debt), overall public sector debt has reached 106% of GDP in 1H24 (JPM estimate). On monetary policy, the key policy rate (7-day reverse repo rate) has fallen to a historical low at 1.8%, not far from the zero lower bound, while domestic banks are struggling amid historically-low net interest margins (NIM).

While we assume the room for fiscal support will not go beyond what has been approved at the March NPC, it is worth noting the 2023 fiscal contraction will not repeat this year. The fiscal package implies a shift from significant fiscal contraction last year (with fiscal drag at 1.4% of GDP) to a marginal fiscal easing (0.1% of GDP) this year. We also note the pace of fiscal utilization has lagged behind normal years – the April Politburo meeting has thus called for early issuance of ultra-long central government bonds (1 trillion yuan this year), and accelerate issuance of special government bonds (3.9 trillion yuan special local government bonds this year).

This suggests fiscal policy will be in growth-supportive mode in the coming months. On monetary policy, the PBOC appears to stay cautious. We now forecast a 10bp policy rate cut and a 25bp RRR cut for the rest of the year (both in 3Q), and our 2024 credit growth forecast is revised down to 8.5%. Housing demand remains weak despite the latest round of demand-side easing measures, as demand continues to be dragged down by weak income growth, weak house price expectations and concerns about home delivery. The new initiative to reduce housing inventory is in the right direction, but the pace of take-up remains uncertain at this stage.

We see a few risk factors (both upside and downside risks) for the 2H growth outlook. First, housing remains the biggest drag. Full-year real estate FAI will likely fall by 9.5%oya, worse than expected at the beginning of the year. This implies a larger macro drag, though it can partly be offset by positive surprise from manufacturing investment. The risk to our new forecast seems balanced. Second, policy measures to boost consumption have been muted, which implies uncertainty on the consumption and service recovery into 2H, but also hints at upside risk if policy support for consumption will gain traction. Third, exports have been an important lifting factor (net exports contributed 14.5% of 1Q GDP growth), but trade tension seems to have increased as reflected in recent tariff increase by the Biden administration and the EU on selected Chinese goods and the criticism of US government on China’s excess supply. The risk may significantly increase in 2025, if Trump wins the election and he has threatened to hike tariffs on Chinese goods to 60%.

Last, but most importantly, policy implementation is still full of uncertainty. Reluctance in monetary easing (constrained by concerns about currency stability and banking stability amid NIM compression) may compromise the effort to return to a growth-supportive policy stance. And it is unclear whether important directional changes in economic policy (to address both cyclical and structural problems in China) can be adopted, e.g. removing the policy bias in favor of production over boosting domestic demand, equal support for manufacturing upgrades and service upgrades, and equal support for SOE and non-SOE sectors.

## Lift from mfg, new economy and exports

The uneven sectoral development of the post-reopening recovery in 2023 has been carried over into this year. 1H growth was lifted by manufacturing investment and production, especially for the high-tech industry and new economy sectors, and exports. The key question in a mid-year pulse check is whether that lift will be sustained in 2H24.

Manufacturing FAI has maintained strong growth in the past 2.5 years, averaging at 8.7%oya (compared to 5.4% average annual pace in the four years prior to the pandemic). Its share in total FAI rose by nearly 6%pts in the past few years amid continuing manufacturing FAI outperformance and lingering real estate FAI sluggishness. FAI growth for the high-tech industry stood out (Figure 2), averaging the past 2.5 years at 13.6%oya (vs. 4.0% for overall FAI), with 14.2% average growth for high-tech manufacturing FAI and 12.6% for hightech service FAI (vs. 1.1% for overall service FAI).

![](images/bf598653c27380031479fbb05d5fd4d118b62c125f546d95e0b003aeae1f107f.jpg)

The outperformance of manufacturing FAI reflects policy support to promote advanced manufacturing development. That came against the backdrop of technology transfer restrictions from the US and the West more broadly, and was part of a domestic initiative to increase self-sufficiency on national security grounds and to promote productivity gains amid a structural slowdown in potential growth. Financing demand for manufacturing sector also outpaces other sectors, including infrastructure, wholesales and retail sales etc. (Figure 3). After a solid growth at 9.6%oya in Jan-May, we expect the policy tailwind will continue to support manufacturing investment in 2H, with high single digit growth (around 8.5%) for the full year (previous forecast: 5-6%).

However, most of the strong manufacturing investment and hence relevant production are not digested domestically as domestic demand remains weak. It is exports, the bright spot in 1H supported by solid external demand, that come into play. In volume terms, exports jumped 13%oya in Jan-May (vs. +2.7% in US\$ terms), after the solid 3.5% growth in 2023. The contribution of net exports to GDP growth rose from -0.6%pt in 2023 to 0.8%pt in 1Q24. Even though net export contribution to growth is small, export sector’s importance to the economy should be large if considering its linkage with industrial production and investment (Figure 4) and large employment in exporting sectors.

There has been a lot of attention on the “new three-wonder sectors” (solar cell, lithium battery and new energy vehicles), but their proportion in China’s total exports remains relatively low at 4.2%pts. While recent tariff increases from the US and the EU are alarming, we don’t think they will pose a significant risk in the near term. The year-to-date solid real export growth is more broad-based across major product categories (Figure 5). With lingering domestic deflation and soft demand, exporters will continue to offer competitive export prices. Along with the perception of state subsidy or industrial policy support, the pushback from trading partners may arise in the meantime.

High-frequency tracking warns of container freight cost rise since late April. Several trans-shipment ports witnessed berthing delays, which may trigger broad-based congestion spreading to downstream ports. It happened as the ripple effects from the Red Sea crisis flowed through, along with solid strength in container demand. The latter is due to 1) US/EU retailers’ re-stocking after a post-pandemic inventory destocking and preparation for year-end shopping season, 2) potential front-loading effects with rising concerns of renewed trade war, ongoing port congestions and risk of USEC port strikes, etc. Speaking of overall external demand, our global team expects a modest growth moderation from 2.7%q/q saar average pace in 1H to 2.2% in 2H (link). Hence, underlying external demand will continue to provide solid lift to China’s exports. Taken together, we think exports will remain as one important engine of China’s growth in the next few months. Our baseline forecast looks for net export contribution to GDP growth to improve from -0.6%pt in 2023 to 0% this year, and the risk tilts to the upside.

## Housing bottom needs policy and patience

China’s housing market has undergone a prolonged correction for eleven consecutive quarters, and uncertainty is still high around when a turnaround will take place. Most housing indicators have entered the over-correction phase. As of May, new home sales (in floor space) fell by 51% from the peak in 2021, and new home starts fell by 64%. The share of real estate FAI has dropped from 14.0% of GDP in 2020 to 8.0% in 1Q24, meanwhile the share of real estate and construction in GDP fell from 14.4% to 12.4% (Figure 6). The collapse in housing activity indicators, especially real estate FAI, has led to major drags on economic growth, trimming real GDP growth by 1.5%pts each year in 2022 and 2023.

Despite remaining administrative restrictions, the NBS 70 major cities’ new home prices declined 6.5% by May compared to the peak in August 2021, along with a 12.4% drop for secondary home prices. The historical housing price correction episodes worldwide show an average downturn magnitude at 17% with average duration of 10 quarters. It suggests room for further housing price decline in China if the administrative price controls are removed. At the over-correction stage, there is also a risk of further decline in both housing transaction volume and home prices reinforcing each other.

Figure 6: Real estate FAI and share in GDP
![](images/78053fcae6b858c74840c1238eeb21770d3f171c9ac1b2dee2de298f655c59cd.jpg)

The continued sluggishness in most housing indicators and acceleration in home price declines from the year’s start triggered a new round of housing policy relaxation in mid-May. The PBOC announced two sets of policy support on May 17, with 1) mortgage policy relaxation aiming to further increase home affordability, and 2) the creation of relending facility to support housing inventory reduction. The most frequently asked questions since then are: 1) what is different this time? and 2) when will the policy impact pass through?

The demand-side easing is a continuation of what has been done since 2022, mainly focused on relaxation in mortgage policy and removal of home purchase restrictions (with a notable exception of tier-1 cities that only marginally relaxed the HPR). More than one month after the nationwide easing, the policy effectiveness seems limited so far. This is not a surprise as most investors did not hold much optimism for a meaningful home sales jump engineered by recent policy easing. In the current environment, soft housing demand is more about concerns of further housing price decline and future household income uncertainty. If home sales disappointment continues, market expectations may arise for further policy support. Aside from further relaxation of HPRs in tier-1 cities, other instruments include easing of resale restrictions, exemption or reduction of transaction taxes, e.g., deed tax (1-3%), value-added tax (5%), stamp duty tax for both (0.05%) etc.

![](images/73922b33c1c69176db50c69d60d3281b7b9143935e850efe15ecf8b88796c56c.jpg)
Source: Centaline, NBS, J.P. Morgan

Amid weak home sales, there are notable differences between new home sales and secondary home sales, and between presale and sales of finished new homes recently, driven by concerns about home delivery especially by private developers. New home sales remained sluggish, whereas secondary home sales outperformed. Similarly, pre-sale was weaker than sales of finished new home. This indicates the liquidity stress for private developers remains an issue despite policy efforts (e.g. white list developers and bank support for normal working capital loans to developers). Moreover, despite outperformance in secondary home sales, pressure on secondary home prices may linger with rising secondary listings. The Centaline’s tier-1 cities’ secondary home asking price index did tick up after the mid-May policy easing. But in absence of a forceful pick-up to above 30, historical experience points to further secondary home price declines though likely at a slower monthly run-rate (Figure 7).

A more encouraging move in mid-May was the PBOC’s creation of a 300bn yuan re-lending facility. We think the new policy initiative points in the right direction, positive for China’s housing market supply and demand rebalancing. However, the key uncertainty is around effective implementation. Since early June, several central government level meetings have emphasized the utilization of this new relending policy instrument. Local SOEs and banks are encouraged to get involved in the purchase of finished but unsold projects for transformation into affordable housing with voluntary participation and reasonable pricing to ensure business sustainability. Given a relatively low rental yield in China (Figure 8), such practice may not be commercially feasible for SOEs as the return may just barely cover financing costs even with an acquisition discount. In this regard, some form of fiscal subsidy to improve SOEs’ participation incentive might be needed. But the requirement of not creating new hidden debt for local governments has imposed additional constraints. Our channel checks suggest limited progress of late.

Figure 8: Secondary housing rental yield
![](images/b7ffab32f5f5035a597d0452cab0243bd9b04b8f9a2b15c4603a3442951ba499.jpg)

Overall, our take on recent housing policy easing is that it signals stronger policy determination and official willingness to control downside risks, but policy effects will take time. The housing sector and overall macro economy may have entered a self-reinforcing downward spiral. While housing remains the biggest drag for economic growth, the absence of a solid and balanced macroeconomic outlook has also weighed on housing demand recovery. Policymakers have multiple objec tives and face trade-offs for some of them: 1) ensuring homebuyers’ rights and interest and prioritizing on home delivery, 2) avoiding further collapse of major housing indicators, 3) controlling the spillover risk to the financial system, 4) orderly default of distress developers, etc. The policy delivery is still at a “just enough mode”, and the gradual adjustment approach is insufficient to stabilize the housing market soon.

The housing market development in 1H is in general weaker than what we anticipated at the beginning of the year. We have revised down full-year forecast of new starts and home sales to a 14% and 13% decline (Figure 9, vs. previous forecasts of 10% decline for both of them). For real estate FAI, with the year-to-date decline at 10.1% and lingering uncertainty on PSL fund utilization, we see a higher probability that the offset from public housing (including urban village renovation related construction activity) may not play out in a timely manner before year-end. Hence, we further revise down our full-year real estate FAI forecast to around 9.5% decline (vs. -6-7% previously).

![](images/36a318769a9bfce58bde7c0badac8c63690682fc37c372feb53e82c31ffe2767.jpg)

Going forward, we closely watch three indicators to gauge the housing market outlook: 1) housing price – a narrowing home price decline or price stabilization could help lift home purchase demand; 2) home sales – it represents the most important funding source for developers, and a return to the medium-term equilibrium level is essential for housing market stabilization; 3) SOE developers’ balance sheet condition – if they face significant liquidity distress for debt repayment, the risk of further housing market correction will rise.

## Weak domestic demand and deflation

China has been an outlier in the post-Covid global inflation dynamics. Despite the re-opening of the domestic economy since early last year, CPI inflation has been trending down, registering four consecutive months of CPI deflation through January this year. PPI deflation has lingered on for twenty consecutive months through May 2024. The general deflation pressure reflects the soft momentum in domestic demand (consumer spending and private investment), ongoing weakness in domestic confidence (across households and the corporate sector), and the drag from the ongoing property market downturn.

On domestic demand, consumption contributed 3.9%-pt to the 5.2% growth in 1Q GDP (partly boosted by LNY-related travel and festive spending), though retail sales seem to have lost momentum in March and April. NBS data suggests average disposable income per capita for urban households rose 5.3%oya in 1Q24, outperforming nominal GDP growth of 4.2%oya. Household confidence appears to remain subdued amid uncertainty on economic and job market outlook, especially amongst upper-middle income families working in service sectors facing policy challenges in recent years (education, internet, healthcare, real estate, financial services). According to the PBOC’s urban depositor confidence survey, confidence in employment and income outlook has fallen to the lowest post-reopening level by 4Q23 and 1Q24 (Figure 10). As such, household savings rate remained rather sticky at 31.5%, sa, in 1Q24, compared to average 31.7% in 2023 (and the pre-pandemic level of 30.0% in 2019).

Figure 10: PBOC urban depositor confidence index Diffusion Index
![](images/23be756a8591047bfafacd589ce57fdf6b911477390308c73d3f1156c9913f71.jpg)

Regarding the underlying trend in CPI inflation, headline CPI rose marginally by 0.1%oya in the first five months of 2024, while core CPI showed a modest increase of 0.7%oya (Figure 11). On the positive side, headline CPI inflation has returned to modest positive zone since February. While PPI remained stuck in deflation in over-year-ago terms, in level terms, May PPI showed the first monthly increase (+0.2% m/m sa) since the beginning of the year, with the pace of annual deflation easing to 1.4%oya (vs. -2.5%oya in April). Meanwhile, in the broader picture, it appears production-side activities remain stronger than domestic demand and consumption recovery. Therefore , we expect China will not return to CPI deflation going ahead, but low inflation will continue this year.

In particular, the end of deflation will be driven by: (1) the global commodity price trend (Figure 12), as our global commodity team expects Brent oil price to stabilize towards an average of \$85 bbl through 2H24; (2) domestic pork prices gradually bottom out - indeed the over-year-ago change in pork prices has returned to positive zone in April and May; and (3) the low-base effect kicking in (CPI inflation softened notably from 2Q23 onwards).

The demand-supply imbalance and excess capacity concerns remain notable, while the recent policy intention to support trade-in for home appliances and NEVs may have a modest impact on supporting consumer spending. In addition, amid lingering housing market softness, weakness in house prices and rental costs continue to drag overall CPI - the rental cost component of the CPI basket remains soft, averaging 0.0%oya in the first five months.

Overall, our forecast for full-year 2024 CPI inflation stands at a modest 0.5%. Together with prolonged PPI deflation (though the deflation pressure appears to have eased somewhat lately), the GDP deflator may continue to stay in deflationary territory. The GDP deflator has stayed in negative territory for four consecutive quarters, coming in at -1.1%oya in 1Q24, and will likely remain in deflation zone through 2Q-3Q. Our full-year nominal GDP growth forecast is 4.7%, still one of the poorest performance in modern history of China (except for the 2020 COVID year).

## Policy dilemma for the PBOC

Despite the deflation pressure discussed above, the PBOC has kept the policy rate unchanged this year, even after the Politburo meeting in late April explicitly guided that the PBOC should use rate cuts and RRR cuts to support the economy. Meanwhile, money supply growth and credit growth (both TSF growth and bank loan growth) have continued to move lower to new historical lows (e.g. M1 growth at -4.2%, M2

growth at 7.0% and bank loan growth at 9.3% in May, and TSF growth at 8.3% in April, Figure 13).

Figure 13: Money supply and credit growth in China subtitle
![](images/fe0a58f7bdac754570d045726cd7daa5a04416478bcd1523722b5b8b511b19d2.jpg)

The PBOC has been defensive about its monetary policy, claiming that the current monetary policy stance is supportive (i.e. expansionary, vs. the usual “prudent” monetary policy stance) in that average bank lending rate has continued to move lower and credit growth is higher than nominal GDP growth. The PBOC explains that credit slowdown is not credit tightening, instead it is driven by transformation of economic structure (shift away from high to low credit intensity sectors) and financial market structure (shift from bank loans to capital market financing).

These arguments are debatable. True, interbank liquidity is not tight (7-day repo rate has been stable around the policy rate most time this year) and credit growth is still higher than nominal GDP growth. However, the gap between TSF growth and nominal GDP growth has been narrowing modestly, similarly if credit impulse is measure by the ratio between TSF increase and nominal GDP (Figure 14). In addition, real interest rates in China are higher than historical average levels, in particular real average bank lending rate (adjusted by GDP deflator) hovered around 5% (Figure 15). The current monetary policy stance can hardly be labeled as expansionary.

The discrepancy between the communication and implementation of monetary policy reflects the policy dilemma faced by the PBOC. In a recent speech by the PBOC governor (link), he stated that China’s monetary policy decision needs to balance near-term and long-term policy considerations, balance between growth and risk prevention (financial stability), and balance between domestic and external policy consideration. The multiple-objective policy mandate means that sometimes objectives conflict with each other, and has led to measured policy responses and slow policy moves.

A consequence of balancing between near-term and long-term policy considerations is the adoption of so-called attenuation approach in policy rate decision. That is, China’s monetary policy is not purely based on counter-cyclical framework (e.g. Taylor rule), instead it will need to take into account the longer-term impact. This clearly draws from the policy lesson from 4-trillion stimulus after 2008 Global Financial Crisis, in which episode large stimulus successfully brought the Chinese economy out of a trap but led to structural imbalances to be dealt in the following decade. In practice, the bar for the PBOC’s policy rate is much higher, and the step size of policy rate change is much smaller than most central banks (Figure 16).

Figure 16: Policy rates in China and the US
![](images/320b4139e13497e45d845be0c3e851823493d9e753d4b9172c339fa51db52e5b.jpg)

The balance between growth and financial stability concern also explains the reluctance for rate cuts. Although rate cut is justified by deflation pressure and weak domestic demand, the concerns of continuous net interest margin compression in the banking system and the concern that lower rate may encourage excess risk taking by entities with soft constraints (such as local governments, LGFVs and SOEs, as structural reforms in these areas are incomplete) led to cautiousness in rate cut.

The balance between domestic and external policy consideration, i.e. between price stability and currency stability, imposes another constraint for the PBOC. The resilience in the US economy and persistence in US inflation have led to US rates high for longer, and the interest rate differentials have been supportive for USD strength. The concern about capital outflows and CNY depreciation pressure have limited the pace of the PBOC’s rate cut.

In addition, there is also a debate whether conventional monetary policy (expansionary credit policy or rate cuts) is effective to deal with the current economic problems. Recent regu latory actions to address the idle credit phenomenon, and deemphasizing on the scale of credit growth, illustrate reassessment of policy transmission. This is why the PBOC has expanded the usage of structural monetary policy instruments in recent years, even though it recognizes that these targeted instruments are only temporary measures.

We expect the PBOC will continue to adopt the multiple-objective policy frameworks, and policy trade-offs will lead to measured policy responses. It is more of a question of timing, not a question of whether, that the PBOC will cut policy rates, cut RRRs, and start Treasury Bond trade in the open market. Our forecasts look for one 10bp policy rate cut and another 25bp RRR cut in 2H (both in 3Q), but TSF growth will slow down to 8.5% (vs. 9.5% last year) and bank loan growth will slow down to 9% this year (vs. 10.6% last year).

## Two meetings in July

Two policy meetings in July are worth watching: 3<sup>rd</sup> Plenum of the 20<sup>th</sup> Party Congress (July 15-18) that will focus on structural reform agenda in the next five years, and the late July politburo meeting that will lay out the policy outlook in 2H24. The two meetings are closely watched by market investors.

Investors look to these two meetings hoping to see policy adjustments that the market thinks are important for near-term and long-term growth. Three questions loom. First is whether Beijing will bolster policy stimulus, e.g. additional fiscal stimulus, more aggressive rate cuts and monetary easing, and more forceful housing stabilization measures. Second, is whether the policy direction can be changed to deal with the structural problems in the Chinese economy, namely demandsupply imbalance, deflation pressure and weak confidence

We do not expect big surprises from the two meetings in July, and we think both near-term policy reaction and long-term policy agenda will follow the direction that has already been laid out. The hope for big policy changes in July is driven by the gap between two narratives on the Chinese economy and the appropriate policy responses, as we have emphasized in 10 Questions about China in 2024.

From the policymakers’ perspective, they are aware of the risks of prolonged and amplified housing market correction, insufficient domestic demand and deflation pressure, but the degree of concern is less than most investors. The fact that real GDP growth is still around 5% despite the big macro drag from housing (which dragged GDP growth by about 1.5%pts every year based on our estimate) suggests that nonhousing economy has been expanding strongly especially in new economy sectors. The government assessment is that “the basic trend of economic recovery and long-term improvement has not changed”. This assessment does not change in 1H: although growth momentum appears to soften notably in 2Q, overall 1H economic growth came in stronger than the market expected in late 2023. The probability of achieving this year’s 5% growth target increased, not declined, in 1H24, hence the probability of additional policy stimulus is low.

The gradual and measured policy responses are also related to China’s different policy lessons from the 4-trillion stimulus after the 2008 Global Financial Crisis, which successfully lifted the Chinese economy out of the trap quickly but also caused a series of structural problems that took big efforts for the government in the following decade. This is why the Chinese government kept on reiterating “no flood-type policy stimulus”, and a policy balance between near-term and longterm policy consideration and between growth and risk prevention.

In addition, the policy framework in China has been evolving over time, but it exhibits continuity under the term of the same top leadership. This is why we do not expect sea-changes in policy framework from the 3<sup>rd</sup> Plenum this time (link).

Related to path dependence and policy continuity, there is also regime stickiness in policy implementation. The market frustration is the lack of concrete actions, even if the problem seems obvious and there is consensus support for policy responses. The top example is the proposal to boost domestic consumption, aiming to mitigate demand-supply imbalances and support reflation in the economy. Despite the policy guidance from the very top to boost consumption, by far the only visible policy action is fiscal subsidy for trade-in consumption for NEVs and home appliance products, yet the scale is modest and the impact is small. At the implementation level, it is due to fiscal difficulties for local governments and reluctance from the central government to increase direct support. But the root problem is the policy regime stickiness, that the government believes in supporting investment and production is more effective (creating jobs, increasing income hence lifting both supply and demand), yet has low confidence on the effectiveness of fiscal transfer to households (households may save rather than spend, even if they spend the policy effect is only temporary). More fundamentally, shifting the focus of policy support from investment to consumption is also related to the reform in governance, i.e. a changing role of the government from investment-focused to service-focused.

The Chinese economy is facing challenges from domestic and external fronts, and the government is guiding the Chinese economy in a structural transformation (as reflected in highgrowth policy framework or recent emphasis on building China-style modernization). The focus is different: while investors seem to focus more how Chinese government will manage downside risks (e.g. housing, deflation or more generally the Japanification risk in China), the government seems to focus on how to develop new economy sectors driven by innovations and green transformation.

## US-China relationship and geopolitical risk

Concerns about China also arise from strategic competition between China and the US and geopolitical risks. Since 2018, the US-China competition has spread from trade area (tariff war under the Trump administration and Biden administration’s friend-shoring efforts) to technology and investment restrictions. The wider use of tariff increases, 301 investigations, sanctions, investment restrictions and industry policy has limited the scope for China to learn by opening up. In addition, the Ukraine war in 2022 has accelerated the shift of international trade from efficiency to a security-based trade relationship and global supply chain relocation, which generally disfavor China as the largest manufacturer and exporter in the world.

In response, on May 19, China launched an anti-dumping investigation into imported polyformaldehyde (POM) copolymer originating in the EU, the US, Taiwan, and Japan.

The US election later this year and the policy implications are a major uncertainty for the Chinese economy into 2025. Trump threatened to hike tariffs on Chinese goods to 60% if elected, and will consider removing China’s PNTR status. Recall that, since the 2018 tariff war when the US increased average tariffs on Chinese goods by about 20%, the share of Chinese goods in US imports has fallen by one third, and the share of China’s exports to the US in China’s total exports has fallen by one quarter. China has diversified its exports to ASEAN, LatAm, Africa and Russia (some exports to ASEAN and LatAm are re-export or trans-shipment to bypass higher tariffs in the US) and accelerated moving productions overseas by outbound FDIs. During the 2018-20 tariff war, CNY depreciated against the USD by 13%. In case of a tariff hike to 60%, the impact on US-China bilateral trade, USD/CNY exchange rate and global supply chain could be more disruptive, assuming the same pass-through coefficient (link). The drag on China’s read GDP growth may go to 1.4%pts.

The heightened risk of tariff war 2.0 and the tightened tech restrictions from the US on China have elevated the debate on whether China’s dominance in the critical minerals supply chain could be weaponized as China’s retaliatory measures (link). Meanwhile, China is a major producer of rare earths, graphite and lithium, but its real dominance lies in its processing capabilities. For the US, prolonged export restriction on critical minerals would accelerate the rise of alternatives to China. From the US perspective, if China were to increase export restrictions or implement full bans, short-term risks are skewed towards sectors heavily dependent on critical minerals such as electronics, oil refining, defense and EVs, but volatility is unlikely to last for a prolonged period.

Even though the US election outcome will not change the direction of US-China strategic competition, it will affect the tactics, the areas being affected (trade, technology, financial, climate change, industry policy and geopolitical risk) and formats of competition, and by extension affect investment strategies and China’s trend growth.
