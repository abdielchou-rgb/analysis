## Market commentary

Treasury yields declined across the curve this week. The 2-year rate decreased by 4bp to 4.82% and the curve bull flattened on a week that saw mixed economic data. On inflation, core CPI increased by 0.3% in April, softer than expectations, and the year-ago figure shifted down from 3.8% to 3.6%, the lowest reading in three years (see A less bumpy road in April, M. Feroli, 5/17/24). However, PPI surprised to the upside, with both headline and core rising by 0.5% in April. The core reading was the firmest since last July (US: Upside surprise in April PPI, M. Hanson, 5/14/24). With both CPI and PPI figures in hand, our economists now expect a 0.23% increase in core PCE for April, which would indicate a step lower in the year-ago rate from 2.8% in March to 2.7% in April, but still above the Fed<sup>’</sup>s 2% target.

Meanwhile, retail sales data came in softer than expected. Headline was flat in April, despite a sizable (3.1%) monthly increase in sales at gasoline stations, and the closely-watched core control group declined 0.3% during the month. There were also downward revisions to retail sales figures in prior months. Overall, consumer spending seemed to have softened in 1Q24, and there appears to be less momentum heading into the current quarter. Our economists expect a 0.4% increase in real consumer spending in April, and now see consumer spending running around a 3%q/q saar pace, previously 3.3%ar. They also expect a slightly slower, but above-trend, real GDP growth rate of 2.3%q/q saar for 2Q24, and now look for 1Q24 GDP growth to be lowered to 1.2%ar (previously 1.4%) given the downward revisions to retail sales (see US: April retail sales disappoint, M. Hanson, 5/15/24).

For now, our economists still expect the first Fed rate cut in July but acknowledge that we will need to see some further signs of cooling in the labor market for this to come to fruition. By week<sup>’</sup>s end, Fed expectations haven<sup>’</sup>t changed much week over week: OIS markets are still pricing in the first full cut by November and a shallower easing cycle in 1H25 (Figure 1Fed xpctaions hv’g muwk r:OIS  l f byN   1H25).

In the money markets, Dreyfus became the latest MMF sponsor to liquidate its institutional prime fund as a result of MMF reforms. For its part, Dreyfus<sup>’</sup> institutional prime fund is on the smaller side: it held \$7.3bn in AUMs, of which \$1.1bn or 15% was exposed to credit (TD \$0.9bn, CP/CD \$0.1bn, and ABCP \$0.03bn). To date, a total of 6 fund sponsors have chosen to either liquidate their institutional prime funds or convert them to government funds. Collectively, they held about \$220bn in AUMs as of April-end. Still, it is worth noting that even when we scoped in all the institutional prime funds that will either convert or liquidate entirely, total CP/CD exposure was just under \$20bn as of April-end (Figure 2). This is de minimis in the context of the overall size of the CP/CD market (>\$1.5tn).

To that end, thus far, we have not seen material impacts to the short-term credit market on the back of MMF reforms. In fact, bank CP/CD spreads have actually moved tighter this past week (Figure 3). As we head into the October implementation deadline, it<sup>’</sup>s possible spreads could widen as remaining institutional fund managers shore up even more liquidity (i.e., shorten WAMs) in anticipation of potential outflows. However, as we discussed previously, the scale of the impact as a result of reforms should not be anywhere close to what we saw in 2016. That is, the impact should be much more benign this time around given 1) the smaller size of the institutional prime fund market, 2) a much more diversified CP investor base, and 3) an industry that has gradually moved away from CP/CD as the source of its main investments. Indeed, as Figure 2 shows, a look at the remaining institutional prime funds reveals that they held only \$147bn in CP/CDs as of April-end, or less than 10% of the CP/CD market. Not to mention, the technical backdrop in the CP/CD market remains supportive of spreads, with liquidity remaining ample in the front end. If spreads were to widen, we believe this will be driven more by other factors (see MMF reform spotlight: Not the same as 2016, T. Ho, 5/3/24).

## MMF holdings update: an eventful April

April was an eventful month for money market funds. The mid-month tax season gave way to \~\$140bn in MMF outflows spread across government (-\$118bn to \$4840bn) and prime funds (-\$23bn to \$1366bn), both institutional and retail, as investors pulled out cash to fund their tax payments. Nevertheless, as expected, MMF inflows resumed later in the month (Figure 4). It was also a month of significantly-negative net T-bill issuance, with T-bill outstandings falling by nearly \$200bn in April alone, the first month-over-month decline since April of last year. Not to mention, market expectations shifted dramatically more hawkish last month with regards to Fed policy, with only 27bp of cuts priced in for 2024 at April-end vs. 73bp at March-end.
