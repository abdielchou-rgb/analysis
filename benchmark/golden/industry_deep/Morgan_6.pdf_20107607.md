# The Viewpoint: An Even Shallower Rate Cut Path

With later and fewer Fed rate cuts, we also adjust our forecasts for Asian central banks – projecting a later start for an even shallower rate cut path. The risks are still skewed towards later and fewer cuts. We add one more rate hike for BOJ in Jan-25.

## Key Takeaways

We had expected a shallow rate cut cycle in Asia. But with the changes to our US team’s expectations of the Fed policy path, this will now be even shallower.

We add another hike for BOJ and remove the rate cuts for RBI and BNM. PBOC, BI, BoK, BSP and CBC will delay their rate cuts, we believe.

High US rates will keep Asian central banks concerned about the effects of potential currency weakness on inflation staying durably within target.

We expect real rates to rise to a peak of 2.4% and above pre-covid levels of 1.5%; the growth downside, mitigated by stronger exports, will be modestly negative.

For BOJ, we retain our base case of a 15bps hike in July, but add a 25bps hike in Jan-25, bringing policy rates to 0.50%.

## The path for rate cuts becomes even shallower

Reacting to the data, markets are pricing in a more hawkish Fed rate path: Our US economics team believes that the significant volatility in the data over the past three months has made it difficult for the Fed to extract signal from noise. As such, markets have shifted the pricing for the first Fed rate cut to September (from July), and they expect fewer than two 25bps rate cuts this year (vs. three cuts as of end-March). This in turn meant that Asian currencies have broadly weakened over the past week. The Bloomberg Asia Dollar Index – which tracks the performance of EM Asia currencies against the dollar – depreciated by 0.5% over the past week. Our FX and rates strategists have also flagged that the outperformance in the US economy could continue to weigh on Asia FX and rates (see Asia Macro Strategy: Three Themes in Asia Local Markets, April 12).

Our US team expects the Fed to cut from July and we delay our Asia rate cut call: Taking on board a better growth outlook due to an expanded supply side and their read that the recent inflation data did not meet the bar for a June rate cut, our chief US economist Ellen Zentner has pushed back her expectations for a first rate cut to July. She and the team now expect three cuts in 2024 (vs four earlier) and another 100bps in 2025 (vs. 200bps earlier) For more details, please see US Economics: Outlook Update: Near-Term Inflation Against a Positive Supply Shock, April 14. Taking on board the changes to the Fed path forecasts, we are also revising our forecasts for the rate cut path for Asian central banks, which we detail below.

## What has changed?

The Fed and currencies remain constraints, not trailing inflation: We have been highlighting that Asian central banks would not cut ahead of the Fed (see The Viewpoint: Why Asian Central Banks Will Not Cut Ahead of the Fed, Feb 26), and had highlighted previously that Asian central banks would likely delay their rate cuts too if the Fed delays its rate cuts due to continued US data strength (see The Viewpoint: What if rate cuts are delayed? Apr, 8). The change in our US economics team’s forecasts means that we take on board this scenario as our base case.

Moving towards later and fewer cuts: We now expect central banks in the region to begin cutting from August onwards (vs. late June previously) once the Fed has embarked on its first rate cut in July as our US team expects. In terms of timing, we now expect BI to deliver the first rate cut in August (vs. late June previously), BOK to cut from October (vs. July previously), and CBC to cut from 3Q25 (vs. 1Q25 previously). For BSP, we now expect a later start in 1Q25 and fewer cuts (100bps now vs. 150bps previously). We are also removing the 50bps rate cuts for RBI and BNM we had penciled in over our forecast horizon on account of both the Fed policy path and also robust growth in India alongside reasonable growth in Malaysia. We are adding one more rate hike for BOJ in Jan-25 and now see PBOC cutting one quarter later from 3Q24.

## How do our forecasts compare with market pricing / consensus?

Our forecasts are marginally more hawkish than markets: For economies where market pricing data is available, our new policy rate forecasts are on aggregate marginally more hawkish relative to markets. By economy, this is largely driven by our policy rate paths for RBI and RBA, and can be explained by our more constructive growth outlooks for India and Australia. In particular, our chief India economist Upasana Chachra recently upgraded her FY25 growth forecast for India to 6.8% (vs. 6.5% previously), while our Australia economist Chris Read attributes his more hawkish policy rate forecast to sticky services inflation from housing & wages alongside positive fiscal impulse given large income tax cuts. On the other hand, we are more dovish on the BOT rate path vs. markets given our view that current policy rates are too restrictive. We also see a lower terminal rate for BOK in this cycle relative to markets on account of our US team’s more dovish rate path for the Fed. For BOJ, our new forecasts are largely in line with current market pricing.

## Japan

For Japan, our chief Japan economist Takeshi Yamaguchi maintains his base case of a 15bps hike in July to bring policy rates to 0.25%, but now expects BOJ to take up an additional 25bps rate hike in Jan-25. This is given 1) the more hawkish recent comments from Governor Ueda that BOJ would consider reducing the degree of stimulus if inflation gradually accelerates as economic and price conditions move in line with BOJ projections, 2) a more hawkish Fed path, and 3) the risk of higher inflation in 2H24 on the back of a weaker JPY and the strength in the Spring wage negotiations results.

## India / Malaysia

For India and Malaysia, we remove the shallow 50bps rate cut cycles we had penciled in for RBI and BNM respectively, and now expect the two central banks to remain on hold over our forecast horizon on the back of stronger growth outcomes. Our chief India economist Upasana Chachra had previously highlighted the risk of a delay to rate cuts/no easing from RBI amid better-than-expected growth driven by capex and productivity (which would imply a higher neutral real rate). For Malaysia, growth has continued to track at a reasonable clip (reaching 3%Y in 4Q23), and alongside continued weakness in the currency means BNM will likely remain on hold through 2025. In our view, should growth and/or inflation in these economies decelerate markedly vs. our forecasts from here on out, then we could see risks that could revive the possibility of rate cuts.

## Korea / Indonesia / Philippines / Taiwan

For Korea, Indonesia, the Philippines, and Taiwan, the delay to the rate cut cycles is predicated on the fact that these economies are more sensitive to FX movements. Given recent depreciation pressures, the central banks are cautious in their approach as they may consider that recent developments could impart upside risks to the inflation path. Inflation for Korea and Taiwan is currently tracking above the central banks’ targets/

comfort zones, and for the Philippines it is expected to rise above the target range in the coming months. In this sense, rate cuts, if implemented too soon, could invite further depreciation pressure and bring potential upside risks to inflation. Indeed, recent moves and comments by these central banks do indicate continued caution over inflation.

## For example:

• BOK continued to show strong caution against the uncertainty regarding the inflation path at their April meeting (see BoK Review: Back to Undecided, April 12).

• CBC raised policy rates amid renewed concerns over a structural upward shift in inflation against the backdrop of the April electricity price hike (see CBC Review: Surprise Hike, a Final Adjustment, March 21).

• BI governor has continued to highlight that the key for the rate cut path would be for the rupiah to strengthen “until it stabilizes around a level that indicates inflation is no longer a concern.”

• BSP revised up its inflation forecast to 4%Y in 2024 (vs. 3.9% previously) at its April meeting. In an interview post the monetary policy meeting, the governor flagged risks that inflation could breach 4% for the third year running as well as guided that rate cuts may only happen in 1Q25.

## Thailand

On Thailand, we see a fundamental need for BOT to cut nominal rates as real rates are currently above real GDP growth, suggesting that monetary policy is currently very restrictive. Headline inflation has slipped into negative territory over the past six months and core inflation is tracking at just 0.4%Y in March. We expect rates to be lowered to 1.5% from the current 2.5%, as we see that level of rates as being consistent with the economic backdrop, which we believe is similar to 2015-18. The risk to the rate outlook is either a delay to the start of the rate cut cycle or a slower pace of rate cuts once they start. Policymakers have been highlighting that they believe the economic recovery is still unfolding and that there are some structural issues which are holding back growth at the moment, which are not to be addressed with rate cuts. Moreover, the low level of nominal interest rates in a relative context and a backdrop of a weak THB will likely add to policymakers' caution on cutting rates quickly. Against this backdrop, we expect that rate cuts in Thailand will likely only happen in August after the Fed has cut and that we will require more softness in the economic data.

## China

On China, our chief China economist Robin Xing continues to expect PBOC to take up modest easing in the form of a 20bps cut in the policy rate and a 25bps cut in the reserve requirement ratio on the back of persistent deflationary pressures as evident from the 1Q24 GDP deflator, currently tracking at –0.8%Y. That said, stronger near-term real growth amid support from external demand and production, along with the delay to the Fed rate cut path, means he now expects PBOC to begin cutting from 3Q24, vs. 2Q24 previously.

## As real rates stay higher for longer

In the near term, economies can manage with higher real rates…: Our new forecasts suggest that Asia ex China ex Japan real policy rates will remain somewhat higher than pre-Covid average levels over the coming quarters, reaching a peak of 2.4% before moderating. While our earlier forecasts indicated that the central banks’ rate cuts would help bring aggregate real rates back to near pre-Covid levels by 4Q24, we now see real rates remaining \~30bps above pre-Covid levels of 1.5% through 1Q25.

…as better US growth boosts exports and in turn capex: Given that rate cuts are delayed because of stronger US GDP and job growth with better supply dynamics, we believe Asia could also benefit from the positive offset of stronger exports and the attendant positive spillover on investment. As a result, the overall downside risk to the growth outlook would be modest. As it is, external demand is already improving. Real export growth in Asia has moved higher to 10.9% in Jan-Feb vs 5.7% in Nov-Dec and Korea’s latest export figures suggest that there has been momentum which carried over. While the export strength is driven more by tech at the moment, we expect this to broaden in the coming months. The global PMI new orders to inventory ratio – which tends to lead regional exports by 5-6 months – has been inflecting higher and has stayed above 1.0 for the past five months. As has been the case in the past, higher exports will have a positive spillover to investment trends in the region, especially for the more tradeoriented economies like Korea, Singapore and Taiwan. As for the domestic demandoriented economies like India and Indonesia, we see structural factors supporting capex growth. The continuation of these positive trends will help offset the downside from higher real rates for a few more months.

# Further delays and higher oil prices could bring downside risks to the growth outlook

Watchful of potential downside risks: We believe that downside risks to the growth outlook could emerge from two factors – a further delay in rate cuts till beyond 1Q25 and if oil prices spike to US\$110-120/bbl and are sustained there. For the first factor, the extent of the downside risk would have to be evaluated in the context of the strength of the external demand environment at that point, and for more domestically demand oriented economies, how strong the support from positive idiosyncratic factors will still be then.

If oil prices rise to US\$110-120/bbl in the next 3-4 months due to supply or geopolitical concerns in a sustained manner, this would create concerns over the inflation outlook. Higher energy prices would lead to higher headline inflation pressure and may impart upside risks to the inflation outlook. Historically, the region’s central banks have focused on the inflation impact of these higher energy prices rather than on the demand destruction aspect of it. Against this backdrop, we think that central banks in the region would be held back in cutting rates. The combination of the supply shock and central banks not being able to cut rates would constrain aggregate demand more meaningfully as compared to scenario 1. Ten of 12 economies in the region are net oil importers and would be affected, but the likes of Thailand, Korea, the Philippines, and India, which have higher CPI sensitivity to oil price increases and run wider oil deficits, would be more exposed.

BSP has hiked the most in the region as it has faced the most intense inflation challenge. We do see some impetus to cut rates by the fact that growth momentum is softening but concerns on the inflation outlook will likely dominate for the time being. We foresee a start to rate cuts in 1Q25, with a total quantum of 100bps over the course of 2025.

We expect CBC to start cutting in 3Q25 with total of only 25bps vs 37.5bps previously. With our US team’s expectations for the first rate cut from the Fed delayed to July now, we believe the CBC likely waits for a sufficient period of time before engaging in its own rate cut cycle. We believe CBC sees the need to keep inflation under control for longer, as demonstrated by the recent surprise hike to 2%. We also recognize stronger exports and capex activity and the positive wealth effect from strong domestic equity markets performance and its support to domestic consumption. Overall, we see Taiwan’s terminal rate at 1.75% by end-2025.

With real rates still tracking above real GDP growth, we see a need for cuts to nominal policy rates in order to reduce the restraints of monetary policy on the economy. However, policy makers have been hesitant to cut by highlighting that the economic recovery is still unfolding and that there are some structural issues which are holding back growth at the moment, which are not to be addressed with rate cuts. Moreover, the low level of nominal interest rates and a backdrop of a weak THB, will likely add to policy makers' caution on cutting rates quickly. Against this backdrop, we expect that rate cuts in Thailand will likely only happen in August after the Fed has cut.
