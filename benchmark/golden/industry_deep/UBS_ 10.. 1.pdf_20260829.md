## SUMMARY

1) What would US fiscal policy look like under a ‘red wave’? What about under divided government?

Our estimation of the net cost of the Democratic and Republican proposals is much closer together than commonly thought. We project fiscal deficits under a red wave to be less than a ½ pp of GDP higher, than under divided government, with deficits exceeding 7% from 2028 onwards, under both scenarios. The additional growth impulse under a red wave is, by extension, also very small. While Republicans and Democrats differ on many of the details, there is bipartisan support to extend the TCJA (the Democrats would only exclude the top 1%). Republicans are more expansionary in a number of areas (e.g. corporate taxes) but they would also go further in clawing back Inflation Reduction Act funding, and would likely use tariffs to fund some of the proposals. Importantly, spending caps are already locked in for 2025; so the tax code would change in the 2026 tax year, with impacts not visible until ’26 and ’27.

2) What is trade policy likely to look like? What would be the impact of 60% (China) and
10% (rest of world) tariffs?

A 60% tariff on China and 10% on the rest of the World (RoW) would increase the weighted average by 3x as much as in ‘18/19. The revenue impact of those measures would be eroded by the fall in demand for tariffed goods, and if one assumes something similar to the earlier China tariffs, US imports would fall 17% and customs duties as a share of imports would revert to a level not seen since 1935. Taking into account the required legal process to implement tariffs, we think it is unlikely new tariffs on China are in place before mid-25. Tariffs on other countries likely not until 2026, and we believe it is possible that the RoW tariff is for a smaller set of countries. The China tariffs alone would add 36-130bp to US core inflation (we believe towards the lower end of that range). RoW tariffs would need to be added to that, but come later.

## 3) How much deregulation can we expect? (+ what to expect in energy)

Democratic administrations have implemented “economically significant” regulation (impact exceeding \$100mn) significantly more aggressively than Republic administrations. The Biden administration has delivered the highest average number of such rules per year (65.3), followed closely by the Obama administration (62.1). While the Reagan administration delivered by far the fewest, at 19.9, the Bush 41, Clinton, and Bush 43 administrations tracked closely together, each averaging between 44 and 45 "economically significant" rules per year. Excluding 2020, the Trump administration implemented 39.3 per year, on average. Our energy team views the potential changes coming in energy (e.g.more friendly regime towards pipelines) as mixed for the oil price (more supply, but also more demand with less focus on fuel efficiency standards).

## 4) What happens if the border is shut?

The vast majority of US population growth is now due to immigration, and looking ahead, by extension, all else equal, most of the labor force growth too. Closing the border gives the labor market less room to run, lowers potential growth one-to-one, and pulls forward inflationary pressures. While Mexico-US relations may strain over these issues, we expect a generally cooperative stance (Mexico sent its National Guard to the southern border in 2019 to prevent Central American migrants making their way north).

## 5) Would the US outperform if Trump wins a clean sweep?

A Trump clean sweep would lead to US equities modestly outperforming owing to Tax cuts, de-regulation, potentially a weaker dollar and tariffs helping the competitiveness of US companies (relative to the rest of the world). Regions at risk are those with high operational leverage, trade surpluses to the US, and exposure to China. The critical issue is whether global growth can accelerate and China can recover. Most at risk from tariffs are: Korea, Germany, Japan. Least at risk India and UK.

6) Will Trump be able to weaken the dollar?

Trump has on several occasions expressed a desire for a weaker dollar. We see little prospect for anything resembling a multilateral accord (a la the Plaza agreement), nor do we see FX intervention as likely. There is no obvious evidence of misalignment (other than JPY) and we doubt that other countries will want to strengthen their own currencies. Tariffs have been touted as leverage to bring about a general agreement to weaken the dollar, but perversely the effect tariffs themselves has the opposite effect (stronger USD).

## 7) How much will the curve steepen and how?

The bear steepener is the consensus red sweep trade, but we are cautious about the curve impact of a sweep. Historically trends in supply can be swamped by much larger shifts in demand in shorter horizons-- any sharp economic slowdown will generate more than enough demand to absorb the supply. Even in the red sweep scenario, nominal auction sizes should be flat until Q2 25, with relatively modest increases in 2025 and 2026. It is not until later in the decade that the big duration shock will hit as growing maturities combine with high deficits to cause auction sizes to rise sharply. We see the curve modestly steeper than forwards over both the 1y and 2y horizons. We prefer to position for a red sweep through long inflation breakevens. Real rates should drive nominal rates lower as the US slows down.

## 8) How will China (and Chinese assets) respond?

We estimate that a 60% US tariff hike on imports from China would have 200-250 bps GDP growth drag. Stimulus can offset about 100bp of that, but we doubt we see outright QE or zero rates, and see limited tolerance for currency weakness. Our survey of Chinese companies suggests most have already diversified their supply chains, limiting the direct earnings impact for listed companies. However, firms are vulnerable to a sharp slowdown in nominal growth and increases in foreign interest rates. In 2018/19 the equity market reacted adversely on the day of tariff announcements. From a sector perspective, low beta sectors (agriculture, banks, and healthcare) were among the best performers while TMT fared the worst (though 2 weeks after the announcements export oriented firms were at the bottom).

## 9) What are the best trades to position for a Red wave?

Given vastly different starting points of the economy and risk premia, Trump 2.0 is unlikely to play out like Trump 1.0. We see long 5y inflation, long JPYCNH, long gold and long US vs European equities as the best ways for a red wave.

## 10) Which sectors win under a clean red sweep?

The S&P 500 advanced 21.5% in the year following Trump's 2016 surprise electoral victory. However, the “Trump trade” (defined by sector and factor leadership) lasted just 1 month post-election. We believe financials would be the clear winner under a red sweep, on less regulation, more robust nominal GDP, a steeper yield curve and increased M&A activity. Financials performed best following the 2016 election, and second best (after Energy) following the debate and assassination attempt. Cyclical groups (save Discretionary) are also likely to outperform following a red sweep. By factor, Value and economically-sensitive stocks are likely to outperform if Republicans win. We believe a continuation of current tax policy is already discounted in stock prices.

## Bonus Essay: Fed independence – could it be affected?

Congress oversees the Fed and can legislate change, though support for its independence has tended to be bipartisan. The most direct way for the White House to influence monetary policy is through the appointment of the seven members of the Board of Governors. The only terms ending during the next 4 years are those of Kugler and Powell. If the President were to invoke the “for cause” clause to remove a Fed governor or Chair, the Supreme Court would likely need to adjudicate. Although the President does not appoint the regional Federal Reserve Bank presidents, in theory they could be removed by a majority of the Governors, who are appointed by the President. A new Chair will likely be appointed in 2026, but it is unlikely that any one individual could sway monetary policy decisions if many other FOMC participants disagree.

## 1) What would US fiscal policy look like under a ‘red wave’? What about under divided government?

Fiscal policy will be front and center for the next administration with large parts of the individual and some elements of corporate taxation enacted in the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA) expiring at the end of 2025.

Figure 1 summarises the policy differences and the likely deficit impact relative to current law under a divided government or a red wave where the majority of the choices are to extend existing tax provisions rather than implementing new tax cuts. On the individual tax side, in a divided government, we think there is scope for an increase in the top tax bracket, as has been proposed by the Biden administration, while the red wave would likely see a full extension of the TCJA brackets as featured in the Republican Party's 2024 platform. Other differences could come in choices on thresholds for estate tax, which could be lower under a divided government, and qualified business deductions for sole proprietors could also be less generous in the divided government scenario. One policy put forward by Former President Trump which featured heavily at the Republican National Convention is the elimination of income tax on tip income. While the policy has garnered attention on both sides of the aisle, we have assumed that the extent of the deduction would be greater in a red wave than in a divided government.

On the corporate tax side, we think that there is likely bipartisan support for the global taxation policies implemented under the TCJA — GILTI, FDII and BEAT — to be maintained, meaning these likely feature in both a divided government and red wave scenario. One key difference on the corporate side, is that we think the corporate tax rate could be lowered to 20% in the red wave while remaining at 21% in the divided government scenario. Other provisions where we may see bipartisan support could be around reforming tax rules for crypto and other digital assets, as proposed by the Biden administration. We could also see some compromises around work requirements but the claw back of Inflation Reduction Act funding would likely only feature in a red wave scenario. We are also not including assumptions around trade policy here — the revenue from which could possibly offset some of the cost of the tax policies. There may be some differences in the areas for compromise with a divided government under a Republican or Democratic President — like estate tax thresholds or tip income — but ultimately the broad contour would likely be relatively similar.

Federal spending caps are basically locked in for 2025 and the expiring tax code wouldn't impact the 2025 fiscal year deficit, so the 2024 and 2025 deficit and growth implications are similar (Figures 2 & 3). The timing of policy changes would see the tax code changing for the 2026 tax year, with impacts visible in both 2026 and 2027. The FY 2026 deficit would likely widen more in the red wave than in a divided government scenario as tax rates rise for the top income group relative to tax cuts for corporations in the red wave scenario. The impact on growth is calculated as the impulse on Q4/Q4 GDP growth under each scenario, not relative to the CBO baseline under which fiscal tightening is estimated to drag on growth through 2026 and 2027. Under a divided government we estimate a \~0.2 pp drag on Q4/Q4 growth in 2026 and very marginal positive contribution, 0.03 pp, in the red wave scenario. Relatively lower tax burdens for both individuals and corporates in the red wave scenario may be more supportive for near-term growth, but results in more deficit widening.

Both a red wave and divided government would see a large deficit widening relative to current law in which the TCJA provisions expire. Over the 10-year budget window the deficit is estimated to widen by \~\$4.1 trillion in a red wave and \~\$3.1 trillion in a divided government, leaving the deficit running over 7% of GDP from 2028 onwards (Figure 4). Spending cuts could be enacted to try to cover the impact of lower revenues but it is hard to see where these cuts would come. As it stands, debt held by the public as a share of GDP would surpass its historic peak (106% in 1946) by 2028 in the CBO baseline, 2027 in a divided government and 2026 in a red wave (Figure 5). This comes with rising net interest cost burdens, Figure 6, which alongside the increasing cost burden of mandatory programs as the population ages, squeezes discretionary spending with this declining by \~1 pp as a share of GDP between 2024 and 2034. Neither of these scenarios address the risks to the US outlook from mounting debt and as the CBO state lawmakers will likely "feel more constrained in their policy choices".

## 3) How much deregulation can we expect? (+ what to expect in energy)

We expect that on balance, a second Trump administration would favor deregulation compared to the current Biden administration, which businesses would likely perceive as a friendlier environment. As the chart on the top right shows, Democratic presidents have implemented "economically significant" regulations at a notably faster pace compared to Republican presidents."Economically significant" rules are regulations issued by executive branch agencies that meet the definition as given in section 3(f)(1) of Executive Order 12866: "have an annual effect on the economy of \$100 million or more or adversely affect in a material way the economy, a sector of the economy, productivity, competition, jobs, the environment, public health or safety, or state, local, or tribal governments or communities."

In the years since the Reagan administration, the Biden administration has delivered the highest average number of such rules per year (65.3), followed closely by the Obama administration (62.1). While the Reagan administration delivered by far the fewest, at 19.9, the Bush 41, Clinton, and Bush 43 administrations tracked closely together, each averaging between 44 and 45 "economically significant" rules per year. Excluding 2020, the Trump administration implemented 39.3 per year, on average. Costs of proposed regulations were also much lower under the first Trump administration, and then rose sharply under the Biden administration (see the first chart below). In the first three years of the Trump administration, the costs of planned regulations averaged \$29.8 billion per year, while in the first three years of the Biden administration, that figure rose to \$100.8 billion. The Trump administration actually implemented regulations costing \$201 billion in 2020, many of which were not proposed and pertained to the COVID-19 pandemic.

Counting regulations, regulatory costs, and the strength and direction of their cumulative economic impact is a difficult effort, and the actual macroeconomic impacts could be ambiguous. Research by Richard Morgenstern, who served in the Environmental Protection Agency from the Reagan to Clinton administrations, found that when new environmental regulations were applied, job losses commenced in some industries, but losses were offset by jobs created in pollution abatement. Looking at the historic performance of the NFIB small business optimism index, as well as the Russell 2000 index (small-cap) compared to the Russell 1000 index (large-cap) it is possible that small businesses could perceive a Trump victory as an economically significant event. Following the 2016 election, the NFIB small business optimism index rose more than 11 points from prior to the election in October 2016 to right after the Trump inauguration in February 2017. The Russell 2000 performed notably better than the Russell 1000 in the days following the November 2016 election.

Our energy equity research team sees potential changes to US energy policy under a Trump administration as mixed for the oil price in the medium-term. In the near-term, we believe a Trump victory would likely be taken slightly negatively for oil. In a broad sense, a Trump victory would be incrementally supportive of US oil & gas production and so incrementally negative for oil prices. The Biden administration has adopted two energy policies that would be liable to be rolled back under a second Trump administration: restriction of oil and gas leasing on federal lands and restriction of pipeline development. In the event of a Trump victory, we would expect his administration to be more inclined to allow leasing and development on federal acreage, which could provide a boost to production. Additionally, if there is a more friendly regulatory environment towards pipelines, it would be supportive of more production because pipeline bottlenecks might be eased, allowing producers to grow and access end markets easier.

In the short-term, we would not see a large jump in production, due to the six to ninemonth lead time from drilling a well to getting volumes to the market, and by the November election, 2025 programs will generally already be budgeted and have less flexibility to move. Medium-term, we may see an increase in production, particularly from private operators, but there’s also an issue of available inventory to drill, so ultimately pricing at that time will be the biggest determinant of the decision to drill, and less so regulation. On the demand side, we see his policies as more likely to be supportive for oil in the long-term. The Trump administration would likely less focused on fuel efficiency standards, and less supportive of a push in EVs over ICE vehicles.

Looking at the past several decades, excluding the 2020 pandemic year, Democratic administrations have implemented new regulations significantly more aggressively.

## 4) What happens if the border is shut?

On former President Trump's campaign web site www.donaldjtrump.com, the first policy listed in "Agenda 47" is the "open border." By all accounts imposing more order on the US southern border appears likely to be a top policy priority for a next Trump administration. In addition, the Biden administration on July 10 announced new tariffs on steel shipped from Mexico to the US, " meant to address the evasion of tariffs by China" said news reports. Mexico could find itself in the middle of two policy challenges facing the next administration.

We published a deep dive into immigration in March. We estimated a surge in net international migration had substantially boosted US population growth and in turn labor force growth in the US, following the borders reopening and travel restrictions lessening in 2021. We walked through the academic literature on the impact on nominal wage growth. In early 2022, we published a note estimating the most pronounced wage gains appeared in segments of the labor market most impacted by the border closures. That came despite the shift in demand from services to goods, and the domestic labor supply shock that disproportionately affected older workers.

In the Census estimates below, immigration appeared to fall from 2016 to 2019, the first Trump administration. Second, population growth attributed to domestic births and deaths has slowed sharply, as seen in the gap between births and deaths, in the chart below. In 2007-2008 the native population growth was 1.9 million. By 2016-2017, that had fallen to 1.1 million (or 0.62% of the resident population). In the most recent estimates, 2022-2023, that had fallen to 0.5 million (or 0.15%). In other words, the vast majority of US population growth is now due to immigration, and looking ahead, by extension, all else equal, most of the labor force growth too.

In figure 27 we show a typical growth accounting estimate of US potential GDP growth, the structural rate of growth of the US economy. The age 16 and over population growth feeds one-to-one into that estimate (see this link for another example). Having run the experiment during the pandemic, lessening immigration too far could repeat that phenomenon in an economy with segmented labor markets. Slower potential growth could also have implications.

While Mexico-US relations may strain over these issues, we expect a generally cooperative stance. Indeed, the recent tariff announcement came with a joint announcement from the two nations' Presidents. In June 2019, President Trump threatened to impose trade tariffs unless Mexico took action to reduce the flow of migrants. Within weeks, Mexico sent its National Guard to its own southern border to prevent Central American migrants making their way north. Amidst US threats of section 232 action over Chinese steel, Mexico proceeded to increase tariffs on over 500 Chinese products in order to pre-empt US retaliatory tariffs.

Coming up in 2026, could be the reopening of USMCA. USMCA’s “sunset clause” can be triggered by any of the three signatories to the treaty as early as July 2026 (six years after the treaty came into effect). Rules of origin and minimum content requirements in manufacturing will be central to the renegotiations. The discussion will not only be on the level of what minimum regional content will be required for a product to obtain zero-tariff treatment under the treaty, but more importantly, on the definition of that minimum content. Last year, the US lost an arbitration panel ruling on minimum content rules on autos over definition of content. Specifically, Canada and Mexico contend that if a Chinese part is imported into North America, and then transformed into a new part with Mexico-based value added, and that new part is then used in the assembly of a car, then that new part should be considered 100% North American from the perspective of minimum content rules. The US contends the original Chinese content should be tracked and recognized as such through the production process. The US may have lost that arbitration process, but come 2026, we think it will try to ensure its definition of minimum content prevails.

## 6) Will Trump be able to weaken the dollar?

The idea that a second Trump administration could try actively to weaken the US dollar has tangible market resonance as November’s US elections approach. As recently as 17 July, in the transcript of a Bloomberg interview, Trump was quoted as saying “so we have a big currency problem because the depth of the currency now in terms of strong dollar/weak yen, weak yuan, is massive” before adding that in his first term he actively pushed competitor countries to work to avoid further currency weakness, including through threats of tariffs. These sentiments have been echoed by Trump’s vice president nominee JD Vance, who has spoken of the possibility of dollar devaluation in order to boost US exports.

In terms of what can be done to weaken the dollar, the idea of a “Plaza 2.0” has garnered some attention. The original Plaza Agreement of 22 September 1985 saw finance ministers and central bank governors of US and some key trading partners such as Germany and Japan agree that "some further orderly appreciation of the non-dollar currencies is desirable" and that they "stand ready to cooperate more closely to encourage this when to do so would be helpful". News of the agreement led the dollar to fall 4% on the Monday after the statement, followed by a multi-year depreciation. The trouble with this premise is that dollar overvaluation is not widely perceived to be a problem at the moment, and the odds of a coordinated attempt to weaken it seem very low. China in particular may not wish to join in with a coordinated policy that requires it to strengthen the yuan. The yen is a possible exception, due to its undervaluation linked to Japan’s loose monetary policy. So while a bilateral US- Japan agreement is possible, this alone would be unlikely to be a game changer for overall US trade flows. Thus the hurdles to weakening the broad dollar through this coordination channel are high.

If direct intervention becomes the chosen path, the Treasury’s Exchange Stablization Fund is the most obvious vehicle for enacting this. As of its May 2024 report the fund had total assets of \$206bio, of which around \$14bio are already held in foreign currency and \$168bio in IMF SDRs, with the rest mostly in non-marketable US Treasury securities. A mechanism exists that allows for the SDRs to be placed as certificates with Federal Reserve Banks in exchange for dollars, and these could potentially be converted into foreign currency. Given that previous US interventions are few and far between and have rarely exceeded \$1bio in size (see Figure 31US FXintervo haslyb >\$1z), selling levels in the 10s of billions of dollars would be a shock to the market and a major statement of intent. Still, to put it in perspective, recent Japanese interventions to protect the yen have come in chunks of \$20-70bn (see Figure 32Recnt Japsirvo hbmu lgf ), with very limited impact in terms of generating a yen appreciation trend, at least until hopes of BOJ rate hikes recently rose too. Direct intervention would be more likely to work, if underlying fundamentals were also supportive and the dollar was already weakening.

An alternative direct approach would be intervention by the Fed under the premise of avoiding disorderly FX markets. In principle, this allows for enormous Fed balance sheet growth and overwhelming volumes of intervention through FX reserve accumulation, as seen in Switzerland in the past decade with the SNB's balance sheet growth. In practice, the political and institutional hurdles to this pushing for approach seem high.

Tariffs remains the most obvious means with which to threaten trade partners in order to bring about a general agreement to weaken the dollar. The problem with this strategy is that tariffs would also have the effect of making US imports more expensive and thereby put upward pressure on the dollar, especially if growth sentiment in more fragile economies such as EM is hit hard too. So the irony is that this key tool Trump might wish to use to push for a political arrangement to weaken the dollar is very likely to strengthen it first. Especially as it's questionable whether the dollar really is dramatically overvalued; it was much more so in the mid 1980s. The size of the US current account deficit is modest at just over 3% of GDP, compared to over 4% as recently as 2022 and over 6% in the mid-2000s. In conclusion, the most obvious path to a weaker dollar still likely lies through reduced US exceptionalism on growth and relatively lower US interest rates, as opposed to specific FX-focused policies. Direct policies to weaken the dollar, while not impossible to rule out, are hard to implement, inconsistent with other policy aims or simply unlikely to bear fruit.

It is very questionable whether threatening tariffs as a tool to pressure competitors can weaken the dollar. More likely the dollar would strengthen instead.

## 8) How will China (and Chinese assets) respond?

We estimate that a 60% US tariff hike on imports from China would have a 200-250 bps drag on China's real GDP growth in the following 12 months, which may be reduced to 100-150 bps with policy support and trade diversion effect. CPI may be 100bps lower than our baseline projection due to weaker demand and more supply at home. Here we assume China does not retaliate in kind. Retaliation may put further downward pressure on real growth and upward pressure on inflation. We expect China's policy response will rely mostly on fiscal policy, which could include tax rebates or relief to corporates, especially exporters, and increased social and infrastructure spending. Monetary policy will be eased further with 100bp or more MLF rate cut, and 10-year CGB yield may decline by 50-100bp to \~1.5%. That said, we think outright QE or cutting rates to zero are still unlikely. We believe the government values a relatively stable currency in the context of financial stability more than any benefit of depreciation.

On the Renminbi exchange rate, the response might not be linear. A US tariff hike would put depreciation pressure on the renminbi, which will likely be amplified by the responding interest rate cuts by PBC. To what extent the renminbi depreciates depends also on the US dollar and on whether the US imposes tariffs on other countries. If only China faces tariffs and USD does not strengthen vs other major currencies, China would likely allow some weakness in USDCNY. Given the pressures from the tariff hike and resulting rate cuts, we think the renminbi could be allowed to depreciate 5-10% against the US dollar but the PBC will likely defend it more seriously at various key points between 7.5-7.8, and is unlikely to let USDCNY go beyond 8.

We have surveyed UBS analysts on c.400 China stocks under our coverage for their estimate on the potential impact of a 60% US tariff on their companies' earnings. For many listed companies, it would seem that they've already diversified their supply chains such that the direct earnings impact from a 60% US tariff would be quite limited at c.1% for those affected (or less than 0.5% of listed company earnings). For MSCI China, we believe the earnings impact has been somewhat priced in for certain sectors with some of the most affected sectors such as machinery underperforming the market by 5% YTD. For the secondary impact, we estimate the lower nominal GDP growth could erase c.5-8% from companies' top and bottom lines. Higher global inflation and interest rates could also be negative to PE multiples with every 100bps increase in the US 10 year interest rate historically resulting in a c.1x PE multiple de-rating.

The export theme has outperformed the broader A-share market by a wide margin YTD, and therefore it is likely that A-share investors have yet to price in the impact of a 60% blanket US tariff. Looking at the 2018 trade war experience, on average there was a c.3% negative shock on the first trading day after the four tariff announcements. Most major indices reclaimed a portion of the loss subsequently as investors started to factor in fiscal and monetary easing. From a sector perspective, low beta sectors (agriculture, banks, and healthcare) were among the best performers while TMT fared the worst. Two weeks after the announcements, however, traditionally export-oriented sectors like home appliances and autos ranked at the bottom while electronics fared much better as investors might have bought back for the technology self reliance theme.

## 9) What are the best trades to position for a Red wave?

Muscle memory from 2016 will likely mean the markets rise going into the election. However, the rally in risk assets is unlikely to sustain this time. US’ domestic and global contexts have changed dramatically since Trump 1.0. In brief, the big differences between then and now (Figure 49) are: 1) The US economy is much more late cycle today, implying limited room for earnings upgrades, 2) Both supply and demand of US debt are utterly changed, 3) The market is already pricing not just low, but lower, tax rates, 4) Already tight risk premia leave little room to further lift valuations (Figure 50), and 5) global, particularly, Chinese growth is lower.

Our recommended trades are guided more by the state of the economy and the profits cycle, than by election outcomes. The gap in fiscal, growth, inflation, issuance outcomes under a red sweep and divided Congress should be limited, especially in the first two years of the next Presidency. Over the medium term, driven by a) higher tariffs, b) lesser migration, and c) an increase in term premium, a red wave may see a worse growth – inflation mix than a divided Congress scenario. In terms of sequencing, higher inflation is likely to be seen before weaker growth. Given long lags of policy set up implementation, it is hard to position based on these views right away, and so in picking our trades to position for Trump 2.0 we want to be careful not to pay away a huge amount of carry. Across assets we prefer the following trades to position into and post the US election.

1) Long US 5y inflation: While real rates are still elevated, inflation breakevens are not far from pre-covid averages. In swaps, markets imply y/y CPI will fall to 1.85% in April 2025, before returning to close to 2.5%. TIPS breakevens are even lower. UBS expects inflation to average above the market implied level in coming quarters. We therefore expect breakevens to carry positively, with risk-reward in favour of a higher inflation riskpremium (Figure 51US 5ybreakvn iflto dOFR ). While tariffs are a level effect, they could add 30-40bps to US inflation in the first year of their implementation. The major risk to this trade would be an economic slowdown that overwhelmed any policy-driven inflationary effects. That risk can be hedged by being long rates. Rates are much more volatile than inflation, so we suggest a 1:4 ratio for the two exposures (long rates and long inflation).

2) Long JPY v CNH : Trump's tariff proposals could easily translate to a 5-10% shock on CNH, sending the pair to potentially 7.80 or so next year (CFETS basket to 90-92). This isn't reflected in forward FX derivatives. For instance, Dec-25 USDCNH 7.70 digital option (5.5% OTMS) is trading at only 11% - cheapest in a decade (Figure 52). In 2018- 2019, every 1% of tariffs was equivalent to about 0.4% shock to CNY TWI (Figure 53RMB tradewigh nxUS foC ps u2018-9 ). This is undoubtedly a crowded trade, but for good reason, we think. If there is a case anywhere in G10 where both sides might agree to push for a step change in the relative exchange rate, possibly via a bilateral rather than a multilateral “Plaza 2”, it is USDJPY. JPY may hurt from weaker global trade at the margin, but will benefit from lower US rates as the economy weakens, should also benefit form higher volatility. Buying the JPY against the CNH rather than the USD reduces the negative carry on this trade by 45%.

3) Long Gold : The decline in rates from here is likely to be vol accretive (due to weaker growth) rather than vol destructive (due to weaker inflation). Gold is among the few expressions that will work as a dollar negative in the context where US rates are falling but risk premium is rising. Fears of dollar debasement because of higher US public debt will also play out in favor of gold. We do not see the positioning of Gold as being crowded, and would only be worried about sentiment on precious metals becoming euphoric should the Gold to Silver ratio drop to the 62-65 range (\~83 currently) (Figure 54). Our current target for Gold is 2600, but we see risks to the upside.

4) Long US v European equities: A Red wave increases the risk that global GDP growth does not accelerate above 3.5%. Our view on European sales and earnings is already below consensus and this could be downwardly revised were China growth not to pick up . While there could be brief spells of US underperformance if Tech falls on of regulation, we think a worse growth-inflation mix in Trump 2.0 could see higher equity volatility and underperformance of cyclicals, which should hurt Europe more. European defence and construction stocks could continue to do well amidst NATO fears and talk of Ukraine reconstruction, but this will not be enough for market to outperform. In the US we would prefer Financials, Energy, Software under a red wave, whereas in Europe we would be defensive on Industrials.

## 10) Which sectors win under a clean red sweep?

In evaluating which sectors and factors (investment characteristics) are likely to benefit most from a “red sweep”, we focus on 4 key inputs: (1) policy differences, (2) policy timelines, (3) the market response to the Trump/Biden debate and the assassination attempt, and (4) the market's response to the 2016 Trump electoral victory.

Investors expect less regulation, and higher inflation, interest rates and nominal GDP under Trump. We assume that more restrictive trade policy, especially towards China, will not go into effect in the early stages of his administration. This was the case in 2016.

The S&P 500 advanced 21.5% in the year following Trump's 2016 surprise electoral victory. However, the “Trump trade” (defined by sector and factor leadership) lasted just 1 month post-election. Given the market’s response to the Trump/Biden debate and assassination attempt, it appears that election outcomes are not fully discounted.

## SECTORS

Financials: This highly regulated group should benefit most from a Trump victory on stronger nominal GDP (banks), increased M&A (investment banks), and a steeper yield curve (life insurers). Financials outperformed most following the 2016 election, and second most following the Trump/Biden debate and the assassination attempt.

Cyclicals: Cyclicals led following the 2016 election, as well as the debate and assassination attempt. Industrials benefited in all instances. Energy underperformed following the 2016 election, but was the top performer following the debate and assassination attempt. Materials outperformed post-election, but not more recently. Importantly, both Energy and Materials benefit from less regulation (more drilling). Discretionary was less impacted during each of these periods.

TECH+: This important group is neither economically sensitive, nor highly regulated.
Based on its history, it is not particularly sensitive to election outcomes.

Health Care: Trump has espoused “most favored nations” pricing on pharmaceuticals, a headwind for the group. However, several companies (Humana, United Healthcare) would benefit from changes to insurance rules. It has historically traded in-line with the market around election outcomes and events.

Defensive Sectors: Less economically-sensitive stocks lagged or performed in-line with the market post the Trump victory and recent events. REITs underperformed following the 2016 election, but outperformed around recent events. Defensives are more domestic, making them less sensitive to trade issues, but more sensitive to tax policy.

## FACTORS (Investment Characteristics)

Value: Low P/E stocks led following the Trump election and recent events. While Financials and Cyclicals tend to be low P/E, our calculations are sector neutral. Put differently, low P/E stocks outperformed across groups.

Growth: High Growth stocks were undifferentiated following the 2016 election, and were laggards around recent events. This is less a comment on TECH+ stocks, and more about the relative performance of faster EPS growers within each sector.

High Tax Rate Companies: Heading into the 2016 election, it was assumed that companies with higher effective tax rates would benefit most from tax cuts. In practice, the impact was more uniform across companies. Analysts are discounting a continuation of current tax policy. We do not believe taxes will be addressed legislatively until late 2024. Returns of high tax rate companies were undifferentiated around recent events.

Economically Sensitive: Beta to the market, economy and interest rates were rewarded following the 2016 election, but were mixed around recent events. We expect upside on these factors should Trump win on stronger nominal GDP and rates.

## Bonus Essay: Fed independence – could it be affected?

In a note published a few days ago, we reviewed the literature, history and institutional relationship between the White House and the Federal Reserve. Among the main points:

1. First, Congress plays an important role in the Fed’s oversight, and the Fed is ultimately accountable to the public and the Congress. Initially (from 1913-1935), the Treasury Secretary and Comptroller of the Currency sat on the Federal Reserve Board. True independence waited until 1951. The legal mandate as we know it today (maximum employment and stable prices) waited until the 1977 Federal Reserve Reform Act. Congress then set numerical targets for unemployment and inflation in the 1978 Humphrey Hawkins bill and required a report to Congress on monetary policy twice a year. More recently, in 2010, the Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act limited the Fed’s emergency powers. Congress oversees the Fed and can legislate change, though support for its independence has tended to be bipartisan.

2. The most direct way for a White House to influence monetary policy is through the appointment of the seven members of the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System. The President nominates Governors. The Senate confirms them. Changing governors can be a slow process, by design. Each term is 14 years, with starts staggered two years apart. Only Governor Adriana Kugler's term ends before 2028 (January 2025). Chair Powell’s term as Governor is up in January 2028 (his term as Chair ends May 15, 2026). According to Section 10 of the Federal Reserve Act the President can remove Governors “for cause,”a threshold that has been interpreted (link) as “inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance,” not policy differences, insulating Governors from threats of removal.

3. The President also appoints the Chair to the Board of Governors, subject to Senate confirmation. The statute does not say whether the "for cause" threshold applies to the Chair. Should the President attempt to remove the Chair, clarity from the courts would likely be needed to resolve the issues in prior precedents and limits of extending "for cause" protection. Any removed Chair, however, could remain as a Governor. Any new replacement Chair would need Senate approval. In addition, the interest-rate setting FOMC elects its own Chair.

4. The 12 regional Federal Reserve Banks are separately incorporated, with boards of directors selected by each district's member banks, who select the reserve bank presidents. The Board of Governors approves their appointments. The Board of Governors could potentially remove them (link). In theory, if the President managed to appoint a majority of governors to the Board, those governors may be able to remove reserve bank presidents, and then have effective veto power over the choices to replace them.

5. Presidents have often been at odds with Fed Chairs. To the extent one party controls both the White House and Congress, the influence over monetary policy could shift, not only via nominees, but because legislation becomes a potential course of action. That could include how the FOMC sets its inflation target, or how aggressively it is pursued.

Overall, the prospects for potentially slow turnover among Governors the next few years could limit the number of appointments to the seven member Board of Governors. The long terms could keep many current FOMC participants in place for most of the next President's four-year term. Chair Powell's term as Chair ends May 15, 2026, and he has expressed his intent to finish his term. Thus, the next administration's Chair may not be in place until May 2026, 1 year and almost 4 months after the inauguration. Historically, not all Chairs have won all policy votes, either, and for some FOMC's many votes were close. Chairs may or may not wield sufficient influence to forge policy decisions that fit their views if many other FOMC participants disagree. Thus, initially, we would expect any new administration would have little unilateral sway over monetary policy decisions, even though tensions between the institutions might rise.

Over time however, the degree of White House influence may grow. Historically, turnover on the Board is faster than the scheduled long terms. The more nominations any next administration puts in place could increase the influence it may have over the Federal Reserve, particularly if the same party had Congressional majorities that wielded the potential influence from Congress.alongside that of the administration.
