# Venture Vision: The Rule of 40 - US

Don't underestimate the power of simplicity. Venture investors swear by the "Rule of 40". Meanwhile, public market investors are generally unaware of it. This global mini-series aims to cast a light on its importance for active managers.

Since 2010, the S&P500 has delivered a 3.9x return. The large cap Rule of 40 stocks in the index delivered a 14.5x return (equal-weight). Mid cap Rule of 40 names have delivered a 6x return over the same period.

![](images/25fce699820fe8b988213959616db34d7b38266dbeb3a1ea452a2ddbea83cb89.jpg)
Source: Refinitiv Eikon, Morgan Stanley Research; Note - For this analysis. we consider large and mid cap to be >\$100bn and >\$40bn market cap respectively.

What is the Rule of 40?: The Rule of 40 is a rule of thumb in venture/growth markets (predominantly used for SaaS businesses) that simply ranks businesses on a combination of revenue growth YoY and EBITDA margin or cash margin (i.e. 60% revenue YoY & -15% EBITDA margin = 45%). Other iterations include the Rule of 20 and Rule of 60. The same principle applies. It is a blunt tool. A high-level screen to homogenise and benchmark thousands of otherwise incomparable businesses. Principally it matters because it allows Venture GPs to screen but also to focus on corrective measures for those falling below this hurdle.

But to what degree do public market companies deliver on this threshold and how does it translate into annualised returns? In the US, the annual average outperformance of the Rule of 40 strategy has been 7% or a median benchmark outperformance of 8% annually. For the large cap Rule of 40, these figures rise to 10% and 12% average and median annualised outperformance versus the equalweight S&P500 back to 2010.

Private companies are now over the hurdle again: According to SVB's latest figures, the median company in venture/growth markets is now back in Rule of 40 territory. At peak in 2021, 20% of companies were achieving greater than the Rule of 40. By early 2023, the median privately funded company had dropped below the Rule of 40, as focus on cutting burn, extending runway and moderating growth were the top priorities being pushed by GPs. Since then, a more resilient US consumer and rising hopes of rate cuts have propelled the number of companies surpassing the Rule of 40 back to more normal levels. Particularly as the S&P500 has recovered, the pivot back to a growth focus in private markets has become clear. However, just as private markets are rebounding on the Rule of 40 metric, public companies surpassing this level have fallen back to pre-Covid levels.

Where is public market Rule of 40 versus history?: The Nasdaq has generally performed better on the Rule of 40 than most indices globally; hardly surprising, given the growth tilt. While the last 30 years are divided above and below the Rule of 40 threshold, the recent period (YTD) has fallen back below the threshold.

How does 2024 look compared to prior years?: Despite the Rule of 40 being formed of a combination of a revenue growth and an EBITDA margin component, through time it is the former that largely dictates the outcome of the overall score. Below we have grouped the Nasdaq100 component stocks in all recent years but then specifically juxtaposed 2024 vs 2021; the "growth at all costs" year.

In 2021, 100 businesses grew top line >30%; this has fallen to 34, 16 and 4 in subsequent years. Even setting a 10% revenue growth hurdle, 334 companies achieved this revenue expansion in 2021; this fell to 185, 149 and now 33 expected by consensus this year. Meanwhile, EBITDA margins for the component equities and at the index level were broadly comparable over this time frame.

How have recently listed Rule of 40 stocks performed?: While we concede that the Rule of 40, 20 or 60 are blunt tools, they are important when it comes to thinking about the readiness for a business for IPO. If we look at the performance of SPACs during 2021/2022, >90% of these businesses were unable to sustain a combined growth and EBITDA margins >40% once listed. Their performance in public markets has proven commensurately weak c-40% from peak (albeit +21% from trough), according to Pitchbook's index of SPACs since Jan 2020.

Strong outperformance: We nonetheless note that those companies most frequently surpassing the Rule of 40 do show remarkable consistency in beating the index. It is not a case, necessarily, of achieving the Rule of 40 the most number of times that dictates performance. Rather, it has been a combination of consistent delivery of Rule of 40 but specifically for mid- and larger-cap companies.

We caveat here, however, that most outperformance using this metric occurred during the 2010-2022 window of time. The AI trade which has dominated the performance of the market cap weighted S&P500 has meant that the Rule of 40 equal-weight calculations we make above have underperformed a resurgent market cap weighted S&P500 in recent months.

Many of the most frequent companies to surpass the Rule of 40 are AI plays (see above); consequently, if we take the last 5 years and benchmark the E/W S&P500 against the Rule of 40 leaders, the outperformance has continued. More recently, the Rule of 40 names to have outperformed have been larger companies that have surpassed the hurdle less frequently through history. This, again, is a function of a handful of AI companies that have not hit Rule of 40 for some years, but which are having a growth and margin inflection.

## FINTECH RADAR - ADAM WOOD & GIULIA MIOTTO

• Nala raises \$40m in Series A: This week (9th July), African remittance app Nala announced that it has raised \$40m in Series A funding. Reports from the press state that the raise was led by Acrew Capital, with participation from DST Global Partners, Amplo, and previous investors. This follows a \$10m raise in 2022 through Accel, Amplo, and Bessemer Partners. Nala, which serves nearly 500,000 customers worldwide, enables users to send money from Europe, the UK, and the US to 11 African countries, almost instantly. The firm is also working to build out its own B2B platform for fast payments in Africa, which will be supported by the \$40m cash injection. According to the company over the past 12 months it has increased its revenue by 10x and over the past 20 months, has increased its volume by 34x.

• Currensea exceeds £1m crowdfunding target: Last week (2nd July), travel debit card company Currensea launched a £1m crowdfunding campaign at an implied pre-money valuation of £28.5m. According to reports from the press, the initial £1m target was reached within a few hours, and at the time of writing, the campaign has raised over £2m from a group of over 1,000 investors (after the target was extended due to the level of demand). The crowdfunding campaign comes after Currensea hit 100k cardholders in March, and according to the company, they are adding 8k cardholders on a monthly basis. It also claims to save travellers at least 85% on every overseas transaction, competing with the likes of Revolut and Wise for travel spending.

## Real-time valuation monitor:

A simple top-down metric to look at is the Refinitiv Venture Capital Index, which provides an aggregated and rebased index of US venture capital-funded firms. The index:

• Leverages Refinitiv private company data of over 22,000 US firms;

• Estimates the value of each company over time by compiling known firm values, estimating missing values and interpolating values between events; and

• Aggregates month-to-month returns to compute a market cap-weighted index tracking the broad US venture capital universe since 1995.

While the log-scale sees a more modest pull-back in valuations of US VC-backed companies, the index as a whole is nonetheless nearly 18% down YoY and 40% off its highs.
