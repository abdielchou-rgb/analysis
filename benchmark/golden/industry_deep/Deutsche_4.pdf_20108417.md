# Risks to Asia's resilience

Asia remained resilient in Q1, with two-thirds of the economies reporting GDP growth stronger than consensus forecasts. Although the region is benefitting from an export recovery led by AI chips this year, there are increasing risks to its growth path beyond 2024. Indeed, the upcoming US elections could be the most consequential political event for Asia in decades, as they could mark the beginning of genuine global fragmentation. While the US has reduced its direct imports from China, China's expansion of its global footprint has raised concerns and scrutiny of the role of third markets. While the US has opted for significant tariff rates, these have been applied only to selected Chinese goods. However, the US has also called for a "strategic" and "unified" front against China's "industrial overcapacity" as the EU inches closer to hiking tariff rates on selected goods from China.

As our baseline assumption does not include broader, significant tariff rate hikes, in the event these risks actually do materialize, we see Asian exports contracting next year, not expanding according to our prevailing baseline view. Ironically, though, increasing anticipation of tariff rate hikes and/or other trade restrictions may see this year's export grow more strongly than expected if firms opt to preemptively stockpile, albeit with a negative payback in 2025, while limiting their investments and strengthening their financial buffers instead.

This risk arrives at a rather sensitive time, as Asia faces a high wall of maturing debt. An external shock like this would likely see a renewed surge in FX volatility, raising the bar for rate cuts by the region's central banks. As we noted in an earlier report from 2022, Asia's most significant vulnerability relative to its EM counterparts is its high domestic debt, although the situation regarding its external and sovereign debt risks has fared better. Despite narrowing credit spreads, monetary tightening since 2022 has left refinancing costs higher, calling for vigilance over financial stability risks, especially in the face of potential growth shocks.

This report contains a detailed analysis of the Asian economies under our coverage.

While the number of harmful trade restrictions has surged in recent years, tariff rate hikes have only made up about 9% of the total. Subsidies (excluding export subsidies) have dominated, accounting for about 40% of the total, followed by contingent trade-protection measures (\~16%) and export controls and subsidies (\~14%). Although the actual impact will be less than a percentage point increase in the US's effective tariff rates on China, tariff rate hikes on selected goods such as EVs (by four times to 100%) were significant. According to our European colleagues, the EU Commission is also likely to hike the tariff rate on Chinese EVs, albeit by just 10-20%, from the prevailing 10%. While the incremental hike may be less, it could be more consequential for China, as it holds almost one-fifth of the EU market share. In short, we may be approaching an inflection point in global trade, especially as we head to the polls in the US.

Although the export recovery is contributing to Asia's growth, this may not last through 2025. In the event of significant tariff rate hikes after the US elections, Asia's exports may contract instead of expanding in low single digits, per our baseline view. Ironically, in the near term, rising anticipation of tariff rate hikes and/ or other trade restrictions next year may see firms preemptively restocking, providing an additional boost to exports this year, but with a significant negative payback next year. Despite stronger exports, such an alternative scenario would limit investment recovery as firms adopt a wait-and-see stance and strengthen their financial buffers against potential negative shocks. In short, the recent positive economic developments – disinflation and the demand recovery amid continued payroll gains, and the export recovery – could well reverse in such an alternative scenario. Worse still, in the event of an an all-out trade war, the region would likely suffer stagflation.

## Economy

China's April economic data remains mixed. At the aggregate level, we estimate that output growth was at 5.1% YoY in April, below 5.3% in Q1 but better than 4.7% in March. The industrial sector outperformed significantly, with 6.3% YoY growth in April, due to stronger export deliveries. April exports growth rebounded to 1.5% yoy from -7.5% in March, marking a significant improvement in all major trade partners and continued robustness in automobiles. Government policies to boost replacement demand for autos and equipment may also have contributed to strong industrial activity. The labor market improved as well. Meanwhile, domestic demand softened, with retail sales (2.3% YoY), investment (3.6% YoY), and services output (3.5% YoY) all reporting slower growth from March and missing market expectations. Property prices and new-home sales dropped further, underpinning the need for additional policy support for the sector.

CPI inflation was stronger than expected. Headline CPI inflation improved by 0.2ppt to 0.3% YoY, beating market expectations of 0.2% and our forecast of 0.1%. On a seasonally adjusted basis, CPI rose 0.24% MoM in April, marking the highest MoM gain since 2023. Energy prices were a key driver of higher inflation, contributing 0.2ppt to headline CPI YoY growth. Food prices also performed slightly better than seasonal patterns. Core CPI was stronger than expected, with MoM growth of 0.3%, outperforming seasonal patterns in recent years, and YoY growth edged up a tenth to 0.7%.

Tourism activity during the Labor Day holiday saw continued improvement from previous holidays, indicating a good start for May. Visitors and spending were up 7.6% and 12.7%, respectively, from the same period last year. However, average spending per person is still lower than pre-pandemic levels in 2019. The movie market showed a similar trend – total revenue was at the same level as in 2023, but the average price was 7% lower than the 2019 level. We think the divergence in volumes and prices was likely due to incremental domestic travelers mostly coming from the lower-income groups, whose income recovered as the economy reopened; a larger share of higher-income groups traveling abroad; and smaller towns and cities gaining popularity.

## Policy outlook

The Chinese government announced another policy package to support its property market, perhaps the largest and most comprehensive yet. It contains a wide range of measures that aim to achieve multiple goals, including boosting housing demand, reducing housing inventory, and channeling fresh government funding to the housing market to support developers. More specifically, the package includes: (1) local governments buying back idle land lots from developers; (2) local governments purchasing excess housing inventory from developers; (3) pushing more ongoing housing projects to meet the standards of the "white list" and requiring commercial banks to support reasonable financing needs; and (4) more easing on home loans.

The package will likely reduce developers' risk of default and modestly boost their investment... We estimate that over CNY1tr in additional funding will be provided to developers – CNY0.5tr of bank lending and CNY0.5tr or more from localgovernment special bonds. If the new measures are implemented effectively, developers could turn part of their illiquid land/housing assets into cash by selling them to government-led affordable-housing projects. Along with the acceleration of the "white list" policy, these measures would also provide a modest boost to housing investment, potentially creating a bottom to property-sector activity.

… while the impact on property sales and confidence may be limited. Although the new measures should help reduce the inventory of finished/unfinished new homes, the bigger problem is, arguably, the escalating inventory of secondary-home listings. This excess inventory will likely continue to exert downward pressure on home prices and hold back potential homebuyers. In addition, we also expect the latest measures to lead to a sudden increase in homebuyers' confidence in developers that have defaulted. They will likely remain cautious about buying unfinished homes. A more sustainable rebound in housing sales could be expected if secondary listings start to decrease and housing prices start to rise. This adjustment could take months or even quarters, despite the latest measures. Before a turning point is reached in the secondary market, we expect property prices to drop further and believe new-home sales will stay low for the next few months.

Fiscal and monetary policy could also be stepped up in Q2 as the Politburo meeting set a proactive tone for future policy. Government bond issuance has been slower so far this year and dragged fiscal spending lower. Against this backdrop, the Politburo has urged the government to speed up bond issuance, including the planned CNY 1tr of special CGBs, to accelerate fiscal spending. On the monetary front, the Politburo has asked the PBoC to use its interest rate and RRR policy tools flexibly. It is rare for the Politburo to make such explicit references to the PBoC's specific policy tools. This is likely a reflection of the weak credit demand given the high real interest rate. We maintain our forecast that the PBoC will cut its one-year MLF policy rate by 10bps in June. Meanwhile, we push out the 25bps RRR forecast cut to June. The issuance plan of ultra CGBs has been release indicating a slower-than-expected pace, reducing the need to inject additional liquidity this month. June could therefore be better timing than May, as CNY100bn more MLF will mature.

## US elections from China's perspective

The US administration recently announced that it will impose new/additional tariffs on USD18bn worth of selected Chinese products. This new tariff list mainly targets products that Chinese policymakers have prioritized to gain a competitive edge – such as EVs, lithium-ion batteries, solar cells, and semiconductors – in which China's domestic investment has grown rapidly in response to US export restrictions. The new tariffs also aim to reduce the US's reliance on China and promote the US's self-sufficiency in certain products, such as ship-to-shore cranes, "certain" steel and aluminium products, and medical products, e.g., masks and gloves. The overall number of products, though, does not appear to be macrosignificant; these accounted for only 4% of the US's total imports from China in 2023, and only 0.5% of China's total exports to the world. The immediate impact of tariffs on China's exports and GDP growth will likely be minimal.

The outlook for US-China relations will likely remain challenging, even regardless of the US election results. US public opinion toward China has reportedly deteriorated since 2019 and remains negative overall. According to the Pew Research Center’s latest survey, 81% of Americans hold an unfavorable view of China in 2024, broadly unchanged over the past few years. This view is widely shared by Democrats (77%) and Republicans (90%). Thus, the US administration’s hawkish stance on China is unlikely to change course, regardless of which party’s candidate is elected president.

US election politics could impact China’s economic outlook through three main channels:

First, trade friction could well be the norm in the future, with tensions potentially heightening if Trump were to be elected. When Biden was elected, the market hoped the administration would roll back some of the bilateral tariffs to benefit US consumers, but that did not happen. The latest tariff announcement, as well as Treasury Secretary Yellen's focus on China's overcapacity during her recent visit there, suggests that more friction could emerge under a second Biden term, although such measures may stay targeted and measured. In contrast with this targeted approach, a Trump presidency could increase the prospect of broad-based trade tensions, as he has suggested the possibility of imposing a broad-based tariff of as much as 60% on imports from China.

Nonetheless, a number of factors would help alleviate the negative impact of a potential tariff increase. (1) China’s exports to the US dropped from 20% of all exports in 2017 to 15% in 2023. Considering possible exclusions and exports of small packages, actual exports that could be subject to additional tariffs may constitute only around 10% of China’s total exports. (2) China’s export destinations are changing. Exports to developing countries have almost doubled since 2017: the share rose from 34% of all exports in 2017 to 44% in 2023. Continued rapid growth of these exports could offset the drop in exports to the US. (3) The substitution effect: If the US imports less from China and more from the rest of the world, it may create a shortage of those products somewhere else in the world that could be filled by Chinese exporters.

Second, China’s growth may also be impacted by a further decoupling of the technology and financial sectors. The Biden administration employed a “small yard, high fence” strategy to restrict China’s access to certain US technology such as semiconductors and AI. It has also introduced measures to restrict Chinese firms’ access to US capital, such as delisting certain Chinese companies from US stock exchanges and prohibiting US public funds from investing in Chinese companies. These measures will likely be maintained, if not enhanced, in the coming years, regardless of the US election results. Such measures will likely negatively affect China’s productivity growth, with the impact likely being felt over the longer term. The IMF estimates that a US-China tech decoupling could reduce China’s GDP by as much as 4% over a 10-year period; however, it would also reduce the US’s GDP by 3% over the same period. It is thus in the economic interest of both countries to avoid a wider tech/financial decoupling.

Third, the US elections also add uncertainty to geopolitical developments. Unlike Biden, Trump has little trust in international organizations and conventions and is more likely to turn back to adopting an “America First” policy. While it is difficult to predict geopolitical events and shocks over the next couple of years, one thing is probably clear: a Trump presidency could lead to higher uncertainty overall – and particularly geopolitically – that could have short-term and long-term ramifications for the global economy, including China's.

## It all depends on the Fed

We met a number of fixed income investors in Singapore recently to discuss India’s near- and medium-term outlooks. The discussions were held against the backdrop of the ongoing general elections in India, global bond index inclusion scheduled from late June, and persistent global geopolitical tensions, but unsurprisingly, the US economy and Fed monetary policy were clearly the dominant topics in every discussion, in terms of informing investor decisions regarding the likely macro outlook for Asia and other EM countries for the rest of 2024 and beyond. Key takeaways:

1) Fiscal consolidation to continue: Most investors agreed that fiscal consolidation will continue under the incumbent government and that the new Union Budget that will likely be presented in the first week of July, after the formation of the new government in June, will stick to the fiscal targets presented in the interim budget on February 1, 2024. The central government's target is to bring the fiscal deficit down to 5.1% of GDP in FY25 and further to 4.5% of GDP in FY26, which most investors felt is achievable, given the conservative and realistic estimates made in the budget. That the actual FY24 fiscal deficit outturn may be lower than the revised estimate of 5.8% of GDP lends stronger credibility to the fiscal consolidation agenda of the incumbent government. The market borrowing numbers for FY25 are also expected to remain unchanged, which helps reduce the uncertainty premium associated with the bond demand-supply dynamic.

Only in an alternate scenario in which opposition parties form the government at the centre could the fiscal deficit targets be revised significantly higher, given the promises that have been made regarding subsidies and other fiscal entitlements. But we didn't find any investor overtly concerned about the fiscal side of the equation, given the baseline assumption that the incumbent government is likely to form the government at the centre with a comfortable majority.

2) RBI rate cuts will likely commence only after the Fed starts cutting: There was almost unanimity among the investors that the RBI will only ease rates after the Fed easing cycle begins. While other key central banks such as the ECB and BOE look likely to ease before the Fed, this may not entice the RBI to start cutting rates, unless the Fed rate cycle turns. The spread between the repo rate and Fed Funds rate is at a record low of only 100-125bps at this stage and the RBI may not risk shrinking this differential further by moving before the Fed. Consequently, when and how much the Fed will cut rates has become the most important driving factor in attempting to determine the timing and depth of the Indian rate-cut cycle.

To be sure, with growth momentum holding up well in India - though likely to ease somewhat in the coming quarters - the RBI does not appear to be in a hurry to cut rates. Also credit growth and credit/deposit ratio needs to moderate, before the RBI can start a rate easing cycle. We have a first 25bps rate cut pencilled in for October, but that can easily be pushed to December or early 2025 depending on when the Fed starts its rate cuts. If the Fed does not cut rates in 2024, then the RBI is also unlikely to ease this year, even if there are rate cuts from the ECB, BOE and other Asian central banks. As far as the quantum of rate cuts is concerned, assuming the Fed cuts 150bps in this cycle, then the RBI could deliver 50-75bps rate cuts. We currently forecast the RBI to cut the policy repo rate 75bps by the first half of 2025, but if the Fed delays its own rate-cut cycle, then timing of rate cuts in India is also likely to be delayed and the quantum of rate cuts could also be limited to only 50bps, if the Fed cuts less than 150bps in this cycle.

3) Almost all investors agreed with our view that the rupee is likely to depreciate gradually after elections: While India's current account deficit (DB estimate: USD35bn; 0.9% of GDP) and Balance of Payments remain favourable for FY25 - we are forecasting USD50-55bn surplus for this fiscal year - thanks largely to USD25bn that will come in a staggered manner due to bond index inclusion, we think the rupee will still depreciate gradually in the second half of 2024, to touch 85 by end-Dec 2024. We didn't find many investors challenging this view, as they agreed that the RBI will probably continue to buy USD in case of capital inflows and eventually allow the range to shift higher for USD/INR, as has been the case in the last several years. Further, there is always the risk of oil heading higher and other key Asian currencies depreciating due to geopolitical risks, which can impact the INR as well.

When India's gross FX reserves hit the previous peak in September 2021 at USD642bn, it was about 20% of GDP. Now, in May 2024, India's gross FX reserves (without counting net forwards position) are at similar levels, but as a % of GDP, that ratio has fallen to 17%. To keep the ratio constant at 20% of GDP, gross FX reserves today should have been higher than USD700bn. Consequently, we think the RBI will continue to buy USD, as and when the opportunity presents itself, setting a natural floor for the INR, and then eventually allow a gradual shift of the USD/INR range higher, while continuing its strategy of maintaining low volatility.

4) India's inflation trajectory, real rates and output gap justify some monetary easing, as per a simple Taylor Rule formula: Most investors agreed that India's interest rates are at their peak and with the RBI unlikely to hike rates, the trajectory for yields should only be downward from here. The question is regarding timing, and for policy rates to come down, or market yields to ease meaningfully, monetary policy easing expectations need to increase, but that is linked closely with the Fed rate easing timing, as previously discussed.

As far as domestic factors are concerned, the RBI's CPI inflation target for FY25 is 4.5% (DB estimate: 4.6%), which results in real rates of about +225bps, on a forward-looking basis, with the effective policy rate sitting at 6.75% at present (as short-term rates are averaging 25bps higher than the policy repo rate of 6.50% due to tight money market liquidity). Even if the RBI works with a higher neutral real rate of 150bps, compared to the 100bps that the central bank has indicated as sufficient post the Covid-19 pandemic, the policy rate can easily come off by 50-75bps, in our view.

The RBI recently argued that since India's potential growth rate is higher than it previously anticipated, the potential neutral real rate may also be higher than the 100bps that was indicated earlier. Per our own assessment, India's potential growth rate is about 7.25%, and after adjusting for deflator issues, seasonality, large base effects and data quality issues, the actual growth rate - while stronger than anticipated before - is still lower than the potential growth rate. This is one possible reason why core inflation has eased so sharply through FY24, apart from getting support from lower input prices. In our view, even assuming India were to grow at 7%yoy in FY25, in line with the RBI's estimate, the output gap would be negative with respect to the potential growth rate of the economy being higher at 7.25%, as per our assessment. Consequently, even after factoring in a higher neutral real rate of 150bps for India, a case can be made for monetary policy easing in the coming quarters, in our view.

5) Reforms agenda to continue after the elections - factor market (land, labour, capital) reforms will likely be accelerated: Most investors agreed with our view that the government will likely accelerate the pace of factor market reforms – land, labour and capital - to be able to leverage the economies of scale in a productive manner. Along with fiscal consolidation and increased focus on factor market reforms, the government's continued thrust on privatisation, digitisation and competition should help India become a USD7trn economy by 2030 and the thirdlargest economy by size over the next few years.

## India's trade linkage with the USA and China

With the US administration having imposed new/additional tariffs on a USD18bn list of selected Chinese products, risks of a trade war cannot be ruled out, particularly post the upcoming US elections later this year. As far as India is concerned, it runs a trade surplus with the USA (USD37bn in FY24; +1% of GDP), while having a sizable trade deficit with China (USD85bn in FY24; -2.4% of GDP).

From a country perspective, the top five destinations of India's exports for FY24 were the USA (17.7% share of total), UAE (8.2%), Netherlands (5.1%), China (3.8%), and Singapore (3.3%). The top five countries of India's imports in FY24 were China (15.1% share), Russia(9.1%), UAE (7.1%), USA (6.0%), and Saudi Arabia (4.7%). The USA and China are India's two biggest trading partners.

The USA, Europe and China together constitute 44% of India's total exports while India currently imports 35% of its total requirement from these three destinations. Strikingly, China's trade interlinkage with India has increased significantly over the last two decades. In 2001, India's exports to China were only 1.9% of its total exports vs. about 4.0% currently, while India's imports from China have increased to about 15.1% vs. 3.0% of total imports in 2001. As a consequence, the slowing momentum of China and the G-2 economy (USA and Europe) could be a negative factor for India's near- and medium-term growth prospects. Given the trade interlinkages between India and G2 + China, our estimates suggest that for every 1% reduction in growth from the baseline estimate of these three countries/ regions, India's growth can be impacted by an incremental 50bps.

## April CPI review: CPI stays below 5% in April, but core inflation momentum picks up

India's April CPI inflation came at 4.83%yoy, broadly in line with the Bloomberg consensus forecast, but core inflation momentum increased significantly in April, led by a few components. While on a year-on-year basis, April core CPI inflation (RBI definition: CPI ex food and fuel) eased further to 3.22% (+0.55%mom), from 3.27% in March (+0.18%mom), the month-on-month momentum increased more than what we had anticipated. Services inflation momentum also increased sharply to +0.55%mom (3.5%yoy) in April vs. +0.28%mom (3.5%yoy) in March, led by personal care and effects services (+3%mom; 7.4%yoy). After the upside surprise in April core inflation momentum, our forecast for FY25 core inflation has increased to 3.6%yoy (vs. FY24 average of 4.3%yoy), from 3.4%yoy earlier.

For FY25, we forecast CPI inflation to moderate further to 4.6% on average (RBI's forecast is 4.5%), from 5.4% in FY24, assuming a normal monsoon. The ongoing heat wave could push up vegetable prices in the coming weeks and months more than what we have factored in at this stage, which could impact the headline CPI trajectory. While this may not change the annual CPI average, it could lead to increased intra-quarter volatility and raise the uncertainty premium regarding the prospect of future monetary policy action.

US CPI trajectory and Fed reaction function likely to matter more than domestic CPI: While India's CPI trajectory will be important for domestic monetary policy considerations, we think the outcome of the US CPI trajectory and the consequent Fed reaction function will be much more critical for influencing the timing and the magnitude of the RBI rate-cutting cycle. While achieving a lower CPI trajectory in India will be a necessary condition for rate cutting to commence, the Fed's reaction function will be far more important, in our view, for the RBI to embark on a definitive rate-cutting cycle.

## S Korea and Taiwan: Tailwinds and headwinds

n Despite a stronger growth outlook, the BoK struck a constructive tone on rates, keeping its options open to ease in 2H. Strong AI chip demand has prompted the CBC to be more optimistic on Taiwan's growth, albeit uncomfortable with its relatively low real rate.

## BoK remains constructive

In response to the stronger-than-expected Q1 GDP, the Bank of Korea (BoK) raised its 2024 growth forecast significantly, by 0.4ppts to 2.5%, as per our expectations. It sees a payback in 2025 though, lowering its growth forecast by 0.2ppts to 2.1%. On the other hand, the BoK left its inflation forecasts unchanged, noting that much of the upward revision in growth (0.3ppts out of 0.4ppts) was due to (IT) exports. It expects relative weakness in domestic demand to continue, suggesting limited underlying inflationary pressure, as reflected in falling core inflation. The latter moderated further to 2.4%yoy 3mma in April, down from 3% in March, while headline inflation was stable at 3%. In contrast to DMs, South Korea's services inflation stayed relatively low, falling further to 2.3% in April from 2.5% in March, while goods inflation stood at 3.9% vs. 3.7% in the same period. More importantly, as we expected, the BoK governor struck a relatively constructive tone (similar to Chair Powell's), hinting that the policy rate has peaked, while the BoK's decision to ease would be data dependent. Recall that the BoK expects inflation to ease in 2H – to 2.4% from 2.9% in 1H – which would result in a passive tightening of monetary policy, with the real policy rate at 1.1% vs. 0.6% without rate cuts.

## Taiwan: Stronger growth and asset prices pose upside risks to rates

## Indonesia’s economy holds steady, while BI focuses on supporting the rupiah.

Indonesia reported a GDP growth of 5.1%yoy in Q1, vs. 5.0% in Q4, largely in line with expectations. Private consumption remained a steady leading contributor to headline growth (rising 5.3%yoy in Q1, adding 2.9ppts), while government spending got an extra boost during the quarter (surging 19.9%, adding 1.1ppts) amid the general election. ## BSP waiting for the right to time to ease

Philippines GDP grew 5.7%yoy in Q1 2024, up from 5.5% growth in Q4, led by household consumption. Although the latter's growth moderated, it stood out as the leading contributor, adding 3.5ppts to headline growth. Net trade contribution also turned positive, adding 1.2ppts, thanks to a rebound in electronics exports (Figure 35) In contrast, GFCF growth slowed sharply to 2.3% in Q1, from 10.2%, adding only 0.5ppts. The latter's weakness was attributed to “tight financial conditions".

Bangko Sental ng Pilipinas’ (BSP) kept its policy rate unchanged at 6.5% at its May meeting, as widely expected, but with a dovish tilt. While noting that there are still “persistent supply-side pressures on food prices”, BSP Governor Remonola signalled that BSP could begin easing as soon as August, despite the view that "the expected path for domestic output growth over the medium term remains largely intact." This is earlier than Q4 or early 2025 cited back in April and well ahead of the Fed’s first expected rate cut in December. Our baseline view is that BSP delivers rate cut in the same month as the Fed, given prevailing pressure on the peso and upside risks to inflation. The peso has already risen above PHP58 against the US dollar and inflation has headed higher, to 3.8% in April, nearing the upper end of BSP’s target range of 2-4%. Moreover, high food prices are likely to keep headline inflation elevated through July, although high base effects may see it fall materially in August.

## Singapore's inventory cycle turns

Singapore GDP rose 2.7%yoy in Q1, up from 2.2% in Q4. On a sequential QoQ saar basis, the economy rose only 0.1%, down from 1.2% in Q4. By industry, Q1 growth was almost entirely supported by services, adding 2.7ppts to headline growth, while manufacturing subtracted 0.3ppts instead. Within services, the finance & insurance sector added 0.9ppts to headline growth in Q1 (up from 0.7ppts in Q4), while concerts and events saw a surge in accommodation and transport & storage, respectively, added 0.1ppts and 0.5ppts (up from 0ppts and 0.3ppts), alongside tourist arrivals rising 49.6% during the quarter.

By demand, growth was led primarily by private consumption, with retail sales up 2.7%yoy in Q1, vs. -1.2% in Q4, as wages rose 6.4% during the quarter (Q4: 2.6%). This may not last, however. According to the Economic Development Board’s Business Expectations Survey, the net weighted balance of hiring expectations (for the next quarter) in manufacturing stood low, at -2% in Q1, while that of services was at +3%, down from an average of +9% in 2023. Indeed, the Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) expects unit labour cost increases "to slow over the course of the year in tandem with the cooling labour market."

On the other hand, the inventory cycle appears to be turning. Restocking added 0.4ppts in Q1 (vs. -3.2ppts in Q4), while net trade's contribution to growth was only 0.1ppts (vs. +4.4ppts), as trade activities suffered a considerable payback during the quarter. That said, rising imports of electronics products and office machines and electrical machinery apparatus bode well for growth in the coming quarters. Investment in technology and digital infrastructure should continue to grow at a steady pace, supported by the government’s push to improve the country’s digital capabilities. Despite the weak performance of the electronics sector in Q1, the Ministry of Trade and Industry expects a gradual recovery supported by demand for semiconductors for the smartphone, PCs and AI end-markets, and kept its economic growth forecast in 2024 unchanged at between 1–3%. Meanwhile, air travel and tourism demand should normalize to pre-pandemic levels over the course of the year.

Headline and core inflation stood steady at 2.7% and 3.1% in April, respectively. Although crude oil prices were volatile in recent months, MAS continues to expect disinflation, with headline and core inflation both ranging 2.5–3.5%, and 1.5–2.5% excluding the GST impact, as its earlier tightening reduced imported inflationary pressure and as labour market conditions ease. Given a relatively weak growth momentum in Q1 and limited inflationary pressure, we see the MAS striking a more dovish tone at the next meeting, preparing the market for its easing in October, although the latter is subject to change depending on the timing of Singapore’s general election.
