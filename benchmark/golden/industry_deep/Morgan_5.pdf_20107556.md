## CPI and PPI remained soft

China<sup>’</sup>s March CPI eased to 0.1%oya (or down 0.1% m/m sa), in line with our expectation (Figure 1). LNY-related seasonal volatility (regarding prices of food, travel and transport costs) accounted for about 92% of the month-on-month decline in March CPI. Aside from that, general pricing power seems to have remained soft, and domestic demand has yet to gain momentum on a sustained basis beyond the holiday effect. Taking 1Q together, headline CPI remained soft, averaging 0.0%oya (or 0.0% 3m/3m saar).

PPI deflation has intensified, as headline PPI fell 2.8%oya or 0.5% m/m sa in March. Energy-related PPI components rose moderately along with global energy prices. Meanwhile, producer prices for selected industrial materials weakened further, reflecting near-term softness in construction demand. PPI for downstream consumer goods remained sluggish. PPI in new industrial sectors, such as NEVs and lithium battery, fell in March amid excess capacity concern.

We expect deflation will end but low inflation will stay in 2024, considering the recent trend in global commodity prices, bottoming of pork prices and a low base. The demandsupply imbalance and excess capacity concerns remain significant. Policy-makers still prioritize promoting industry upgrade and investment although some consumption supporting measures are included. Our forecast for full-year 2024

CPI inflation stands at 0.5%. Together with prolonged PPI deflation, GDP deflator deflation may widen in 1Q to -1.6%oya, compared to -1.4%oya in the previous three quarters.

On monetary policy, in our baseline forecast, we expect the next policy rate cut to happen in 2Q.Though soft March data for CPI and PPI point to renewed concerns about deflation pressure, the timing of the next rate cut by the PBOC will be complicated by other considerations. These include banks<sup>’</sup> net interest margin (NIM) pressure, as well as external concerns, especially the market<sup>’</sup>s repricing of the timing and pace of the Fed rate cut outlook, which exerts depreciation pressure on the CNY.

## Exports eased in March

Following the significant upside surprise in January-February, China<sup>’</sup>s export activity eased in March. Seasonally adjusted, exports fell 1.9%m/m, sa, following the 7.1%oya average growth in Jan-Feb. Smoothing out monthly volatility, export sector activity showed solid momentum in 1Q, with strong underlying growth at 33.3%3m/3m saar by March. Meanwhile, nominal imports rose 1.8%m/m, sa, or 6.5%3m/3m, saar. Processing imports expanded at a solid pace in 1Q, while major commodity imports (crude oil, iron ore, copper) showed somewhat mixed performance.

By product, exports of low-end consumer goods have outperformed in recent months, growing strongly at 44.5%3m/3m, saar in March (Figure 2). Exports of high-tech exports also picked up, rising 4.4%m/m, sa in March (or 16.4%3m/3m, saar), and auto exports jumped 19.4%m/m, sa in March (rising 5.2%3m/3m, saar). By major destination, exports to the DM markets (the US, Europe, and Japan) held up well in March, with easing after the strong Jan-Feb gains. Exports to EM Asia posted further gains in March while exports to some other key EM markets, including LatAm and Africa, moderated, following significant outperformance in Jan-Feb.

![](images/bedf0165f6559c0bc55c1db1df49b230853363bb1a12d4a4364b37a4495fdbe6.jpg)

China exports activity held up better than expected in 2023, benefiting from its diversification efforts across major markets (EM Asia, LatAm, Russia, and Africa) and by products (with rapid expansion in new energy products and motor vehicles). In 1Q, the export sector has seen solid near-term growth momentum, consistent with constructive signals on global manufacturing activity. Looking ahead, we expect relatively steady growth trends in China<sup>’</sup>s export activity for 2024 amid an improving global growth outlook. Following solid manufacturing FAI growth and capacity expansion in the past two years, and amid lingering softness in domestic demand conditions, we expect Chinese exporters will continue to push for expanding their global market share. Trade partners<sup>’</sup> potential reactions will, however, become a growing concern this year.

## FX reserves beat expectations again

China<sup>’</sup>s FX reserves beat expectations again in March despite a narrower-than-expected trade surplus, rising another US\$19.8bn to US\$3,245.7bn. The US dollar index inched up from 104.2 to 104.5 in March, leading to an estimated currency valuation loss of US\$4.3bn. We tweak our current account surplus forecast higher to US\$30.6bn (vs. US\$22.4bn on average in Jan-Feb), and thus estimate an implied capital outflow of US\$6.4bn, compared to the revised estimate of a US\$7.9bn inflow for February.

From November 2022 to March 2024, China increased its gold reserves by 10.1mn oz, or16.1% (Figure 3). Amid the recent gold price rally, the monthly gold reserve increase slowed to 0.16mn oz in March, compared to the average pace of 0.47mn oz in 4Q23, or 0.62mn oz in the prior 16 months. While gold prices have surged since late February, the onshore gold price (in CNY terms) and offshore gold price (in USD terms) point to consistent CNY depreciation pressure.

Figure 3: China official reserve asset: gold
![](images/2d503094099d26fba02ebbd949af90648dfa80caf72c3c54d50f34957e8a2288.jpg)

Lately, the onshore/offshore gold trading price implies the USD/CNY rate at around 7.32. While historical data suggest the onshore/offshore gold trading price implied USD/CNY and spot exchange rate tend to co-move, the gap has opened since September 2023. Back then, the PBOC started to shift its focus of the managed floating RMB exchange rate regime from “floating<sup>”</sup> to “managed<sup>”</sup>. While gold prices onshore vs. offshore signal further room for CNY depreciation, we think it still depends on the PBOC<sup>’</sup>s CNY fixing.

## Credit growth slowed

China<sup>’</sup>s credit growth slowed in March as expected. TSF growth registered a new record low at 8.7%, compared to 9.0% in February (Figure 4) and loan growth slowed to 9.6% (vs. 10.1% in February). Details show that credit support for the economy remains steady in manufacturing and government-supported areas. Our measure of the credit impulse, the gap between TSF growth and nominal GDP growth, stayed unchanged at 5.0%-pts. We expect TSF growth has touched the bottom in March and will pick up in the coming months.

The slowdown in March TSF growth is mainly due to the PBOC guideline that the pace of credit extension should return to normal, compared to a significant front-loading in 1Q23. The TSF increase in 1Q23 accounted for 41.9% of the full-year TSF increase in 2023, while in normal years 1Q credit extension usually accounts for about one-third of the full-year quota. For this year, the PBOC has instructed banks to return to a normal pace of credit extension. Using our fullyear 2024 forecast of 9.5% TSF growth, 1Q TSF flow will account for 34% of the full-year quota, consistent with the seasonal pattern in normal years.

![](images/96209cea0ca6f547e6999da16ae89dd85802124005fde5a3b438a3e363737aa6.jpg)

The more difficult decision for the PBOC is the interest rate policy. The decline in CPI and PPI in March points to lingering deflation pressure, and the GDP deflation may stay longer than we initially expected (we now expect the GDP deflator will stay in negative territory until 3Q24). This will justify further rate cuts. Our forecast pencils in the next rate cut in the current quarter. In practice, the pace and magnitude of rate cuts are constrained by the net interest margin (NIM) pressure faced by banks and CNY depreciation pressure on the external front. In particular, the unexpected stickiness in US inflation has led to a repricing of the Fed policy outlook, pushing up UST yields and USD strength.

## Taiwan: Inflation eased to 2.1%oya in March

Taiwan<sup>’</sup>s headline CPI inflation eased to 2.1%oya in March from 3.1%oya in February (Figure 5), or falling 0.5% m/m sa (vs +0.7% m/m sa in February). Breakdown details show declines in food prices (-0.4% m/m sa), along with stable energy prices (0.0% m/m sa). Core CPI inflation eased to 2.13%oya in March (vs 2.89%oya in February), with major swings arising from certain service prices with notable LNYrelated volatility. Putting the first three months together, it appears the underlying inflation trend has been easing steadily, as headline CPI rose by an average 2.34% oya in 1Q24, compared to an average 2.9%oya growth in 4Q23.

Looking ahead, the key concern for Taiwan<sup>’</sup>s near-term inflation picture relates to the electricity price hike effective April 1. The central bank estimates that the 11% electricity price hike will likely raise average 2024 CPI inflation by about 0.27%-pt. Beyond the near-term electricity price hike, underlying pressure for core CPI inflation will likely moderate down the road amid softening employment and domestic demand growth. We expect CPI inflation will likely stay above the 2%oya-handle through early 3Q, and moderate to below 2% towards late 3Q-4Q. Our baseline scenario looks for the Taiwan central bank to keep policy rates on hold for the rest of 2024, though the probability of another 12.5bp hike in June cannot be completely ruled out – for which the upcoming inflation prints should be monitored closely.

## Exports bounce led by tech and US demand

Following solid sequential growth trend in 4Q23, Taiwan<sup>’</sup>s 1Q24 trade reports show ongoing strong momentum in export activity. March merchandise exports came in well above expectations, rising 18.9%oya on a 10.4%m/m sa jump (or growing 11.8% 3m/3m saar). Tech exports showed a strong gain of 11.0% m/m sa or up 14.3% 3m/3m saar (Figure 6). The breakdown highlights strong underlying expansion in exports of information and communication products (+17.9% m/m sa, or 131.3% 3m/3m saar), reflecting strong growth in exports of AI-related products. The breakdown by export destination highlights ongoing outperformance in exports to the US (+74.9% 3m/3m saar) and the ASEAN region (+43.9%), while exports to China/ HKSAR continue to underperform (-18.3%).

We look for a steady upward trend in Taiwan<sup>’</sup>s tech exports in the coming quarters, including the structural boost of AI-related products and HPC-related demand, while the tech sector<sup>’</sup>s de-stocking pressure begins to ease. A constructive outlook for global goods demand, supported by resilient corporate sector fundamentals and solid labor income trend, bodes well for Taiwan<sup>’</sup>s export sector activity going ahead.
