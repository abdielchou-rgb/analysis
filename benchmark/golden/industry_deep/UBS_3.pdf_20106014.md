# US Economic Perspectives US Inflation Monthly: Seasonality and shipping

## Three US inflation questions that have been on client minds recently

1. What will be the impact of the upcoming revisions to CPI seasonal adjust factors? We estimate there will be some flattening in the downtrend in monthly core CPI changes, but much less flattening that occurred in the annual revisions last year. We estimate the large increases in March, April, and May 2023 will be revised down about 5bp each, while the smaller increases in January, July, September, and November will be revised up about 4bp each.

2. Do we expect a pickup in inflation early in the year? Price changes are usually largest early in the year and seasonal adjustment often does not fully account for that early-year strength. As a result we expect a pick up in the pace of monthly core CPI and core PCE increases for the next 2 to 3 months. However, weakening used car prices and rent increases partially offset the strength in other components, and even excluding used car and rents the uptick is projected to wane by the spring. Despite the step up in the monthly pace, 12-month inflation should continue to fall solidly.

3. How will recent shipping troubles affect US inflation? At current levels the impact will likely be very small, less than 5bp.

## More slowing in inflation expected over the next few releases

We project core PCE prices to rise 15bp in the December data released at the end of next week, with the 12-month change dropping to 2.9% (the first time below 3% since March 2021, right at the start of the inflation surge) and ¾ of the way back to the FOMC's 2.0% inflation target from a peak of 5.6% in February 2022. The 6-month annualized change in core PCE prices is projected to be below the FOMC's 2.0% inflation target for a second month in a row (at 1.8%).

## Three current inflation concerns

Beyond the usual queries on our forecast for US CPI and PCE inflation, three questions have come up often in client interactions over the past few weeks and seem to be dominating the conversation. They are :

1. What will be the impact of the upcoming revisions to CPI seasonal adjust factors?

2. Do we expect a pickup in inflation early in the year?

3. How will recent shipping troubles affect US inflation?

We will take each question in turn:

## 1. What will be the impact of the upcoming revisions to CPI seasonal adjustment factors?

The revisions to CPI seasonal adjustment factors will likely be much less impactful this year than they were last year.

Last year the update of the seasonal factors pushed down some of the large core CPI increases early in the year and pushed up the weaker months later in the year. These changes led to a notable flattening in the downward trajectory of core CPI inflation (Figure 2). Prior to the revision, the monthly changes suggested that core CPI inflation was slowing rapidly and was nearly down to the pre-pandemic pace in the final three months of 2022. In contrast, the post-revision monthly changes suggested that core CPI inflation was slowing only modestly, if at all.

Seasonal adjustment revisions usually take a little off of the months with big increases and add a little to the smaller months

This year revised CPI seasonal factors will be published on Friday, February 9 ahead of the release of the January CPI on Tuesday February 13. Federal Reserve Governor Waller has said he is "watching closely" this year's revisions given the changes that last year's revisions caused to the inflation picture.

Last year's revisions were big and some are concerned that this year's will be big too.

Like last year we expect the revisions to CPI seasonal adjustment factors will moderate the downward trajectory of monthly core CPI change as the higher readings early in the year will be revised down while the lower readings late in the year will be revised up. However, we expect the flattening to be much less pronounced than last year. In particular we estimate that core CPI monthly changes in March, April, and May will be revised down by about 5bp in each month, while the changes in January, July, September, and November will be revised up by about 4bp each.

We should note that our estimates are subject to considerable uncertainty. Less than half of the categories used to create the seasonally-adjusted core CPI are, in fact, currently seasonally adjusted. (28 out of 61 core CPI categories, and 44 out of 81 headline CPI categories). However, whether a category is seasonally adjusted or not can change from year to year, and the BLS often does interventions to remove outliers — procedures that are difficult to anticipate and replicate. We derive our estimates of the seasonal adjustment changes by looking at the revisions to our aggregation of the core CPI when we shift the data window used to seasonally adjust categories forward from the January 2015-December 2022 time period used last year to January 2016-December 2023.<sup>(1)</sup> This procedure picked up just over half of the revisions to the core CPI monthly changes for calendar year 2022. (Correlation of the predicted revisions to the monthly core CPI changes in 2022 with the actual revisions is 0.54.)

Not seasonally adjusted data will not be revised and 12-month CPI inflation rates, which following BLS custom are calculated using not-seasonally-adjusted data, also will be unaffected by the revision to seasonal adjustment factors.

There will be some pass-through of these revisions to PCE prices, though the impact will likely be small. For PCE prices only the months of October, November, and December 2023 will be revised at this time. Earlier months will not be revised until the next NIPA annual revision (which we believe will be in September of this year).

The effect of CPI seasonal adjustment revisions on PCE prices will probably be small

## 2. Do we expect a pickup in inflation early this year?

Inflation tends to be higher in the first couple months of the year compared to later in the year. The January non-seasonally-adjusted core CPI increase is almost always higher than either the average increase in the prior 11 months (Figure 5) or a centered 12- month moving average of core CPI changes (Figure 6). The fact that these January increases have been stronger in recent years (even prior to the pandemic) means that seasonal adjustment factors have been only partially adjusting for the January strength and some seasonal pattern remains even after statistical adjustment — a phenomenon

Inflation is often high early in the year and seasonal adjustment often does not fully capture it.

Different data windows are used for vehicles and fuel oil consistent with the shorter windows the BLS uses for those items.

This effect is more notable in core PCE prices than in core CPI prices. However, as the stronger monthly changes are in response to normal early-year price changes and residual seasonality, we expect the initially elevated pace to moderate during the first few months of the year and see this temporary strength as entirely consistent with a longer-run slowing in inflation. Even with the early-year boost, 12- month core PCE is projected to decline to 2.4% with the release of the March data in late April and 2.1% with the release of the May data in late June.

## 3. How will recent shipping troubles affect US inflation?

International shipping costs have jumped in the past couple of weeks. However, at their current levels, we expect they will have a minimal impact on US inflation. Our more complete response is in a recently published note: The inflation impact of the spike in global costs is likely (very) small, however, a rough summary is as follows. As of yet, the shipping cost increase, and overall supply chain impact, is only a small fraction of increase that we saw during the pandemic. (Figure 9) At the height of the shipping troubles during the pandemic the cif/fob ratio, which is a rough estimate of the cost of international shipping as a share of the imports value, rose from 2.7% to only 4.2%, suggesting an impact on US prices of 25bp or less at those elevated levels. Translating that to the current increases would suggest an impact of less than 5bp on US prices. That said, there could be additional costs, such as the cost of shipping delays, that magnify the direct shipping costs.

The direct impact of the high shipping costs is likely to be less than the monthly noise in the inflation release.

## Near-term inflation forecast December PCE inflation

Both December headline and core PCE prices, released at the end of next week (Friday, January 26), are projected to have increased 15bp. Over the past 36 months one standard error around these estimates has been 4bp (with a median absolute error of 2.5bp). With these increases the 12-month change in core PCE prices is projected to slip to 2.91% in December — the smallest since March 2021 and roughly ¾ of the way back to the FOMC's 2.0% inflation targe from its 5.6% peak in February 2022. The 12-month change is down 137bp from 6 months ago. At that pace 12-month core PCE inflation would hit 2.0% in the April data, released in late May, though our forecast has that 2.0% level not being hit until September.

The annualized 6-month core PCE price change is projected to edge down just a couple of basis points to 1.82%. The 12-month change for headline PCE prices is projected to be 2.58%

## Upcoming CPI prints

We are currently forecasting a 13bp increase in the headline CPI in January (seasonallyadjusted; 39bp not seasonally adjusted) with 12-month inflation dropping from 3.35% to 2.93% — its first time below 3.0% since March 2021. Retail gasoline prices again look to decline on a seasonally-adjusted basis and the headline increase is also held down by a smaller projected core CPI increase than in the past 5 months. Specifically we forecast the core CPI to increase 22bp in January. This would be the 4th smallest increase in the 35 months since the surge in inflation started in March 2021. 12-month core CPI is forecast to hit 3.7% with the January data, which would be its smallest increase since April 2021.

The January core CPI increase is held down by a projected 3¾% decline in used motor vehicle prices based on the January JD Power Used Car Guide. This drop in used vehicle prices holds down the core CPI by 14bp compared to the 49bp increase in used vehicle prices in December. Additionally we project a little slowing in the pace of housing rents with the increases in owners' equivalent rent (OER) easing from 47bp to 40bp. (See the December US Inflation Monthly for the details behind our OER projection, which proved quite close in December (48bp projected, 47bp actual).) Outside of used vehicle prices and housing rents we forecast a pick up in the monthly core CPI pace from a 18bp increase in December to 32bp January driven by residual seasonality (noted in the first section above). A 32bp nonhousing rent, non-used vehicle core CPI increase would be larger than 8 of the past 9 months, a stretch during which increases averaged 13bp. We will adjust these numbers as additional relevant data comes in.

Looking further ahead than next month's print, over the next six months we forecast published seasonally-adjusted core CPI increases will begin to look like pre-pandemic monthly increases as rents slow, used car prices decline, and ebbing demand and easing supply conditions lead to a slackening in the pace of other prices. ## Inflation expectations

There is little sign of inflation expectations becoming unanchored. Consumers' expectations of inflation over the next 5-to-10 years from the University of Michigan survey were at 3.2% in the final November release, but dropped back to 2.9% in the final December release. While this is a pickup relative to pre-pandemic readings (when actual PCE inflation averaged a bit below the FOMC's 2.0% inflation target) they remain solidly within the 2.7% to 3.2% range they were for nearly all of the 1997 to 2013 period when PCE inflation averaged close to the Fed's 2.0% target.

Over the past 25 years inflation expectations in the Michigan survey are positively correlated with past inflation, but negatively correlated with future inflation.

## Box: Rules of thumb for impacts of key economic shocks

We usually rely on estimated time-series models to bring in the impact of various factors on our forecast. However, rules of thumb are often useful to give a rough idea of how important a shock is likely to be. Below are a few key rules of thumb that we use:

 1 percentage point increase in the unemployment rate pushes down core inflation by 8bp.

 Permanent \$10 per barrel level shift in the price of Brent crude pushes up the level of CPI by 40bp and core CPI by 4bp.

 A one percent decline in the dollar increases consumer import prices by 0.15 percent.

 A one percent increase in import prices results in a 0.12 percent increase in core consumer prices.

 A 1% increase in CRB spot food index equals about 8bp in prices of food at home with about 2/3rds in the first 6 months and 1/3rd in the six months after that. (CPI food at home is 8% of the CPI basket.)

## Components of core PCE inflation

Figure 55 through Figure 59 show 3- and 12-month changes for the main components of our core PCE projection along with our forecasts and model projections. The models are estimated taking 2023Q4 as data and forecasted starting 2024Q1.

1. Core goods prices (25% of core PCE, Figure 55) have slowed considerably over the past year and are projected to continue to slow over the next handful of months as demand weakens and the effects of easing supply constraints continue to ripple through the production and distribution network (particularly for vehicles). Goods prices deflation is projected to hit its lowest point around the middle of the year.

Goods price inflation should slow as demand eases and the effects of looser supply conditions ripple through the system.

Our Phillips curve model for the category has adjusted down considerably over the past few quarters and now the model is roughly in line with our projection next year.

2. We forecast monthly housing services increases (essentially owners' equivalent rent plus tenants' rent, 20% of core PCE, Figure 56) will slow over the next few quarters as the easing in rents for new leases filters into CPI/PCE housing rent inflation.

Our model for housing services inflation based on fundamental rent market factors (the unemployment rate, rental vacancy rate, and housing affordability) shows a roughly similar slowing to our baseline forecast.

3. Price inflation for PCE health services (19% of core PCE, Figure 57) is projected to run a little above the pace over the past year as the price reduction from the ending Medicare Covid reimbursement rate increases is past. However, the upward push is only moderate as the Medicare adjustment for wage increases (which first appeared in the October PCE prices) was estimated by the Center for Medicare and Medicaid Studies to be a touch lower than last year and wage growth in health care has been slowing for the past year.

The 12-month change in shelter rent inflation is roughly at its peak.

4. PCE food services inflation (6% of core PCE, Figure 58Fod servic) has been strong through most of the pandemic. We have seen slowing in recent months and expect further easing of the pace of increases with moderating wage increases.

5. Inflation for other core PCE services (31% of core PCE, Figure 59) has slowed in recent months and we expect the pace will ebb further as wage growth slowly eases.

## Key risks to our modal forecast

We view our projection as the most likely (modal) scenario and see risks on both sides of the inflation forecast with the overall uncertainty surrounding the inflation projection remaining larger than usual over the past 20 to 30 years (though down from a year or two ago). Over the next few months we see the risk two-sided around our forecast with a slight upward skew in January and February.

We think that inflation risks are skewed to the upside through the end of the year.

## Upside risks

1. Stronger-than-expected residual seasonality: As noted in the front section, price increases are often strongest early in the year and it is possible that we might not have put enough in for the pick up.

2. Continued tight labor market passing into services prices: While there are signs that the labor market is easing, it remains tight. If the economy does not go into the recession that we have written into our baseline forecast, and wage growth remains elevated, it is likely that non-rent services inflation will be higher than we have forecast over the next year. Our rough estimate based on a fairly steep Phillips curve, is that the labor market continuing at close to its present level of tightness could add 50bp to our core PCE inflation projection in 2024.

3. Rents re-accelerating: While we are not seeing a lot of strength in new tenants rents, the solid housing market and rising existing home prices could start to push up new tenants' rents and, eventually, CPI rents.

## Downside risks

1. A slowing economy: The recession that we have forecast for next year could be sooner, be deeper, and have a greater slowing effect on inflation than we have anticipated. In particular, in recessions prices for financial services, nonprofit services, and travel-related services generally fall quite notably.

2. Declining market rents and a faster slowing in CPI rents: Our forecast has CPI rents returning to just above their pre-pandemic pace in the first half of this year, but with market rents running below their pre-pandemic pace late last year and early this year according to some indexes (along with the the huge drop in the BLS new tenant rent index in Q4), it is possible that CPI and PCE rents also will slow to below their pre-pandemic pace. A note from the SF Fed suggests CPI housing rents slowing to a 0% 12-month change by the middle of this year. If this turns out to be correct it would lead to rents contributing about 1.2pp less to headline CPI inflation, and 60bp less to core PCE inflation, this year than in our baseline.

## Comparison to the forecast models

Our forecast models have continued to revise downward since peaking last year. With our models using our Q4 projection as data the models are taking on board 2 quarters of core PCE increases at 2.0%. All the models expect something in the 2.4/2.5% range in the first couple of quarters of this year and the inflation trend has fallen notably as actual inflation has come down. As with most Phillips curve style models they were unable to forecast the inflation as it surged upward and, as expected, they have not been a good guide as inflation has fallen. In particular they do not include an effect for changes in the mix of demand and the supply constraints that hampered efforts to satisfy this changing mix. We believe the combined effects of the changing demand mix and supply issues were far more important in driving the inflation surge over the past 3 years than were the standard Phillips curve drivers of aggregate resource slack.

Our Phillips curve models have performed poorly during thus inflation experience..

Our core PCE inflation forecast is still below these models in the second half of next year, though the gap has narrowed considerably as inflation has eased and the models have revised downward.

## Consensus inflation forecasts

As 2023 is winding down consensus projections have moved down toward our projection, which has been on the low side of consensus for most of the year. Our PCE inflation forecasts this year (2024) are 0.5pp to 0.8pp below consensus, though the CPI forecasts are closer —only 0.1pp to 0.5pp below consensus. It seems that consensus expects a much larger closing of the CPI-PCE wedge than we do. We expect that solid rent and CPI medical insurance increases in the first half of the year should keep CPI inflation elevated compared to PCE price inflation (at about 60bp, double the average pre-pandemic wedge, but down a bit from 2023).

Our inflation forecast is on the low side of consensus next year — particularly for PCE prices.
