## The Totality of the Totally Confusing Data

Another US labor market report, and another large divergence between the establishment survey and household survey of employment. US Treasuries and the US dollar reacted to the strong establishment survey, instead of the weak household survey, in part because investors view the establishment survey as a better barometer for labor market activity.

• The monthly household survey samples approximately 60,000 eligible households (note: the BLS will cut the size by 5,000 households to a total of 55,000 a month starting in 2025).

• The monthly establishment survey samples approximately 119,000 businesses and government agencies, representing about 629,000 individual worksites.

Our economists discussed the divergence and how the higher amount of immigration in 2022 and 2023 may be a culprit (see US Economics: Breaking Even and Immigration Headcount). Indeed, the establishment survey showed monthly job gains averaging 118k more than the household survey over the past 2 years, after adjusting the household survey for methodological differences between the two surveys (see Exhibit 1 ).

However, the major divergence between the establishment survey and household survey occurred this year, i.e., 2024 year-to-date. Consider the following facts:

• During the first 5 months of 2024, the establishment survey showed 300k more jobs each month, on average, than the household survey (after adjusting the household survey for its differences from the establishment survey).

• During the first 5 months of 2022 and 2023, the establishment survey showed a respective 70k and 62k fewer jobs each month than the household survey.

• Over the entire 2022-2023 period, the establishment survey showed only 40k more jobs each month, on average, than the household survey.

Of course, as we suggested above, the establishment survey samples twice as many businesses as the household survey samples households. At the same time, the household survey benefits from a higher response rate than the establishment survey (by 26pp).

• In addition, the response rate to the establishment survey fell more since the pandemic than the household survey response rate (16pp vs. 13pp, see Exhibit 2 ).

The birth-death model adjustment adds another confounding issue to the establishment survey. Over the past 5 months (January through May 2024), the birth-death model added 93% of nonfarm payroll jobs on a non-seasonally-adjusted (NSA) basis.

• This 93% compares to 66% over the same period a year ago (January through May 2023) and 41% the year before that (January through May 2022).

Exhibit 3 and Exhibit 4 look at the January to May period since the advent of the birthdeath model.

• The Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) suggests that total nonfarm payroll gains during this period have decelerated sharply over the past 3 years (from 1,524k in 2022 to 649k in 2024).

• At the same, the BLS suggests that net job gains from business births-deaths have remained largely the same during this period (618k in 2022 to 603k in 2024).

Of course, there may be good reason(s) – either related to the construction of the birthdeath model itself or fundamentals – why job gains from business births have remained steady over the past 3 years, despite the decline in other job gains.

Still, the BLS suggests "that while both the business birth and death portions of total employment are generally significant, the net contribution is relatively small and stable." As we discussed above, the net contribution is not very small at the moment.

The nature of the recent US economic data – not just labor market data, but also other activity data as well as inflation data – lends itself to investor confusion. In addition, it places more onus on communication from FOMC participants.

Indeed, the more economic data confuse investors instead of clarify, the more investors rely on the perspective of those in charge of monetary policy. FOMC participants have filled this need for perspective with their increased public communications.

This dynamic has meant that FOMC participants have become instrumental in moving markets and creating volatility, especially when the totality of the data is totally confusing (read about how Fedspeak drove yields higher last summer in The Real Drivers of the US Treasury Bond Market Rout).

## Flow of Funds Suggests Monetary Policy Restriction

The Federal Reserve released its 1Q24 Financial Accounts of the United States, or Z.1 report. We use data in the report, including investor transactions in various financial asset classes, to better understand financial trends across the economy.

For example, data on quarterly debt growth inform our understanding of the stance of monetary policy. Many investors seem to disagree with the views of Chair Powell or NY Fed President Williams when they suggest the stance of monetary policy is restrictive. To many investors, monetary policy doesn't seem restrictive yet.

Exhibit 5 and Exhibit 6 show quarterly debt growth on the balance sheets of the public sector (federal government) and private sector (households and nonfinancial businesses).

Exhibit 5: US federal government debt growth as a % of GDP, Q/Q SAAR, through 1Q24
![](images/970a6e33965b388f78b47d5f4a3d68fb92c736d72e6f7ae81d394ecbdf6e196e.jpg)
Source: Morgan Stanley Research, Federal Reserve, Bloomberg

Exhibit 6: US household and nonfinancial business debt growth as a % of GDP, Q/Q SAAR, through 1Q24
![](images/3461d57e861861688a29edd040a1e0123ed67861d7944eff38433cc586c460be.jpg)
Source: Morgan Stanley Research, Federal Reserve, Bloomberg

The debt growth data show clearly how the private sector added leverage to balance sheets before the pandemic, immediately after the pandemic, and more recently. We think comparing these three periods to the levels of monetary policy rates suggest policy is restrictive today vis-à-vis the private sector.

• As the Fed normalized monetary policy rates in 2016-2019, private sector debt grew at 6-7% of GDP, on average.

• After the Fed took rates back to zero and conducted QE in 2020-2021, private sector debt growth accelerated to 11% of GDP, on average.

• After the Fed took rates to 5.25-5.50% and conducted QT in 2022-2024, private sector debt growth decelerated to 3-4% of GDP, on average.

Of course, the deceleration in private sector debt growth has not fed into broad aggregates of economic activity, such as GDP growth – at least not enough to sustain fears of an economic hard landing. Significant debt growth on the balance sheet of the public sector may explain why.

In any case, debt growth data allow us to understand the perspective of Chair Powell and NY Fed President Williams regarding the stance of monetary policy: it's restrictive, and will remain so until the Fed begins to lower rates.

## Canada vs. the US: The Path to Confidence

The BoC's "increased confidence" at the June meeting contrasts with the FOMC's signaling at its May meeting. The FOMC signaled last month it wants to gain greater confidence that inflation is moving sustainably toward 2% before reducing the federal funds rate, while the BoC said that its confidence has increased.

Some may find this difference in the level of confidence surprising. At first glance, price pressures appear to be running at around the same rate in Canada and the US ( Exhibit 7 ).

What is the BoC seeing that the Fed isn't seeing yet? We note three key differences:

1. Underlying inflation deceleration: Inflation in Canada has dropped below 2% when excluding volatile components ( Exhibit 8 ). And there is a clearly discernible downtrend in that measure over the past year.

The US does not publish an equivalent series, but broadly comparable measures like the New York Fed's Multivariate Core Trend measure have not shown meaningful deceleration in recent months. According to the NY Fed, its model removes "transitory variation" from core inflation pressures.

2. High-frequency disinflation: The BoC's recent shift in tone reflects a slowing pace of inflation in 1Q24, in contrast to the United States.

Core inflation (ex-food and energy) in Canada has been within the BoC's 1-3% inflation control target range for the past four months when annualizing the three most recent prints. And the three most recent prints have been below 2% ( Exhibit 9 ).

Governor Macklem has emphasized the lagged effects of an economy that moved into excess supply in 2H23 as a key driver behind the recent progress.

Meanwhile, the US economy experienced inflation volatility during the first quarter (see US Inflation: After the Upswing). The three-month moving average of US core PCE is currently around 3.5%.

Exhibit 9: In 2024, the speed of price deceleration has been more pronounced in Canada than in the US
![](images/3807c376218e872d0279bfef02492fc9924731c91d60b3beb805a8672d17f28a.jpg)
Source: Bloomberg, Macrobond, Morgan Stanley Research

Exhibit 10: The Canadian unemployment rate has trended higher
![](images/a0bec431d2379ce8b612e4044073d615a585aeeb060ba3d0539dcf2aa43330ab.jpg)

3. Rebalancing of the labor market: The Canadian unemployment rate has risen by 1.2% since mid-2022. By contrast, the US unemployment rate has not trended higher at the same pace as in Canada ( Exhibit 10 ).

In sum, a combination of deceleration in core inflation pressures and a loosening in the labor market has been sufficient for the Bank of Canada to gain confidence.

We expect that a similar deceleration in high-frequency measures of US core inflation and labor market loosening will provide the Fed with similar confidence later this year. In the meantime, however, we expect USD/CAD to rise as the BoC continues to act on its stated willingness to diverge from the Fed.

## Euro area

In Europe this week, the focus was on the ECB meeting, which was rather uneventful. No forward guidance was offered on future meetings, and the ECB maintained a data-dependency approach, this leaves the Bund susceptible to external spillovers.

Given that the market is already discounting a very hawkish path on rates, the bar for having another wave of front-end-led sell-off looks contained to us. Valuationwise, the Bund continues to trade very cheap vs. our fair value, with this dislocation gap widening to \~45bp, the summer seasonality should help in correcting the cheapness. We envision a bull-flattening move on the curve as the front-end will remain anchored in the upcoming future.

On the modelling front, this week we suggest a framework for projecting EUR rates vol going ahead. We modelled a weighted average index of 1m expiry on 2y, 5y, 10y and 30y normal vols. We end up with a five-factor model dependent on: (i) 10y swap, (ii) ECB pricing max/min, (iii) Fed pricing, (iv) V2X vol, and (v) 2y2y inflation.

The UK focus should shift towards labour market data and supply considerations. On the former, wage growth numbers will likely be key when it comes to the price action. On the supply side, a new 10-year syndication is anticipated in the upcoming week, and we expect issuance size at £8.5 billion in cash and \~£7 million/bp in DV01 terms. ## Japan

We continue to see the JGB market primary driven by the Fed policy outlook. Market participants have focused more on FX developments to assess the BoJ's future policy trajectory given that it will probably take another couple of months for the overall wage growth/inflation picture to become clearer. They broadly believe that the USD/JPY level will remain elevated under the Fed's higher-forlonger policy, and it will eventually lead to a reacceleration of Japan's inflation led by higher import prices.

That said, the recent weak US economic data might force them to rethink such a view to some extent, causing a significant unwinding of short JPY duration positioning, which has been one of the popular trades globally.

We retain a bullish bias on JPY rates duration in the near term in anticipation of crowded positions continuing to be unwound, particularly if the upcoming US CPI print shows a consecutive slowdown as our US economists expect. We maintain long 20y JGBs on 10s20s30s fly and long 10y JGB ASW. We also stick with pay 10y on 2s10s20s fly with an eye to the medium term.

## Monthly payrolls becoming a Rorschach test

It is true that Treasury yields had fallen a fair bit in anticipation of a weak payrolls number – the "whisper number" was close to 140k – and a strong optical headline drove a reversal in yields as some of that positioning unwound. However, it is not so clear that markets should react to the latest payrolls data with continued upside momentum to higher yields. And that is because the details of the payrolls report have a little something for everyone – a Rorschach test for markets.

Our economists noted the rise in the unemployment rate in the May report – a sign of slack, keeping the Fed interested in rate cuts. And yet, markets largely traded off the headline payrolls surprise ( Exhibit 11 ) (the correlations with unemployment rate, wages, and participation data are much weaker). Headline payrolls surprises have been largely positive in recent months, keeping an upward bias in yields. Broadly, labor market surprises have been positive, which has been influential in moving yields alongside ( Exhibit 12 ).

For a start, the services employment sub-indices have been reeling below 50 for both the ISM and the S&P Global surveys ( Exhibit 22 ). Overall, while US Treasury yields stay indexed to the headline payrolls ( Exhibit 11 ), we think the broad composite of labor market conditions has been weakening – something not reflected in Treasury yields. We think, now that the payrolls event has passed, the remainder of the labor market data can show the true weakening of the labor market, helping support duration.

Given our understanding that the Treasury market may be incorrectly pricing just the headline surprises, ahead of the CPI print (where our economists expect continued descent) and the FOMC meeting next week (see our preview below), we stay with our long duration bias – and maintain long 3m10y receivers.

## June FOMC preview: The pathways for rate cuts

This meant the market has been range-bound – with 10-year yields in a 4.30-4.70% range since mid-April, when March CPI delivered a third consecutive upside surprise to start 2024.

Nevertheless, 10-year yields are \~60-80bp higher than where they began the year, reflecting the effect of the three upside surprises in CPI earlier this year ( Exhibit 17 ). Interestingly, it's the CPI prints that have moved yields, not the FOMC meetings ( Exhibit 18 ).

We think it is likely that such surprises could come from the press conference rather than the dots themselves.

One possible pathway investors can consider is if the Fed can contextualize the recent uptick in inflation as being driven by residual seasonality ( Exhibit 21 ) or being too narrow – essentially a view on the inflation path, rather than simply following the realized trends. In collaboration with our economics team, we noted in a recent report that residual seasonality played a key role in distorting the optics of core PCE, and that there could be deceleration in PCE ahead. If the FOMC or Powell discusses such insights, it could prompt the market to reverse much of its inflation-driven upside in yields.

If Powell shares more concern about the labor market, it could also open the door for markets to price more cuts.

Confidence in the market’s Fed “higher forever” scenario looks to be waning if it confirms two straight months of slowdown in US inflation data. Sensitivity to declines in JPY rates had meanwhile declined from -14pt in March 2022 to -8pt in March 2023, suggesting that ALM efforts might now be largely complete on the assets side of the ledger.

It is interesting to see ESR declining even as interest rates have risen. In particular, the sensitivity of qualifying capital to the 50bp raise in JPY yields (+1%) was less than in March 2022 (+3%), which is an indication that the decline in valuation on both assets and liabilities due to higher interest rates has now become better balanced.

This may actually leave lifers with an incentive to sell super-long JGBs. In any case, our suspicion is that lifers might now have more reason to sell than buy from an ALM perspective. We therefore expect any demand to be limited mostly to that associated with new policy premium revenues, and would accordingly caution against positioning too wholeheartedly for a rally in 30s and/or 40s.

We also suggest sticking with a long 10y JGB ASW position, with 10y ASW having now cheapened to the point where it is on a rough par with the GC-TONA spread (see Exhibit 53 ). We see scope for a recovery to around -10bp driven by the influence of the aforementioned BoJ “stock effects”.

Our mid-year outlooks are finally done and published, which means it's time to market them. One of the most common questions we've gotten is: doesn't this outlook look a lot like regime 1, where real rates fall and rising equities bolster breakevens, which means USD should weaken?

## Appendix: Swiss Rental Reference Rate

At the end of every quarter, the prevailing average mortgage rate in Switzerland is calculated. This figure is then rounded to the nearest quarter of a percent, to produce the "rental reference rate" to which rental contracts are indexed.

Any quarter-end changes made to the rental reference rate are then valid from the beginning of the third month following the calculation (e.g., if the mortgage data were collected, averaged, and rounded at the end of December to produce the reference rate, this rate would be valid for the month of March onwards).

Each 25bp change in the rental reference rate corresponds to a 3% adjustment of rent. If the rental reference rate increases from 1.5% to 1.75% (like it did in September 2023), rents are adjusted upwards by 3% (like in December 2023) and vice versa.

## Summer seasonals

As spring gives way to summer, we provide a seasonal outlook for swaptions. Across various expiries and tails, we look at the trends of vanilla vol, expiry switches, and tail switches, using our backfilled SOFR dataset from 2014 (i.e., N=9 or 10, depending on the day of the month). We identify trends by looking at two factors:

1. Sharpe ratio: For each day of a given month, we compute the average (across years) move observed on that day relative to month start, and divide this number by the standard deviation (across years) of the move relative to month start.

2. Frequency of move: For each day of a given month, we compute the number of times a positive or negative trend has been observed on a given day, across years.

Note, while we think it is meaningful to look for historical patterns in markets, we do not advocate trading on these relationships without a convincing justification for the pattern.

## Trading Strategy 1 – "Trade Longs/Fade Shorts"

When the 5-day moving average crosses above the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract (long duration) and hold for a 25-business-day period. When the 5-day moving average crosses below the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract and hold for a 25-business-day period. In short, this strategy buys futures when the Simple Moving Average Crossover (SMAX) generates both a long and a short signal, given the historical outperformance of long signals traded long and underperformance of short signals traded short. Given that the SMAX could generate both a long and a short signal within the predefined holding period, an investor may have a 200% long position since each of the two signals would be traded in separate portfolio sleeves.
