# FX & EM Fixed Income Strategy: After You, Core Rates

Ioana Zamfir, Min Dai, Eli Carter

Over the last few weeks, very tight EM/US rates spreads have prompted investor questions regarding the value left in bullish EM rates positions. Our analysis suggests that valuations are at their tightest in at least 20 years, implying that a broader EM rates rally from here predominantly hinges on core rates moving lower. While we maintain a bullish medium-term view, we note that there are only a handful of rates markets which still offer value in this environment (China, Colombia and Mexico).

Generally, we prefer EM FX over duration plays at the moment. However, we also see stronger dispersion within EM FX, in the aftermath of the easing delivered so far. We think that higher-carry currencies should remain supported, but our analysis suggests that carry strategies have mostly favoured a subset of five over the last six months: COP, MXN, ZAR, INR and BRL. The latter two remain our favourites as well, but we avoid funding longs with USD and generally recommend lower-yielding G10 and EM currencies as funders instead (e.g., CLP, CNH, TWD and CHF).

## We are all Fed watchers

Do EM local markets still offer value? Investors have asked us questions in recent weeks about whether EM local markets still offer value despite tight spreads over US rates. Before we look into valuations, we take a step back to review the past performance of the GBI-EM index ( Exhibit 1 and Exhibit 2 ). The result is quite telling.

At a country level, Mexico and Colombia bonds still offer quite a bit of value over US rates, while some CEE countries (Hungary, the Czech Republic) also maintain decent spreads over Bunds. At the other end of the spectrum, Asia markets and parts of LatAm (Brazil, Chile, Peru) offer little value over US rates at current levels. However, in Asia, spreads are tight because of better inflation dynamics (see Asia Macro Strategy: Stay Long Carry ).

Generally, valuations in the long end look slightly better than valuations in the front end, though the overall message remains the same.

On aggregate, core rates currently explain \~30% of the price action in EM rates, almost twice as much as they did a year ago (\~15%). Dispersion among countries remains high, though in markets such as Chile, Singapore, Mexico and Korea, US rates explain 50% or more of aggregate price action.

Once again, as the EM valuation cushion versus core rates has been eroded over recent months, we doubt that EM rates can decouple from core rates at these levels and rally more without additional support from the FOMC or the ECB.

Delaying DM easing to 2H24 would pose important risks for EM central bank pricing: To be clear, our G10 economists expect the first FOMC and ECB cuts in June, which should open up (more) room for EM central banks to deliver cuts. Should DM cuts be delayed further, we could see increased sensitivity from EM central banks to global conditions, particularly for those that have been easing already.

In CEEMEA, we could see a mix of pausing cuts and smaller cuts. In LatAm, we think that lowering the pace of cuts would be the more likely option. AXJ (ex China) central banks would likely delay the start of their easing cycle until after the FOMC cuts, as noted by our economists here.

Given valuation constraints in the near term, we prefer to be more selective in terms of exposure, with a focus on curve trades or relative value trades, and to maintain directional exposure only in key places. We stay received 3-year TIIE and 1-year CNY NDIRS, but we like paying 1-year INR NDOIS. In terms of more hedged expressions, we like 2s10s steepeners in IBR and 1s3s flatteners in KRW. See Strategy implications for our full rationale for each region.

## High-carry FX is still the way to go for now

In EM FX, we started the year with a neutral stance but a bullish bias on USD (see Global EM Strategist: Turning Neutral on EM FX, January 8, 2024). Given the USD rally seen in the past two months, we have gradually toned down our caution and closed long USD against CLP, THB and PHP (see Global EM Strategist: Kenya Leads the Way, February 20, 2024).

High-carry EM FX should continue to do well but there are only five liquid carry proxies left: Uncertainty about the near-term path for global rates has helped to keep FX volatility low, boosting the appeal of carry plays once again, That said, the EM rate cuts delivered in this cycle have led to broader differentiation between currencies and have narrowed the universe of 'high carry' currencies.

On a volatility-adjusted basis, most EM currencies continue to offer some carry, but over the last six months carry strategies have preferred a particular set of five: INR, MXN, COP, ZAR and BRL. Our two main favourites are BRL and INR, as we think that upcoming rate cuts and local noise could trigger higher volatility in MXN and COP, while ZAR's narrative remains unappealing.

We expect high-carry FX to remain supported versus lower-yielding peers, but we are very hesitant to fund EM FX longs with USD, where we maintain a bullish bias. We prefer to fund longs with lower-yielding G10 and EM currencies instead, via trades such as long BRL/CLP, short TWD/INR and short CNH/INR.

## What is the consensus among EM local market investors?

A more constructive medium-term view: After a strong quarter in 4Q23 and outperformance versus the benchmark, most GBI-EM investors started the year with a more constructive stance – growth in EM is picking up and a decent number of EM central banks are in the rate-cutting cycle amid lower inflation. Valuations of local bond yields are a concern, but they are sensitive to UST yield moves.

OW duration versus MW FX: Hence, at the start of 2024, investors continued to stay OW EM duration, while they had been increasing their EM FX risks gradually towards the end of 2023 ( Exhibit 13 and Exhibit 14 ). However, the sell-off in UST and depreciation of EM FX have caused most managers to become slightly more cautious and have prompted them to cut risk a bit in both duration and FX.

## Strategy implications

In LatAm, we maintain directional rates exposure in Mexico via 3-year TIIE receivers, as valuations versus US rates remain quite attractive and the terminal rate pricing (\~8.00%) has more room to drift lower. We also like 2s10s IBR steepeners, as we see substantial risks of an acceleration in the pace of cuts in the near term. In FX, our favourite currency remains BRL, but we expect it to remain range-bound versus USD in the near term.

In Asia, we have a preference for FX over duration as we think that the duration rally would need FX appreciation first given the tight spreads. We favour carry trades in Asia as Asian central banks are cautious in cutting rates. We are short CNH/THB and short EUR/ KRW. We hold short CNH/INR and short TWD/INR.

In rates, we like receiving 1-year CNY NDIRS, long CNY FX-hedged 1-year CGBs, paying 1- year INR NDOIS and 1s3s KRW flatteners (see Asia Macro Strategy: Stay Long Carry ).

## EM sovereign credit in short:

A frequent initial question is how much further the index can tighten given the recent run. We look at three scenarios: US credit spreads could move to five-year lows and pull the EM index 40bp tighter; EM could tighten on a relative basis to trade flat to US credit, pulling the index 25bp tighter; and while valuations are overall stretched at a country level, there are various pockets of cheapness that in total could contribute around 20-30bp to the index level. The subsequent question is whether to position for this or if our current neutral view still applies. In short, while we still see numerous idiosyncratic opportunities to be long, it seems too late to turn to an outright bullish view. Valuations play a key part in this cautious view, as do technicals, where there has been a lot of supply but still no fund inflows, raising the spectre of supply indigestion. ## The bullish case for EM

A very strong February, led by HY: While index total returns of 1% in February do not stand out, what did stand out was the outperformance of HY. For sovereigns only, HY outperformed IG by 3.1%, which is the highest relative monthly outperformance since June 2023. However, in June 2023 IG sovereigns at least also saw positive outright returns, which was not the case in February. Higher UST yields led to negative total returns of 1% in February for IG sovereigns ( Exhibit 18 ). While not the strongest relationship, this would historically have also put pressure on HY sovereigns, yet instead they returned 2.5% ( Exhibit 19 ).

As we have long noted, lower-rated HY valuations are the only cheap part of the index and had to be the driver of any overall spread tightening. Yet spreads have also now come a long way. Specifically, with index spreads at 370bp they are now 40bp tighter than our year-end forecast of 410bp and much tighter than spreads of around 460bp that we forecast could be reached in 1H24 before tightening back.

We lay out a few scenarios where spreads are in fact justified and could tighten even further. In other words, what is the bull case from here?

## 1) US credit spreads tighten further to five-year lows

While there has been a lot of excitement around EM issuance and structural reforms in certain countries, in the end the key driver of the rally has been strong broader risk appetite, including the S&P, and in particular US credit spreads. Notably, across both IG and the 'low risk' HY bucket (BBs and strong single Bs) EM has actually lagged the tightening by US credit spreads ( Exhibit 20 ). Only for the 'high risk' HY bucket (most single Bs and performing CCCs) has EM outperformed the US equivalent CCC bucket.

As an extension, a continued rally in US credit should also pull EM tighter with it. Moving the three US credit buckets used in Exhibit 20 to their five-year tights would see US IG tighten by 16bp to 80bp, US BBs tighten by 33bp to 195bp and US CCCs tighten by 306bp to 444bp. These levels are in general either late 2019 or mid-2021. Just as with EM, the most upside from here would be for the lowest-rated credits. Assuming the first two

buckets do tighten in line with US credit and the 'high risk' HY bucket tightens by 150bp, the index would be 40bp tighter.

Given the strength of the US economy and some nascent signs of early credit cycle dynamics, reaching five-year lows could potentially happen. That said, it's not the base case of our US credit strategists, who see spreads slightly wider from here.

## 2) EM closes valuation gaps to US credit

As per Exhibit 20 , EM credit has recently lagged versus US credit and is not at the tights. So far we have explained this underperformance by diverging growth and flows paths (see Global EM Strategist: No Sliding Out of DMs, February 5, 2024). This could reverse.

On growth, surprise indices that had diverged between US and EM recently left room for a reversal, especially due to the mean-reverting nature of these indices. Recent data have shown exactly this ( Exhibit 21 ), with EM data coming in better across all three regions ( Exhibit 22 ). From here, our US economists expect US growth to remain strong. This leaves the most likely source of improvement coming from EM moving higher. China improving would be an important driver yet in this case our economists expect that Beijing will likely maintain its reactive easing approach, with marginal fiscal easing at the upcoming NPC, which means that growth and inflation may remain subpar. Outside China, we think that activity stands a better chance of improving. Indeed, as per our 2024 outlook, the quarterly EM versus DM growth differential should trough in 1Q24.

## Assessing the base case versus bull case

In a bullish scenario there is clearly still further room for EM to tighten: US credit spreads could move to five-year lows and pull the EM index 40bp tighter. EM could tighten on a relative basis to trade flat to US credit, pulling the index 25bp tighter. Finally, while valuations are overall stretched at the country level, there are various pockets of cheapness that in total could contribute around 20-30bp to the index level. In a super bull scenario, they could all happen at the same time.

The first question is what probability we should assign to these scenarios. Tighter US credit spreads from here only feature in our US credit strategists' bull case that comes with a 20% probability, with spreads otherwise seen wider. EM outperforming US credit on a relative basis stands a good chance of happening for the year as a whole as per our 2024 outlook, even if not in the near term. Finally, of the four idiosyncratic drivers, CEE credits is the one we have highest conviction on today together with parts of the risky LatAm sovereigns. The other buckets have moved a lot already and with spread moves likely more contained from here.

The second question is then what that means for our strategy recommendations. So far our view had been that while 2024 would deliver double-digit returns, there would be a better entry point in 1Q to add risk, meaning that we stuck with a neutral stance in recent months. This has been too cautious.

However, today it seems too late to turn to a bullish view, even though the index has a few ways of tightening: Valuations play a part in this cautious view, as do technicals, where there has been a lot of supply but still no fund inflows, raising the spectre of supply indigestion (see EM Technical Watch, February 27, 2024). Cash levels have also come down. Finally, risk sentiment is looking very stretched as measured by the Morgan Stanley GRDI (Global Risk Demand Index – US Pat. No. 7,617,143), which at 1.94 is very close to the 'complacency' level (see STGRDI Index on Bloomberg).

That said, it does seem too late for the index to widen all the way to 460bp by end-1Q24, with a pullback perhaps just taking the index north of 400bp instead. Also, with our US Treasury strategists no longer bullish on duration in the near term, limiting IG total returns, we also move neutral IG versus HY.

## Our current stance on Asia FX and rates

We toned down our caution: After being cautious on EM Asia in early January (see EM Strategy: Asia Macro Strategy: Three Themes Revisited, January 9, 2024), we toned down our caution two weeks ago by closing all long USD exposure (see EM Strategy: Asia Macro Strategy: Can Asia Generate Alpha for GBI-EM Investors? February 19, 2024).

As discussed in FX & EM Fixed Income Strategy: After You, Core Rates , we don’t see much value in local bond yields given the tight spreads over UST yields. This makes EM duration a UST trade, i.e., EM only rallies when the UST yield starts to rally. In addition, EM FX needs to appreciate first before EM rates.

Hence, EM FX is likely to be the major driver of EM local market returns in the next few months: From the beginning of 2024, many investors have been confused and surprised by strong US data as the perception of the US economy has been that high interest rates should finally weigh on growth and allow the US to have a soft landing.

Watch out for high population growth in the US: There are some signs of this such as retail sales, but overall US data points have been better than expected, particularly in the job market. One possible explanation could be the rise in immigration. On February 7, the Congressional Budget Office (CBO) published new population estimates showing faster population growth because of an increase in immigration, especially undocumented immigration.

The CBO correspondingly increased prime-age population growth from 0.2% to 0.8% in 2023, from 0.5% to 1.2% in 2024, from 0.4% to 1.2% in 2025, and from 0.2% to 0.9% in

2026 (see Friday Finish – US Economics: Immigration, Labor Supply, and Potential Growth, February 23, 2024).

What does higher immigration mean? As these immigrants go to the job market, the increased supply could alleviate wage pressure. Meanwhile, their production and consumption could help growth.

Inflation could be lower while growth could be stronger. Isn't this the best combination or Goldilocks for the Fed and the market? Lower inflation allows the Fed to cut interest rates gradually while still decent growth removes the urgency for the Fed to cut aggressively. In other words, improved demographics in the US put it in a better position versus Europe and China.

Medium-term factors favour USD: Meanwhile, US leadership in AI and rising geopolitical tension between the US and China could increase the gap between US productivity and its global competitors. Total output is capital input times labour input and productivity. The US seems to be ahead of other countries in two out of three growth drivers. This would boost USD over the medium-to-long term as FX is driven by growth differentials.

However, in the short term, we stick to the playbook we laid out two weeks ago. We believe that the market should trade range bound for the time being with an eye on the June FOMC for the first rate cut. Compared to central banks in CEE and LatAm, Asia central banks are much more cautious about rate cuts (see Asia Economics: The Viewpoint: Why Asian Central Banks Will Not Cut Ahead of the Fed, February 26, 2024). This is because:

1) Asia's real rates are much lower than those of the US: With the rise in US real rates and more modest hiking cycles of AXJ central banks, Asia's real rate advantage over the US has eroded. AXJ ex China's real rate advantage over the US (calculated based on 12- month forward CPI) peaked in February 2022 and has turned negative since early 2023.

## Why do we prefer KRW to TWD?

KRW and TWD are often seen as having similar drivers: Both economies are exporters and run current account surpluses. Both have high exposure to the semiconductor/tech cycle, and their currencies are sensitive to equity flows.

Why we are constructive on KRW but not TWD: As a result, some investors have questioned why we are constructive on KRW but like using TWD as a funding currency. There are a few reasons for this divergent view:

1) Carry: TWD is the lowest-yielding currency in AXJ, with the cost of carry of being long TWD punitive at around 3.7% for one year. In comparison, the 1-year cost of being long

KRW is about half of TWD’s, at around 1.8%. Even after normalising for the different level of volatility in the currencies, TWD still offers the lowest carry/vol ratio in AXJ while KRW sits in the middle of the pack.

This makes the timing of any long TWD trades crucial. There is potential for TWD to outperform should USD and US yields fall, but given that we expect more range-bound trading in the next few weeks, we believe that TWD's low yield would render it a funding currency for now.

## Analyst Stock Ratings

Overweight (O or Over) - The stock's total return is expected to exceed the total return of the relevant country MSCI Index or the average total return of the analyst's industry (or industry team's) coverage universe, on a risk-adjusted basis over the next 12-18 months.

Equal-weight (E or Equal) - The stock's total return is expected to be in line with the total return of the relevant country MSCI Index or the average total return of the analyst's industry (or industry team's) coverage universe, on a risk-adjusted basis over the next 12-18 months.

Not-Rated (NR) - Currently the analyst does not have adequate conviction about the stock's total return relative to the relevant country MSCI Index or the average total return of the analyst's industry (or industry team's) coverage universe, on a risk-adjusted basis, over the next 12-18 months.

Underweight (U or Under) - The stock's total return is expected to be below the total return of the relevant country MSCI Index or the average total return of the analyst's industry (or industry team's) coverage universe, on a risk-adjusted basis, over the next 12-18 months.

Unless otherwise specified, the time frame for price targets included in Morgan Stanley Research is 12 to 18 months.
