# The Return of M&A

We believe that a cyclical and structural rebound in M&A is coming. We surveyed 150 industry teams across Morgan Stanley Research and discuss why, where and how activity could return.

The cyclical case for a 50% rise in activity: Our Financials sector equity analysts expect global M&A volumes to rise 50% versus 2023, as leading indicators flash green, banks point to deal pipelines building, and headwinds to corporate confidence ease. A new survey of 150 industry teams across Morgan Stanley Research sees activity rising in half of covered industries globally (and only declining in 6%).

The structural case is driven by dry powder, private markets, regional shifts and new innovations: 2021-23 may have seen US\$4-11 trillion less activity than macro fundamentals would suggest, supporting a multi-year catch-up. Regionally, we see a 'drought' ending in Europe and Australia, while Japan continues a structural shift towards greater corporate efficiency and activity. Necessity and opportunity should drive more M&A in non-listed privates, where 1,200+ unicorns are currently valued at US\$1 billion+ and private equity-backed names look for exits. Global listed non-financials hold US\$5.6 trillion in cash while private market investors sit on US\$2.5 trillion of dry powder, providing fuel for activity. And rising demand for AI capabilities, the clean energy transition, innovation in life sciences, reshoring and geographic diversification in a multipolar world should structurally support M&A over the next cycle.

Which sectors? Health Care, Real Estate, Staples and Technology stand out from our analyst survey, while we also note the strong case for M&A within private markets, given incentives to act. Quantitatively, we screen for companies with similar properties to those favored by public and private acquirers.

Which regions? Europe and North America are the major regions with the most positive expected skew of activity, but conditions in Australia, India, Korea and ASEAN are also favorable. In Japan, M&A is part of a broader corporate reform story that continues to make it a preferred market.

Strategics versus sponsors – financing advantage versus urgency: Despite the rise in yields, our analyst teams see funding deals with cash/debt as more attractive than equity. Ample cash balances and wide-open investment grade markets mean that strategic activity has a financial advantage, while sponsors may face more urgency whatever the macro 'weather'. Unusually low bond prices and change-of-control language can drive opportunity, but should not disrupt broader activity.

## Executive Summary: The Return of M&A

Corporate activity, like the economy, is cyclical. Low rates and a rapid reopening from Covid drove a flurry of activity in 2021, and the highest volumes since 2007.

But then the boom reversed. Aggressive hiking cycles by the Fed, ECB and BoE caused interest rates to rise at the fastest pace in 40 years, driving funding costs sharply higher. Higher funding costs let recession predictions flourish and C-Suite confidence slump. Sharp declines in global equity markets widened buyer/seller bid-ask spreads significantly, delaying deals already in the pipeline. US and European banking stress in spring 2023 led lenders to be more conservative.

And so while 2023 was a strong year for markets, with global equities up 23% and credit spreads tighter, global M&A volume was down 35%, the lowest reading since 2004 (before adjusting for inflation). Last year's volume as a percentage of US nominal GDP was the lowest in at least three decades.

We think that this 'winter' for M&A is thawing and activity is set to return cyclically and secularly. In 2024, we see multiple headwinds to M&A abating under the Morgan Stanley economic and strategy base case, including funding costs, inflation fears, recession concerns and corporate confidence. And a unique survey of our equity sector analysts, across 150 global industries, sees a global case for more activity. Our Financials equity analysts forecast global M&A volume to rise by an above-consensus 50%Y in 2024.

Greater corporate activity is one of several factors driving our optimism for Japan and European equities, while our survey also flags ASEAN, Australia, Korea and India for more deal-making. The sector implications for M&A are broad (our analysts see activity increasing in 50% of global industries, versus declines in only 6%), but Health Care, Real Estate, Staples and Technology stand out.

Expecting more M&A off a low base, we realize, is the 'easy' part of the analysis. For investors, the bigger uncertainty may be where, when and how it happens, and how sustained it could be. That analysis will fill the bulk of this report, as we marry bottom-up insight from Morgan Stanley industry teams around the world and refreshed quantitative screens for names that could see activity. Our key takeaways:

• Secular, not just cyclical: The case for a cyclical rebound in M&A is supported by top-down, bottom-up and qualitative approaches. But there's a structural story too. We think that 2021-23 saw far less activity than fundamental and macro factors would suggest, supporting a multi-year catch-up. Regionally, we see a 'drought' ending in Europe and Australia, while Japan continues a structural shift towards greater corporate activity. Necessity and opportunity should drive more M&A in non-listed privates, where 1,200+ unicorns are currently valued at US\$1 billion+ and private equity faces pressure to monetize. Global listed non-financials hold US \$5.6 trillion in cash while private market investors sit on US\$2.5 trillion of dry powder, providing fuel for activity. And rising demand for AI and cloud capabilities, the clean energy transition, innovation in life sciences, reshoring and geographic diversification in a multipolar world should structurally support M&A over the next cycle.

• Quantifying the rise: We put numbers around the inherently uncertain question of volumes. Our Financials sector analysts see M&A volumes up 50% versus 2023. Further back, over the last three years we think that global M&A volumes have undershot what macro factors would predict by US\$4-11 trillion, providing space for multi-year strength.

• A global story: Surprisingly, Europe sees the most M&A optimism from our equity analyst teams of the major regions. Japan sits at the crossroads of a cyclical rebound and major structural reform that makes activity more likely. Australia, India, Korea and ASEAN were notable for analyst optimism on more M&A. We note that more corporate activity is a driver of our ongoing constructive view on equities in Japan and Europe.

• Sectors in focus: Energy, Health Care, Hotels, Real Estate, Retail, Staples, Software, Technology and EU Telecom are sectors where Morgan Stanley analysts are most focused on the impact of M&A. We flag relevant sector insights.

• Screening for single names: For investors, M&A activity can be impactful but uncertain. While we have no knowledge of potential candidates for activity, we can quantitatively screen for stocks with greater similarity to targets of the past. We offer several approaches.

• Strategics versus sponsors – economics versus urgency: Wide-open IG issuance markets in the US and Europe and elevated yields mean that strategics enjoy a financial advantage. But non-listed companies may face more urgency, whatever the macro backdrop, as sponsors need to monetize and unicorns face shortening runways.

• Sweating the (credit) details: M&A can have a significant impact on bond-level returns, especially if they contain CoC language, given that many IG bonds still trade with historically low dollar prices. While we don't think that this disrupts activity overall, it could be a driver of single-name opportunities. We screen for low-priced bonds with CoC language.

• Risks – recession and regulation: Fears of recession held back activity last year, and a significant downturn would impede a return of M&A. However, Morgan Stanley's economic base case remains a soft landing. Regulation is another key risk, and has already been relevant to activity this year. Morgan Stanley analysts considered regulation in our survey of the M&A outlook, and did not see it as a prohibiting factor overall. They saw it as a relatively larger barrier to M&A in North America, China, South Korea, Communication Services, Financials and Technology. They saw it creating the fewest challenges in ASEAN, Australia, India, EM, Energy and Real Estate.

# Why? The Case for More M&A

## Clearer skies ahead

Ryan Kenny, Connell Schmitz

Our Financials sector equity analysts have been calling for a 2024 rebound in global M&A activity since July last year, when they upgraded the Midcap Advisors industry view to Attractive (see Midcap Advisors: Let's Make a Deal; Upgrade Industry to Attractive, July 12, 2023). At the time this was a controversial call as deal announcements had yet to trough. Since then, our conviction in a 2024 rebound has only increased as deal announcements inflected positively year on year, one of the main drivers of our recent upgrade of Large Cap Banks industry to Attractive in addition to lighter expectations for the Basel Endgame (see Large Cap Banks: The Future's So Bright, We Gotta Upgrade, January 30, 2024).

While consensus expectations moved towards us as a soft-landing narrative took off, there remains a large debate around the timing and size of the coming M&A wave: Our expectation is that global M&A deal completions will begin inflecting positively year on year in 1Q24 and build through the year, fully normalizing as a percentage of nominal GDP by 2026. This view is above consensus. We model completed global volumes up \~50%Y in 2024, a large inflection on a percentage basis but off a very low base, as last year's M&A activity as a percentage of US nominal GDP was the lowest in at least three decades. This level of activity does not appear priced into equity markets, with pure play investment banks trading \~2-3 turns below normalized P/E multiples. We think that there's still time for investors to play the M&A theme ahead of the rebound and as it builds.

We expect deal activity to improve in 2024, even in our bear case: It's hard to imagine a year worse than 2023, which had the lowest completed M&A volumes in a decade and the lowest relative to nominal US GDP since at least 1996, which is as far back as our database goes. Given that deal announcements have already inflected by 36%Y and management team commentary that deal pipelines are growing and of high quality, we model 2024 completed activity up 20%Y even in our bear case. More detail on our bull and bear scenarios can be found here.

Strategics expected to lead the rebound, sponsors to follow: Skew of announced deal activity is a clear indication that strategic deals should lead the rebound. Strategic- and sponsor-announced deal volumes represented 80% and 20% of volume, respectively, in 4Q23; this is the lowest share of announced deal volume for sponsors since 2Q20, down 30pp from the peak of 50% in 2Q22. That said, sponsor-backed deals generally face less antitrust scrutiny, so the time from announcement to close is shorter, meaning that sponsor-backed deal completions could begin to move in line with strategic deals once sponsors begin to deploy dry powder more rapidly. The prospect of lower rates and a more stable financing market backdrop could be a catalyst to get financial sponsors off the sidelines.

Top four reasons why our Financials sector equity analysts are bullish on M&A:

1. Leading indicators continue to point to a rebound in M&A deal activity: Our heat map framework of leading indicators for M&A activity shows the majority of indicators flashing green in both 3Q23 and 4Q23. In particular, every major global equity market index is up year on year (ex Hang Seng), VIX is 43%Y lower, FX volatility is 32-43%Y lower, spreads are lower, HY issuance is up 91%Y, liquidity is improving and corporate cash levels are higher. While interest rates remain elevated, greater clarity on a Fed pause or potential rate cuts should drive activity higher. Deal announcements have already inflected, up 36%Y; we expect announcements to continue accelerating and completions to follow.

2. Pent-up demand: Two years of extraordinarily weak M&A activity should drive up activity levels off a low base as CEO confidence builds. CEOs typically want their companies to grow over time, and inorganic options have always been key levers available to achieve that growth. The last two years have provided opportunistic CEOs, boards and financial sponsors time to build an inorganic growth strategy, evaluate prospects and begin preliminary discussions. This long preparation period should translate to an acceleration in deal announcements and completions as the market turns.

3. Structural drivers of M&A should support this cycle: We see several structural drivers of M&A over the next cycle, including demand for AI and cloud capabilities, the clean energy transition, innovation in life sciences, reshoring and geographic diversification in a multipolar world.

4. Clarity from recent court decisions should increase appetite for large-cap M&A: Courts are beginning to provide clarity on interpretation of antitrust laws. This is important because CEOs can better understand what types of deals are likely to be challenged, how courts will interpret existing law and what the timeline of a potential legal challenge looks like. In addition, in some industries like banking, the Fed and OCC appear more open to M&A.

## Macro headwinds to corporate confidence are reversing

## Andrew Sheets

M&A underwhelmed last year despite strong equity markets and a solid economy. Four factors denting corporate confidence were likely part of that story. All are reversing.

1. High and volatile funding costs: Funding costs matter for corporate activity, whether as a direct cost of financing or the opportunity cost of spending cash that could otherwise earn a return. For most of the last two years, those costs in the US and Europe were shooting higher.

The Fed and ECB both raised policy rates at the fastest pace in 40 years, while longerterm borrowing costs often doubled or tripled. Mindful that corporate management may take a longer-term view than investors, as recently as October 2023 US BBB rated corporates were staring at the one of the fastest 24-month rises in borrowing costs in over 100 years.

Exhibit 6: As recently as October 2023, BBB companies faced one of the largest 24- month increases in borrowing costs in the last century

Source: Bloomberg, Morgan Stanley Research

This move in borrowing costs was more than unusually large or fast; it was unusually uncertain. Following the global financial crisis (GFC), weak growth, forward guidance and successive waves of QT had kept interest rates low, stable and predictable, and that trend continued with pandemic support. But as policy tightened amid the highest inflation in a generation, rates were unshackled, with widely divergent views on what would come next. Interest rate volatility (i.e., uncertainty) jumped, and remains high by post-GFC standards (it’s more in line with a pre-GFC world).

Exhibit 7: While equity uncertainty (i.e., implied volatility) fell, rate uncertainty remained high
![](images/77d304078b7da5f5df9919937526f7750c97ed4c4f2d4be7962f0c408d8b6dea.jpg)
Source: Bloomberg, Morgan Stanley Research

Today, all these elements are improving. Our economists and market pricing see the Fed and ECB making meaningful cuts over the next two years (through end-2025, we forecast the Fed to cut by 300bp and the ECB to cut by 200bp). US 10-year yields have fallen from their autumn highs. And while rate volatility is still elevated, it has begun to come down (gradually).

This is having an impact. Since the end of 3Q23, corporate yields have fallen across US IG, US HY and EUR IG bonds. New issue markets are wide open at these better borrowing levels (see Investment Grade Credit Strategy Brief: Strong Supply Meets Stronger Demand, February 16, 2024).

2. High and/or 'sticky' inflation: While inflation was a driver of higher financing costs, it deserves its own mention as a more existential fear in the eyes of corporates. Over the last 18 months, there was clear concern that inflation might be sticky, fail to return to target, and require even more punitive policy measures. At the extreme were fears of a 1970s-style outcome where inflation rose, improved, but then reaccelerated. That period ultimately required much higher rates and two recessions in the early 1980s to bring inflation back under control – not exactly the environment that makes one eager to take acquisition risk.

Exhibit 8: Businesses became more worried about long-run inflation than consumers...that's changing. Long-run inflation expectations for US businesses are now lower than in early 2019
![](images/15b8caf0682b5283097bac6ea8297e77fe9b36e10fb910ef64846ed1e0c2690b.jpg)
Source: Bloomberg, Morgan Stanley Research; Note: Businesses is the Atlanta Fed's long-term business inflation unit cost mean, Consumer is expected change in prices, median, over the next 5-10 years from the U. Michigan survey.

However, more recent data are helping those fears recede. Over the last six months, core inflation has been falling faster than markets or central banks initially expected in both the US and eurozone. Inflation expectations suggest that both regions will see inflation near 2%Y in 12 months' time. Our economists forecast core inflation to continue to decline towards 2%Y in the US and Europe through end-2025, successfully completing that 'last mile' of inflation improving back to central bank targets.

And what about unexpectedly high inflation readings for January? While these were higher than expected, our economists see this as a 'bump', not a new trend (see Friday Finish – US Economics: Live from the NABE in DC, February 16, 2024).

3. Fear of recession: Fear of a sticky inflation regime could have given companies pause about strategic transactions. But so too could the other side of this debate – widespread fear that the large rate increases necessary to defeat inflation would lead to recession in the US, Europe and elsewhere.

This was, until recently, a widespread concern. In October 2022, for example, a Bloomberg survey of 42 economists put the probability of recession over the next 12 months at 60%. Federal Reserve recession models suggested high chances as well. And to the more casual recession forecaster, 2023 offered plenty of signals which suggested that the risk of a downturn was elevated, including an inverted US yield curve, falling PMIs and weak industrial production, all while data in Europe and China were softer than expected.

Exhibit 9: Manufacturing PMIs have been improving gradually since last summer
![](images/f0c44e2a58cfa89bf3f5c00e2da8908fe9257a2bff7b152a423aa88b6e7cb777.jpg)
Source: Bloomberg, Morgan Stanley Research

Risks to growth remain. But in Morgan Stanley’s base case, 2024 should see lower inflation with positive growth in the US and euro area as a soft landing is achieved. PMIs are improving across the US, Europe and Asia. Leading indicators are no longer falling. We see the US economy growing by \~1.7% 4Q/4Q this year, while the euro area grows by 0.9%.

4. Poor equity performance for the average company: 2023 saw strong gains for equities at the index level, but performance was not nearly as rosy for the average company. While the S&P 500 gained 26% in 2023, the median S&P 500 stock was actually down 5% through October. This is another factor which could help to explain lower levels of C-Suite confidence and aggressiveness last year than headline indices might otherwise suggest.

Performance since late October has been kinder to the median stock. Broadly, we think that this is helpful to confidence at the average company relative to last year.

## There is improvement across all these headwinds to corporate confidence

Global corporate activity has been much lower than economic data or the stock market would suggest. We think that concerns around funding, inflation, growth and stock performance help to explain lower corporate confidence than headline measures would suggest.

The good news? Each of those factors is now improving. Economic data have been resilient, global PMIs are rising, core inflation is moving lower, the median company's stock is doing better and the cost of equity or corporate debt has fallen over the last three months, There is regional variation, of course, but what's notable is that many of these trends, and their improvement, have global characteristics. We think that all support a better 'top-down' picture for corporate activity and M&A.

# Quantitatively, how much did M&A volume undershoot? How much could it rebound?

Diane Ding, Krystal Qian

Quantifying the 'undershoot' in activity: Since M&A activity has always been cyclical, another way to think about the cyclical and structural case for M&A is to reframe the question:

• Over 2021-23, how much did global M&A activity diverge from what economic variables would predict? More activity than expected may represent an overshoot that needs to be worked off; less could represent an undershoot that will ultimately be made up.

We built two quantitative models to answer this question – one centered on fundamental variables for global equities and a year-on-year growth rate, and a second that incorporates a larger share of macro factors forecasting outright volumes. Returning to a common theme across this report, we think that approaching M&A from multiple angles is the best way to address its inherent uncertainty.

In both cases, these models explain over 60% of the variation of global M&A volumes from 1997-2019. We think that this is a reasonable amount of accuracy from such a topdown approach, and reinforces the desire to fly with multiple instruments when making projections.

We then ask these models, which are reasonably good at explaining global M&A from 1997-2019, to explain 2020-present. And that's where things get interesting. In both cases, volumes over the last three years have significantly undershot what the 1997-2019 experience would suggest.

Specifically, projecting M&A growth rates based on global equity fundamentals would have suggested \~US\$20 trillion of activity over 2021-23, based on relationships from 1997-2019, while projecting activity levels based on a wider set of macro variables suggested US\$27 trillion. Yet actual activity was just under US\$16 trillion, leaving a cumulative US\$4-11 trillion undershoot of M&A volume over the last three years. For context, global M&A volume has averaged \~US\$5.5 trillion/year since 2014, per Dealogic data. Both the high and low end of this range represent a large gap to make up.

While the wide range in these estimates speaks to the uncertainty of forecasting M&A, we are encouraged that they both point (materially) in the same direction. And while they support the case for more activity, they are not essential to that story.

Our Financials equity team's above-consensus call for a 50% rebound in 2024 global M&A volume is simply bringing levels back to the 2014-19 average, before any adjustment for inflation or a multi-trillion USD undershoot. And even if this 'gap' is only partially made up through subsequent activity, it represents a sizeable tailwind to volumes over a multi-year period. Put differently, we don't think that the cyclical or structural case relies on activity 'catching up' to what macro variables imply. But we do think that this large gap provides a quantifiable channel through which volumes could surprise positively over a multi-year period.

M&A activity has been unusually depressed in recent years, and far more than historical predictors of activity would imply. We find it remarkable that a period with depressed M&A activity is characterized by an overall economic environment which our econometric analyses associate with particularly strong M&A activity. While many factors were at play, we think that high and uncertain financing costs, high inflation, growth concerns, narrow breadth and low confidence were all significant contributors leading to this gap between predicted and realized activity. We believe that all those factors are now improving, and should continue to do so in the Morgan Stanley base case. This is supportive for the return of M&A.

## Japan: More outbound, more buyouts

Jonathan Garner, Daniel Blake

We are bullish on Japan equities, highlighting a secular trend of rising return on equity and improved corporate governance. Exhibit 18 shows that M&A deal count and volumes for Japan have been broadly stable in recent years, with a slight rise towards the end of 2023. ## Asia ex Japan: A contrast to Japan, but bright spots exist

## Jonathan Garner, Daniel Blake

We are cautious on Asia ex Japan and emerging markets in general from an index perspective. Instead we recommend focusing on individual market opportunities and in particular India and parts of ASEAN (Singapore and Indonesia) among the larger markets.

The M&A picture overall in Asia ex Japan is in sharp contrast to that of Japan, with deal count and volumes currently exceptionally low by historical standards with no evidence of recovery at the end of 2023 and into 2024. The biggest drag on activity is China/Hong Kong, which have dominated historically. This is due to the confluence of a severe slowdown in nominal GDP growth in China as well as heightened regulatory activity in a wide range of strategic sectors – both as it impacts onshore activity but also in relation to cross-border transactions. In contrast, three large M&A transactions involving inward M&A to Australia have already been announced in 2024 (in the Materials and Technology sectors).

## Who? Quantitative Screens for M&A

If M&A activity picks up, which companies have higher chances of being impacted? This question may be one of the most important for both equity and credit investors, but is also one of the most difficult to answer given the inherent uncertainty of activity. We think that quantitative screens provide a rule-based way to address this question, but the nature of acquisitions means that uncertainty remains high: in all cases, the acquisition of any particular company is, historically, a rare and unlikely event.

## Screening for M&A activity: Morgan Stanley ALERT

## Diane Ding, Michelle Weaver

We have developed quantitative models to forecast the likelihood that a stock in the US or Europe receives a tender offer in the coming 12 months; these models are called ALERT (Acquisition Likelihood Estimate Ranking Tool) and ALERT-E (for Europe), respectively. Our models are agnostic about the method of funding, the type or location of the acquirer, the premium for the offer, if any, the existence of any regulatory hurdles, the willingness of any of the parties to participate in M&A, or even whether a potential deal ultimately closes. Across both models, stocks that are smaller in size, cheaper on price to book, higher quality, and whose sector has seen an elevated number of offers emerge from the screen as more likely to receive tender offers. Each model also has number of factors exclusive to that model.

We published these models until 2022 and are bringing them back out for this comprehensive look at M&A, given that they were both developed using several cycles worth of data (ALERT 1983-2012, ALERT-E 1998-2017). For more on the US model, including full methodology, see Introducing ALERT: The Acquisition Likelihood Estimate Ranking Tool. January 22, 2013. For more on the European model, including full methodology, see Introducing ALERT-E: Our European M&A Likelihood Model, August 14, 2017.

We emphasize that inclusion in ALERT does not, in itself, indicate a high likelihood of corporate activity. On any single name in the top quintile, Q1, of ALERT, there is still a low probability that it will receive an offer – less than 5% in our backtests. This number is low because we are trying to predict rare events. For the entire universe, the probability is under 3%.

## Screening for private equity activity: Introducing the Morgan Stanley Private Activity Similarity Score (PASS)

## Krystal Qian

Morgan Stanley PASS (Private Activity Similarity Score) is a new quantitative screen that comes out of our Quantitative Investment Strategies (QIS) existing research on private equity. It looks to (a) identify the factors that are characteristic for US private equity targets over the last five years, and (b) screen for public companies with factor exposure most similar to those targets. We summarize the latter as a 'similarity score': on a scale of 0-100%, how similar are these companies to those that private equity has targeted. In Exhibit 29 , we show the 30 names with the most similar characteristics to these recent private equity targets.

We emphasize that this analysis is completely backward looking, based on historical acquisition data. It will not capture a change in preferences by acquirers. Similar to ALERT, it also does not consider the existence of any regulatory hurdles, the willingness of any of the parties to participate in M&A, or even whether a potential deal ultimately closes. We do not have evidence that inclusion in PASS makes a company more likely to be acquired over time.

## Methodology to identify companies similar to private equity targets

Here we give a brief summary of our approach. Interested readers can find more details in Liquid Access to Private Equity-Like Returns, March 2, 2023.

We fit a binomial Logistic Regression analysis using Barra exposures of private equity targets and non-targets in the Russell 3000 in the previous five years. We then concentrate on the more liquid Russell 1000 when we forecast and screen for the companies with the highest probability of being a private equity target. More specifically, we run the following approach:

• For the Logistic Regression analysis, we define

(0, if the company is not a PE target in the 5 year lookback window
1, if the company is a PE target in the 5 year lookback window

In other words, for each 1-year period of the 5-year lookback window, we collect Barra style and sector factor exposures at the announcement dates for targets acquired in the 1-year period and at the beginning of the year for nontargets in Russell 3000. We then stack all the 1-year data (5 in total) together by concatenating to arrive at the final data.

• Due to the imbalance between the number of targets and non-targets, we run a weighted Logistic Regression procedure on the training data by assigning weights inversely proportional to class frequencies.

Karen Chen, Joyce Jiang, Vishwas Patkar, Andrew Sheets

Our proprietary survey of 150 Morgan Stanley equity analysts sees a stronger case to fund deals with cash/debt (43%) than equity (10%). Funding with debt: For investment grade issuers, the market remains open for companies that have funding needs. January started the year on strong footing with record gross issuance at close to US\$200 billion in the month, while February was also healthy. The majority of the funding came from Financials, but non-financial corporates have also started stepping in. The record supply was met by strong demand from investors that have funds to put to work and also want to lock in yield levels before potential rate cuts diminish those opportunities. ## Bond covenants not an impediment to M&A overall; they still matter on a case-by-case basis

Jenna Giannelli, Brian Gibbons, Andrew Sheets

But there's another side to M&A's fixed income story. Due to the sharp rise in bond yields over the last two years, the average price of investment grade bonds is well below 'par'. Some of these bonds contain 'change of control' (CoC) covenants that can force an issuer to redeem these bonds above par under certain acquisition scenarios, which is unusually punitive given current (low) bond prices. Could this be a hidden cost to dealmaking that holds M&A back?

While significant in specific cases, generally speaking we don't think that it will be:

1. We estimate that CoC language is present in about \~30% of US IG debt. While this still represents \~US\$1.8 trillion of outstanding bonds, it is a distinct minority of overall IG debt. It tends to be more common for BBB rated IG issuers.

2. CoC language is generally meant to protect bondholders against adverse transactions they have no say over (shareholders vote, bondholders do not). In the vast majority of cases, CoC language only triggers if the issuer is downgraded below 'investment grade' (below Baa3/BBB-). In turn, many types of strategic activity may not trigger a CoC event, even where such language exists. In an M&A environment where strategic acquirers have an upper hand over private equity, it is even more likely that transactions wouldn't result in the type of downgrade that would trigger CoC.

3. CoC is much more common in US high yield issuers (88% of HY bonds by market value, versus 28% in US IG). But mitigating this effect somewhat is that the average price of high yield bonds rated BB/B is actually higher (\~96c) than those rated IG (\~92c), representing a lower cost to triggering CoC. We'd further note that restrictive covenants on outstanding debt is a long-standing issue for anyone targeting a sub-IG corporate acquisition.

CoC language in bonds could scupper transactions if the cost of paying a CoC price represented a large share of the overall corporate market cap. In most cases, it doesn't. Across 600 of the larger US IG issuers, the average cost of redeeming all bonds with CoC language at 101c is less than 1% of the current equity market cap. As such, we don't think that this issue acts as a major impediment to M&A overall.

CoC caveats to consider: While change-of-control put rights protect bondholders in the event of a takeover (and act as a possible sponsor deterrent), there are often several conditions that must be met to constitute a CoC ‘event’. A change in beneficial ownership will often be the driving force. However, those CoC provisions – including rating conditions, downgrade requirements, holding company exceptions or permitted holder carveouts – could act as means for sponsors to avoid a CoC trigger, whereby bonds would stay outstanding. These triggers, for instance, have been a point of discussion on Macy’s, creating incremental volatility in the structure as investors debate whether a CoC will definitively trigger.

While these screens attempt to provide a high-level overview and narrow the focus of investors, CoC language can vary significantly issuer by issuer and bond by bond. This has been, and will remain, and ongoing theme of our sector credit research.

Conversely, we also acknowledge the potential downside risk to investors: While CoC and make-whole take-outs present upside to LBO target bonds with such protections, there is also significant risk in those that lack them. For instance, where CoC provisions are lacking in higher-quality bonds, the likelihood of a BBB (or higher) bond soon becoming a single B like bond post LBO is a significant credit negative. While many IG indentures contain limitations on liens (and would require existing bonds to be secured pari passu with any new secured debt issued), the result is still often spread widening, and downside price action in the notes.

Upside beyond \$101 (or potential sponsor derailment?): Under most high yield bond indentures, limitations on restricted payments and liens are present. These limitations curtail a company’s ability to make restricted payments (the issuance of debt to pay to shareholders in an LBO is considered a restricted payment) or issue incremental secured debt (often required to finance large LBO structures). Where capacity under these covenants is insufficient, depending on the size of the transaction, bonds with such protections must be redeemed, often at make-whole prices. While make-whole prices could be close to par or the \$101 CoC, they are often materially higher, depending on duration and required make-whole spread of the bond. This could act as a further upside catalyst for bondholders, but could also derail sponsors from a deal if take-out costs become uneconomical at much higher dollar prices. Like CoC uncertainty, bond trading volatility can also arise if investors are unclear as to whether sufficient restricted

payment, liens or secured debt capacity is available, and could leave trading prices (prior to a transaction) as a 'blended average' of possible CoC, tender or make-whole prices.

Finally, we'd note that it will not always be in the interest of a bondholder to trigger CoC (often at 101c). If a HY issuer is purchased by an IG-rated company, it's very possible that the spread difference between the two could lead the HY bond price to rally well above 101. It's another instance where generalization can be tricky, and issuer-level analysis is important.

# Does Private Equity 'Work' at Current Yields?

Michael Cyprys, Ryan Kenny, Connell Schmitz

Our view is that strategic activity will lead the rebound, but sponsor activity will quickly follow: While sponsors are more sensitive to the level of interest rates given their use of leverage to fund deals, the prospect of lower rates and a more stable financing market backdrop should be a catalyst to get financial sponsors off the sidelines. Commentary from the sponsor-skewed investment banks in our stock coverage supports deal activity increasing, with Moelis (MC) management highlighting during 4Q23 earnings that the sponsor pipeline is building with an expectation for a gradual build to start in 2024 before eventually moving "rapidly". In addition, sponsor-backed deals generally face less antitrust scrutiny, so the time from announcement to close is shorter, meaning that sponsor-backed deal completions could begin to move in line with strategic deals once sponsors begin deploying dry powder more rapidly.

Following a record year in 2021, supported by low rates and fiscal/monetary stimulus, aggregate sponsor-backed M&A deal volumes (measured by value) fell 29%Y in 2022 and then a further 52%Y in 2023. Interestingly, over the same period the total count of deals announced did not fall as significantly as the total value of deals: deal count in 2022 was actually up 1%Y in 2022 and down just 26%Y in 2023. This suggests that on average deal sizes collapsed during the last two years of elevated rates. This makes intuitive sense in a credit-constrained world because smaller deals generally are less likely to require leverage to get across the line, and when leverage is employed, the dollar amounts involved are smaller and easier to fund. However, we do acknowledge imperfections in the data because certain deal values are not disclosed. This means that the aggregate deal count is always more comprehensive than the aggregate deal value and the proportion of deals with disclosed deal values is not necessarily consistent over time.

# The Case for More M&A in Private Markets

Edward Stanley

644 US businesses have gone public at a valuation of over US\$1 billion over the past 20 years, according to Bloomberg data. By contrast, there are currently 661 US-based venture capital-backed unicorns with a valuation of over US\$1 billion, many of which investors, founders and employees hope to achieve an exit on. We see strategic M&A as a more likely scenario than an IPO for many of these companies.

Private market consolidation is not only likely but also necessary: Start-up options are expiring out of the money at record levels according to Carta. Bridge financing and convertible notes – allowing businesses to delay revaluation – are becoming more challenging to obtain and cash burn remains unsustainable for many businesses. The IPO markets appear to be opening but not for unprofitable business which many unicorns still are, even having 'got fit' since 2022. This consolidation will likely be both opportunistic and by necessity.

M&A through necessity: Liquidity conditions have tightened substantially over the past two years. There are few better ways to show this than by tracking the number and valuation of new unicorns minted over time. After a period of c.1.5 unicorns and US\$4 billion of unicorn value being minted per day (on a 90-day rolling average), both figures are now trending towards zero. In the absence of the latest AI unicorn minting flurry, these figures would in fact be zero – the lowest level since 2017. It is these conditions which give us additional confidence that we are now in or approaching a 'buyer's market' for these assets as they struggle to raise further funding/follow-on rounds from venture/ growth backers.

# Investment Implications – Sectors in Focus

Ryan Kenny, Andrew Sheets

Combining these top-down and bottom-up perspectives on a coming rise in corporate activity, we dig deeper into sectors and stocks that could be most impacted.

## Financials

Ways to play the capital markets rebound: Optimism around an uptick in corporate activity is one of several factors that have driven our US Financials team to upgrade US Midcap Advisors (see Midcap Advisors: Let's Make a Deal; Upgrade Industry to Attractive, July 12, 2023) and US Large Cap Banks (see Large Cap Banks: The Future's So Bright, We Gotta Upgrade, January 30, 2024) on higher investment banking fees. Within our US investment banking coverage, we view GS (OW), EVR (OW) and LAZ (OW) as the best ways to play a rebound in capital markets activity.

European Financials should also benefit from greater corporate activity, albeit on a smaller scale; our constructive view on European Banks is driven more by what we see as resilient earnings and compelling valuations (see Banks: Earnings resilience to pay off, December 4, 2023). Still, we expect green shoots in IBD, and for 2024 see revenue up 40% for Global IBs in aggregate. Within our European banks coverage, we view BARC (OW) and DBK (EW) as the best ways to play this theme.

Within our Diversified Financials coverage we view BX (OW) and KKR (EW) as the best ways to play a rebound in capital markets activity. We view BX as a core holding in alts for investors looking for exposure to the group, with best-in-class franchise, brand, and unrivalled product capabilities and distribution breadth. KKR stands to benefit from cyclical transaction fees, resourceful deployment of the balance sheet that boosts earnings, and undemanding valuation. Within our European coverage we view EQT (OW) and ICP (OW) as best plays on a rebound in capital markets activity given scope for improving carry fees and broader positive implications for fund raising as cash flows to LPs improve. We would also highlight ENX (OW) within our exchange coverage as well placed given its more pro-cyclical model (\~30% of revenues linked to cash equity trading and listing). Combined with growing synergy delivery linked to in-sourcing of clearing, we expect this to lead to a \~10% EBITDA CAGR in 2023-25, well above average in our global exchange coverage.

US Banks: The US banking system has been consolidating over the last several decades, with the number of banks consolidating from 14,400+ in 1980 to 12,300+ in 1990, 8,300+ in 2000, 6,500+ in 2010 and \~4,100 today. The US banking system remains highly fragmented relative to other countries and our view is that the need for scale is accelerating, which should drive up bank M&A over time.

Following regional banking stress in 2023, we think that the value of holding diversified, core low-cost deposits has gone up significantly. This could be a catalyst for further consolidation as it is expensive for banks to invest in products and capabilities to attract new core depositors, especially when the largest banks are investing tens of billions of dollars annually into technology and offer cutting-edge mobile apps and payment

systems. Regulatory requirements are also getting tougher and the bank supervision process is getting stricter, meaning that banks have to have better internal control systems and better risk management. This will also require elevated investment spend and scale. One way to get scale is to merge.

Many areas of banking benefit from scale, including client onboarding, transaction processing, infrastructure (including IT, legal, compliance and risk), in addition to many other functions. Scale can also help to support brand value, which can be leveraged to attract and retain clients. The need for scale is increasing given materially higher regulatory standards, in particular for banks in Category III (US\$250-700 billion in assets) and Category IV (US\$100-250 billion in assets), with tougher supervision likely for all banks at or above US\$50 billion in assets. The second major driver is technology. With AI just beginning to impact how banks are managing their business, the stakes are getting higher to increase technology investment spend. Larger banks invest 9-22% of revenues in technology. While we don’t have a similar number for banks in Category IV and below, the absolute dollars of investment spend would be significantly lower even if they spent a similar percentage of revenues, showing the importance of scale.

With that said, there are three headwinds to bank M&A in the near term. First, many banks are sitting on negative unrealized losses in the securities and loan books, driven by Fed rate hikes. When a bank is sold, these securities and loans get marked to market. This headwind should fade over time as securities roll to par and if the Fed cuts rates. Second, concerns around credit quality in Commercial Real Estate (CRE) loans could keep potential acquirers on the sidelines, as banks don't want to buy someone else's potential problem. Third, regulators have been slow to approve bank M&A. As these headwinds fade, we expect that bank M&A will pick up. The rationale is that managements and boards are focused on improving bank ROE, since bank stocks are primarily valued based on expectations for ROE. Bank mergers should enable acquirers to increase operating leverage, driving ROE higher than they can achieve on their own.

European Banks | Domestic M&A to continue; IBD revenues to bounce back strongly: The European banking sector has seen some large transactions over the recent years, e.g., UBS-CS, Santander-Popular, Caixa-Bankia, Intesa-Veneto, but it is still much in need of more consolidation, in our view. We see room for in-market consolidation to continue, with Italy to be the main market in focus, given fragmentation.

On the investment banking side, we flag that not all IBs stand to benefit from the trend of higher global M&A equally. French banks are much less exposed to IBD revenues, for example, whereas we flag that Barclays (particularly exposed to sponsors activity), Deutsche Bank and UBS screen well here (we are 10-20% ahead of consensus on IB revenues for these two names). We would additionally flag that with a relatively higher cost/income than peers at a group level, the impact to the bottom line can be more material for DBK and UBS in 2024. By business mix within IBD, we note that DBK and Barclays are skewed to DCM, which therefore should help revenues in the first half of 2024 (Dealogic data suggest revenues up 25%Y in DCM, while down 24%Y on M&A and flattish in ECM), whereas in the second half, once M&A activity really picks up, UBS stands to benefit the most.

Traditional asset managers: For traditional asset managers facing structural pressures, M&A remains as an option and tool to bolster growth, with most firms targeting acquisitions which enhance their alternative and/or private market capabilities. Increased investor interest in private markets has piqued traditional managers' desire to diversify their asset mix with less correlated strategies. We would not be surprised if this trend of private credit additions continued given the attractiveness of the asset class and the secular growth opportunity.

Alternative asset managers: Unlike traditional asset managers, we expect alternative asset managers to benefit from secular tailwinds which should support double-digit industry growth, but we still see a role for M&A that helps to diversify either investment capabilities (i.e., building out multi-asset exposures, which in some cases transcend private markets into public) or the investor base (i.e., access to growth from channels including insurance, retail and international LPs).

Exchanges: Turning to the exchanges, their core transactional businesses are largely mature and M&A is being used to facilitate extension into the Information Services sector, which offers highly recurring revenues in faster-growing end markets that command higher valuations. We also see firms using M&A to acquire technology capabilities.

Brokers: The brokers/wealth managers are a diverse group, with the role of M&A dependent on the focus area of each company. The wealth brokers face a consolidating industry where the technology expectations of advisors necessitate investments that only the most scaled platforms can meet.

## Energy

We have long argued that well-structured M&A is a path to create value in a market focused on FCF, returns and inventory quality. Activity in 2022 and 1H23 focused on smaller, bolt-on transactions (largely privates selling to larger publics). More recently, we saw the return of large corporate deals, including XOM/PXD and CVX/HES (see here and here) – two of the largest energy transactions in decades, bringing deal value in 2023 to the highest levels in over 10 years. Notably, recent deals been completed with low premiums in the 5-15% range, even for high-quality assets, well below the \~30% average in the few years pre-Covid.

Looking ahead, we expect M&A to continue: We expect transactions with clear strategic rationale, accretion to financial metrics and shareholder returns, and enhancement to inventory to be received positively. Over time, we would expect industry consolidation to lead to fewer but higher-quality companies, a positive for the investability of the sector (see Energy: Revisiting The '24 Setup: Cash Comes Cheap, January 8, 2024).

## Health Care

Heath Care was a clear standout in our global analyst survey, with 13 of 18 global teams covering different segments of the industry around the globe expecting M&A to increase (and none expecting a decline). Interest rate stability, sector fragmentation and company desire to find growth were all key drivers of this optimism.

In US Health Care Services, our analysts believe that the need for scale and declines in interest rates could drive renewed M&A activity. The sub-sector of benefits navigation and advocacy could serve as a particularly interesting case study. M&A is a theme across Health Care in ASEAN. In US and European Biopharma, European Biopharma faces generic competition to \~US\$66 billion of revenue by the end of the decade, with \~US\$180 billion in revenue from legacy franchises beginning to come off patent for US Biopharma. Given the magnitude of upcoming LOEs and the strength of balance sheets across the industry, we believe that conditions for M&A in US and European Large-Cap Biopharma are favorable.

## Hotels

A return of M&A could also be supportive for asset-light hoteliers given the value it can create and the relatively low valuations in Europe. Historically, major transactions (e.g., Marriott-Starwood, Accor-FRHI) have led to material SG&A savings as well as commission savings for owners from OTA renegotiations. The hotel industry remains fragmented, with the world's largest operator Marriott having a global market share of only 7% of rooms, and the top five companies combined only controlling \~25% of the market, well below other consumer-facing travel segments (e.g., >80% for top five in US airlines, car rental and cruise).

In the US, deal-making has been dominated by the Choice/Wyndham deal discussions, potentially signalling a return of industry consolidation (see here). For more, see Hotels: The Return of M&A? October 17, 2023, and Leisure & Hotels: 2024 Outlook: 4 Top Picks, 2 Bottom Picks, 5 Possible Surprises, January 9, 2024.

## US Real Estate

In 2023, 11 deals to acquire publicly listed REITs were announced, with a total deal value of \~US\$61 billion and 96% of the value reflecting acquisitions by listed REITs. We note that the four factors which have determined performance of the stock post the deal announcement were: 1) Immediate earnings accretion; 2) Leverage neutral; 3) Maintained or improved portfolio quality; and 4) Strategic benefit such as economies of scale and improved cost of capital. Our analysis of 64 M&A transactions for US\$337 billion of value from 2017-22 suggests that the average deal premium was 12.8% and \~40% of transactions by value were public REIT privatizations.

We highlight eight potential scenarios where M&A could unlock value and note that we have no knowledge of any discussion involving any of the companies in our theoretical scenario. See The REIT Weekly: Where could we see REIT M&A in 2024? December 11, 2023, for more.

Self Storage REITs: Following the EXR and LSI deal last year, we see scope for further consolidation in the space. We see potential for M&A activity in names where the balance sheet remains underlevered and where transactions could accelerate the growth of thirdparty management platforms, increase scale in existing markets as well as add new markets.

Apartment REITs: There was not much M&A activity in apartments in 2023, but the group trades at an average of 15.2x consensus 2024 FFO as of early February 2024, a slight discount to the REIT weighted average of 17.2x given supply fears, with very low leverage of 4.0-5.0x. We see scope for both public to public deals as well as public to private. Indeed, the record US\$300 billion of real estate dry powder on the sidelines may find a public versus private valuation disconnect compelling. Valuation on SunBelt apartment REITs as well as small and mid-cap apartment REITs could be in focus as long-term demand trends remain favorable.

Industrial REITs: Private capital continues to have interest in industrial assets and there is wide dispersion in valuation from 16-26x consensus 2024 FFO (with a weighted average of 23.0x) as of early February 2024. Indeed, LXP (not covered) suspended its strategic alternative process in March 2022 but has said that it remains open to opportunities to maximize value as shares trade at 14.0x consensus 2024 FFO. We note that leverage is elevated at 6.9x.

Retail REITs: Following the REG and UBP deal as well as the KIM and RPT deal, we see opportunity for further consolidation in the space. We see several strip center REITs in the 11-12.5x consensus 2024e FFO trading range that could potentially be consolidation targets where there is geographic and strategic overlap with peers trading at higher multiples. Lastly, we also note that SITC has announced a spin-off to unlock value. As we discussed in The State of CRE Monthly, February 5, 2024, we note that while broader CRE distressed sales remain low (increasing by a mere 20bp quarter on quarter to 2.0% of total sales in 4Q23), retail (in addition to office) is among the sub-sectors garnering the most distressed activity. Big players are waiting on the sidelines for distressed opportunities, armed with their \~US\$300 billion+ of dry powder earmarked for real estate.

Triple Net REITs: Following the O and SRC deal in 2023, we see opportunity for further consolidation in the space. There are 15+ publicly traded triple net REITs and we see potential for activity in names with low leverage and that trade at relatively inexpensive multiples.

Health Care REITs: Following the PEAK and DOC deal in 2023, we see a higher bar for M&A activity in 2024. We note that Health Care REITs may lack the cost of capital for accretive deals and we don't think that senior housing players are interested in reducing exposure to senior housing by adding to outpatient medical and skilled nursing sectors.

Office REITs: There has not been much M&A activity in office this cycle given balance sheet leverage and different geographic focus, which likely remains a headwind to activity next year. SLG, VNO and PGRE have stated that they are focused on unlocking value through asset sales or strategic defaults on debt. As we recently discussed in The State of CRE Monthly, we note that while broader CRE distressed sales remain low (increasing by a mere 20bp quarter on quarter to 2.0% of total sales in 4Q23), office (in addition to retail) is among the sub-sectors garnering the most distressed activity, which may present opportunities.

CRE Services/Brokers: Consolidation of a fragmented industry is a recurring theme in the commercial real estate broker space, and we would expect smaller tuck-in deals versus public to public transactions. Indeed, CBRE (not covered), the largest global player in the CRE services space, announced earlier this month the US\$800 million (plus up to US\$250 million earnout) acquisition of J&J Worldwide Services, a leading provider of facilities maintenance for the US federal government. As CRE transaction activity begins to recover, the higher-margin capital markets and advisory segments for CRE brokers will also begin their recovery, given their role in the associated transactions. As a result, as rates step down and M&A activity picks up, we see an opportunity for CRE brokers to outperform.

## European Telecom

Our analysts see three drivers for an eventful M&A year in the European Telecom space, which could skew the sector's risk/reward upwards. For more details, see European Telco: M&A Landscape: Get ready for an eventful 2024, January 29, 2024.

#1 Acquisitions – incremental buyers still at the gates: We drilled into listed Telecom acquisition activity over the past four years and flagged the important role played by shareholders with longer-term investment horizons. In small/mid caps, we noted similarities in private buy-outs with opportunistic moves from anchor shareholders, and we assessed the characteristics of the remaining assets in the space. Looking into the new category of anchor shareholders emerging in big caps, we believe that opportunistic stake taking and raising could continue in 'value' names.

#2 Intra-market consolidation – room for more: Europe has the most fragmented Telecom market in the world. The 27 countries of the EU and the UK have a combined population of c.515 million people. We estimate that there are c.95-100 mobile network operators among these 28 European nations. This implies just 5 million POPs per operator on average versus 31 million in Japan, 107 million in the US and 467 million in China. We screened 13 European markets, mapping ARPUs, leverage, smaller players' financial health and recent M&A newsflow. We concluded that there is an appetite from the industry to consolidate markets, in particular when fundamentals are challenged and where smaller players are keen to exit/consolidate. A crucial catalyst could be a change in antitrust approach under the next commission (post June elections). We note that more than two-thirds of European markets have at least four players, leaving plenty of room for more consolidation.

## Technology (especially Software)

Information Technology has some of the most favorable current dynamics for M&A globally of any sector in our proprietary survey. Out of 20 global sector teams covering Information Technology around the globe, 40% believe that M&A will surprise markets to the high side (with another 40% believing that M&A will meet expectations). Within that, we'd give special mention to Software.

Software is an area where private equity-backed deals continue to dominate the landscape (five private equity-backed deals in 2023 paid an average multiple of 7.1x TTM sales), a factor which helps to explain the prevalence of software names in our PASS

framework earlier in this report (see US Tech: 4Q23 CIO Survey – AI Rises to the Top of the CIO's Priority List, January 11, 2024). Communication Software is a segment where strategic activity could offer a quicker way to expand platforms, addressing the point product problem (see Communication Software: 2024 Outlook: Contacting an Agent for Growth, December 14, 2023).

Technology (including Software) also has an unusually large private market ecosystem that will be important to monitor (see Internet & Software: GenAI Privates: Where Are We Now and 12 Debates From Here, December 20, 2023).

Our goal in this note is to assess potential industry M&A, not to highlight any specific transactions; we have no knowledge of any discussions involving any of the companies in our scenario analysis. Nor does our analysis take into account the willingness of any of these companies to merge, or comments their managements may have made about M&A, as our intent is simply to identify as fully as possible potential merger scenarios. The object is to demonstrate scope for continued industry M&A.
