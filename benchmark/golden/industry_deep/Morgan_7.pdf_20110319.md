## Schrodinger’s Cut

Global developed market central banks have already been easing in recent months. The Bank of Canada (last week) and the ECB (this week) have cut rates twice so far this cycle, and the Bank of England once. Next week, it is all but certain that the Fed will join the chorus. But the question remains as to whether the magnitude of the rate cut will be 25bp or 50bp. The odds of a 50bp cut have fluctuated considerably in recent weeks, ranging from a low of 10% to as high as 45% (Figure 1), in response to economic data (such as a firm CPI report and Friday's soft import price data) as well as nuggets of information (such as the WSJ and FT reports that the FOMC was considering whether to cut rates by 50bp). This heightened sensitivity is consistent with the current backdrop. Just last week, we noted that our measure of policy uncertainty (which is based on a de-construction of option-implied probability distributions into intuitive weights that are associated with each policy outcome) was likely unsustainable, as expectations had quickly coalesced around an accelerated easing path which was by no means a foregone conclusion (see Rates, unlike the economy, are not yet in “equipoise”). Indeed, near term policy uncertainty is once again back at elevated levels as evidenced by the diminished weight on the dominant policy scenario calculated from Z4 SOFR futures options prices (Figure 2). This same metric suggests that policy uncertainty is rather low at the M5 horizon, which is indicative of the fact that it is the pace which

remains uncertain for now.

What does this mean for yield levels and curve? As seen in Figure 1, markets are currently pricing in near 50-50 odds, meaning that we are now in a superposition of the 25bp and 50bp states of the world. But, much like the proverbial Schrodinger's Cat whose state-of-being is indeterminate until the box is opened, Schrodinger's Cut will be resolved into a 25bp or a 50bp cut at next week's FOMC meeting. As a consequence, yields at the front end will likely finish next week either higher (in the case of a 25bp cut) or lower (in case of a 50bp rate cut). Moreover, given our earlier observation that the uncertainty is mostly around near term pace, a 25bp rate cut is likely to result in a flatter curve at the very front end, while a 50bp cut will likely result in a steeper curve. One way to position for this is to look for efficient conditional trades designed to position for higher as well as lower yields.

To position for higher yields in the event of a 25bp rate cut, we recommend initiating a 1s/3s conditional flattener in a selloff using 3M expiry payer swaptions. As seen in Figure 3, the 1s/3s curve has been directional with yields in recent month, and is likely to flatten if front end yields rise. Additionally, 3M expiry swaption ATMF implied volatility in 1Y and 3Y tails are approximately equal, allowing for a conditional bear flattener to be constructed premium neutral at the forwards. Moreover, the residual from this relationship has been mean-reverting and is currently about 3bp. Therefore, we recommend conditionally positioning for a flatter 1s/3s curve in a selloff using 3M expiry payer swaptions (see Trade recommendations).

To position for the other eventuality, we recommend initiating exposure to a conditional steepening of the 2s/10s curve in a rally, financed by selling 24% risk-weighted receiver swaptions on 7-year tails. The 2s/10s curve is also fairly directional and is likely to steepen in a rally. However, implied volatility on 2Y tails is higher than 10Y tails, because of which a conditional 2s/10s bull steepener struck at the forwards requires a premium outlay. One way to make the trade more efficient is to sell 24% risk weighted 3Mx7Y receiver swaptions, which is sufficient to make the package premium neutral, but still leaves significant directional exposure in the weighted swap yield spread as seen in Figure 4. Therefore, we recommend initiating exposure to a steeper 2s/10s swap curve in a rally using 3M expiry receiver swaptions, financed by selling 24% of the risk in 3Mx7Y receiver swaptions (see Trade recommendations).

Another way to gain asymmetric exposure to a larger rate cut than currently priced into forwards is to buy the USZ4 factor-weighted CTD basis. Currently, the forward 1M term repo as of the start of December is around 4.5%. Given the USZ4 CTD's coupon and price (4.625% and \~110 respectively), carry during the delivery month could turn positive if that forward repo were to decline to 4.2% or lower. This is significant because USZ4 is currently priced to early delivery, but if forward carry during the delivery month were to turn positive, futures will reprice to late delivery, which means the gross basis (at an early Dec horizon) will widen to the tune of 1 month's positive carry. In contrast, if the forward repo rate during December were to rise, early delivery will remain optimal and the gross basis (at a 12/2/24 horizon) will likely converge to zero. Thus, a long CTD factor-weighted basis position, with repo termed out to first delivery day, has a payoff profile that resembles that of a call option on forward repo rates (Figure 5), and can therefore be thought of as a cheap option on more aggressive Fed cuts. To be sure, this embedded repo option in the USZ4 basis is slightly out of the money (by about 25bp). Nonetheless, given that the basis net of carry to early December is currently close to zero, we see this as a cheap way to position for the prospect of a more aggressive Fed easing cycle. Therefore, we recommend buying the USZ4 factor-weighted CTD basis (with repo termed out to 12/2) (see Trade recommendations).

Finally,in the spirit of gaining asymmetric exposure to yields going in either direction, we also recommend buying the WNZ4 CTD factor-weighted basis as an asymmetric way to gain bearish exposure. This basis is currently trading near 1/32nds net of carry. This is an unusually narrow net basis for this sector, but for good reason - the CTD now has a much higher conversion factor making the wildcard (which has been the main reason historically for a wide net basis in this sector) worth much less. However, in a selloff and/or long end curve steepening scenario, the CTD is likely to switch to lower coupon issues (first the 4% Nov '52, and eventually the 2% Feb '50s). Thus, in a sustained selloff, both the CTD switch and the increasing wildcard option would both likely result in a widening net basis, as we can see in our projections (Figure 6). Therefore, we recommend buying the WNZ4 factorweighted CTD basis (with repo termed out to 12/2) as a way of gaining option-like exposure to higher yields (see Trade recommendations).

## Options

The current backdrop of elevated near term policy uncertainty also keeps us bullish on volatility for now, as we head into the "Schrodinger's cut" at next week's FOMC meeting. One, as we noted earlier, markets are priced squarely to a near 50% probability for both a 25bp and a 50bp cut, which speaks to maximal near-term uncertainty. Two, September meeting is also an SEP meeting, where the FOMC "dots" will be closely watched for YE24 and YE25 projections. Given the potential for rates to recalibrate to a deeper or shallower path based on new information next week, we believe conditions remain favorable for maintaining a long volatility bias for a while longer. This is especially true after the declines in implieds this week. As seen in Figure 7, this week's decline in swaption implied volatility has been in excess of the expected change (i.e., change in our empirical model estimate of fair value in each sector). Therefore, we continue to see the case for a long vol bias as compelling.

To be sure, there are counter-arguments. For one, even after this week's declines, implied volatility continues to look rich relative to our estimates of fair value, as also seen in Figure 7. But as we noted a few weeks ago, our long run empirical fair value model does not account for the effects of near term policy uncertainty, which we believe is a sufficient offset to counter the apparent richness of implieds (see Hopscotch).

Another risk to a long vol bias is if next week's FOMC meeting produces significantly greater clarity around policy, which could result in lower risk premia and lower volatility. One interesting way to initiate a long gamma bias, while also mitigating this risk, is to buy risk premium in the swaptions market while selling it in equities - i.e., buy swaption volatility versus a long position in S&P 500 futures. Although both equities and rate volatility are driven by numerous other factors, overall risk premia is a common driver, and one that will likely be dominant in the near term as it can be impacted by Fed messaging. Moreover, currently, risk premium is likely being underpriced in swaption volatility relative to the risk premium in stocks. One reductionist way to see this is to explore the empirical connection between delta hedged swaption returns and equity returns. We do this in Figure 8, where we describe a simple empirical model for rolling monthly delta hedged short straddle returns using only two factors: in-sample monthly S&P 500 returns, and ex-ante levels of implied volatility. As seen there, equity returns are indeed a statistically significant driver of short gamma returns, with right-way dependence - i.e., falling risk premia and higher stock valuations produce higher short gamma returns, and vice versa.

As a reminder to our readers, we view swap spreads through the lens of two summary metrics of the swap spread term structure, term funding premium (TFP, which impacts the slope of the spread curve) and zero-duration spreads (ZDS, which defines the level at which the front end of the swap spread curve is anchored). We calculate these metrics by regressing maturity matched swap spreads in all benchmark tenors versus modified duration of the corresponding bonds, where we define zero-duration spreads as the intercept from this regression, and we define term funding premium as the negative of the slope (for details, see Term Funding Premium and the Term Structure of SOFR Swap Spreads). In order for us to take a view on the evolution of the swap spread term structure, we model these metrics using intuitive supply and demand factors.

Over the past three weeks or so, term funding premium has become well anchored and has remained stable. This is likely a reflection of the upside pressure (from the longer term upside risk to UST supply) being counteracted by the likely stronger demand from bond funds, which continue to see increases in AUM. This means that zero-duration spreads have become much more significant in driving the outlook for swap spreads overall. For several months now, zero-duration spreads have narrowed relative to fair value and our expectations of a normalization have been very slow to materialize, which led us to turn neutral last week.

Going forward, we continue to see a neutral stance as appropriate in the near term. In our view, higher GC repo rates relative to SOFR was one of the drivers of narrowing in zero-duration spreads in recent months. To capture the impact of this factor, we include it in our fair value model and make a few other changes. Our updated fair value model now includes four factors to explain zero-duration spreads - (i) the level of term funding premium itself (measured in bp per year), (ii) the size of the Fed's balance sheet (measured in \$tn), (iii) the O/N GC minus SOFR differential (we use a 21 day moving average for smoothing purposes) and (iv) the trade-weighted dollar index. Details of this model for zero-duration spreads are shown in Figure 10, and Figure 11 demonstrates the in-sample effectiveness visually. Importantly, using this revised fair value model, we find that zeroduration spreads are only modestly narrow relative to fair value, to the tune of \~2 - 2.5bp (as also seen in Figure 10). In addition, with O/N RRP balances now near \$285bn and with QT likely to continue for a few more months, GC rates risk being pressured higher relative to SOFR, which (given a partial beta of \~ minus 0.7) would, in turn, pressure zero-duration spreads narrower. Therefore, we remain neutral on swap spreads across the curve on an outright basis.
