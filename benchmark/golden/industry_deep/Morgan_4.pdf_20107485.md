# The Art and Science of Picking Themes

The AI & Diabesity themes rumble on. This has led to a recurring question: Is it better to hold on to a winning theme and risk selling too late...or find emerging themes and risk pivoting too early? Emphatically, the former.

In this short report, we attempt to provide some clarity and quantitative backing for the more strategic, asset allocation questions we have been receiving in recent weeks:

1. Buy too early vs Sell too late… History says hold on. Returns in year 1 for leading themes have averaged 66%. We appreciate the benefit of hindsight here. So, for those worried about buying too late (or selling too late), these themes typically follow-through to deliver 17% in calendar year 2. By contrast the S&P500 has returned 11% annually over the last decade. On the flip side, being "too early" to new themes is fraught with its own challenges.

2. Tech vs Sustainability… Sustainability has seen greater breadth of returns than technology related themes. This appears to be driven by greater discount rate sensitivity and relative margin volatility vs tech themes.

3. Volatility vs Outperformance… In the year in which a theme performs best, drawdowns are relatively modest; fewer than a quarter of months were down. Sharpe ratios, however, tend to worsen materially in year 2 of themes.

4. Momentum vs Other Factors... In the 1990s the best performing themes slightly underperformed the best returning GICS sector, now they tend to outperform by 15%. Most of this is driven by the momentum factor.

5. Headline Theme vs Sub-Theme… Do not rush to broaden out from the leaders. However, in year two - as shown now with AI - there is merit in moving down the sub-theme stack as first derivative plays can start to exhibit worse sharpe ratios and there is more risk-adjusted upside in subthemes (i.e. Edge AI, Adopters, Power, Data Centres).

As it relates to the current debates on AI and Diabesity - 2023 performance from both themes was "average" compared to leading themes through history. And while 2024 is off to a strong start - both themes are in the upper quartile of year-two return distribution - it is not clear that either is overextended relative to themes of prior decades. Nor are valuations rich relative to their own history or comparative thematic valuation metrics through time. Still, this work suggests that 18-24 months into a theme, broadening out to sub-themes is a winning strategy. We explain how to do this for AI here.

## 5 Rules of Thumb For Thematic Investing

## Theme Collection and Analysis

What constitutes a “theme”?: This is an enduring, and arguably unanswerable, question. In the absence of an agreed upon structure for themes, this is a field of investing fraught with subjectivity. We generally all agree on what country or regional investing is. Thanks to the GICS classifications, we can also generally agree on the definition of sector investing. Themes by contrast can be macro or micro; cross-asset; long- or short-duration; tactical or secular. While MSCI, S&P Kensho and others have frameworks for thematic investing, none have achieved broad consensus adoption.

How then can we benchmark or track themes in an adequate way?: Our readers may disagree with our choices of the best performing themes each year (from Exhibit 2 for example). Our points of justification are nonetheless as follows:

• We remove all single country performances - otherwise Japan, the US and various emerging nations would have appeared at least twice each. We include BRICs as a theme given the interplay with security and multipolar themes.

• To focus in on themes backed by some probable (or perception of) earnings substance, we remove non-equity and bubble-like instruments, such as crypto assets.

• From here we use two tools, the first is Solactive indices performances. These are objective and the longest-dated means of comparing a third-party compiled view of theme performance.

• Prior to 2007 - when Solactive did not exist - the search was more manual, selecting for the best performing stocks and sectors globally to determine what the most prominent theme driving those performances was. After this we found listed benchmarks or indices on a case by case basis to back-fill annually.

• For the final years - 2023/24 - there are not sufficiently established pureplay Diabesity/GLP-1 indices to use. In this case we used our team's Morgan Stanley exposure guide to this theme on a market cap weighted basis.

## (1) Buy too early vs Sell too late?…History says hold on

Particularly in light of strong performance in a number of themes in recent years – most notably AI and GLP-1 – we are asked a question with rising frequency and urgency:

• Is it better to hold on to a winning theme and risk selling too late or attempt to find and pivot into a new emerging theme and risk being too early?

While we have a number of quantitative metrics we track to spot emerging themes – and will explain again below – the overwhelming body of evidence over the past 30 years would suggest investors are better off holding on to themes that are working over pivoting into new ones.

It is near impossible to quantitatively prove the latter point. However, the clear evidence of outperformance relative to the market by adhering to the former heuristic – i.e. sticking to the themes that have momentum – should be enough for most investors not to have to worry about the alpha generation of being on emerging themes from the ground floor.

Back to 1997, the best performing themes in the market each year delivered average and median performances of 66% and 64% respectively. If investors had simply held on to these themes beyond the end of a the calendar year in which they led the market, then the subsequent calendar year returns average and median were 17% and 15% respectively.

In other words, even if investors are 6 months late into a developing investment theme and hold for the year after it has delivered its best performance, history suggests this would still generate double digit returns; and typically comfortably ahead of comparative returns of the S&P500.

There are, of course, valuation versus more fundamentally driven themes - and which will determine the urgency with which investors may want to exit certain trades. Given the data sources of index level analysis, it is not possible to go into the degree to which each leading theme back to 1997 was driven by upgrades versus reratings.

The received wisdom, however, is that as the momentum factor has grown in importance for thematic investing (see further detail below), by extension most of the returns must therefore have come from re-rating rather than thematic-driven earnings upgrades. This mindset is particularly prevalent now in the AI theme where comparisons are frequently made to the fiber/tech/venture/internet themes of 1997-2001. This, in our view, is oversimplistic and in fact more often than not, isn't correct.

When looking at the leading enablers per theme (or largest equity component of the thematic index for the year in question), the key themes in the market today are proving to be substantially more earnings driven than the leading themes from previous years and decades. This conclusion further reinforces our view that the optimal strategy is not to pivot to emerging themes too soon but rather to hold on to themes which are performing (i.e. AI and Diabesity) and which we do not believe are yet over-extended on valuation grounds.

## (2) Tech vs Sustainability… Sustainability has seen more extremes

Over the period for which we have data, sustainability-focussed themes have accounted for half of the best performing years' thematic returns. While it may come as a surprise to many readers, Technology-related theme performances have in fact been relatively narrower in their range of returns outcomes.

There is no simple explanation to (1) why sustainability themes have been the greatest one- and two-year outliers to the upside, nor (2) why sustainability themes have a greater chance of mean-reverting to the downside in year two.

Exhibit 9: Performance of Top Themes over two year buy-and-hold re-based to the start of first year of outperformance

However, for the themes that did outperform or underperform most dramatically, the cause can largely be attributed to two factors:

• Margins - Many of the sustainability themes which have seen extreme performance to the upside of the downside have tended to be driven less by growth acceleration/deceleration and more by margin compression or expansion. This convexity in profitability means that every 10bps of margin expansion in the leading theme of 2013 - Wind - is substantially more important for returns than 10bps of margin expansion for the semiconductor players, whose margins start from a substantially higher base.

• Rates volatility - falling interest rates in 2020 and then levelling out (or beginning to discount hikes) in 2022 saw the long-duration sustainability themes such as solar and renewables more generally gyrate from being the best performing themes to being the worst performing themes in quick succession. This also has an outsized tendency to occur in January each year.

## January reversals

This analysis is an additional caveat to the above work. It shows how abruptly themes can turn on and off, and particularly around calendar year-ends. We have analysed 70 bubbles over the past 100 years - micro, macro and thematic. We monitored them 3 years prior to peak and 3 years post peak to find they behave in remarkably predictable ways. However, one aspect on which we did not elaborate in that piece was the importance of January as the most frequent month of reversal. These January performance inversions are particularly relevant for themes that delivered >20% upside or downside in the year prior. From China Tech and Biotech in 2019, EVs in 2021, SaaS in 2003 and 2022, history is full of examples of January reversals in both directions.

## (3) Volatility vs Outperformance...Sharpe ratios worsen in Year 2

It is not clear whether there is a sharpe ratio difference between technology-related and sustainability-related themes over the past 20 years. Much like the analysis above where sustainability themes book-ended the performance both to the upside and downside, the same applies for the risk-adjusted returns of sustainability themes relative to technology themes. As expected though – in aggregate – sharpe ratios in subsequent years to peak performance tend to see a major deterioration in sharpe ratios. Even if returns on average were still 17% in year-two, this came with substantially more volatility.

## (4) Momentum vs Other Factors...Momentum, increasingly so

It comes as no surprise that if one were to cherry-pick the best performing themes in each year with perfect hindsight, they would outperform any market or sector benchmark. This, we therefore appreciate, is a farcical comparison. However, what we do note is that through the years - shown in decade buckets below - the best performing themes have on average (and median) been widening their returns outperformance versus the S&P500 or the leading GICS sector return.

In the 1990s, the best performing themes outperformed the market by 37%, this has extended to a 61% outperformance in the 2020s. In the 1990s and 2000s, the best performing themes were in-line or slightly below the best returning GICS sector in that year. By the 2010s and 2020s, the gap to the best performing GICS sector had widened to 24% and 15% respectively.

## (5) Headline Themes vs Sub-Themes...Year 2 for Sub-Themes

The final question we face is when is the optimal time to try to broaden out into other less-crowded ways of playing themes. This is an extension of the point around diminishing year-2 Sharpe ratios discussed above. In AI, that could mean a broadening out from first layer winners like NVIDIA, Microsoft and SKHynix into power enablers, grid infrastructure, data centres, EdgeAI, or efficiency winners. In Diabesity this could be a broadening out from Novo Nordisk and Lilly into fill-and-finish consolidation targets or even the inverse plays in the perceived disrupted companies - i.e. sleep apnoea, insulin pumps and high calorie foods.

Here, we believe the idiosyncracies of each theme and each market backdrop mean there are few satisfying rules-of-thumb to convey. However, we are able to provide a means of benchmarking where our analysts believe there is the most or least prospective upside to their target prices on a 12-month view - see below (or full report here) for AI benchmarking on the broadening out of the theme.

The second approach to timing the pivot into sub-themes, or alternatively (and conscious of the contradiction to the first point in this report) trying to get into emerging themes before the rest of the market becomes aware of them, we have built a dashboard. This quantitative approach to finding themes which are gathering momentum but are not yet on the radar of most generalist investors is not simple to back-test in terms of success in picking the right themes – and even less so the right stocks to play the theme – and we see two main reasons. The reason being, themes tend to hit the generalist investor zeitgeist when:

1. There has been a technological catalyst/launch; OpenAI's release of ChatGPT in November 2022 for example. Machine learning had been evolving since World War 2 and the Transformer paper was published in 2017. It took a further 5 years and a major and unforeseeable product release for investors and corporates to see its potential. Even still, our screen had been showing gathering momentum throughout the run up to ChatGPT in AI, but was nonetheless unable to foresee this catalyst.

2. There has been a regulatory catalyst; the IRA regulation is the best example of this. A number of sustainability themes which were impacted by the IRA – i.e. EVs, Solar, Hydrogen, Carbon Capture to name a few - were showing momentum for a prolonged period running up to the IRA's publication. Yet, it was only after the details on subsidies were announced that the market was able to start calculating earnings upside and bidding up multiples in anticipation of these long-duration earnings coming through.
