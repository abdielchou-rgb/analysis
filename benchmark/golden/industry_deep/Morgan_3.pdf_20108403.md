# The Viewpoint: Feedback on Japan from Japan

We published a Bluepaper on Japan last Monday and then spent the next few days meeting with investors at our inaugural Japan summit. FX, inflation and the BOJ were key topics of discussions as investors wanted to understand potential risks and where the balance of risks would tilt.

## Key Takeaways

Investors acknowledge that there has been progress made in Japan’s reflation journey, but there were some lingering concerns about its sustainability.

The recurring theme was investors wanted to understand more about the risks to the base case, specifically what could derail the positive outlook.

■ Our base case is that Japan has entered a new equilibrium of moderate, sustained inflation, higher nominal GDP growth and stronger ROE.

■ We see wage growth rising to 3%Y in the coming months. Wage gains are more broad-based including in the SME sector than last year.

■ A virtuous cycle of prices and wages is unfolding, helping bring strong nominal GDP growth.

In this report, we address feedback we have received from investors. Specifically, we will look at these five commonly asked questions.

1) What gives us the conviction that Japan’s inflation will be sustained?

2) Inflation appears to be eroding purchasing power and holding back private consumption. Will this dynamic change and if so, when?

3) A weaker FX has helped to drive inflation but it seems that it is now reaching a level which is forcing the BOJ to respond. What are the implications?

4) With the BOJ already behind the curve, won’t policy rates have to quickly rise above 2% over the next 12-18 months?

5) What are the other risks we should monitor?

## Feedback from Japan

Our key call – A new equilibrium is taking hold in Japan: In our Bluepaper, A Revitalised Japan, we highlighted that Japan is moving towards a new equilibrium of sustained, moderate inflation and stronger nominal GDP growth. Macro policies should stay accommodative, ensuring that the r-g gap stays favourable. A positive wage-price cycle is emerging, thus sustaining moderate inflation. A conducive macro backdrop, coupled with strong reform momentum, should allow ROE to keep rising.

What is the feedback from investors?: Sifting through the numerous inquiries that have come through our inboxes and thinking through the live conversations with investors at our Japan summit, we’d highlight a few themes. Investors were mostly constructive as they acknowledged that Japan has indeed taken a step out of the long shadow of deflation. But there were some lingering concerns as to whether inflation would be sustained over the medium term. Investors were also keen to understand the interplay of FX, inflation and BOJ policy – as they assess whether this would present upside risks to inflation and therefore a faster pace of policy rate hikes.

We address the five most frequently asked questions in detail below.

## 1) What gives us the conviction that Japan’s inflation will be sustained?

Lingering concern around the sustainability of Japan’s reflation: Most investors we spoke with acknowledged that Japan had made progress in leaving the deflationary environment behind. But there were some who had lingering concerns about whether moderation inflation could be sustained. A common refrain is that we have had similar reflationary cycles in the past, but sustained inflation did not take hold, so why would this time be different? Once imported inflation cools, headline inflation will also likely move back below target.

Global reflation and JPY depreciation have helped…: To be sure, the post-pandemic global reflationary environment and JPY depreciation have provided one round of reflation. For instance, import prices did rise to 49%Y at the peak in Jul-22, which in turn lifted core goods inflation to a peak of 5.4% with a lag ( Exhibit 1 ). Headline inflation picked up to a peak of 4.4%Y in Jan-23 as well.

…but strengthening wage-price pass-through will sustain moderate inflation: There are a number of factors which will keep headline inflation at moderate levels over the next 12-18 months. Our chief Japan economist Takeshi Yamaguchi expects headline CPI inflation to be 3%Y as of end-2024 and 1.5%Y as of end-2025.

First, correct domestic policy choices have given us the current backdrop. The BOJ has been patient and kept its monetary policy accommodative, allowing real rates to decline. In turn, the real rates to real GDP growth gap (r-g) has widened to -490bps on a 4Q trailing basis in 1Q24, well above the 200bps threshold that we had identified in our previous work (for more details, please see Bluepaper: A Revitalised Japan). We expect the r-g gap to stay favourable, averaging -230bps over 2024-25, which will continue to provide an accommodative policy environment to sustain the positive narrative on Japan.

Second, the key differences from past cycles are that (a) wage growth has been strong for the past 9 months and (b) the pass-through from wages to prices is increasing. Wage growth has risen to a 32-year high, and scheduled earnings on a common sample basis – the key wage growth metric we track – has been above 2% for seven of the past nine months. Our Japan economics team has estimated that the pass-through from a 1%pt increase in wages to prices had turned positive from 4Q22 and remained at 0.5pp in 4Q23. For details, please see Japan Economics: Has Japan's Inflation/Wage Growth Finally Become Sustainable? Revival Of Wage-Price Pass-Through). Increasingly, we have been moving more and more towards domestic sources of inflation.

Wage growth will stay strong…: As it is, there have been two consecutive years of strong wage growth negotiated between the unions and corporates. The 2023 Shunto wage agreement yielded a final base-up wage growth tally of 2.1% (excluding seniorityrelated pay increases) and the current tabulation of the 2024 Shunto wage agreement is at 3.6%, the highest since 1991. As the economy-wide wage measure of scheduled earnings growth on a common sample basis is highly correlated with the base-up portion of the Shunto wage growth, our chief Japan economist expects the strong Shunto wage results will mean wage growth will rise to around 3% as negotiations are completed in 3Q24, and he believes BOJ might be holding a similar outlook. Given the higher labour cost share of services, the team expects services inflation will gradually pick up again because companies will revise their service prices following this year’s spring wage hikes. Indeed, services inflation has now averaged 2% over the past six months, vs. an average of just - 0.1% in the preceding two years.

…supported by a structural labour shortage: Structurally, our Japan economics team expects that the labour shortages (less slack) will be a key factor in supporting wage growth. Post 2013, policy makers pushed for a higher female participation rate and the policy has paid off to such a great extent that Japan’s prime age female participation rate of 76% has surpassed that of the US (56%) implying limited scope to improve labour supply ( Exhibit 9 ). Moreover, the baby boomer generation is beginning to exit the workforce, adding to labour market tightness ( Exhibit 8 ).

Wage pass-through will remain significant: As we have highlighted before, there are a number of important changes to underlying behaviour in the economy which suggest the wage pass-through will remain strong.

First, the corporate sector has shed its past practice of no wage hikes and no price hikes.

Second, feedback from firms surveyed by the BOJ suggests that they are raising prices even though input costs (ex-wages) are moderating – a sign that they are increasing prices in response to higher wages.

Third, a recent media report suggests that larger firms will now be more tolerant of their suppliers as they hike prices, which suggests that SMEs will now have more flexibility to raise wages and prices as well.

Fourth, our Japan economics team also notes that the base-up portion of Spring wage results for businesses with under 300 employees this year is holding strong at 3.2% even in the fifth tabulation round results ( Exhibit 6 ), whereas wage growth softened in the later rounds last year as more firms were included in the results. This suggests that even SMEs are taking a more positive stance on wage hikes, meaning that wage hikes could be more broad-based than last year (see Japan Economics: Monthly Labour Survey (March 2024 Prelim): Signs of Wage Hikes Spreading to SMEs). To be sure, another round of Shunto wage agreement in 2025 that is strong and broad-based would remove all doubts that this new dynamic is sustainable and on a firm footing.

## 2) Inflation appears to be eroding purchasing power and holding back private consumption. Will this dynamic change and if so, when?

Investors see negative real wage growth crimping consumption: Investors have raised the concern that despite strong headline wage growth, real wage growth is still negative. As consumers continue to bear the brunt of higher inflation, this is adversely affecting their purchasing power, which in turn weighs on domestic demand and consequently will be a drag on inflation.

However, we expect real wage growth to turn positive: Currently, real wage growth deflated by headline CPI is at -0.3%Y and 0% when deflated by core CPI inflation. We expect that headline inflation will decline to around 2% by mid-2025, while wage growth based on wage negotiations is expected to rise to 3% by 3Q24. In 12 months' time, we expect CPI inflation will be down to 2% and real wage growth will improve to 1%.

Support to consumption from one-off income tax cut: While it will still be some time before real wage growth will turn positive, in the interim we believe consumption will be supported by a one-off income tax cut (0.6% of GDP) from June 2024 (Japan Economics: Govt Decides on "Comprehensive Economic Package for a Complete Exit from Deflation" (2 November 2023)). With this backdrop, we expect real consumption growth to improve to 0.6%Y in 4Q24 (vs. -1.9%Y in 1Q24) and further to 1% in 2025, up from 0.6% in 2023.

## 3) A weaker FX has helped to drive inflation but it seems that it is now reaching a level which is forcing the BOJ to respond. What are the implications?

Our base case is that the yen appreciates modestly: Our US economics team forecasts core PCE inflation will moderate to an average of 0.2% month-on-month which will allow the Fed to start cutting policy rates from September 2024. With this backdrop, our Japan macro strategist Koichi Sugisaki sees USDJPY ending the year at 146.

The BOJ has been clear that it responds to the ‘underlying’ inflation outlook: We believe the BOJ will broadly stick to its framework of assessing upside risks to inflation on account of FX depreciation. This means that the BOJ may not use monetary policy directly to address yen weakness but may be considering the effects of yen weakness on prices to wages and wages to prices.

However, if US inflation surprises the market to the upside and market pricing for the Fed rate path moves higher, the yen could face more depreciation pressure. In that event, while we do see some risk of an additional rate hike of 25bps in Jul-25 if ‘underlying’ inflation and wage growth turn out to be stronger than our forecast, we don’t see aggressive rate hikes in our base case. In any case, this measured pace of rate hikes should be characterized as monetary policy turning less accommodative as our Japan economics team estimates that Japan's nominal neutral rate is around 1% or marginally higher.

Trailing depreciation of the yen and impact on import prices has narrowed and is imparting less impetus to inflation at the margin: Investors have been focused on the USDJPY level and its impact on Japan’s inflation as the JPY reached new lows in recent months. However, we would note that it is important to consider the USDJPY impact through import prices on a %Y basis given its correlation with core goods inflation. On this, the trailing import price trend on a %Y basis does indicate that core goods CPI could rise over coming months; however, the magnitude of increase will be less than in 2023 given the %Y increase in import prices is tracking at around 6%Y, much lower than the near 50% peak seen in 2023. As reference, analysis from BOJ estimates that a 1% depreciation in would lift CPI inflation by 0.045% (as of 4Q21) four quarters later, up from an estimate of just under 0.03% in the mid-2000s amid higher import penetration in recent years (see BoJ Watch & Macro Strategy: Will the BoJ Move On Yen Weakness? (2 April 2024)).

## 4) With the BOJ already behind the curve, won’t policy rates have to quickly rise above 2% over the next 12-18 months?

Some investors expect a more aggressive pace of tightening than we do. Some investors are of the view that BOJ is already behind the curve. What this means, in their view, is that the BOJ will be forced to aggressively tighten monetary policy in the next 12- 18 months, lifting policy rates to 2% or above. The logic behind this argument usually stems from model estimates of a Taylor Rule and indeed the interest on this topic has picked up recently given that Professor Taylor himself will be presenting to the BOJ board this week.

We don’t think so because we don’t see a sustained overshoot of the inflation target: At the outset, we’d note that Japan is facing more limited demand-side pressures as than other DMs. We think core inflation is already down to 2%, and we expect it to remain around 2% going forward. Moreover, until recently BOJ officials have been highlighting that underlying inflation has not reached 2%. In March, Governor Ueda presented a view that Japan's inflation expectations are somewhere in the 1% to 1.5% range and hence still have some distance from 2%.

Still need to keep favourable r-g dynamics: We can also address the issue of the pace of rate hikes from another perspective, that of debt-deleveraging. As we have highlighted before, we believe policy makers should maintain a favourable r-g gap until deleveraging headwinds have abated. Our previous work suggests that maintaining a gap of at least 200bps has been vital to the successful reflation and deleveraging of economies.

## 5) What are the other risks we should monitor?

Domestic risks revolve around wage pass-through to prices: Domestically, the key risk we will watch for is if inflation surprises to the downside. In our view, such a scenario could materialize if the wage to price pass-through turns out to be weaker than our estimates. We would note that recent analysis from BOJ currently indicates a smaller wage to price pass-through than our estimates, though it still does point to the fact that the pass-through has now increased as compared to the early 1990s.

US recession is the key external risk: Externally, the key risk to our constructive outlook on Japan likely stems from a US recession. In the Global Economics Mid-year Outlook, our global economics team laid out the downside scenario for global growth, whereby a stagflationary shock in the US stems from a sooner end to the immigration surge and thus more persistent services inflation leads the Fed to stay higher for longer. This would ignite recession fears in the US, resulting in weaker external demand for Japan, a stronger yen and potential downward pressure on inflation (for details, please see Global Economics Mid-Year Outlook: Are We There Yet? (19 May 2024)).
