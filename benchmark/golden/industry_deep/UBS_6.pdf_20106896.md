# India Economic Perspectives

# How is India faring on external stability metrics? What does it mean for INR?

## Indian economy continues to show resilience to external risks

The Indian economy has been in a sweet spot lately, with manageable macro risks. We forecast another year of strong growth for India, at 7% in FY25. Historically, periods of high growth in India have been associated with widening current account deficit (CAD). The key question is: how is this cycle different? Here are our key findings: 1) We constructed a simple interactive economic model to compute India's sustainable CAD. Based on our analysis, the threshold CAD is in a range of 2.2-2.5% of GDP. 2) In recent years, CAD has not breached this sustainable range due to additional buffers created in the form of a higher services trade surplus and buoyant remittance flow. 3) India remains vulnerable to high global oil prices (importing 87% of its oil demand). 4) Measures to boost services and manufacturing exports are necessary to support India's higher potential GDP growth.

## We estimate India's CAD will remain comfortable in FY25

We estimate India's CAD narrowed to less than 1% of GDP in FY24 (vs. 1.2% forecasted earlier). We believe this improvement has been largely led by: 1) A contained goods trade deficit (on lower commodity imports, especially oil and coal). 2) Improved net services receipts to 4.7% of GDP in FY24 (UBSe) vs. 3% of GDP during the pre-pandemic period. 3) An increase in remittance inflow toward 3.5% of GDP in FY23-24 (UBSe) after it was largely steady, at 2.5-3% of GDP, in FY16-20. 4) Lastly, macroeconomic stability. Heading into FY25, we estimate CAD will modestly increase to 1.3% of GDP.

## What level of global crude oil price can India sustain?

India remains vulnerable to higher global crude oil prices. Looking at India's oil price sensitivity, we calculate a US\$10/bbl average increase in global crude prices would widen India's CAD by cUS\$14bn (0.4% of GDP). Cheaper oil imports from Russia (a c35% share of India's crude oil imports YTD in FY24), buoyant services exports and resilient remittance inflow have all helped build a buffer against global oil price volatility. We estimate India can sustain global crude oil prices up to US\$90/bbl, all else remaining unchanged, but the lower oil goes, the better it would be for India (Figure 18).

## Financing to get support from bond index inclusion; FDI inflow continues to lag

India holds the fourth largest FX reserves in the world (US\$617bn, as of mid-Feb 2024), which seems comfortable as a reserve adequacy metric. While expensive Indian equity market valuation could create Fll flow uncertainty, we believe India's growth resilience, along with the forthcoming bond index inclusion (scheduled as of June 2024 onward and could lead to US\$30bn of flow/60% of the FY25 current account balance), will keep capital account in a surplus. However, FDI inflows to India have slowed significantly versus the recent peak (largely due to equity capital repatriation), raising concerns if the country is really gaining prominence in the China+1 supply chain shift.

## INR's near- and medium-term outlook has improved

Looking at INR on a real effective exchange rate (REER) basis and adjusting for the productivity differential with trading partners, our model indicates INR is trading past its equilibrium value. Although balance-of-payment (BOP) has been resilient lately, UBS's EM Strategist does not expect runaway gains amidst RBl's unstated FX policy of REER management. Nonetheless, UBS sees merit in INR carry trades vs. CNH (link) and recommends owning 1-year OTM INR call options vs. USD (link). Reflecting the CA balance forecast revisions, we shift our USDINR end-FY25 forecast from 84 to 82.

# We estimate India's CAD to remain comfortable in FY25

We estimate India's CAD narrowed to less than 1% of GDP in FY24 (vs. 1.2% forecasted earlier). We believe this improvement has been largely led by: 1) A contained goods trade deficit (on lower commodity imports, especially oil and coal). 2) Improved net services receipts to 4.7% of GDP in FY24 (UBSe) vs. 3% of GDP during the pre-pandemic period. 3) An increase in remittance inflow toward 3.5% of GDP in FY23-24 (UBSe) after it was largely steady, at 2.5-3% of GDP, in FY16-20. 4) Lastly, macroeconomic stability. Heading into FY25, we estimate CAD will modestly increase to 1.3% of GDP. This would be well within the estimated sustainable threshold for India's CAD (between 2.2-2.5% of GDP). Our estimates assume India's imported crude oil cost averages US\$80/bbl in FY24/FY25 compared with US\$93/bbl in FY23.

## 1) Goods trade deficit has narrowed

India's goods trade deficit narrowed significantly to 6.9% of GDP in FY24 (annualized) vs. 7.8% in FY23, as imports contracted more than exports. Looking at the breakdown, contraction in goods imports was largely led by easing in global commodity prices, especially crude oil and coal. Among other commodities, transport equipment, pearls and precious stones, chemicals, and vegetable oil contracted, while gold and electronic goods imports increased in FY24. The slowdown in exports was largely led by lower petroleum products, gems and jewellery, and chemicals exports, while engineering goods and electronic goods were largely stable. (See Appendices for commodity details.)

## 2) Net services exports continue to surge

India's services trade surplus improved further to 4.7% of GDP in FY24 (annualised) vs. 4.3% of GDP in FY23 (largely led by relative slowing of services imports vs. services exports). Notably, India's services exports have increased 1.7x to US\$342bn in FY24, registering a CAGR of 12.5% vs. the pre-pandemic period (when it stood at cUS\$200bn during FY18-20). Indeed, India's share of the global services trade rose from 3.4% in 2019 to 4.4% in 2022. Looking at the breakdown, the bulk of the pick-up in services exports lately has been driven by software services and business consulting services. The latter could be partly due to India emerging as a dominant player in outsourcing and offshoring services.

## 3) Remittances bringing stability to current account balance

Notably, India has the second-largest overseas population after China and is the biggest recipient of remittances (in value terms) in the world, accounting for a c15% share of global inward remittances. We estimate remittance inflow to India has remained robust, at US\$120bn in FY24 (3.4% of GDP). Notably, remittance inflow to India averaged 3.5- 4.0% of GDP during FY09-13, when the Brent price averaged around US\$95/bbl. Besides higher oil prices, this period coincided with a change in motivation of remittances from consumption to investment to take advantage of falling asset prices, rising interest rate differentials (along with several other measures by RBI to attract NRI deposits) and significant INR depreciation.

However, the trend was not sustained and remittance inflow moved lower, toward 2.5- 3% of GDP during FY16-20, when the Brent price averaged around US\$55/bbl. Between FY21-24, remittance inflow has again moved higher, toward 3.4% of GDP, due to: 1) higher global crude oil prices; and 2) a tight labour market and wage hikes in developing markets.

Notably, over the years, the migration pattern in India has shifted from unskilled/semiskilled in Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries to a dominant share of high-skilled jobs in high-income countries such as the US, the UK and East Asia, where the postpandemic recovery led to a tight labour market and wage hikes boosted remittances. High energy prices favoured the employment and incomes of less-skilled Indian migrants in GCC countries. As per the Fifth Round of the Survey on Remittances for 2020-21 by the RBI (link), between FY17 and FY21, the share of remittances from the US, UK and Singapore increased to over 36% from 26%, while the share from the five GCC countries (Saudi Arabia, United Arab Emirates, Kuwait, Oman and Qatar) dropped to 28% from 54%.

## What level of oil price can India sustain?

The UBS global oil team expects oil prices to rebound from the current level, as it continues to expect the oil market to return to a large deficit in 2Q24 as oil demand bounces back seasonally and non-OPEC supply growth slows down. It expects the Brent crude oil price to average US\$82/bbl in 2024. It views risk as skewed to the upside vs. the current level. The two key downside risks to its forecast are further US supply upside and weaker oil demand.

Since India is a net oil importer (it imports 85% of its oil requirement), crude oil price movement has an important bearing on macro stability risks. So far, the direct adverse impact of higher oil prices on India's inflation, fiscal deficit and growth has been largely contained by policy intervention (retail prices of gasoline and diesel have been unchanged since May 2022 and India continues to buy Russian oil at a reasonable discount). However, there are risks for the external sector. Looking at India's oil price sensitivity, we calculate a US\$10/bbl average increase in global crude prices would widen India's CAD by cUS\$14bn (0.4% of GDP).

## Appendix 1: Methodology – what is the sustainable CAD?

We seek to determine the sustainable level of CAD for India using a procedure adopted by an RBI study: "Sustainable Level of India's Current Account Deficit", by Rajan Goyal (2012). The RBI study estimated India's sustainable CAD to be around 2.4-2.8% of GDP by applying a model akin to "Domar's Model of Debt Sustainability". We estimate the sustainable level of CAD for India by applying some modifications to the RBl study. We estimate an intertemporal model of the current account to derive at an estimate of the sustainable level of CAD that ensures net external liabilities do not grow without bound, emphasizing the role of real GDP growth, inflation, capital flow and the cost of external borrowing, as described below:

$$
c a d n = \frac { \left[ \left( r \times \displaystyle \frac { d } { l } \right) - \left( y + \pi ( 1 + y ) \right) \right] \times l } { ( 1 + y ) ( 1 + \pi ) }
$$

$$
c a d = c a d n + r \times l
$$

..where y is the real growth rate of the economy and π is the inflation rate, r is the nominal rate of interest, l is net external liabilities and d is external debt. cadn is CAD net of interest payment.

From the equation above, the following relationships between net external liability compared with the rate of interest and growth rate can be observed:

1) The larger the growth rate, keeping everything else the same, the larger the size of CAD that is sustainable.

2) Similarly, the lower the interest rate, keeping everything else the same, the larger the size of CAD that is sustainable.

Based on our analysis, the threshold CAD level for India is around 2.2-2.5% of GDP in a steady state.

## Appendix 2: Methodology – what is the right value of the Indian rupee?

We estimated the fair value of the Indian rupee as of end-March 2023 by applying some modifications to the ICRIER research paper: "Is the Rupee Over-valued?", dated June 2015. The six-currency trade-weighted REER stood at 102 in Jan 2024. It should be noted that REER should also be reflective of productivity improvement compared with trading partners. Thus, we need to adjust for the productivity differential to arrive at a correct measure of trade competitiveness.

The Harrod-Balassa-Samuelson (HBS) theorem draws on work done by economists Roy Harrod (1933), Bela Balassa (1964) and Paul Samuelson (1964). According to this model, a rise in productivity of tradables increases the price of non-tradables, which then inflates REER. This inflation is "spurious", in the sense that there is no impact on competitiveness. Thus, it needs to be adjusted for productivity differential. We worked out the equilibrium value for REER during 2004-23, assuming the currency was fairly valued in 2004:

$$
