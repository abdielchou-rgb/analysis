# Asia's alternative scenario

In our latest Asia Macro Insight report, we identified risks to Asia's resilience. In particular, the upcoming US elections could be the most consequential political event for the region in decades, given their potential impact on global trade. An alternative scenario which sees aggressive tariff hikes by the US and China on each other's products, may see not only their own growth significantly weaker, but others' as well in Asia - as illustrated in Figure 13 - with Vietnam likely to be the most affected and India the least. Moreover, the negative effects on Asia could last for years, with the most impact felt during the second year. Such an external shock would see Asia's FX volatility sharply higher, delaying rate cuts further. Unfortunately, its maturing debt profile may render Asia more vulnerable than others.

## Risks to Asia's resilience

Asia kicked off 2024 on a relatively positive note, faring better than other EMs, with two-thirds of the economies reporting Q1 GDP growth that was stronger than consensus forecasts, as exports rebounded.

Asia has weathered the impact of rising geopolitical tensions and trade restrictions relatively well. That said, the importance of Asia – and China – in global supply chains and trade has deteriorated. Global trade as a share of GDP has largely moved sideways, as the global supply chain has adjusted. Yet, while China's share in US (direct) imports has fallen, its leading role as a world exporter has not changed.

While the number of unfavorable trade restrictions has surged in recent years, tariff rate hikes have only made up about 9% of the total. Subsidies (excluding export subsidies) have dominated, accounting for about 40% of the total, followed by contingent trade-protection measures (\~16%) and export controls and subsidies (\~14%). Although the actual impact of the US' effective tariff rates on China will form less than a percentage point increase, tariff rate hikes on selected goods (such as EVs, raised four times to 100%) were significant. According to our European colleagues, the EU Commission is also likely to hike the tariff rate on Chinese EVs (albeit by just 10-20%) from the prevailing 10%. While the incremental hike may be less, it could be more consequential for China, as it holds almost one-fifth of the EU market share. In short, we believe we may be approaching an inflection point in global trade, especially as we head to the polls in the US.

In our view, the upcoming US elections may prove to be the most consequential political event for Asia in decades, as – depending on the outcome – they could mark the beginning of genuine global fragmentation. While the US has reduced its direct imports from China, China's expansion of its global footprint has raised concerns and scrutiny over the role of third economies (especially those that are geographically close to the US) in enabling China. Moreover, the US and the EU have raised concerns over the potential economic fallout from China's "industrial

Although the export recovery is contributing to Asia's growth, this may not last through 2025. In the event of significant tariff rate hikes after the US elections, Asia's exports may contract (instead of expanding in the low single digits, as per our baseline view). Ironically, in the near term, rising anticipation of tariff rate hikes and/ or other trade restrictions next year may prompt firms to preemptively restock, providing an additional boost to exports this year, but with a significant negative payback next year. Despite stronger exports, such an alternative scenario would limit investment recovery as firms adopt a wait-and-see stance and strengthen their financial buffers against potential negative shocks. In short, recent positive economic developments – disinflation and the demand recovery amid continued payroll gains, as well as the export recovery – could very well reverse in such an alternative scenario.

Moreover, the negative effects on Asia would last for years, with the most impact felt during the second year.

We believe that a significant external shock could see Asia's FX volatility surge once more, and it could limit the capacity of the region's central banks to ease and provide a buffer for the economy against such external headwinds. Although capital controls might be employed, they stand as either the least favored (or the last) policy option. Advanced Asia has seen stickier inflation than its ASEAN counterparts, but the latter's policymakers remain more sensitive to US rates, given their more challenging external balances and weaker FX fundamentals.

Such an external shock may result in a surge in FX volatility that further delays Asia's monetary easing. The monetary tightening of the past few years has left refinancing costs sharply higher, calling for vigilance over such a latent credit risk – especially if accompanied by a negative growth shock.

Unfortunately for Asia, such a risk may be arriving at the most inopportune moment, as the region faces a very high wall of maturing debt (this year and next). As we noted in an earlier report in 2022, Asia's largest risk is its high debt. The region's situation regarding its external and sovereign debt risks has fared better than that of its EM counterparts. However, its relatively high domestic debt has left the region far more vulnerable to high interest rates. Although the IMF has warned against "premature" policy easing as a potential catalyst for a further rise in debt, in our view, rate cuts may not arrive soon enough for heavily indebted economies with very high walls of maturing debt, albeit dominated by local currency debt. (See Appendix A for a more detailed profile of maturing debt for individual economies.)

On a positive note, credit spreads have narrowed in the US and elsewhere. And in economies such as South Korea, with household debt moving sideways, corporate loans have taken over as the main source of bank asset growth, further propelled by refinancing needs. Moreover, despite no rate cuts by the BoK, lending rates and credit spreads have fallen in recent months, amid easing liquidity conditions.

An alternative scenario that sees Asia under significant external pressure would likely require the region's governments to do much of the heavy lifting, as monetary easing would not be easily deployed, due to a likely surge in FX volatility, at least until the volatility eases. While the region's government debt position is better than those of its EM counterparts, as a manufacturing center, Asia remains more vulnerable to global fragmentation.

Macroeconomic fluctuations often account for most of the risks associated with exposures to instruments that promise to pay fixed or variable interest rates. The longer the maturity of a certain cash flow and the higher the move in the discount factor, the higher will be the loss. Upside surprises in inflation, fiscal funding needs, and FX depreciation rates are among the most common adverse macroeconomic shocks to receivers. But counterparty exposure, issuer creditworthiness, client segmentation, regulation (including changes in assets holding limits for different types of investors), changes in tax policies, currency convertibility (which may constrain currency conversion, repatriation of profits and/or liquidation of positions), and settlement issues related to local clearing houses are also important risk factors. The sensitivity of fixed-income instruments to macroeconomic shocks may be mitigated by indexing the contracted cash flows to inflation, to FX depreciation, or to specified interest rates - these are common in emerging markets. The choice of the proper fixing (or metric) is particularly important in swaps markets, where floating coupon rates (i.e., coupons indexed to a typically short-dated interest rate reference index) are exchanged for fixed coupons.
