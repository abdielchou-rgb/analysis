# Rebalancing fundraising with shareholder return: the effects

Against the challenging capital market backdrop, we discuss where we see relative value among China's brokers. This leads us to upgrade CITICS-H and East Money and downgrade CICC-H.

Most major brokers we cover have de-rated to near 10-year lows: We think the heavy discount stems from concerns about brokers' future business and fears of rising regulatory uncertainty. Another worry is the quick cooling of private equity and venture capital – so-called long-term capital investments.

Capital market has supported industrial fundraising and supply chain upgrades, but the cost is disappointing investor returns: This has led to temporary capital market austerity and reduced brokers' business visibility. Underwriting has been the first to suffer as regulators rebalance fundraising with shareholder return.

More diverse and inclusive IPO mix could improve listed firm quality and investor return and help underwriting business recover. We think the right conditions are in place for this already, while a growing M&A market could also help achieve these purposes with some M&A dynamics already changing.

Long-term rule-setting and consistent enforcement are key: A robust regulatory framework should reduce the need for short-term administrative interventions, which have caused market confusion.

Where we see alpha while segment as a whole is yet to bottom out: Position for potential beneficiaries of the overarching shift from fundraising to improving shareholder return, as well as players that can maintain franchise strength during the shift and accelerate market share gains when the cycle turns.

East Money (300059.SZ) – upgrade to OW: After three years of de-rating, P/E premium to traditional brokers has narrowed from \~13x to only 3.7x. We see an attractive entry point. ## A closer look

## The fundamental backdrop

## It might feel like it's getting more challenging to invest in China's brokers:

• The industry's revenue fell 21% in 2022 and was stagnant in 2023. Our covered Ashare brokers' revenue fell another 22% yoy in 1Q24.

• Average ROE of our covered A-share brokers declined to 7% in 2023 from 11.9% in 2021.

We see more widespread cost-cutting efforts in 2024, but some non-market-oriented measures could affect brokers' long-term ability to attract talent.

To be clear, the segment has gone through cycles before... Our covered A-share brokers' ROE fell from \~20% in 2015 to 5.8% in 2018 as the leverage-driven bull market collapsed and macro uncertainties such as trade tensions arose.

...but most of the major brokers we cover have de-rated to near the lowest level in the past 10 years… A-share brokers now trade at 0.6-1.9x forward P/B, while H-share brokers trade at 0.3-0.5x forward P/B.

...while ROE at 7% in 2023 is still above the 5.8% trough seen in 2018.

What we think the heavy discount stems from:

• Concerns associated with brokers' future business development, in areas like trading, underwriting, margin finance, derivatives, mutual fund management, etc.

• Fears of rising regulatory uncertainty as the capital market is undergoing a fundamental shift of priority from fundraising to improving shareholder return.

Another worrying sign is the quick cooling of private equity and venture capital – the so-called long-term capital investments: PE and VC investment volume fell to the lowest level in eight years after a 23% decline in 2023, following a 36% drop in 2022. We think several factors could be leading to decreasing participation of market-oriented long term capital:

• A heavy concentration in manufacturing firms given policy encouragement,

• Challenges exiting after IPOs,

• Lack of visibility of future new listing pace, and

• Rising influence from government-backed LPs (limited partners) that are less market-oriented.

This has caused some concerns about future IPO supply and whether the capital market would have enough capacity to support innovation in a sustainable way.

Why brokers have underperformed: the cost for the capital market to support industrial fundraising has been temporary capital market austerity.

The underwriting business is among the first to bear the impact from the shift in priority away from fundraising: IPO volume dropped 39% in 2023 and another 84% in 1H24. Investment banking revenue for leading underwriting franchises such as CITICS, CICC, and HTSC dropped 27%, 47%, and 25%, respectively, in 2023. We believe this shows in part the cost of the capital market's increasing support for the industrial sector.

Inarguably, China's US\$10tn equity market has played its part in supporting the country's industrial upgrade in the past 4-5 years: Between 2020 and 2023, over 1,600 companies raised a total of Rmb1.96tn through IPOs, exceeding the total IPO volume of the 10 years preceding 2020. Over 60% of the funds raised went to various manufacturing firms, especially those listed on the STAR Board and ChiNext Board.

There is ample evidence that firms backed by the capital market are, on the whole, able to accelerate capex and R&D investments after listing at a pace notably faster than the national average. This has translated into fast top-line growth overall as these manufacturers have taken market share in both the domestic and global market, while industry leaders have also been emerging in some sectors.

However, the volatile equity market and disappointing shareholder returns in the past few years indicate that the support is not without cost: In fact, we estimate that investors would be losing money on 81% of the new IPOs since January 2020, if they bought them at the close of the first trading day and held through June 2024.

One of the main causes is deteriorating earnings: around half of newly listed firms since 2020 have reported profit declines or even losses in the year immediately following their IPO.

To make things worse, typically high IPO valuations – 40-60x trailing P/E on average – suggests that earnings volatility has not been adequately priced in, further adding to derating risk.

These issues have led to rising investor complaints and attracted more policy attention since 2H23.

The result – a period of effective austerity:

• Freezing new IPOs,

• Stricter delisting enforcement,

• Short-selling bans,

• Heightened scrutiny of quant and derivatives,

• Cutting fund manager fees, etc.

...which has reduced visibility for brokerage businesses.

Investment banking business could benefit from:

1. A potentially more diverse and inclusive IPO mix

2. A growing M&A market

## A potentially more diverse and inclusive IPO mix

Improving the quality of listed firms now looks to be a pressing issue for both market participants and regulators: Analysis over a longer time frame, the past 10 years, reveals that A-share listed firms as a whole didn't generate enough earnings growth to sustain stock performance:

• The annual earnings growth (from 2014-2023, on a like-for-like basis) of A-share firms is only 6.2% on simple average, lower than the average nominal GDP growth of 7.9% during the same period.

• This explains the typical shortage of bulls and prevalence of bears in the A-share market, in our view.

To address the issue, we believe a potentially more diverse and inclusive IPO mix, with more decisions left to the market, could help: That means the IPO market could welcome innovative hard tech firms as much as the leaders in non-industrial sectors, as long as they can generate quality earnings growth and share it with investors.

We think the conditions are right: In China Financials: Regulatory Reset + Industrial Policy Support – Implications for Financials (19 May 2022), we wrote that multiple policy shifts in 2021 were necessary to reorient various resources from service to industrial sectors, given the rapidly declining industrial contribution to GDP. We see clear progress in:

• China's industrial upgrade accelerated in the past three years: This is best evidenced in rising export market shares.

• There are clear signs of reallocation of resources and talent: They include rising industrial credits from banks and more graduates looking for jobs in manufacturing sectors.

• The regulatory framework for many service sectors is more mature: We cite areas such as e-commerce, online lending, payment, gaming, after-school tutoring, etc.

Therefore, we think the conditions are right for China's capital market to strike a balance again between industrial and non-industrial sectors without fearing that resources may be pulled away by capital-light service sectors.

This matters in three important ways: First, China's industrial-focused development model provides a sustainable way to drive income generation, balanced consumption growth, and therefore expanding TAM for manufacturing and non-manufacturing business alike. Having more of these non-industrial market leaders listed in either the onshore or HK market would benefit the domestic investment banks, which can better serve companies in the home market.

Second, China's highly efficient and sophisticated industrial supply chains could be leveraged by non-industrial firms to develop globally competitive businesses.

Third, we believe market leaders in various areas should all emerge through fierce competition, given the ease of business formation and a large number of market entities, and they should demonstrate strong competitiveness and potentially make good-quality assets for the capital market. We believe allowing more of these competitive firms to

access the capital market, irrespective of the industries in which they are operating, is the way to better leverage China's industrial strength and generate sustainable return to over 210mn retail investors.

## A growing M&A market

M&A is potentially another area of opportunity to drive brokers' investment banking franchises: It is also gaining more focus from policymakers aiming to strengthen listed firms' competitiveness. Forming more sizable industry leaders in the equity market is also in the interest of investors because it could add to the stability of the market and attract more long-term capital.

Owing to the lack of market-oriented deals, China's M&A market is underdeveloped as compared to the US, as evidenced in the very small fee pool, which suggests limited valueadded from investment banks.

Positively, we believe some of the dynamics are changing, which could lead to more M&A opportunities: We cite:

• More privately-driven industrial investments in the past several years,

• Fewer IPOs in the near to medium term, and

• Regulatory encouragement.

CICC is currently the #1 M&A advisor in China, but we believe CITICS is also well placed to benefit from potentially growing M&A demand, by leveraging its large client base and CITIC Bank's.

Long-term rule setting and consistent enforcement should reduce the need for short-term administrative interventions, which have caused market confusion.

Policies have caused some short-term pain… The transition from fundraising to supporting shareholder return has been accompanied by multiple CSRC policies since 2H23, covering a wide range of areas including listing process, trading practice, fee arrangements, and the ongoing supervision of various market participants.

Some of the short-term administrative measures – such as halting IPOs and banning shortselling – are aimed at quickly addressing market concerns, especially those from retail investors. However, we think they have also led to investor concerns about the outlook for capital markets businesses – and hence de-rating of broker stocks.

...but lay the foundation for long-term gain: On the other hand, we think efforts to improve long-term rule building and consistent enforcement will work more effectively to contain capital market risk, support investor confidence, and stabilize expectation. In 2024, we have been encouraged to see much progress made in updating existing regulations or making new rules to keep up with new business developments, such as:

• Stricter IPO rules and higher standards to ensure new IPO quality,

• Stricter delisting rules,

• A new regulation on program trading to create a more level playing field while also drawing experience from other mature markets,

• Updated regulations on principal shareholder sales to better align interests between large and small shareholders,

• A standard for the CSRC's own administrative punishment measures to ensure consistent enforcement.

We think more progress in long-term rule setting will reduce the need for short-termfocused administrative measures, and provide more clarity as to where the brokers should invest their capital and talent to drive innovation and higher value-added.

Key concerns to be addressed with a market orientation: As the Third Plenum has reiterated, the market should still play a determining role in resource allocation and improper government intervention needs to be prevented. We think this should reduce investor concerns about non-market-oriented factors interfering with brokers' business over the long run.

In fact, we will discuss in more detail how market-oriented ways will probably be more effective in addressing certain issues that concern both regulators and investors:

• An improved legal framework for investor protection: This could include a more market-driven class action lawsuit system – similar to that in the US – which mobilizes a large number of legal and finance professionals to scrutinize listed firms and seek compensation on behalf of ordinary investors.

• A more rational investor approach to IPOs: IPO overpricing is likely to persist for a while given the speculative demand from a large retail investor base. However, tolerating some volatility will enable market participants to adjust expectations by themselves and become more rational in future IPO investments.

• Trailing commissions on mutual funds: This could be a market-oriented way to balance fund managers and wealth managers, and incentivize both to add more value for investors: more sustainable returns and longer holding periods. We think regulatory-driven mutual fund fee reform could achieve more long-term benefits if it can drive structural changes to align asset and wealth managers' interests with those of investors, besides simply cutting some fees in the short term.

Particularly, if regulators will not change how mutual fund trailing commissions are to be determined, it should remove a major overhang for East Money, which has greater revenue exposure than peers to trailing commissions.

## What this points to for IPO volume and A-share ADT

We expect gradually normalizing fundraising activities and trading volume recovery, backed by better shareholder return. The corporate earnings cycle should play a bigger part in determining how soon we could see rebound in such activities.

Based on the changing monetary policy framework and moderating window guidance on loan growth, we have become more confident that rationalization of industrial credit should come before mid-2025 – please refer to: China's 3D Journey – Financials: Assessing the cost of supporting ongoing industrial upgrades (21 May 2024). This should slow down capacity building and help restore manufacturers' margins and profitability.

As a result, improving earnings should help IPO volume to recover from an extremely low base towards 2026. We expect around Rmb110bn in IPOs in 2024, -68% yoy, followed by a rebound to Rmb220bn in 2025 and Rmb350bn in 2026 (similar to 2023's level). If more diverse service sectors outside of manufacturing could get better access to the IPO market, we believe the recovery should come earlier.

We also think the efforts to support shareholder return will translate into a rebound in ADT in A-shares from \~Rmb829bn in 2024e to Rmb885bn in 2025e and Rmb945bn in 2026e.

## The forces that drive competitiveness

To shape brokers' competitive edge amid the new dynamics in the IPO underwriting market dynamics, we see several areas for differentiation, including:

• The ability to provide comprehensive services,

• Having a strong proprietary PE/VC investment arm,

• Close collaboration with banks, and

• A strong franchise in HK.

We think underwriting market share will further be consolidated by the top brokers.

On the other hand, we think deep industry know-how and value-added in deal pricing will be key to capturing M&A opportunities: This is an area where smaller or boutique players could also have competitive offerings as long as they develop capability in adding value.

Another area we expect to drive diverging competitiveness in the future is the way different brokers handle cost-cutting during downturn: We think it is key to strike a balance between cost control and proper motivation of employees. It is extremely important to have rule-based and well-designed cost management measures with some structural changes, which should better support efficiency gains and help the brokers to maintain long-term competitive edge. Different responses to cost management could lead to diverging competitive strength in the future, even among the top industry players.

## Upgrade East Money (300059.SZ) from EW to OW

We think this Internet-based retail broker will be among the first to benefit from gradually recovering retail investor confidence and trading activity, which should lead the recovery of other capital market activities such as fundraising. This should also allow it to accelerate share gains in brokerage, margin, and mutual fund distribution by converting its large user base, which continued to grow 17% during the downturn from December 2021 to June 2024.

After three years of de-rating, 12-month forward P/E is currently 17-18x, and its premium to traditional brokers has narrowed from \~13x to only 3.7x. We believe this offers an attractive entry point. We think East Money still deserves a premium to traditional brokers in view of its scalable business model, cost efficiency, and market share gain potential.

## Upgrade CITICS-H (6030.HK) from EW to OW

We like CITICS' balanced business mix, which has already helped it register much lower quarter-to-quarter earnings volatility in the past several years. Its more stable cost-income ratio has also benefited from its prudent and consistent client-centric strategy.

More importantly, we think varied strengths – its comprehensive offerings, a large PE/VC investment arm, potential collaboration with CITIC Bank or CITIC Group companies as, well as an established HK franchise (CLSA) – should continue to strengthen the firm's client franchise and help it consolidate market share in underwriting, M&A, and other capital market services.

Hence, we expect CITICS to lead peers in ROE recovery. We project a rise to 8.1% in 2025, with the potential to return to double-digit ROE if market conditions improve more meaningfully.

This could help CITICS-H re-rate to 0.6-0.7x 12-month forward P/B, highest among our covered peers.

## Downgrade CICC-H (3908.HK) from OW to EW

We choose to move to the sidelines for now. CICC is very competitive in orchestrating mega-deals and has a strong equity franchise. However, the shifting IPO dynamics and tightened scrutiny on equity trading, short-selling, quant, and derivatives could continue to weigh on the firm's investment banking and equity and derivatives businesses.

In addition, CICC's elevated cost-income ratio at 69.2% in 2023 implies more imminent pressure to rein in the cost base. However, the notably expanded headcount – +62% from 2020 to 2023 – could pose challenges for the firm to contain cost while maintaining proper incentives to the top talent at the same time, which is crucial for the firm's longterm competitive strength.

We think these factors combined will bring some uncertainty to the firm's ROE in the next couple of years. The stock has de-rated from \~0.6x forward P/B in 4Q23 to 0.3x P/B currently, showing that the concern is partly priced in.

## Where we could be wrong

IPO mix does not broaden: Although we think a more diverse mix of IPOs could help improve listed firm quality, it's possible that policymakers may still want to focus capital market resources on supporting mostly hard tech and high-end manufacturing candidates. In this case, IPO volume could remain low for a longer period, given the higher focus on quality than quantity, which would also limit the size to which the capital market can grow over time. Brokers' addressable market from underwriting and trading could grow more slowly under this scenario.

Various administrative measures pertaining to capital market activities persist for too long: This could stem from weaker investor sentiment arising from macro pressure, low tolerance of market volatility, or challenges in improving long-term rules. Brokers' business in underwriting, securities lending, and derivatives could remain suppressed in this case as they would receive blame for market decline. Market ADT could moderate further with lower institutional participation, such as quant, amid ongoing tight scrutiny. Retail investors still might not come in if risk appetite is low.

# China's capital market has served its industrial sector well for fundraising – but at a cost

## Expanding R&D, capex, revenue, and global market share show the progress achieved with rising capital market financing support

Despite increasing tariffs and greater restrictions on exports over the past few years, China has continued to expand its global market share through years of industrialization, resulting in comprehensive and competitive supply chains. We have discussed these trends in:

• China's 3D Journey – Financials: Assessing the cost of supporting ongoing industrial upgrades (21 May 2024) and

• China Financials: Regulatory Reset + Industrial Policy Support – Implications for Financials (19 May 2022).

This progress is best illustrated as follows:

• Of the more than 1,200 product categories that China exports, based on HS 1988/92 four-digit code products, over a quarter, or 393 products, hold >25% global export market share. By comparison, the number of export products with >25% market share was just 103 in 2001 when China first became a member of the WTO.

• Of the \~1,400 products listed under HS 1988/92 four-digit codes, China had 812 products with top three global export market share in 2022 – the largest among peer countries. The US has seen its number of products with top three market share decline from 545 in 2009 to 466 in 2022.

## Such support also came at a cost: weak earnings and high valuations of new listings have led to rising equity market volatility

For those investors who bought into these newly listed companies, we believe returns have generally been disappointing. Based on our tracking of the cumulative (as of June 2024) total returns of all new listings since 2020, 48% of new IPOs have lost money for investors, with a median return of only 2.7%. The average return, while looking attractive at 37%, is actually the result of a small number of outliers and not representative of the broader return picture.

Also, the above calculation is based on the IPO price, which only successful IPO subscribers can get. As new listing prices often surge on the first day of trading, alongside turnover, we use the closing price of the first trading day as the starting point for cumulative return. The result shows that 81% of new IPOs end up losing money (as of June 2024), with average return of -24% and median return of -40%.

We think this reflects how secondary market investors are much more likely to lose money than make money when they invest in newly listed stocks (since 2020 at least). The large difference in terms of stock performance between the first few days and subsequent trading also shows that IPO stocks since 2020 may have offered short-term speculative opportunities but lacked sustainable investment value beyond that.

We believe a major reason for the suboptimal returns is sluggish earnings growth of these firms after IPO: Our analysis (please see Appendix at the back of the report) reveals that around half of each of the past three years' new listings reported profits that declined or turned negative in the immediate following year. While this could be attributed to Covid, macro pressures, or a focus on market share over profitability, the high valuation at IPO suggests that these risks were inadequately priced.

Therefore, in our view, expensive IPO valuations combined with weak earnings trends have made these stocks vulnerable to secondary market volatily and weighed on equity investor returns.

With more investors realizing that low-quality IPOs have drained market liquidity without creating sufficient shareholder returns, their complaints grew louder in 2023. Regulators soon stepped in and announced temporary tightening of IPOs from August 2023. This was followed by a period of effective austerity: freezing new IPOs, stricter delisting enforcement, short-selling bans, heightened scrutiny on quant and derivatives, cuts to fund manager fees, etc., which reduced visibility for brokerage businesses.

The underwriting business was among the first to feel the impact: IPO volume dropped 39% YoY in 2023 and another 84% YoY in 1H24. Leading underwriting franchises such as CITICS, CICC, and HTSC saw their investment banking revenue fall 27%, 47%, and 25%, respectively, in 2023.

## Moderating PE/VC investment over the past two years could affect sustainability of capital market support to innovation

Policy focus shift and IPO market reforms, including the launch of the STAR board, have led to a rising share of manufacturing firms among PE/VC investments, up from \~30% before 2019 to >70% in 2023 based on PE data. This has served to gravitate capital market resources as well as talent away from various service sectors towards industrial sectors, and has contributed to innovations in areas such as semi, auto, batteries and solar.

However, after peaking at Rmb1.4tn in 2021, PE/VC investment fell a sharp 36% in 2022, even though IPO volume reached an all-time high level in 2022. PE/VC investment further declined 23% in 2023 with the absolute amount falling to its lowest level since 2015 at Rmb690bn. This has led to some concerns as to whether PE/VC funds will continue to have enough capacity to support industrial and hard tech firms' innovation at an early stage.

We think a key reason for the drop in PE/VC investment volume is changing IPO dynamics as this is the predominant way such firms exit their investments in China. Specifically:

• Volatile stock performance and still inadequate liquidity on the STAR board after listing could make exit more challenging when pre-IPO investors' lockup period ends.

• The high policymaker preference for industrial firm IPOs led PE/VC investments to be increasingly concentrated in industrial areas. However, it takes much longer for industrial market leaders to emerge than service market leaders. Hence good investment targets are increasingly harder to find.

• Sharp decline of new IPO volume in 1H23 of over 30% yoy following weak market conditions has likely made PE/VC funds more cautious in making new investments.

• Regulators have started to proactively tighten on new IPOs since August 2023, adding further uncertainty around future exits of PE/VC investors.

In addition, there has been a trend in recent years of PE/VC investments being increasingly driven by government-associated or SOE LP (limited partners), likely because local governments and SOEs have been tasked with supporting industrial innovation and growth. However, the model of government-led PE/VC is somewhat different than a purely market oriented-one. The former's objectives usually go beyond purely financial returns, which could also include the need for investees to set up local production, or headquarters, that could generate tax revenue as another form of payback.

Given the divergence in objectives, we think it is challenging for government-associated PE/VC funds to raise more funds from market investors, meaning they will have to rely increasingly on local governments' own fiscal strength, which varies widely across the country.

## We expect more diverse IPOs to bring in quality firms – key to better investment returns

## Over a longer time frame, A-share companies as a whole have not generated enough earnings growth to drive sustainable equity market performance

A sustainable supply of high-quality firms that can provide consistent earnings growth is key to driving healthy growth of the capital market as well as offering stable returns to investors, in our view. As CSRC Chairman Wu pointed out at the recent Lujiazui Forum, capital markets should have broader coverage and target companies offering potential productivity gains including both emerging industries and traditional industries that can be upgraded.

Besides the weaker earnings trends over the past 3-4 years, a longer time horizon looking at the past 10 years still reveals that A-share listed firms as a whole did not generate enough earnings growth to support sustained stock performance. The annual earnings growth (from 2014-2023 on a like-for-like basis) of A-share firms is only 6.2% on simple average – lower than the average nominal GDP growth of 7.9% during the same period. This corresponds to a 3.5% annualized price return from 2014 to 2023 for the Shanghai Composite Index; when dividend yield is added back in, this is just in line with earnings growth.

Over the past 10 years, excluding the leverage-driven short bull market in early 2015, relatively longer bull runs only occurred when earnings growth strengthened notably, such as 2016-2017 and 2019-2021. We therefore think profitability should be the most important consideration when it comes to new listings from the perspective of shareholder return.

## Policies driving resources shift to industrials were necessary in 2021 – but conditions are now right to welcome a more diverse IPO mix

From time to time, regulators have implemented implicit window guidance on what industries can get listed as A-shares. Sectors like financials, fintech, internet, and some other service sectors have at times faced more restrictions. Since 2021, a combination of policies, including the regulatory reset and financial cleanup, has been reorienting all kinds of resources back to industrial sectors. We discussed in China Financials: Regulatory Reset + Industrial Policy Support – Implications for Financials (19 May 2022) the rationale behind such a concerted policy shift, with one important reason being that industrial development will still likely offer the most sustainable productivity gains at China's current per capita GDP at just over US\$12,000.

As discussed in the above mentioned report, we understand the necessity and urgency of the coordinated policy shift towards industrials a few years ago, as the share of manufacturing in China's GDP had seen a declining trend in the 10+ years leading to 2021. In particular, the sequential slowing growth of China's labor force and the equity market's preference for the capital-light service sector was likely going to continue diverting the resources allocated to manufacturing sectors and reducing industries' competitiveness. This prompted the government to aim to strike a balance between growth in both sectors over the following 5-10 years, leading to an increasing presence of manufacturing firms in A-share IPOs since 2021.

## Industrial-focused economic model supports income growth, offering room for quality companies outside of industrials to emerge

As discussed in our Bluepaper, China's unique economic development model has shaped a bank-centric financial system, with more underlying assets suitable for a banking system rather than a capital market. This will likely limit the relative size of China's capital market compared to that of the US. As a result, we think the capital market needs to be even more focused on selecting the right assets in order to achieve quality growth and generate sustainable returns to investors.

In our view, companies suited for the capital market should be transparent, innovative, shareholder-friendly, and offer strong earnings growth potential. These qualities could also change as an industry develops and evolves. For instance, there used to be very few listed firms in the restaurant business because a common perception was that such businesses are operationally heavy, non-standardized, and lack scalability, but as the industry has evolved, some companies have innovated in terms of ways to manage supply chains, control quality, and improve marketing efficiencies. Such progress has helped to improve investor appetite from both the primary and secondary markets towards restaurants/catering businesses because they can potentially offer faster profit growth now.

Importantly, China's industrial-focused development provides a sustainable way to drive income generation for households. Despite Covid, trade tensions and the domestic property reset – which led to concerns about balance sheet recession – we have continued to see resilient household financial assets growth of 8-13% as well as consumption and payment CAGR of around 7% over the past three years. Solid growth of the household income pool and spending power provide an expanding TAM for many different service and manufacturing business.

China's market is not only big but also competitive. Given the ease of business formation and the large number of market entities in China (see below), we believe that market leaders that can emerge from fierce competition should demonstrate strong competitiveness and potentially make good-quality assets for the capital market.

In addition, the previous regulatory reset and progress in industrial upgrades have led to more balanced income growth and shifting consumption drivers – a trend we categorize as consumption upgrade in lower-tier cities and rural areas vs. consumption downgrade in higher-tier cities. This change creates new business opportunities and potential for new market leaders to emerge.

Last, non-industrial firms could also develop a globally competitive business by leveraging China's highly efficient industrial supply chains – one such example is PDD. We believe this is also part of the benefits of China's industrial-focused development model, which should be utilized by the capital market and fully shared with domestic investors.

As a result, we think a more diverse and inclusive IPO mix would help to introduce more high-quality companies to China's capital market and generate more sustainable returns for investors.

## Extensive policy response to normalize with market taking greater role

## Policymakers are driving a fundamental shift of focus from fundraising to improving shareholder return

In light of rising market volatility and weak equity returns, policymakers have been ramping up various measures to revitalize the capital market since July 2023. By April 2024, when the State Council officially released its latest guidelines for the capital market – please see China Brokers: A Prudent Tone Set For Capital Markets (15 Apr 2024) – policymakers had laid out the guiding principles of capital market development as strengthening regulation, preventing risks and promoting high-quality development. This represents a change in tone from the 2014 guidelines, which called for accelerating development and an increasing proportion of direct finance. In our view, this is a fundamental shift of focus towards shareholder return from supporting fundraising.

CSRC policies since 2H23 have covered a wide range of areas including the listing process, trading practices, and ongoing supervision of listed firms as well as intermediaries such as brokers or asset managers. Improving the quality of future IPOs and strengthening supervision of listed firms is at the core of the initiatives to improve shareholder return, in our view. On this objective, we have seen policies aimed at both near-term and long-term effects. ## We believe short-term administrative measures will gradually be replaced by improved long-term rule-setting

We think ultimately a market-oriented capital market system with proper rules, which should also evolve over time, is key to fully leveraging the capital market's role in efficient resource allocation. The regulator's recent administrative measures – while potentially effective in the short term to address issues such as low-quality IPOs, major shareholders taking advantage of smaller shareholders and rising volatility from derivatives or algotrading, etc. – could result in reduced visibility for market participants if they are kept in place for too long. This concern has also led to further de-rating of broker stocks in 2024.

In July 2024, the Third Plenum reiterated the need to better leverage the capital market's determining role in resource allocation and pricing, and prevented improper government intervention in resource pricing (including capital). From the perspective of the capital market, we see this as a reaffirmation that market forces will play a bigger role in trading strategy, fundraising pace, industry mix, etc. over the long run. We think this could reduce investor concerns regarding non-market-oriented factors influencing brokers' businesses over the long run.

As CSRC is keeping pace on the regulatory front, we expect short-term measures to gradually be replaced with improved formal regulations. In addition, consistent enforcement of rules, regardless of market conditions, will be important to help the market build stable expectations. We believe the recently issued Standard of CSRC's Administrative Punishment Measures will provide a helpful way to increase transparency and clarity of rule enforcement.

In fact, we believe a more market-oriented financing system would not only support the capital market's healthy development in the longer term but could also be key to addressing many of the issues that regulators are trying to tackle now. We discuss three concerns of regulators that we think could be best addressed using market-oriented methods rather than administrative measures:

• Investor protection

• IPO overpricing

• Regulators plan to cut mutual fund fee rates in light of poor mutual fund returns

## A market-oriented system of shareholder protection: experience from securities class action lawsuits in the US

Much has been done over the past 12 months by regulators in China to better protect shareholder interests:

• Cracking down on financial fraud by listed firms;

• Tightening principal shareholders' sale of stocks to better align their interests with smaller shareholders;

• Gradually tightening short-selling. Although in our view short-selling is hardly a cause of market decline, it is usually blamed by retail investors as such;

• Ramping up stricter oversight on quant funds, which many retail investors believe took advantage of them;

• Encouraging higher dividend payout and implementing punishments if listed firms

pay out too little.

As the CSRC is pushing for stricter enforcement of delisting rules to clean low-quality firms out of the capital market, how to properly protect investor interests during the process will be key. In our view, the legal system could play a larger part in protecting smaller shareholder interests. On this, we think the US capital market's securities class action is a good parallel to draw.

China has been rolling out its own securities class action in recent years, mentioned again more recently by CSRC Chairman Wu Qing at the Lujiazui Forum. The new 2020 Securities Law officially introduced special representative lawsuits in the securities market, which are seen as the Chinese version of securities class action. Based on our understanding, these new Chinese processes have a number of things in common with US securities class action:

• The plaintiff is usually a professional agent representing shareholders. In the US it is usually a law firm; in China it is the Investor Protection Institution, which is affiliated with the CSRC.

• The lawsuits will cover most of the affected shareholders, who by default opted in unless they specifically choose to opt out.

• If the case is won, all affected shareholders will be compensated based on their holdings and time of purchase.

• There is zero cost and little effort required from shareholders upfront, making it easier for them to participate.

We think securities class action could help protect smaller shareholder interests in China given the dominant presence of retail investors (>210mn retail investors account for >60% of trading volume). Therefore, securities class action lawsuits could offer a low-cost and efficient process led by legal professionals to seek compensation for a large group of retail investors whenever their interests are damaged.

A market-oriented system could leverage more professional resources to scrutinize companies and protect small shareholders, in our view. At present, the Investor Protection Institution – basically an extension of the CSRC's regulatory power in view of its affiliation – is the major force initiating such lawsuits, especially in major fraudulent cases. However, in the US it is usually various law firms that proactively monitor wrongdoings of listed firms and file such class actions because their incentives are based on the compensation amount if the case is won. We think this incentive-based mechanism leverages a greater amount of professional resources beyond regulators' capacity to scrutinize listed firms and compensate smaller shareholders when their interests are damaged.

## Market mechanism important to correct IPO overpricing

In the eight initiatives rolled out on June 19 to deepen the STAR board reform, the CSRC aims to reform the listing process to encourage more rational IPO pricing and longer holding periods. Subsequently, the Shanghai Exchange announced that it will now exclude the top 3% highest IPO bidding orders in the STAR board pricing process compared to the top 1% in normal practice. While this change could reduce some irrational pricing in a technical way, we still think the ultimate answer to the IPO overpricing issue will depend on market-oriented adjustments, which is simply to allow the market to rebalance IPO supply and demand.

The overpricing issue, especially for the STAR board, reflects typically high demand for IPOs as investors see very good short-term reward by bidding for new shares and selling them on the first day when they get listed. This directly results from speculative IPO investment demand that developed under the previous merit-based IPO process.

Historically, under the merit-based IPO system, a new A-share IPO would almost always surge on the first day of trading, effectively making it a low-risk arbitrage opportunity. There were two main drivers of this:

• Before registration reform, IPO pricing was capped at 23x trailing P/E. This tended to undervalue new listings, or helped to create the perception of undervaluation, which usually led to a strong rally shortly after listing.

• Under the previous merit-based IPO system, the CSRC would carry out detailed examination of IPO candidates, which many investors viewed as a guarantee of quality to support performance after listing.

While these factors are now gone following registration-based reform, we believe the tendency to speculate on new IPOs will remain, which is the root cause of overpricing. This is because China's A-share equity market is still dominated by retail investors. Such investors tend to be underallocated in IPOs compared to their holding and trading volume share in the equity market:

• We estimate that retail investors account for around a quarter of A-share tradable market cap. However, excluding the holdings of controlling shareholders, who tend not to sell or trade, retail investors should make up well over 50% of actually tradable market cap.

• Retail investors accounted for over 60% of trading volume in 2023.

• However, typically 60-80% of IPO shares are allocated to institutional investors with only 20-40% allocated to retail.

Unsatisfied IPO subscription demand from retail investors can be easily satisfied in the secondary market once shares are listed, creating large trading turnover shortly following listing. This tends to push up share prices and in turn attracts more bidders for future IPOs and keeps IPO pricing high.

We do not expect IPO allocations to change in the near term, so some level of overpricing is likely to continue, but we think market-oriented adjustments could help rationalize IPO pricing. With median first-day returns falling and the chance of losing money rising (30% of IPOs fell below their offer price on the first day of trading in 2022 vs. nearly 0% in 2020 and 2021), it is understandable to us why IPO volume started to trend down between 2H22 and 1H23, falling >30% yoy in 1H23. The lower speculative IPO bidding demand has stopped the rising trend of IPO pricing, which started to come down in 1Q23.

On the other hand, regulatory action to further tighten IPOs since August 2023, in our view, has likely intervened in the market adjustment process and increased the scarcity value of new listings by suppressing IPO supply via administrative means. In order to better leverage market-oriented adjustments in IPO pricing dynamics, we think it's necessary to have higher tolerance for short-term volatility. If this is allowed, not only could the market develop more rational pricing towards future IPOs, brokers underwriting business could also benefit from higher visibility with less administrativedriven tightening.

## Regulators try to address disappointing mutual fund returns by cutting fee rates, but we think trail commission rate is best left to the market to decide

In July 2023, the CSRC laid out its agenda for a comprehensive set of fee reforms related to mutual funds to address investor complaints about disappointing mutual fund performance – please see China Brokers: Mutual fund fee cut: what are the impacts? (10 Jul 2023). The reform started with the reduction of actively managed equity fund management fee rates and custody fee rates in 4Q23, followed by cutting mutual fund trading commissions (among other trading-related adjustments) in July 2024, and cutting distribution-related fees which we expect to be implemented by the end of 2024.

We believe reducing some costs for investors is only a small part of the reform benefit, and the impact should be manageable for brokers. Also, putting in place a proper fee structure to incentivize a client-centric mindset for both fund managers and wealth managers could benefit investors longer-term, in our view.

## Reducing volume-based fee rates in 2024 should have a manageable impact on wealth managers' revenue

As analyzed in Asia Primer: China Mutual Funds: As Momentum Fades, What Next? (8 Sep 2022), we estimate wealth managers, including banks, brokers and online platforms, took away 47% of the total fund industry fee pool (Rmb400bn) in 2021, where 70% of their revenue is sales volume-based. We think the revenue split shows that wealth managers' value-added is mostly in driving sales of mutual fund products and launching new funds during a bull market.

Attractive subscription fee rates, usually at 1-1.5% for actively managed equity funds, also incentivize wealth managers to encourage higher client trading turnover on mutual funds because they can generate 1-1.5% fees every time a client buys a fund compared to AUMbased fees that depend on a client's holding period. This has often led to volume-driven sales practices by wealth managers but momentum-driven behaviors in turn have resulted in a higher turnover of investors' mutual fund positions and overallocation when the market is rising, leading to subsequently poor returns.

## Trailing commission rate should be left to the market to decide because it incentivizes both wealth managers and asset managers to improve their value propositions to investors

While still pending, we do not think fee rate reform should interfere in the way trailing commission rates are determined. Trailing commission, which is the percentage share of management fees for wealth managers, is determined by the bargaining power between wealth managers and fund managers. Currently the average trailing commission rate is 29% in 2023, where we think the leading wealth managers could take a higher share on average, while still below the regulatory-set cap of 50%. We believe trailing commissions provide a market-oriented mechanism to incentivize both wealth managers and asset

managers to improve their value-added, which will benefit investors.

## For fund managers:

• They have to build a reliable track record and demonstrate their ability to generate sustainable good returns in order to be recognized by investors. If they are able to do this successfully and develop their own profile, they can reduce their reliance on wealth managers to increase their AUM for them and potentially have more bargaining power to keep a bigger share of their management fees themselves.

## For wealth managers:

• We believe AUM-based trailing commission fees will become more important for future revenue streams following reductions in upfront volume-driven fees. To earn these fees, wealth managers will need to encourage clients to hold onto their positions for longer as the fee is based on AUM and holding time. This is no bad thing, in our view – longer holding periods can often help investors achieve more stable returns.

• To earn a higher trailing commission fee rate, we think wealth managers will need to demonstrate the value of their channel not just in selling funds to investors but by keeping investors invested in these funds, thereby increasing the stability of the investor base and stickiness of AUM to fund managers. This also leads to longer holding periods by investors and requires wealth managers to improve their asset allocation capabilities and prevent too many funds being sold in an overheated market.

Particularly, if regulators do not change how mutual fund trailing commissions are to be determined, we believe this would remove a major overhang for East Money, which has greater revenue exposure than peers to trailing commissions.

## How should brokers adapt to changing capital market dynamics?

Ultimately, in order to defend their revenue share in the securities industry, we think brokers need to continue improving the value-add of their services in investment banking, wealth management and asset management. In particular, as regulators are reprioritizing the capital market away from fundraising towards improving shareholder returns, we believe brokers will need to align their business structure, resource allocation and capability development towards discovering truly high quality IPO candidates with reasonable pricing, and helping both institutional and retail investors achieve better returns and properly manage risk.

## Earnings cycle will play a bigger role in future IPO volume following gradually normalizing regulatory environment

First of all, we believe there will be a period of short-term pain as the market digests the lower quality firms as transitioning to new dynamics takes time. The proactive tightening since August 2023 has already led to a notable reshuffle of IPO pipelines. Withdrawals of IPO applications have increased notably since 2H23: 144 companies withdrew applications in 2H23 compared to 74 in 1H23 and 147 in 2022, and the number in 2024 YTD is >300. As of July 2024, there were 379 firms in the IPO pipeline, down 60% from the same period last year. In our view, the cleaning up of the IPO pipeline reflects a higher focus on quality as many IPO companies in the last few years have reported profit declining or turning negative in the year following listing.

## Gaining a competitive edge in new IPO dynamics

We believe a wide range of industry knowhow, the capability to source high quality candidates from different sectors, value-add to IPO pricing supported by research and market insights, distribution network, as well as the ability to provide full value chain service will be key factors to gain market share in the underwriting business. Specifically, we think the below areas could drive further differentiation under the new dynamics of the IPO market.

## Ability to provide comprehensive financial services

We think investment banking offerings serve as a good way to onboard corporate clients by providing specialized capital market services. However, a stickier and longer-term relationship would require a wider range of value-adds from the brokers. While being leaders in their own industry, we think many private businesses might be less familiar with capital market functions and how these could be leveraged to accelerate their business growth. This is the area where brokers have expertise. More diverse financial services will be important to strengthen the relationship and bring in more revenue sources, such as: private banking and wealth management for executives and key shareholders, M&A opportunities, liquidity management and asset management demand, etc. We think the leading brokers will also compete on these comprehensive offerings in order to win future investment banking deals.

## A strong proprietary PE/VC investment arm

Having a dedicated PE/VC arm and investing in early stage firms could help brokers secure client relationships for future IPO pipelines. We think first-hand experience in the private equity market is also helpful in keeping bankers up to date on the latest investment trends in the primary market, as it will impact the structure of IPO pipelines in the following years. While most of the larger brokers already have PE/VC platforms, their AUM differs a lot even among the leading brokers.

CICC has the largest private equity arm within the domestic brokers with AUM of over Rmb400bn in 2023, equivalent to 2.8% market share in the highly fragmented PE/VC market. It has developed strong ties with many local government and SOE-led industry investment funds, which have become the major driver of primary market investments recently. CITIC Securities' private equity arm follows with Rmb100bn AUM in 2023, the second largest within the domestic brokers. It is also the most profitable among domestic broker-affiliated PE firms, generating over Rmb1.9bn revenue and Rmb1.1bn net profit in 2023. We believe their leading positions in the private equity business among brokers has helped CICC and CITICS to rank among the top 3 underwriters in A shares for quite a long time.

## Close collaboration with banks

We believe brokers that have closer ties to banks will also be better able to leverage this relationship to source good quality IBD clients. Within China's bank-centric financial system, commercial banks have the widest reach of corporate clients at different growth stages and different sizes, and are therefore equipped with the most comprehensive data and knowledge on corporates' day-to-day operations as well as changing industry competitive dynamics. This could be a valuable resource for brokers to expand their capital market services such as debt underwriting, structured finance, and potential IPOs.

The banking relationship could also empower brokers' offerings in M&A, as many of the potential buyers or sellers likely have no presence in the capital market but are served by banks. The on-the-ground experience of serving a large number of corporates along the supply chains makes banks better positioned to discover potential M&A opportunities that could be referred to their broker partners. At the same time, banks could provide M&A loans to facilitate large ticket size transactions.

While this has always been the case, we believe the importance of leveraging banking relationships will grow going forward, due to:

• Rising focus on IPO firm quality. Brokers will need to leverage the bank's data and insights into the particular firm as well as its upstream and downstream supply chains and broader industry landscape to develop a better understanding of the firm's profitability, cash flow, growth potential, its market position and potential risks.

• Banks have notably increased their service reach to early stage and hard tech corporates in the past few years, under policymaker's encouragement. According to our channel checks, banks have also improved their service capability with innovative ways to provide credit to these early stage firms, which used to be financed by PE/VC investors. We think it will be mutually beneficial to both brokers and banks as they collaborate on providing comprehensive service offerings to these emerging and high growth potential firms.

Within our coverage, CITIC Securities and CITIC Bank are under the same CITIC Group, while CMS and China Merchants Bank both belong to the China Merchants Group.

The potential for brokers to benefit from closer cooperation with banks is huge, in our view. Notably, while CITIC Securities with its >Rmb1.4tn balance sheet (2023) is already the largest broker in China, it's much smaller than a typical mid-sized bank in China: CITIC

Bank as a mid-sized bank has a balance sheet of >Rmb9tn. CITIC Bank's 2023 revenue and profit at Rmb205bn and Rmb67bn. respectively, are roughly equivalent to half of the overall broker industry's revenue and profit.

In 2023, CITIC Bank had a tech innovation loan book of Rmb415bn, +20.5% yoy, >Rmb100bn in M&A loans, served 159k large corporate clients and had close cooperation with 1,011 listed firms. Both CITIC Bank and CITIC Securities are among the top 3 nongovernment bond underwriters in China, across the exchange market and interbank market, with >11% market share combined. This shows the strong client base served by the CITIC Group companies where we see many opportunities for cross sell synergies. Therefore the potential for CITIC Securities to leverage collaborative opportunities with CITIC Bank is a unique advantage not enjoyed by many other brokers, in our view.

## More capability building needed for brokers to capture the growing M&A opportunities

We believe there are many areas that brokers need to improve their value add in order to capture the opportunity and grow the M&A market. In our view, the most important value adds that could be provided by financial advisors include:

• The ability to discover potential buyers and sellers based on deep industry knowhow;

• Ability to advise on proper pricing to be acceptable to both parties;

• Organize a transaction with the most suitable structure, payment method and arrange for financing if needed.

While large brokers such CICC, CITICS are already #1 and #2 players in the M&A space, we think smaller or even boutique players could also have competitive offerings as long as they develop these value adds. Therefore M&A and financial advisory is not necessarily an area where we should see market share concentrated on the large players.

## Balance cost control with proper motivation during down cycles will further differentiate brokers' long-term competitiveness

We wrote in early 2023 that higher focus on cost management will be crucial for brokers to muddle through the current industry down cycle, (please see: China Securities: Potential cost savings to support top brokers' earnings near term (2 Mar 2023)) and we maintain this view in 2024. However, as equity markets remain volatile, we also occasionally see some misunderstanding of the compensation in capital markets vs. other industries, which in our view could potentially lead to irrational cost management decisions that puts brokers' long-term competitiveness at risk.

## Human capital is the single most important factor for financial services firms and justifies a higher share of the cost base

Historically, China brokers have largely maintained a 45-60% cost income ratio, where usually ⅔ of the expense goes to employee salaries. The notable revenue pressure and lagged cost cutting in 2023 have resulted in a 57% cost income ratio on average for our covered brokers, which is at the higher end of the historical range of 45-60%, but in line with financial firms in most other regions outside of China. As a comparison, US large banks with a capital market focused business reported 61% cost income ratio on average in 2023.

## Rule-based, delicately designed cost management measures should work more effectively than simple pay cuts or caps

Faced with falling IPO volume, trading volume, regulatory-driven fee cuts, and rising public disapproval of high compensation, many brokers have notably tightened cost management since the end of 2023. However, some measures could be less effective and would materially impact employee motivation and in some cases lead to the loss of top talent. These measures could include: broad-based base salary cuts, setting a unified salary cap, notably delaying the previous year's bonus without justification, or even asking employees to return part of the compensation they've already received.

While it is normal for performance-based compensation to drop along with declining firm revenue, we think aggressively cutting base compensation or setting a cap risks hampering morale and employee productivity in too broad a way. The most talented employees might leave for more market-oriented places, such as some global firms, while others might treat work more passively ("lay flat"), as in many cases salaries in the financial industry even after a cut are still higher than in many other sectors. This would be a drag on firm efficiency and work against the purpose of cost management.

We think brokers that are able to strike a balance between cost cutting and proper motivation of employees will gain a competitive edge and reputation in the industry. This would require more carefully designed cost management measures than those simple approaches described above, in our view.

Importantly, we think any cost cutting measures should be fully rule-based. This means measures like delaying or even clawing back bonuses, if they are not based on predecided rules or any wrongdoings from the employee's part, could negatively affect employee confidence on the firm. This could potentially reduce the securities industry's long-term ability to attract talent and encourage more short-turn behavior and higher personnel turnover in the future.

Additionally, we think a balanced combination of pay cut and rightsizing of headcount could work more effectively in controlling costs while maintaining incentives to remaining employees, as compared to a broad-based pay cut. This is especially considering most of the brokers have notably expanded headcount since 2019: large brokers such as CITICS, HTSC and CICC saw headcount grow 69%, 63% and 88% from 2019 to 2023, respectively.

Notably, part of the growth was driven by expanding investment banking teams in anticipation of larger IPO volume following registration reform. CICC, CMS and Haitong's investment banking employees increased 133%, 110%, 51% during this period. While the number of IPOs did rise as expected, from \~200 in 2019 to 500 in 2021, it came down to just over 300 in 2023, and all the way down to 38 in the first five months of 2024 due to regulatory tightening.

However, most of the leading brokers still have over 1,000, in CICC's case >2,000, investment banking employees as of the end of 2023. With the fundamental shift in the capital market's priority from fundraising to investor returns, along with much higher requirements on listed firm quality, we don't expect the number of IPOs to return to the previous peak in the next 2-3 years despite some recovery, therefore can't support the much expanded teams.

## How we suggest investors position among different capital market names

In our view, the capital market's austerity will continue to pressure brokers' earnings in the near term and we factor these impacts into our forecasts.

This implies trading velocity will moderate from 315% in 2023 to \~290% in 2024, as the high velocity program trading, which was 30% of A share volume in 2023, will be met with higher scrutiny as well as higher trading costs. We expect a modest rebound in ADT of mid single digit in 2025 and 2026, driven by recovering retail investor sentiment.

We also expect tightened regulation on derivatives to result in a notable slowdown in leading brokers' balance sheet-driven growth. On the other hand, while a falling bond yield since 2H23 has supported bond trading income, we believe monetary policy shifting from a quantitative to a rate-based system as well as the PBOC's preference for higher longterm rates suggest further upside to bond trading gains like in the past 12 months might be limited. Therefore, we expect brokers to generate largely flat investment income for 2025e and 2026e. ## Upgrade CITICS-H from EW to OW: stable franchise to ride through cycles

In our view, CITICS' edge is not only in its size, being the largest broker in China with a >Rmb1.4tn balance sheet, but also balanced mix in business lines across retail and institutional brokerage, investment banking, equity and derivatives, FICC, and asset management. Revenue contributions are balanced across business lines where CITICS holds top market share in each: It has #2 market share in brokerage as measured by trading volume, #1 share in margin finance, #1 share in domestic equity and bond underwriting, while its asset management arm (not including China AMC, the mutual fund subsidiary) had >Rmb1.3tn AUM in 2023, the largest among all brokers. The balanced business setup helped CITICS deliver a resilient earnings trend, especially much lower quarter-to-quarter volatility than peers, that has been tested during the down cycle since 2022.

More importantly, we believe CITICS' investment banking franchise has the right ingredients needed in the new capital market dynamics, which we discussed earlier: a comprehensive set of offerings leveraging on its diverse business mix, a large PE/VC investment arm, and the potential to collaborate with CITIC Bank or CITIC Group companies as well as an established HK franchise (CLSA). The combination of these strengths should allow CITICS to accelerate market share gains in investment banking and other businesses across both the retail and institutional sides, in our view.

The firm has also handled cost control well with its cost income ratio managed within a tight range of 49%-54% in the past four years across up and down cycles, compared to wider swings of over 10 percentage points that were seen at many other peers. In our view, this demonstrates CITICS' prudent operation which could improve the consistency of its strategies and avoid sharp turns that could hurt its competitiveness.

Despite similar challenges as faced by other brokers in 2024, we think CITICS' competitive strength in the above areas should lead peers in ROE recovery to 8.1% in 2025 with the potential to return to double-digit ROE if market conditions improve more meaningfully. With a new price target of HK\$14.10, we upgrade CITICS-H to OW from EW.

## Upgrade East Money from EW to OW: much more attractive entry point given market share gain potential

East Money's core offering is a comprehensive digital information platform combined with low cost and convenient trading in stocks or mutual funds. The firm is a direct beneficiary of higher retail investor confidence and trading activity. We think East Money's capability to accelerate market share gains in brokerage, margin and mutual funds are intact despite a much slower pace during the market downturns in the past two years. The capability stems from its large and trading-focused user base and a scalable business model by converting this traffic. Notably, its main East Money App maintained an upward trend in MAU in the past two years, reaching 22.6mn in June 2024, up 17% compared to Dec-2021, after which market volatility rose notably. The MAU rose >180% when compared to Dec 2019, showing the high stickiness of East Money's user base once they start to use the company's offerings.

Given the fundamental shift in priorities by the capital market from fundraising to improving shareholder returns, along with many policy efforts and rule-building to improve listed firm quality, protect smaller shareholders and punish fraud, we think it will translate into improving retail investor confidence over time and drive higher retail engagement. In our view, East Money should benefit directly from this trend as it could accelerate the conversion of users to brokerage clients, while existing clients could trade more actively and contribute to fee income upfront. Notably, we see more room for its market share in margin finance, at 2.8% in 2023, to catch up with its brokerage market share at \~4% in 2023. Better retail sentiment will help accelerate this pace, which will improve the ROE of its capital as it will be redeployed from fixed income investments to margin finance.

On the other hand, we think the concerns on mutual fund fee rate cuts, which started in 4Q23, are partly reflected in earnings and the expectations are well priced in. While current market debate is on potential further cuts to trailing commission rates by the end of 2024, which could impact East Money's mutual fund revenue in addition to previous management fee rate cut, our analysis suggests that the trailing commission rate, as a mechanism to balance wealth managers and fund managers, should be left to the market to decide. Therefore we are more positive that East Money can largely maintain the margin on the mutual fund business, given its strong bargaining power and a large AUM base.

While we are baking in a modest \~7% growth in market ADT in 2025 and 2026, we expect East Money's brokerage revenue to post double-digit growth assuming 20-30bps market share gains each year. We think market share gains in margin finance should drive >30% growth in interest income for 2025 and 2026 which should be a main support to earnings. With a low cost base, we expect earnings to return to \~18% growth on average for 2025 and 2026, following \~2% decline in 2024. If market conditions were to improve more meaningfully, we think East Money, as a retail broker, its earnings will also benefit sooner than other traditional brokers, as trading volume and margin demand which directly represent investor risk appetite, should lead the recovery of other activities such as IPOs.

Since May 2022, East Money's forward P/E premium to major A share brokers has narrowed from \~13x to only 3.7x by July 2024, reflecting slower market share gains, greater-than-expected cyclical pressure which resulted in a weaker earnings trend. We think East Money still deserves a premium to the traditional brokers due to its scalable business model and market share gain potential. In particular, East Money's cost income ratio at \~30% is structurally lower than traditional brokers at well above 50%, due to the scalable business model. With its notably narrowed premium to traditional brokers, we think current valuation offers a more attractive entry point. We upgrade the stock to OW from EW with our price target unchanged at Rmb14.00.

## Downgrade CICC from OW to EW: still uncertainty in future ROE path

CICC felt a notable impact from the capital market adjustments: investment banking income dropped 47% in 2023 with a notable cool down and regulatory tightening in the IPO market. While CICC specializes in creating mega deals, the fragile market environment has pushed back many large deals which weighed on CICC's revenue more than others. Rising scrutiny on equity trading, short sell, quant and derivatives have also impacted CICC's ability to expand business in these areas where it has leading edges.

We think another challenge is how to achieve cost optimization while maintaining marketoriented incentives to its professionals. Notably, CICC's cost income ratio rose over 10 percentage points from 58.9% in 2020 to 69.2% in 2023, the second highest within our coverage (Haitong is the highest at 80% but with more serious revenue issues), with headcount up 62% during the same period, the fastest within our coverage. This could lead to more pressure than peers at CICC to optimize the cost base and also more difficult given the notably expanded team, while revenue uncertainty in areas such as investment banking and equity trading will likely linger for a while. It could be even more challenging to maintain proper incentives to the top talent at the same time, which is crucial for the firm's long-term competitive strength.

While the firm's ROE has slipped to 6.4% in 2023 from 12% in 2021, we still see some uncertainties on both the revenue and cost fronts in the near term which might add to the challenges for CICC to restore ROE to an industry leading level as before. Positively, we expect a gradual recovery in HK's IPO and trading volume should lend more help to CICC than peers. As such, we downgrade CICC to EW from OW.

## 2. Summary of capital market policy changes since 2H23

Most of the administrative measures to support investor confidence are aimed at addressing certain market concerns in the short term

Temporarily tightening new IPOs. CSRC announced its plan to temporarily tighten new listings from late August 2023 while restricting refinancing activity of firms with <1x P/B, below IPO price or recording continued losses. As a result, the exchanges basically froze new IPO applications for about six months until June 2024, so IPO volume fell >80% yoy in the first five months of 2024.

In April 2024, CSRC revised the Rules on IPO onsite check to increase the portion of firms to be randomly examined by CSRC from 5% (set in 2021) to 20%. The regulators also made it clear in the rules that withdrawing an IPO application will not stop the candidate from being examined by CSRC or the exchanges. We think the change sends a strong signal to the underwriters about information disclosure quality of IPO candidates, as there's a higher chance that they will face direct inspection and potential punishment from the regulator.

Strictly enforcing regulations and punishment. CSRC has increased its focus on cracking down financial fraud and other non-compliant activities. According to CSRC:

• the number of cases investigated rose 19% to 717 in 2023;

• the number of penalties rose 40% to 539 in 2023;

• the number of market participants penalized rose 43% to 1073 in 2023;

• the number of persons facing market entry ban increased 47% to 103 in 2023;

• the amount of fines increased 140% to Rmb6.389bn in 2023.

Restricting principal shareholder sales of stocks. On 27 August 2023, CSRC issued a notice to tighten the requirements for principal shareholders to sell their stake (please see: China Brokers: New requirements on principal shareholder sales good for investor confidence (28 Aug 2023)). Based on the raised requirements, principal shareholders will be barred from reducing holdings if one of the following situations exists for the listed firm: 1) its stock price falls below the IPO price; 2) P/B falls below 1x; 3) cumulative dividends are <30% of average annual profit in the past three years. We estimate that \~2,000 firms among the total of 5,000+ A-share firms might not meet these new requirements based on FY22 financials from WIND.

Restricting short sells. While in our view margin short was only a very small portion of the total margin finance business and unlikely to be the cause of a major market correction, CSRC still addressed market concerns, especially from retail investors, with a series of tightening measures on securities lending.

The CSRC on Oct 14 announced that it will introduce countercyclical adjustments to margin short, including: 1) raising the margin ratio from 50% to 80%, and to 100% for private funds; 2) disallowing the SPV used by management and key employees for participating in a strategic IPO allocation to lend stock; and 3) restricting stock lending by other strategic investors shortly after IPO.

On 28 January 2024, CSRC stopped securities lending of restricted shares, and reduced the efficiency of securities lending practice.

On 6 February 2024, CSRC effectively put a full stop to new short-selling via China Securities Finance (CSF) and banned brokers from lending stocks to investors doing intraday trading;

On 11 July 2024, CSRC required all existing short-selling via China Securities Finance (CSF) to be run off by the end of September 2024.

## In recent months, much progress has been made in updating existing regulations or making new rules to keep up with new business developments

In April 2024, CSRC, Shanghai Exchange and Shenzhen Exchange raised multiple IPO requirements. Revenue, profit, cash flow, and market cap requirements were all raised for Mainboard and ChiNext IPOs. In general, we estimate these quantitative requirements have increased by 30-70% across different metrics.There are also higher requirements on a candidate's tech innovative nature, such as R&D expense or number of patents, to qualify for a STAR Board listing, which effectively raises the bar for IPOs.

In April 2024, CSRC and the two exchanges updated delisting rules, adding more situations, such as financial fraud and deteriorating corporate governance, that could trigger forced delisting, as well as raising the minimum revenue and market cap that could trigger a delisting.

In May 2024, CRCS released the Regulation on algo-trading shortly after a consultation period. The new rule is designed to ensure a level playing field for smaller retail investors, and covers the whole value chain along algo-trading and also has a focus on high frequency traders. Specifically, CSRC requires higher reporting standards from high frequency traders, and gives mandates to exchanges to charge higher fees as well as take additional actions when they see breaches.

Brokers will be closely involved in quant funds' reporting requirements, including the fund's:

• AUM

• leverage

• trading strategy

• trading frequency

• trading infrastructure, etc.

Additionally, brokers will also participate in the daily monitoring of quant funds' behavior, and specifically the following:

• The number of trades submitted or withdrawn reaches a certain threshold within a short period

• Large transactions within a short period of time, causing volatility in specific markets and securities

In May 2024, CSRC released the Regulations on listed firm shareholder sales, which effectively turned the various requirements in the previous notice into official regulation. This should fix some loopholes on principal shareholder sales and better align their

interests with smaller shareholders.

The CSRC issued a consultation paper on 7 June 2024 of the Standard of CSRC's administrative punishment measures. These are the first such rules to clearly outline detailed standards CSRC will apply when giving out penalties. We see the new rules as important in terms of stabilizing market expectations and ensuring consistent enforcement, given a bigger part is played by various administrative measures, including investigation and penalties, in recent years.

The CSRC had a second round of consultation on the Regulations on derivatives in late 2023, and we expect the rules to be finalized in 2024. This will ensure CSRC is up to date with the rapidly developing OTC derivatives business in recent years.
