## Summer showdown

Jack Meaning, Barclays, UK | Abbas Khan, Barclays, UK | Moyeen Islam, Barclays, UK | Lefteris Farmakis, Barclays, UK | Zoso Davies, Barclays, UK | Emmanuel Cau, CFA, Barclays, UK | Matthew Joyce, CFA, BBI, Paris

Excerpted from UK election: Summer showdown, published on May 23, 2024

Prime minister Rishi Sunak has surprised markets by calling a general election for 4 July, right at the start of his "second half" timeline. Polling indicates a defeat for his ruling Conservative party but we see limited fiscal headroom, whoever wins. For now, there are positive implications for sterling and for stocks.

• The UK will hold a general election on 4 July, according to an announcement by prime minister Sunak on Wednesday evening. Polls suggest a win for the opposition Labour party, which has held a steady lead of around 20 percentage points over Mr Sunak's Conservatives since the turn of the year<sup>1</sup> . We look across asset classes and consider what this means for economics, FX, rates, equity and credit.

• Economics: We expect little diference to the aggregate fiscal outlook, whichever party wins, but talk of a deeper relationship with the EU (in the event of a Labour victory) could provide a boost to both GDP and supply, over time. We await manifestos for details. In the meantime, a July contest adds conviction to our call that the BoE will hold of from cutting rates in June.

• Rates: Whatever fiscal promises might be made over the next six weeks, the reality is that none of the main political parties will wish to unduly spook the bond markets. This implies little room for material moves in asset swaps although the market's knee-jerk reaction to uncertainty is generally to steepen the curve and tighten spreads, purely as a reflection of a higher localised term premium.

• FX: The prospect of closer ties with the EU should help to unwind at least some of the pound's Brexit premium. We retain our EURGBP forecast of 0.83 by year-end and 0.82 by Q1 25 but see risks of a more front-loaded outperformance.

• Equities: UK stocks have enjoyed a strong run of late, and the announcement of a snap election could boost sentiment further. We continue to like parts of the market that are sensitive to growth and rate cuts, such as Banks, Housebuilders, Retail, Leisure, Real Estate and Utilities.

• Credit: We see few immediate catalysts to direct investor perspectives on UK credits, beyond a handful of individual issuers. As such, we see little premium in £-IG versus €-IG. A general uncertainty premium may build, but we would not expect underperformance greater than single-digit-basis-points unless the Labour manifesto produces bigger surprises.

## What next for Banks if rates go down?

Emmanuel Cau, CFA, Barclays, UK | Matthew Joyce, CFA, BBI, Paris | Magesh Kumar Chandrasekaran, CFA, Barclays, UK | Emmanuel Makonga, Barclays, UK | Arihanth Bohra Jain, Barclays, UK | Paola Sabbione, BBI, Milan | Claudia Gaspari, Barclays, UK

Excerpted from European Equity Strategy: What next for Banks if rates go down?, published on May 22, 2024

Rate cuts may prompt profit-taking on Banks post c20% rally ytd, and rotation to Insurance/Div Fin. Scope for CoE normalisation and re-rating keeps us strategically OW Banks, but diversified revenue stream vs. NII reliance and capital return drive stock picking now. Our analysts advise on positioning in banks and insurance.

Fatigue and rate cut angst may prompt profit-taking on Banks. SX7P is up 20% ytd and looks quite extended. Expected ECB/BOE cuts in June may be seen as a threat to NIMs, and thus could be an excuse for investors to take some profits on banks after a strong run and post the bulk of dividend payments. It may prompt rotation to the lagging and lower-beta Insurance sector as well as diversified financials, and broader rate cut beneficiaries like real estate and our BCEUERCB basket.

Top down, we see any potential air pocket as a buying opportunity for Banks: 1) Valuations still look too depressed vs. fundamentals, with P/B at 0.86x vs ROE at 12.2% for MSCI Europe Banks. Although P/E has re-rated ytd, at c.7.5x it is still below the 10.9x average, the gap between price and EPS that opened up in 2022 remains, and CoE looks too high; 2) We expect earnings to be resilient as the rate cut cycle should be shallow and is unlikely to take us back to the NIRP/ZIRP era. IBES estimates of 2% EPS growth look conservative, particularly if PMIs keep rising; 3) Credit activity is showing signs of life, with volumes and asset quality both likely to get a boost from rate cuts; 4) Banks are leading the buyback surge in Europe, ofering the secondhighest total yield at 10.6%. Two-thirds of the announced buybacks are yet to be executed; 5) Banks positioning is higher but not crowded and still below pre-SVB levels. Big picture, we believe the reversal of the post-GFC Banks vs. Insurance underperformance has legs.

Bottom up, our sector analysts remain Positive on European Banks. They think that the rerating will continue and support the performance further from here, with cost of equity normalizing. They expect this will be driven by further evidence of earnings resilience after rate cuts materialize, with reduced concerns about worsening asset quality. Upcoming ECB/BOE cuts mean investors may look for rotation, and favour banks that can keep growing earnings due to 1) diversified revenue stream (fees from IB picking up for example), 2) late cycle NII where repricing from higher rates is yet to come and 3) capital returns. Their preferred names are UCG, BNP, Lloyds and NatWest, which fit those themes.

Bottom up, our sector analysts remain Neutral on European Insurance. A potential higherfor-longer environment is more supportive of banks' earnings gearing to interest rates. Nevertheless, from a top-down perspective, they continue to believe that Insurance remains inexpensive and a good source of defensive yield. The current ECB/BOE rate outlook is supportive: Insurance’s consistent 6% dividend yield has proven appealing historically, particularly with rates at 0-2% not low enough to hurt the business but underscoring the attractiveness of yield. Capital return is well underpinned by solvency and liquidity at all-time highs and they believe capital management will continue to drive the diverging fortunes for European Insurance stocks. Their preferred names for exposure to this theme are AXA and Munich Re (both Overweight).

## Sovereign emissions: Nature vs nurture

Zornitsa Todorova, Barclays, UK | Carlos Eduardo Garcia Martinez, BCI, US | Maggie O'Neal, Barclays, UK | Charlotte Edwards, CFA, Barclays, UK | Jordan Isvy, Barclays, UK | Christian Keller, Barclays, UK | Amruta Ghare, BSIPL, India

Excerpted from Thematic Macro Research: Sovereign emissions: Nature vs nurture, published on May 22 2024

We find that 40% to 60% of the diferences in sovereign emissions can be explained by inherent structural factors such as demographics, geography and climate. Adjusting for these forces, we arrive at a novel measure of sovereign emissions that better captures eforts by policymakers to protect the environment.

Evaluating sovereign emissions closely resembles the nature vs nurture debate, as we must consider both the inherent structural factors of countries such as demography, geography and climate, and the discretionary policies they adopt to protect the environment and promote sustainability.

Depending on their circumstances, countries could have diferent expected level of emissions – for example, countries that regularly experience very hot summers or cold winters, will have a higher demand for heating and cooling services, and hence higher emissions. Countries cannot change the characteristics they are born with, but they can influence the negative impact some of their circumstances have on the environment by taking active policy measures.

Structural factors explain almost 40% of the variation in carbon intensity (sovereign emissions per unit of economic output) for developed economies and more than 60% of the variation for emerging economies, according to our analysis of 21 countries over a 30-year period to 2021.

Adjusting for these structural forces, we arrive at a novel measure of carbon intensity that better captures discretionary green efort, and makes an important first step towards a fairer and more equitable comparison across countries. Countries such as the Netherlands, the UK and Korea rank high on this adjusted measure, whereas the US, Japan and China rank lower.

Equipped with this new measure, we also show that over the past 10 years, an increasing number of developed countries and some emerging market countries have both reduced their carbon intensity through active policy efort and achieved high economic growth. Those countries share two common characteristics: they are twice as energy eficient, and have a 40% higher share of energy from renewable sources compared to countries with high growth but also high carbon intensity. This suggests that investments in clean energy will likely become a key theme for investors in the future.

Achieving net zero is a global public good which will require policy interventions, drastic shifts in behaviour, and above all a great degree of international cooperation to ensure that the strategies are realistic and fair.

## A Second Wind

Venu Krishna, CFA, BCI, US | Japinder Chawla, CFA, BCI, US | Rex Feng, BCI, US | Madhuresh Rai, CFA, BCI, US | Young Chen, BCI, US

Excerpted from Long & Short of It: A Second Wind, published on May 21, 2024

Long-only equity exposure no longer historically extended; hedge funds emerged as dip buyers through 1Q earnings; systematic funds rebuilt equity longs post-April sell-of with room to add further; retail investor sentiment on the rise but no meme stock fever; MFs stay heavily overweight Big Tech while HFs lean cyclical.

The April sell-of reset the extended equity positioning of long-onlys from a post-COVID record to a level slightly below the LT median. The catalyst was macro jitters tied to slowing growth and sticky inflation with a healthy dose of technical and systematic de-risking in the mix, potentially leaving equities on stronger fundamental footing after a solid 1Q24 earnings season. Accordingly, hedge funds showed more resilience with no signs of capitulation in reaction to last month's stagflationary scare. In fact, we believe better-than-expected earnings appear to have motivated dip buying by hedge funds, especially in the equity futures market.

Systematic funds have rebuilt equity longs rapidly post April sell-of, but we see room to add further exposure. While systematic funds all cut their equity exposures in the April sell-of, CTAs and Vol Control funds have been quick to re-accumulate their longs. However, risk parity allocation hasn't recovered and has now dipped below the historical average. Should volatility continue to remain muted, we see room to add further equity exposure from these investors. Digging deeper into the CTA positioning, we note that their EU equity allocation remains much more elevated while we see room to add further within US and Asian equities. Notably, however, as per our estimates, the unwind risk within CTAs' elevated bond shorts remains high, even for relatively smaller declines in yields. Lastly, with the recent correction in oil prices, CTAs have trimmed their extreme longs in the commodity, while the overall commodity allocation within risk parity has also declined but remains near 10yr highs.

Retail investors sentiment on the rise, but no, this is not 'meme stock fever 2.0': Over the past month, the S&P skew has flattened over what we believe to be a mix of hedge monetization (during the mild April sell-of), as well as, more recently, due to upside buying. The latter has coincided with a jump in the call buy-to-open/sell-to-close volume ratio from retail investors, indicative of their renewed enthusiasm. While it is tempting to associate this with the recent resurgence in interest around meme stocks, single-stock options volume has actually declined over the past month. Additionally, the percentage of total call options traded by retail investors is far below the peaks in 2020/21 (34% vs 48%), thus indicating this is not just a 'meme stock fever 2.0'. In terms of potential market catalysts, all eyes are on NVDA earnings to be announced on Wed, 22-May (Aft-Mkt), with a consensus implied move of 7.3%. This is less than the 2y average realized move (as well as what consensus implied last quarter) of 8.5%. Additionally, currently NVDA volatility skew is less inverted, i.e., investors apparently do not see as much upside risk as they did last quarter.

Retail equity flows stumbled in April but seem to be on their way back up. According to EPFR fund flows data, retail flows to credit funds continue to exceed those of equity funds, with US IG credit remaining the heavy favorite. US money market funds saw their biggest monthly outflows in the last 5 years during the month of April. While some of the money has found its way back into money market funds in recent weeks, the trend is evidently in the negative direction, in our view. Notably, retail flows to US IG credit funds have picked up at the same time that money market inflows have downshifted.

Mutual funds stay heavily overweight Big Tech; hedge funds lean cyclical. Our analysis of holdings data suggests that fast money investors are mostly in lock-step with their long-only counterparts in terms of sector preferences. Tech/Big Tech, Energy and Industrials are the biggest consensus overweights, while Healthcare is the biggest consensus underweight. However, hedge funds diverged from LO by increasing their exposure to cyclicals, which suggests to us that they are more optimistic on the US macro outlook. In terms of factors, both mutual funds and hedge funds added back Momentum exposure while maintaining large-cap bias.

## Services don't serve rate cuts

## ...while services inflation remains sticky across regions...

A look across the inflation data so far this year provides a nuanced picture. Overall, the decline in inflation rates from the highs reached during the pandemic remains a success, and the

outlook is still broadly benign despite the occasional upside surprises, and markets' strong reaction to them in a 'data-dependent' world. Indeed, the more recent releases in the US and euro area suggest that the strong prints earlier in the year were likely outliers, and we see similar signs in the UK's recent upside surprise.

That said, recent prints do not instil confidence that inflation is on track for a return all the way to the 2% target. The disinflation trend since the pandemic peaks had been driven by a sharp reversal of energy and core goods prices as the supply-side shocks that had caused them faded. Services inflation, however, had remained elevated, as demand for services soared when lockdown restrictions were lifted and the supply of labour in this typically labour-intensive sector was constrained. In order to make 'the last mile', as some policymakers put it, to the 2% headline inflation target, services inflation would have to contribute, absent a sharp drop in commodity prices and/or significant core goods deflation. Restrictive monetary policy would facilitate that, as it would help reduce demand over time, thereby easing labour market conditions and, eventually, services inflation.

Although unemployment rates have bottomed and wage growth has peaked on both sides of the Atlantic, overall labour market conditions remain tight. In the US, the driver seems to be continued robust demand (while migration is fuelling labour supply), whereas in Europe and the UK, it is rather the lack of labour supply, amid ageing populations. But the result is the same: a tight labour market with wage growth still too elevated to allow for more pronounced disinflation in the services sector.

This seems to be what is causing services inflation to remain elevated across advanced economies and the disinflation trend to slow. While theory suggests that prices of tradeable goods should be much more correlated across countries, the typically domestically driven services inflation is currently also co-moving across regions. Labour market tightness seems to be the common factor.

At the moment, labour markets are showing signs of cooling (eg, vacancies to unemployment ratios) and the direction for inflation is for a slower decline rather than a re-acceleration. This keeps the probability of central bank hikes low, even for the Fed, which oversees the economy with the most robust demand. However, given the current inflation backdrop, there is no urgency for policymakers to cut soon or fast. In short, higher for longer seems to be firmly back.

## ...cementing higher-for-longer policy

## The FOMC is no longer itching to cut

In the US, we expect next week's April PCE inflation to confirm that the Q1 prints overstated underlying momentum but that price pressures nonetheless remain firm. Working from CPI and PPI, we think core PCE rose 0.24% m/m, about 8bp lower than in March, implying an unchanged annual rate of 2.8% y/y. This should be driven by reasonably broad-based disinflation across core services, while core goods may have accelerated slightly.

We think such an outcome would be consistent with our outlook for one Fed cut this year, in September at the earliest, should the moderation in core PCE be sustained in the coming months, but almost equally as likely in December. This week's May FOMC minutes and speeches reinforced the Fed's commitment to keeping rates higher for longer. For example, in line with Chair Powell's post-meeting press conference, the minutes dropped previous language that

"almost all participants judged that it would be appropriate to move policy to a less restrictive stance at some point this year." If anything, the minutes hinted at a more hawkish reaction function than markets perceived from the Q&A, as a "number" (in our view, four or five) of participants expressed doubts that policy is suficiently restrictive, plausibly involving additional rate hikes if conditions are not taking shape for a sustained return to 2% inflation in the coming months.

Besides inflation, the FOMC participants viewed aggregate demand as continuing to grow at a strong pace, but not accelerating, and poised to slow gradually, in line with our outlook. The incoming data on activity is similarly nuanced. The S&P global PMIs strengthened notably this week and durable goods orders beat expectations, pointing to resilience and allaying fears about a significant softening in demand. Next week, we expect Q1 GDP—which surprised to the downside—to be revised lower to 0.8% q/q saar from 1.6% q/q saar, and April real PCE to slow to 0.1% m/m after a robust increase in the prior month.

## The ECB is likely to take it slowly

Similarly, next week's euro area HICP inflation is unlikely to lend support to a fast easing cycle, as we think the headline will edge up 0.1pp to 2.5% y/y while core remains unchanged at 2.7% y/y amid a re-acceleration in services, ofset by softer core goods. Admittedly, much of the tailwinds for the May print come from mechanical base efects related to fuel and transport services (Germany's €49 monthly transport ticket) and volatile components. Even so, the deceleration in underlying services growth is also likely to be sluggish, as the details of the April print reveal sequential growth may not be accelerating, but is also making limited further progress.

In addition, euro area negotiated wages for Q1 picked up pace to 4.7% y/y from 4.5% y/y in Q4, slightly above many market participants' expectations prior to this week. That said, the uptick was driven by the treatment of one-ofs in the German public sector and the ECB blog talked down the increase, saying that alternative ECB trackers and other indicators point to moderating negotiated wage pressures.

Beyond inflation, this week also brought further signs of improvement on activity, with the May flash composite PMI edging up by 0.6pt to 52.3, beating our expectation and the consensus estimate. Improvements were the strongest in the forward-looking components and manufacturing (though the latter remained in contractionary territory at 47.4), while services were unchanged at 53.3, comfortably in expansionary territory. Next week, the German ifo survey, the European Commission survey, and the April money and credit data will give us further indications about the economic recovery.

We therefore do not see the recent dataflow as endangering June as the beginning of the ECB's easing cycle. This was also confirmed by some hawkish Governing Council (GC) members this week. For instance, Bundesbank President Nagel said: "If the situation stays as it is now and the projections don’t say something completely diferent — but I don’t assume that — then the probability increases that we will see the first interest-rate step." He also indicated that he expects wage growth to moderate.

We think the data will sway the majority of GC members to opt for a more cautious and gradual approach to easing. Accordingly, we now no longer expect a cut at July's non-forecast meeting, instead pencilling in quarterly cuts at the forecast meetings (June, September, December), followed by additional 75bp of cuts in 2025. Our forecast for the terminal deposit rate remains unchanged at 2.5%, consistent with our and the ECB staf's range of estimates for the neutral rate (2.0-2.5%), to be reached in June next year.

## The BoE will likely wait until August

We also saw a less encouraging inflation print in the UK this week. Although headline price growth slowed sharply to 2.3% y/y from 3.2% y/y, and core eased to 3.9% y/y from 4.2% y/y, this was significantly above expectations. The upside surprise was driven by services, which printed at 5.9% y/y, only 0.1pp lower than in March, compared with the 0.6pp deceleration we and the consensus forecast. Much as in the euro area in the beginning of the year, this was led by a number of components that reset infrequently, possibly representing direct and indirect indexation to past inflation, as well as the volatile items. This lowers the likelihood that the April print represents a durable acceleration in underlying services momentum, but it nonetheless implies a higher inflation profile over the next year. As a result, we raise our 2024 core inflation forecast to 3.6% from 3.3%.

We think this week's upside surprise will concern the Bank of England, which has made services inflation a central part of its reaction function. Thus, we no longer think the MPC will have suficient confidence in the disinflationary process to cut Bank Rate in June, instead delaying the easing cycle to August. Given the uncertainty about whether or not the bar has been met to begin cutting and likely volatility in inflation between the August and September meetings, we think the Bank will then skip September and resume cutting in November. By then, services inflation will have decelerated more notably, unemployment may be rising, activity slowing from H1 24, and the MPC will likely have more confidence in its own forecast, justifying sequential 25bp cuts in December and February. Thereafter, we expect the MPC to return to cutting at forecast meetings, ending in August at the terminal rate of 3.75%, the same as in our previous projection, as our view of the neutral rate has not changed.

For the near term, our changed call for the BoE to hold of from cutting in June is also reinforced by the prime minister calling a general election for 4 July, as starting an easing cycle in the middle of an election campaign is optically problematic and the election communications blackout period would hamper the MPC's ability to tee up and subsequently explain a June cut. Besides the implications for the Bank, we expect little diference to the aggregate fiscal outlook, whichever party wins, due to the limited fiscal space. However, in the event of a Labour victory, talk of a deeper relationship with the EU could provide a boost to both GDP and supply.

## We retain our above-target inflation forecast in Japan

Even in Japan, we remain of the view that inflation will prove more persistent than in the past, despite continued disinflation in the spot data. In April, nationwide CPI eased by 0.2pp to 2.5% y/y, while core and BoJ core fell more meaningfully, to 2.2% (-0.4pp) and 2.4% (-0.5pp), respectively. The deceleration was quite broad-based, with smaller contributions from components such as food other than perishables, culture/recreation services, education, utilities, and furniture/household goods.

However, as the historically high shunto wage hikes are implemented and gradually passed through to prices, this should put upward pressure on services inflation. At the same time, we see the rollback of energy subsidies, higher oil prices, and JPY depreciation as tailwinds for goods inflation. As a result, we maintain our core CPI forecast for inflation to remain above target at 2.8% in FY24 and at target in FY25. Our BoJ core projection also sees these more domestically driven price pressures stabilising at target.

## Thematic

## IT Hardware and Communications Equipment (Neu)

IT Hardware Week Ahead: Earnings Preview with Some Rating and Target Adjustments Our checks and VAR surveys continue to show a more stable, but challenging, IT spending environment. For most areas, we believe the on-prem recovery that was expected in 2024 is likely pushing to 2025. AI of course is the standout, though exposure to this theme within our group is limited. We think PC units have stabilized, but remain concerned on ASP and margins. Some companies are seeing strength in AI servers with higher ASP (and lower GM), but traditional servers have yet to recover from a weak 2023. Storage and Networking seem more mixed, but neither has seen an acceleration in growth yet. For DELL, we raise numbers on better AI servers as wins have exceeded our expectations. Because GM is so low, we see more limited impact on EPS, and believe over time that the lack of meaningful earnings and cash flow upside will cause the multiple to revert back toward historical ranges in the high-single-digits. For HPQ, we are changing our rating to Equal Weight from Underweight with no changes to estimates. We are using a higher multiple as we believe unit stabilization and some hope around AI PCs will support the shares at these levels. We still see some 2H risk, but see downside as more limited. On PSTG, the shares have meaningfully outperformed YTD (up 70% vs COMP up 10%), and we see the risk/reward as more balanced at these levels. While we like the share gains and move to software/services, we believe the AI impact and HDD replacement opportunity are fully baked into the current share price, and we change our rating to Equal Weight from Overweight Regarding NTAP, we remain Equal Weight. We are concerned that product GM may revert lower, but see stability on the software and services businesses. And on HPE, we see continued nearterm struggles in networking, and expect HPE to close the gap in AI servers over the next few quarters. The JNPR deal should be EPS accretive once closed.

## Single Stock

## Marex Group PLC (MRX; OW/Pos)

## Quality Business at an Attractive Price: Initiating at Overweight

We initiate coverage of Marex with an Overweight rating and \$25 price target. Marex fills a critical role as a clearing firm, agent, market maker and solutions provider for commodity producers / consumers and financial institutions that need to access exchanges around the world and manage risk. The industry itself is characterized with higher barriers to entry given technical and regulatory complexity, declining competitive intensity (increasing bank regulation, many FCMs exiting over the past \~20 years), and Marex's share remains low in what we see as a very large global TAM. Looking forward, the company has demonstrated an ability to grow throughout all market environments organically and inorganically, and we see ample room for continued growth from both product and geographic expansion, as well as M&A (which is not factored into our model), and we think shares are attractively valued at just \~7x our FY25 adj. EPS estimate of \$2.86.

## Single Stock

## Rubrik, Inc. (RBRK; OW/Pos)

Security Use Case Driving Share Gains and Bigger TAM; Initiate at OW

We initiate coverage of Rubrik with an OW rating and a \$41 price target for three reasons: (1) the market spends \~\$12B on backup software annually, and ransomware is making this more of a security decision as enterprises' last line of defense – along with this disruption, Rubrik is taking share from legacy vendors with its cyber resilience portfolio; (2) backup data is valuable real estate in our view, as Rubrik is able to run additional security scans (beyond core backup) like anomaly detection and sensitive data monitoring, which expands TAM, grows deal sizes, and supports the 126% NRR we saw last year; (3) putting these together, we estimate RBRK can grow Subscription ARR 20-25% annually over the next two years, with FCF break-even in FY26 – given RBRK's SaaS transition, break-even ARR Contribution Margin could be a better indicator of profitability, with EBIT margin happening later in our forecast period as the income statement catches up in the transition.
