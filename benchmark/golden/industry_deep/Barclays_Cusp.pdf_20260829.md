## Factor Findings: Rate Cuts and Elections

Venu Krishna, CFA, BCI, US | Japinder Chawla, CFA, BCI, US | Rex Feng, BCI, US | Madhuresh Rai, CFA, BCI, US | Young Chen, BCI, US

Excerpted from U.S. Equity Insights: Factor Findings: Rate Cuts and Elections, published on August 20, 2024

Our historical factor analysis of Fed rate cuts and US presidential elections indicates that the former was positive for Total Yield and the latter for Value and Small caps. Results run counter to our fundamental preference for Growth and Large cap, warranting a close eye on these upcoming catalysts.

We take a closer look at factor performance around Fed rate cuts and US presidential elections throughout history. Our analysis uses Barclays US long/short factors, which are equal weighted and sector capped. Our primary data set goes back to 1995, but we back-fill data to 1984 in order to capture a longer time frame (11 Fed rate cutting cycles, 10 US presidential elections).

"First" rate cuts were initially positive for Yield (dividends + buybacks) and negative for Volatility. Yield delivered modest but consistently positive returns over the t+3m and t+6m time frames, while Volatility was consistently negative over the same; this makes sense as Yield becomes more desirable as rates decline while Volatility's performance was influenced significantly by recessions. Small-cap returns lagged large caps in the 9m period post cuts before recovering. This aligns with our prior work identifying small caps as a late recession/early expansion plays.

US presidential elections have historically been a positive catalyst for Value and Small caps. Value and Small caps consistently generated strong returns over the 3, 6 and 12 months post-election days. Momentum returns are mostly negative in the very short term (t+1m and t+3m); we think this could be attributable to a rotation into Value-oriented names.

Results run counter to our fundamental views. We continue to believe that the fundamental profile of Growth and Large caps (sales & EPS growth, balance sheet strength, valuation) and easing rates argue for owning these factors over Value and Small caps. However, the historical performance of Value and Small caps especially around US presidential elections presents a clear risk to our views and we are keeping a close eye as these catalysts approach.

## FX Views

## The great American unwind, the Fed and the dollar

Themistoklis Fiotakis, Barclays, UK | Sheryl Dong, Barclays, UK

Excerpted from FX Views: The great American unwind, the Fed and the dollar, published on August 22, 2024

The dollar has dropped on two key narratives: Fed easing and the unwind of the large US asset overweight. We show the Fed has been the more potent driver. Looking ahead, there is space to reassess US terminal rates (and the dollar) lower, but neither is this our view nor is the risk-reward great from a positioning standpoint.

No great American unwind yet: The unusual mix of risk aversion in early August and a weaker dollar has given rise to a narrative of unwinding overweight positions in US cyclical assets. From a big picture perspective, US asset ownership (vs ROW) has ballooned, mostly on valuation gaps. But unless one assumes very structural views where the pillars of the global economy for the last 10 years start to reverse, we think the bar for a sustained flow-driven dollar depreciation trend is high.

Easing financial conditions in the driving seat: A better documented driver for the weak dollar, we think, is the Fed-driven easing in US financial conditions. Indeed, the dollar move has been highly correlated to shifts in real rate diferentials. These shifts, however, have already gone some way, and gaps are now historically unremarkable.

Move could extend but risk-reward is not great: If data continues to surprise negatively and the market reassesses its view on r\*, there is additional space for dollar depreciation. But positioning has already moved from max dollar long to max dollar short. And, our economic and rates views remain more geared towards ROW economic softness.

Decent Q2 earnings. The Q2 reporting season saw EPS growth improving sequentially in both Europe and US to 3% and 10%, respectively, well above consensus estimates. Full-year earnings momentum turned lower as results season began, although it has stabilised most recently. Fed speak at Jackson Hole will be key to fine tune policy expectations. Dovish price action across rates, equities and USD may see some travel & arrive. Dovish set-up. While DM yields overall have fallen quite dramatically in recent months on weaker data and cutting cycles beginning in Europe/UK, US yields have fallen even more. Yet, the direction of policy travel is what matters, in our view, and it looks pretty clear that easing is on its way. This should ultimately preserve the soft landing narrative.

## Europe

Calm after the summer storm

Søren Willemann, Barclays, UK

Excerpted from European Credit Alpha, published on August 23, 2024

CDS indices have fully retraced the summer weakness, while IG credit, and insurance hybrids, remains well of the tights. Though Main is tight to IG credit, the upcoming roll, cleaner investor positioning and a likely pick-up in supply could lead to Main staying tight to IG in the medium term.

After the sell-of in early August, markets have retraced a large portion of the move and have traded in a narrow range in the past five sessions. Surveying the spread changes since 31 July (Figure 11) is instructive: CDS indices have retraced fully, while €IG corp spreads are still 9bp wider.

Adjusting for the beta of each market, the sell-of has had a notable compression footprint, with higher-beta credit outperforming their historical beta. The most pragmatic reason for this is arguably that during a "summer-illiquid" sell-of, the easiest-to-sell assets were straight IG credit, so they moved the most. Another potential reason for the low-beta sell-of is that Bund yields dropped meaningfully, and with marginal demand seemingly yield-based, this made lowbeta credit more vulnerable. €IG yields are now back near the YTD lows. We will have to wait until September to get a sense for whether the low all-in yield yields will be a headwind to IG demand, and/or if it will lead to increased demand for higher-beta credit to meet earlier yield bogies.

The one major exception to the "high-beta outperformance" pattern is that of insurance hybrids, which have underperformed other parts of credit lately, see Figure 12 showing Inco T2 vs Bank T2 (RT1 vs AT1 shows a similar pattern.)

The other notable dynamic from Figure 11 is the outperformance of CDS vs cash. Even if this now leaves Main tight to €IG cash (7.5bp on a 3yr horizon), Main could remain tight to cash in the medium term due to a number of factors: Investor positioning is much cleaner in Main now, the upcoming roll on 20-Sep could lead to a reluctance from investors to buy protection, and IG supply could pick up meaningfully from here as well.

The earnings season for IG companies saw an incremental rise in the cautious tone around full year outlook from firms, and our equity strategists note that net guidance upgrades for the second quarter results in Europe were the weakest since 2020. Given that EPS growth for 2024 looks to be back loaded in Q3 and Q4 for Europe, estimates for coming quarters need to hold, in order for our and consensus estimates of mid-single digit EPS growth for the whole of 2024 to materialise. With persistent weakness in manufacturing PMIs, this could lead to more earnings disappointment later in 2024.

Bank earnings were generally strong, with our equity research team noting that profit before tax beat consensus by 10% in aggregate, helped by short-term NII upgrades and fees driving revenues higher. Our bank analysts argue that key credit metrics of European banks were also resilient in Q2 earnings. CET1 ratios and MDA bufers remained robust and a number of banks upgraded their asset quality outlook as loan loss provisions came in below their initial guidance for FY24 cost of risk.

Put together, this supports our continue preference for banks (and insurers) over non-financials.

## EM Credit: Hoping for a summer dull

• The market remains highly data dependent, looking for clues about the depth of any US slowdown. After a choppy summer market, with little data between Jackson Hole and the next job report (Sep 6), EM credit investors might finally get a short period of calmer markets.

• EM HC supply has been elevated across all regions, except Asia, taking some pressure of issuers in the last stretch of the year. EM HY supply has remained low, in line with the post-COVID supply regime.

## Policy easing amid political uncertainty

Excerpted from Global Economics Weekly, published on August 23, 2024

Initial messages from Jackson Hole confirm that progress on disinflation has paved the way for the Fed to join the global easing cycle. Meanwhile, data suggest a gradual easing, in our view, and political uncertainty could still complicate the policy outlook. Next week, the focus turns to US PCE and European inflation.

## Global easing cycle underway

"The direction of travel is clear"

The annual Jackson Hole Economics Symposium is a time for central bankers from the Fed, and around the world, to take a step back and reflect on the bigger picture. This year, Powell's comments recognised that that picture is much more favourable than at any time since the pandemic. While the Jackson Hole participants have been careful not to declare victory over inflation too emphatically in the oficial communication, the inflation scare that has been top of mind over the last few years is now largely in the rear-view mirror.

Accordingly, global monetary policy is still in the early stages of an easing cycle, even if the cycle is not fully synchronous across regions. For example, Latin American central banks were earlier to hike and have been early to cut while developed markets central banks have slightly trailed emerging central banks and policymakers in emerging Asia have been even slower. However, the broad global trend to cutting rates has been firmly in place for some time, and Powell confirmed that the US is joining this trend by saying "the direction of travel is clear."<sup>1</sup> There is also little uncertainty about the immediate next steps by the key central banks: barring an extreme shock, both the Fed and the ECB should cut rates 25bp in September.

## Central bankers remain noncommittal on pace

## FOMC speakers suggest gradual cuts, but willingness to do more

Instead, the main uncertainties are the pace and depth of the ongoing cycle. In turn, these depend on the data (trajectory) and the policymakers' reaction function. On the latter, Powell's comments at Jackson Hole suggested no major shift, pointing to a gradual easing cycle in the modal path. For example, he characterised the economy as still growing at a solid pace and said there is good reason to think the strong labour market will be maintained. At the same time, he was clear that the FOMC is prepared to ease more aggressively if downside risks to employment materialise. For instance, the comment saying that "we do not seek or welcome further cooling in labor market conditions" opens the door to a possible 50bp in September should the unemployment rate jump another 0.2pp in the next labour market report.

Comments from other FOMC participants have been consistent with Powell's remarks. Boston Fed President Collins said that "data will tell us what kind of pace makes sense,"<sup>2</sup> but a "gradual, methodical pace" would likely be appropriate as the Fed should "preserve that healthy labour market" but she is not seeing any "big red flags" in the economy. Similarly, Philadelphia Fed President Harker remarked that "a slow, methodical approach down is the right way to go."<sup>3</sup>

This comes on the heels of the July FOMC minutes, which signaled that the FOMC was ready to initiate cuts and turned more cautious on the labour market, even before the July employment report. However, the minutes did not provide any explicit guidance about the magnitude of a rate cut in September or any guidance beyond the next meeting. We interpret this as pointing to a gradual pace of 25bp increments as the baseline (recall that Powell leaned against 50bp cuts in the July press conference), even if the FOMC is prepared to react more aggressively should the data deteriorate unexpectedly.

## ECB to retain meeting-by-meeting communication style

In Europe, central bank communication, including the July minutes released this week, also points to continuing the monetary easing cycle at a gradual pace. However, the state of the debate at the ECB, at least in July, does appear somewhat diferent. While the Fed minutes showed focus increasingly shifting to preserving the soft landing, the ECB minutes suggest the Governing Council was still focused more on the risk of sticky inflation, even as it "acknowledged that the short-term outlook for growth had deteriorated." The fact that Europe is perceived to be experiencing an economic recovery rather than a slowdown, albeit a shallower one than hoped for, and that Europe's unemployment rate remains near all-time lows likely go some way to explaining this diference.

Chief Economist Lane's comments at Jackson Hole, scheduled for soon after we go to press, could reveal whether any such diferences remain today and ofer clues about the ECB's path forward. That said, it is likely that he, and other speakers, will remain noncommital about the path after September.

## Data call for gradual policy normalisation

## Employment revisions should be taken with a pinch of salt

With no commitment by the policymakers, it is thus up to the data to shape the easing cycle, and we think the latest prints also support gradual moves as the most-likely scenario. For example, this week, markets in the US focused on the BLS' preliminary estimate of the benchmark revision to nonfarm payroll employment, which indicated that the payrolls in the year ending March 2024 may have been overstated by 818k, or nearly 70k/month. However, we caution to take this with a grain of salt, for a few reasons. First, this week's numbers likely

substantially overstate the magnitude of the eventual revision because initial QCEW estimates — which are the basis of the revision — have in the past been systematically revised upward, reflecting biases related to the drop in initial response rates for establishments in the survey in recent years. Second, this week's estimates are likely not news to the FOMC. Third, downward revisions to payrolls in the year to March 2024 should not be interpreted as signalling further revisions to future data, eg, current payrolls.

Next week, we expect personal income and spending estimates to show the US consumer remaining robust in July, with real spending growing at 0.2% m/m. We also expect the second estimate to revise up Q2 GDP 0.1pp to 2.9% q/q. On the inflation front, our estimates point to core PCE at 0.17% m/m in July: soft enough to justify a cut but not so soft as to raise the risk of undershooting the inflation target. Markets will also watch the Nvidia earnings report on 28 August.

## Mixed signals in Europe

Meanwhile, in Europe, the incoming data are quite mixed, which makes it dificult to interpret them as a strong signal in either direction. On inflation, this week's final July HICP suggests a touch firmer trajectory as core goods inflation is normalising higher while services remain sticky, and we nudged up our core inflation forecasts. On the other hand, Q2 ECB negotiated wage growth indicator surprised to the downside, decelerating to 3.6% y/y from 4.7% y/y, below our tracking estimates (3.9-4.2% y/y), which seals the deal for the September cut, in our view. Next week, August flash HICP should bring a sizeable step down in headline inflation, to 2.2% y/y from 2.6% y/y, but we think this will be temporary and driven by energy, while core should move sideways.

On activity, August PMIs surprised to the upside as the composite reading accelerated 1pt to 51.2, driven by services. However, the subindices paint a more-nuanced picture, and the French Olympics may have been a one-of boost. Next week, we will follow the ifo and European Commission surveys and July unemployment.

## Our calls expect moderate cuts from central banks

In all, we retain our baseline call that the Fed will initiate a gradual cutting cycle in September, cutting 25bp at each of three remaining meetings this year before slowing the pace to 25bp a quarter next year through September, for a total of 150bp of cuts over the cycle. For the ECB, we pencil in two more 25bp cuts this year, in September and December, but our baseline sees it

skipping October. Next year, we forecast 75bp of cuts by the ECB, for a terminal rate of 2.50%. At the Bank of England, we think the MPC will skip September and cut again in November, by 25bp, before delivering additional 100bp of cuts in 2025.

In contrast, our EM central bank views — and the market pricing — are as diverse as those economies. For instance, in Latin America, we expect 400bp of additional cuts by the middle of next year in Colombia, 175bp in Mexico, and 125bp in Peru and Chile. In contrast, our forecast sees only one more 25bp cut in Brazil, while the market actually prices 140bp of hikes. Elsewhere, we expect somewhat more easing in some EEMEA economies (Turkey, Hungary, Russia, Czech Republic) but less in others (Poland, Israel, South Africa). We also expect smaller moves by certain Asian central banks (Philippines, Korea, India) and no cuts in Thailand or Malaysia.

## Politics add uncertainty

Range of possible implications of Japanese political leadership change for the BoJ Japan remains a key exception to the global easing cycle, instead normalising policy from the previously extremely accommodative setting. In addition, Japan stands out from other major developed market central banks in the direct importance of politics for monetary policy. Specifically, questions around the implications of elections came back to the forefront this week, with the announcement of PM Kishida that he will not run for re-election as president of the ruling LDP party in late September. As a result, there is a growing expectation that a general election will be advanced to this autumn.

The race for succession is currently unfolding, and it is unclear who will succeed Kishida. Given this uncertainty, we make a few observations. First, Kishida's approach of encouraging companies to raise wages is widely shared by the party and is unlikely to change regardless of the successor. Second, the political uncertainty lowers the probability of further BoJ rate hikes at the September or October meetings. Third, the implications for the BoJ's trajectory could be minor, but they range from hawkish to dovish. Presumptive candidates Ishiba, Kono, and Motegi are viewed as relatively hawkish on monetary policy, but have based that position on the need to correct excessive JPY weakness and may not be overly hawkish once in ofice. In contrast, the victory of Takaichi could create headwinds for further monetary normalisation.

Besides the politics, incoming data remain important for the BoJ as well. This week, headline nationwide CPI moved sideways at 2.8% y/y, but the BoJ core measure decelerated below 2% to 1.9% y/y (-0.3pp). However, looking ahead, we continue to expect upside in both goods (due to JPY weakness and higher transport and material costs) and services (due to the pass-through of higher wages to prices in the second half of the year). If the data evolve as we expect, we pencil in two more BoJ hikes, in January and July 2025. Next week, we will watch Tokyo CPI.

## US presidential election too close to call

In the US, the connection between politics and the Fed is less direct than in Japan, but diferent policy decisions likely taken by the two possible future administrations could have diferent implications for rates. However, the US presidential race remains far too close to call. Harris currently leads in the average of national polls by 1.5pp, but this is well within the margin of error, and some indicators have recently swung back in Trump's favour. After this week's Democratic National Convention, Harris may enjoy some bounce in the polls,<sup>4</sup> but this historically fades over a few weeks. Looking ahead, the presidential debate on 10 September should be another key test. In addition, we are watching the developments around Robert F. Kennedy Jr, who the news reports suggest has suspended his campaign and endorsed Trump.<sup>5</sup>

## German regional elections could put pressure on the national coalition

In Europe, Germany and France are likely to dominate the political headlines. In the former, there are regional elections in two eastern states (Saxony and Thuringia) on 1 September, and polls suggest a defeat for the three parties composing the federal government, putting additional pressure on the already weak coalition in Berlin. Meanwhile, in France, President Macron has started consulting political leaders to appoint a new prime minister, although there is still no precise timeline.
