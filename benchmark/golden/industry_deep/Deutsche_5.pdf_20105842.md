# Strategy 2024 APAC Outlook

Date 11 January 2024 Strategy Update

# Too bad, for too long, in too many places

We see APAC equities benefitting from: 1) Liquidity trends moving in the region's, and in equities', favour, and 2) Falling inflation and interest rates providing a lift to earnings, while GDP growth rates remain steady. While the index P/E is above average, this reflects a fairly poor run of profitability. The MSCI AxJ has a Bloomberg consensus EPS lift of 7.5% anticipated, so our estimate of a 15% rise in the index requires minimal rerating. Equities, however, across most markets are one standard deviation low in most and 2 s.d. low in parts, so there's a path to multiple upside. We note inside how many markets have had as many or more down years than up in the last two decades, and HK has had four down years in a row, so is testing the bounds of market history across APAC. HK/CH and SE Asian markets, after a tough decade, appear to have a similar setup to the mid-2000s, and we expect them to rally around 20% each this year. We think it might be tougher going in IN and TW, which we expect to consolidate at these levels, while we expect JP and KR to advance 5%-10%.

We believe the best way to position for this is to pivot away from leaders, towards laggard sectors and markets. We think the major risk for investors is growth not coming through as expected should the global economy slow. This is particularly risky for tech-related stocks in hot areas where capacity is being added rapidly and valuations are elevated.

We see SE Asian markets and especially HK as the most promising, due to attractive valuation levels, oversold markets, and being well positioned in areas we are bullish about, such as commodities and tourism to destinations beyond the popular areas that people prioritized traveling to post-lockdowns. We are more cautious on manufactured goods, due to the ramp up in supply underway as supply chains get duplicated in home countries and as governments in China, the US and elsewhere provide support to businesses to fund green and advanced industries.

# Too bad, for too long, in too many places

## We expect double-digit gains across HK/CH and SE Asian markets

We expect another up year as we see the 2020 sell-off as a bear-market low and we only sit 36% above that, and 23% above the October 2022 lows. We do not see this upcycle ending without taking out the 220 level high. The MXAP forward P/E is 15.5x, and 16.5x has been a cap since 2010 (other than when Covid ruined profitability in 2020). This raises two obvious questions: 1) Can earnings jump to drive the index, and 2) Can the index move to a valuation level that investors have not pushed it to since pre-GFC times? We think the latter could happen if mainland investors skew savings to equities, which we think will happen but is likely to play out over more than a year. So we forecast APAC stocks to rise 10-15% this year, as high single digit earnings growth combines with the forward P/E shifting back near the top of its range.

While that may seem punchy, note that profitability has contracted, as can be seen by comparing P/B, which is around 25% off prior peaks, to P/E, which is around 10% below prior peaks. This may reflect a cyclically lower level of ROE, or it may reflect a secular shift, as lower funding costs from the ZIRP policies pulled down ROIC expectations.

We think if earnings are to rise substantially, it will be because of a demand-supply squeeze in key areas, such as China consumer, a commodity price boom, or from friendshoring driving a capex boom. We find these themes though too uncertain to put much weight in, so prefer targeting value stocks.

Japan, Korea, Taiwan and India led the region in 2023, with 19%+ returns, but other markets generally went nowhere, and HK/CH had another double digit down year. HK indices have fallen 14%-23% in each of the last four years. It now resembles the set up for 2003, when after three down years HK, JP, PH, SG, and the US each had 26%+ rallies. The only example of more than three down years in a row we find was Thailand during the Asia Financial Crisis, led by its GDP contracting 10% across

1997 and 1998 - a proper depression - whereas HK & CH are currently only in a growth slowdown; and even in Thailand's case, the fifth year was down just 5%, as selling neared exhaustion levels after four years. Investors in HK seem exhausted, having lost interest in all but the narrowest pockets of the market. While the Philippines is also four years into a bear market, in its case the drawdown has been lighter. From a risk:return point of view, the HK market looks particularly attractive, while the more elevated levels of markets like India and Taiwan are unlikely to handle bad news as easily.

It's remarkable to us that across the 14 markets in the chart below, there have only been six cases of markets rising 20%+ two years in a row since the GFC 15 years ago. If we look at the 15 years before that, there were 15 cases - even though, or perhaps because, that period included the Asia Financial Crisis and the dot.com crash. Half the markets we cover have only generated a 1%-4% CAGR over the last 20 years - well below nominal GDP in those periods for all but Japan.

APAC is largely out of favour with investors due to macro concerns. Macro issues often distract investors from oversold situations, as seen in 2002, 2009, and 2020. Much of APAC has been in fairly tight trading ranges over the last 10+ years in USD terms, causing stock markets to lag nominal GDP. The HSCEI, for example, has lagged by a 10% CAGR over the last two decades - we assume this is a world first in a functioning economy. HK, CH, MY, PH and TH are even trading at or near the low end of their USD trading ranges, reflecting exhausted investors bases. However, the Indian market reminds investors of what could be, having risen sixfold from GFC lows in USD terms. It could have been called a "macro call" a decade ago, and underweighted...

The setup for 2024, in HK, and to a lesser degree CH and much of SE Asia, seems like a lower beta version of the mid-2000s, when a string of mainly down years from the Asia Financial Crisis and dot.com crash depressed stocks; whereas the recent issues have come from a China crisis and Covid.

We believe India should be avoided. In the last 10 years the Sensex has risen five times as fast as EPS. So while the macro backdrop is solid, we think the equity market could be fragile on any disappointments. Taiwan has been the SPX Asia, mirroring the US market. With elevated valuation, we see it as vulnerable and would avoid it. We are neutral on Japan, which has tended to follow solid years for the equity market with mild years. Our preference in North Asia would be Korean and Japanese non-tech.

## The catalysts

Investors have been burned by bonds, and property yields tend to be well below interest rates and often a fraction of equity earnings yields. Higher interest rates have tempted investors back into bonds and cash, but confidence in bonds will now be fragile. Liquidity has ended up in bank accounts, and we expect it to flow to equities in the medium term, after interest rates fall and time deposits expire. Our US team forecasts a 225bp series of cuts through 1Q25. We see a combination of falling inflation and interest rates and robust GDP growth creating a positive environment for risk assets in the region.

While this also reflects lower demand, the economics team expects GDP to be robust, helped by an export acceleration.

ASEAN and advanced Asia are forecast to see growth accelerate. The SE Asian equity markets have been sharply underperforming their own GDP growth in recent years. These markets are already benefitting from the China-plus-one strategy, so seem to have a potential catalyst.

## There's more to markets than macro

In 2009 global GDP rose 2.6% vs. 2.5% in 2008, yet AxJ indices were up 60%+ typically. If you wait for sharp macro upturns, you risk missing many of the strongest rallies. When the macro news stops getting worse, markets tend to rally, and we expect this will be the case in HK/CH especially. If ranking countries for macro risk, Russia would have been at the top of many lists a year ago, yet Russia's MOEX Index rose 44% in 2023, or 18% in USD terms - vs. a 12% return on the MSCI World ex US index. When we hear a market is a "macro trade", as we do now for HK, and much of SE Asia, that suggests exhaustion in this analyst's experience.

## Funds flows

The global property and bond secular bull markets appear to be over, which leaves investors with a choice between cash and equities - especially in closed markets like China. This we see as long-term bullish for equities, particularly in the markets that have been derated since the GFC. We believe investors burned by the bond market will initially move into cash, but as interest rates get lowered they will likely go to equities to chase yield, and will likely be cautious in reallocating to bonds and wealth management products.

A year ago we pointed out that the largest funds held three-times more Microsoft as they did the top 25 H Shares - which has been a good trade, so now the multiple is likely around four-times. This does reflect the concentration investors have in the Magnificent Seven, and investors in them may suffer as other branded stocks have, whether "Meme stocks", "BATs", "dot.coms", or the "Nifty fifty". Once a name for a group of stocks is created, leadership change tends to follow. Japan entered a 50-fold rise in the two decades from 1970, just as the Nifty Fifty entered its decline. After Japan's fall, SE Asia and dot.coms had a bull run, and after their crashes, debt did well. The US market accounts for 46% of the world equity index, which is the highest it has been in 20 years; there is potential for liquidity to get redistributed in Asia's favour, should the US struggle in 2024.

## Exhaustion, rotation & buybacks as potential drivers

Hong Kong stands out, in our view, for volume of exhausted investors. Given that so much of the market's capitalization is tied up by major shareholders and MPF funds, we expect ownership is now in strong hands, and company buybacks are rising, as high cash balances at corporates combine with lower expansion demand. In China we note that fund flows into the markets have been improving.

## China

As China cycles through a year of weak property spend, it enters 2024 with a lower base of comparison, making the ongoing property market crash less of an issue for GDP growth.

DB's economics team notes that a positive shock is required to break the vicious cycle of lower consumer spending, lower profitability, and lower forward expectations. The team expects policy to help achieve this, with an extra 1.5%-2% of GDP spent by the government to boost the economy, and so forecasts GDP growth of 4.7%. The team also sees China's real interest rate as too high, and expects a 45bp cut to the MLF rate by mid-year, and probably a 50bp cut in the reserve asset ratio.

China's Central Economic Work Conference points to a focus on: 1) Higher quality growth, 2) Better aligning macro and "non-economic policies" (e.g. less regulation that impacts the economy), 3) Active efforts to reduce real estate risks, with no mention of "housing is for living, not speculation" for the first time since 2016, and a more balanced tone on local government debt risks, 4) Excess capacity in some industries was added as an area to address (with renewable energy and autos expected to be targeted), and 5) A warm welcome to foreign investors and visitors.

## India

DB forecasts a better balanced economy in 2024, with growth edging down to 6%, but CPI dipping 1pp, with rates to track that drop through 1Q25. The economics team also sees a large drop in the fiscal deficit, to 4.5% of GDP, which will help reduce the cost of capital in the economy. Also adding downward pressure on the cost of capital will be India's inclusion in the JP Morgan Global Bond Index, which should bring USD25bn of net buying. The Modi government is widely expected to prevail at elections again, which would be positive for the ongoing reform agenda. So overall, it appears a good backdrop for investors - albeit seemingly priced in to equities.

## SE Asia

We like SEA for its combination of attractive valuation multiples and solid economies, helped in part by being beneficiaries of the China Plus One trend. We find the Philippines' equity prices attractive in absolute terms, and Malaysian equities cheap vs. their historical multiples. Our order of preference is PH>MY>SG>TH>ID.

Our economics team forecasts growth to be fairly stable, with Indonesia growing 5% and Malaysia 4.3%, while rates in Indonesia come down by 100bp through early 2025. The China plus one strategy is a driver for processed commodities in Indonesia and Malaysia. However, in Malaysia the ending of the subsidy programme is expected to keep inflation, and so interest rates flattish.

The Philippines had the most aggressive rate hike cycle in Asia, rising 450bp, and it has run a tighter fiscal policy. Our economics team estimates inflation will slow by 2pp to 4.1% in 2024, so expects a 150bp cut in rates. The team expects GDP growth of 5.4%.

Thailand's recovery has been disappointing, with Chinese tourism not rebounding to pre-Covid levels. Its efforts to stimulate are counterbalanced by concerns over fiscal sustainability. Lockdowns do seem to have reset travel demand lower, but we expect this to rebound through the medium term.

## South Korea and Taiwan

South Korea best highlights the trend of tech getting revalued upwards, and other sectors typically downward, with energy, consumer discretionary and financials all down 33% plus against 20Y averages of P/B, P/E and P/E1, while utility P/E and P/E1 are down 70%. South Korea and Taiwan are estimated to see their GDP accelerate from 1.3% and 1% to 2.1% and 3%, helped by an export pick up. The Taiwanese election will keep getting the headlines, but moving beyond that will probably be a positive to sentiment. The semiconductor-led growth we expect in Taiwan has been factored in to the TWSE, making it less attractive to us than South Korea, or most other APAC markets.

## Japan

We expect the Japanese market to advance mildly, by around 10%. This is based on valuations being above average but trending up, but a value gap emerging vs. many other key markets after 2023's rally. Our Japan economist forecasts base salaries to climb 2.5% and notes that unions have upped their ask from by 1.5pp. He sees this as likely as productivity has been rising more rapidly than wages in recent years, pushing the real wage gap to the widest in his time series that dates back to 1980. However, this is seen as not preventing inflation from trending down. It is also expected that the BOJ will exit NIRP and yield curve control in January.

Macroeconomic fluctuations often account for most of the risks associated with exposures to instruments that promise to pay fixed or variable interest rates. For an investor who is long fixed-rate instruments (thus receiving these cash flows), increases in interest rates naturally lift the discount factors applied to the expected cash flows and thus cause a loss. The longer the maturity of a certain cash flow and the higher the move in the discount factor, the higher will be the loss. Upside surprises in inflation, fiscal funding needs, and FX depreciation rates are among the most common adverse macroeconomic shocks to receivers. But counterparty exposure, issuer creditworthiness, client segmentation, regulation (including changes in assets holding limits for different types of investors), changes in tax policies, currency convertibility (which may constrain currency conversion, repatriation of profits and/or liquidation of positions), and settlement issues related to local clearing houses are also important risk factors. The sensitivity of fixed-income instruments to macroeconomic shocks may be mitigated by indexing the contracted cash flows to inflation, to FX depreciation, or to specified interest rates - these are common in emerging markets. The index fixings may - by construction - lag or mis-measure the actual move in the underlying variables they are intended to track. The choice of the proper fixing (or metric) is particularly important in swaps markets, where floating coupon rates (i.e., coupons indexed to a typically short-dated interest rate reference index) are exchanged for fixed coupons. Funding in a currency that differs from the currency in which coupons are denominated carries FX risk.
