# Fact and Figures: Gold -Breaking the rules… continue to be overweight gold stocks.

UBS forecasts higher highs and higher lows. We see 7 key reasons for staying overweight.

## The structural reasons:

1) Central bank diversification, amid geopolitical tensions and sanctions risks. For illustration purposes, if all central banks especially in emerging markets had a minimum of 10% of their reserves in gold (compared to the Fed and the Euro area who have 76% and 57% of their reserves in gold respectively), the increase in gold demand would be 75% of global supply. This is not UBS' base case, though we expect official sector diversification into gold to continue.

2) Diversification into gold with increased US fiscal risk and fallout from the China triple bubble. The US fiscal position increases both the credit risk of US government debt (Fitch downgraded US government debt in August) and in our opinion, the only practical way to stabilise US government debt to GDP and keep unemployment at low levels is to have real rates at significantly lower levels (which would hugely support gold). China has had a triple bubble (credit, real estate and excess investment) and is now suffering the fallout from this. The clear risk is that China might be forced to increase money supply aggressively. An estimate of gold ETF holdings and net speculative futures positions is around 0.4% of global AUM, according to Joni Teves, our US Precious Metals Strategist; we feel gold is an under owned hedge/store of value against these risks. We also believe that there is a clear risk that central banks subtly raise their inflation target, once inflation is back to target. 3) Dis-intermediation risk by crypto currencies is low (unlike 2020-2021).

## We see the macro headwinds becoming tailwinds:

4) A falling TIPS yield and dollar. Historically, the ‘normal’ macro drivers of gold are the TIPS yield (a proxy on real rates), the dollar and the price of financial risk as proxied by the relative performance of banks. We see the TIPS yield falling from here. UBS Precious Metals Strategy's gold model estimates that each 1% off the TIPS yield corresponds with a 9% increase in the gold price historically, though the beta has fallen to about 4% in the last couple of years. UBS sees the DXY slightly up by end of year, but down from current levels by end 25. It is possible that these 'normal' macro drivers could push gold up by another 10%.

## Other factors which are supportive

5) Gold is not yet back to its old highs in real terms (for that to happen, gold needs to rise 40%) and gold is inexpensive against other real assets such as equities.

6) Positioning. Our Head of US Equity Derivatives Research, Maxwell Grinacoff believes investor positioning is not fully reflecting the price action we have seen in gold recently and could be due for a catchup. 7) Gold stocks look inexpensive (on a 40% P/E discount to the market vs its norm). Gold stocks have abnormally lagged the gold price -normally gold stocks have a beta of 2X to the gold price. This implies gold miners could be up 45% from current prices. The UBS preferred gold miners globally are: Anglogold Ashanti, Barrick Gold, Centamin, Endeavour Mining, Evolution Mining. Maxwell Grinacoff also highlights the following trade: Sell GLD Jul 210/200 put spread vs. buy GLD Jul 240/260 1x1.5 call ratio, \~flat premium (GLD spot ref = 220.31; 23% net delta).

# Fact and Figures: Gold -Breaking the rules…continue to overweight gold stocks.

Over the past few weeks, we have had a lot of questions on gold. In our re-initiation we argued that investors should be overweight gold stocks and in our 10 surprises highlighted the risk of a sharp gold rally. The official house view carried out by Joni Teves on gold is higher highs and higher lows. We highlight the upside risks below and why in our opinion there are 5 key structural supports for the gold price.

## Structural supports

## Structural Support 1: Central bank diversification.

We can see that central bank buying of gold has accelerated since 2022.

Figure 1: Official sector gold buying
![](images/42a8a563c14c338c5ebf7c0b7c53e389a89b991f82dd5e2a45eb8cd590c33157.jpg)
Source: IMF, Metals Focus, World Gold Council, UBS.

We expect official sector gold buying to continue. There is a large divergence in holdings of gold reserves between major DM and EM central banks, with most of the latter having less than 10% of their reserves in gold. This is mainly because of legacy gold holdings from the Gold Standard among DM central banks. That said, even among EM central banks, comparisons of holdings across regional peers suggest that there is room for diversification to gold to continue, amid persistent geopolitical tensions and risks of sanctions.

Figure 2: Some major central banks have a low percentage of their reserves in gold
![](images/deb1cb41009a86d5cda9cf3ffca98213b9f0ab13ce8edee50fca0f8f1a81b481.jpg)
Source: UBS, Refinitiv Datastream

For illustration purposes, if all these central banks were to have a minimum of 10% of their reserves in gold (while the Euro area for example has 57% of their reserves in gold), they would need to buy roughly \$12Trn of gold, which is 75% of the current market cap of gold using the World Gold Council's estimates of above-ground stock. This is not the base case of UBS Precious Metals Strategy, though on the global equity strategy team we continue to see this as being a key risk.

## Why do central banks want to diversify away into gold?

The initial wave of gold reserves accumulation occurred alongside a rise in central banks' total foreign assets, which made diversification appealing. Given EM central banks historically had zero to very little allocations to gold, building gold reserves made sense. Exposure to the US dollar via holdings of the currency as well as US Treasury securities also meant that concerns about US dollar weakness additionally supported the move to diversify to gold. Recently, one issue is the rise in geopolitical tensions and risks of US sanctions (with the US freezing \$300bn of Russian reserves).

Moreover, global central banks may be taking a view that US Treasuries may now be structurally more dangerous (because of both the dollar risk and the level of the fiscal deficits), see the point 2 below and the second point in the macro headwinds become tailwinds section: A weaker Dollar.

## Structural Support 2: The increased unsustainability of US government deficit.

The problem is not only the level of government debt and deficits but the structural change within fiscal spending philosophy in areas such as the UK or US- namely politicians look as though they will continue to spend until something breaks (the exception to this is Europe). US government budget deficit is forecast by the CBO (Congressional Budget Office) at 5.3% of GDP in 2024 and close to that level every year up to 2032 and government debt to GDP is forecast to rise to 119% by 2029. Moreover, if the Trump tax cuts are made permanent, that would add c\$3.5trn to the deficit through 2033, according to the CBO (c13% of GDP).

If we take the worse case scenario in the US, then the amount of fiscal tightening required is 5% of GDP. When markets lose confidence usually they focus on the worst case scenario.

The problem is that this sort of tightening required to stabilise government debt to GDP in the US would cause a recession and that in turn would result in disinflation and much lower real bond yields. Hence, we believe that we need much lower real bond yields to get a sustainable equilibrium of stable government debt to GDP ratio and at the same time have a stable but low unemployment rate.

The alternative to this would be a government default (we believe there is near zero risk off that but it would of course be very bullish for gold).

Our view for a long time has been eventually we may end up in some form of bond funding crisis (if politicians do not at some point commit to credible fiscal orthodoxy) and this in turn would require a Plaza type accord by those central banks concerned to cap bond yields in the same way as the US did (in March 2020 or between 1942 and 1951) and that in turn would drive down real bond yields. Those regions with strong fiscal positions (ie parts of Europe, Switzerland, and Singapore etc) would undergo very large currency appreciation, which in turn would force them to cap currency strength by expanding their balance sheets.

As a warning shot, we have already seen US government downgraded to 'AA+' from 'AAA' by Fitch in August 23.

The mere threat of fiscal unsustainability leading to governments driving down real bond yields drives investors into safe real assets (highly rated equities or gold).

We on the global equity strategy team believe gold could become an asset class again in its own right yet gold holdings are very low. As a proxy of market positioning in gold, our precious metals team looks at gold holdings via ETFs and net speculative long positions in futures as a percentage of funds' AUM. The chart below shows that the percentage of gold to total AUM is below the highs during the COVID pandemic and well below the peak in late 2012/early 2013. Plotting this percentage against the Bank of England's gold holdings shows similar trends, the latter used in this case as an indicator for investor gold holdings. Although there is no data on actual investor gold allocations, our colleagues in Precious Metals Strategy think that given these data points generally move together, it supports the view that broader market positioning in gold is low.

## Structural Support 3: Increased China risks might require printing.

China has had a triple bubble (credit, real estate and overinvestment). Credit to GDP in the private sector is way above the US, the investment share of GDP is c20pp above the US (and higher than any other economy has ever had) and real estate appears to be still both overvalued and overbuilt (we wrote about this in our 10 surprises note). Real estate prices are falling and we are seeing deflation (PPI, export price deflators).

The fiscal position in China also looks quite alarming in our view. The IMF forecasts a structural budget of 7.6% of GDP with total private and public debt to GDP at 312 % compared to 247% in the US.

The risk is that China may find it increasingly hard to export its excess capacity (for geopolitical reasons) and thus China might have to resort to the printing press and given that it has net foreign assets, it is in a position to do this. Clearly the other main store of value in China (ie housing) is still falling in price, again encouraging gold demand.

## Structural Support 5: The risk that eventually central banks raise their inflation target.

We believe on the global equity strategy team that there is a risk that central banks, once inflation is back to target, may end up slightly altering their inflation targets (for example to target 2% inflation over a longer period of time) in order avoid the risk of QE in the future and accommodate higher cost push inflation (from decarbonisation and deglobalisation). Indeed , the ex Chief Economist of the IMF, Oliver Blanchard believes that the price mechanism works better at 3-4% inflation than 2%. There is precedentthe BoE has changed its inflation target twice (Gordon Brown changed the inflation target from 0-2.5% RPI to CPI of 2% in 2003 and George Osborne in 2012 allowed the central banks more than 2 years to get back to 2% inflation if inflation was a recession) and the Fed moved from a hard 2% inflation target to an average inflation target of 2% in 2020 and changed its employment mandate from full employment to ‘maximum employment.

There are some suggestions already that central banks maybe willing to go a bit ‘soft’ on inflation.

It is surprising that the Fed at the last FOMC was so dovish (with 9 FOMC members forecasting 3 rate cuts in 2024 up from 6) despite a 20bp rise in core inflation for 2023 and 70bp rise in growth.

## The macro headwinds become tailwinds.

Gold has in the past year ignored its normal macro drivers pointing to fundamental supports listed above.

The Global Equity Strategy's model suggests that the gold price is normally driven by 3 key variables: the price of financial risk (as proxied by the relative performance of banks or the price of banks credit default swaps) - the more investors are worried about the stability of the financial system, the more they buy gold; the real bond yield (as proxied by the TIPS yield) because clearly gold does not produce an income (hence the misperception that gold is an inflation hedge, it is a negative real bond yield hedge); and the dollar (because it is a dollar-denominated commodity and the vast majority of gold demand is non US).

We can see below that gold is now nearly 18% expensive on our model that incorporates these factors. We put this performance of gold down to the structural factors listed above (That said, from a narrative perspective, gold is likely still benefitting from consensus expectations that the Fed's next move is to cut rates and real rates are likely to fall).

## A fall in the TIPS yield.

Without going into laborious detail, it looks as though there has been a c5-7% boost to GDP growth since the start of 2023 from excess savings (now largely run down and are now just 2.8% of GDP), fiscal easing (which on IMF numbers added 2% to GDP in 2023) and immigration (which rose to 3.3m from a pre pandemic 0.4m). Meanwhile, there has been a little change in the household or corporate debt service ratio because of the maturity of debt. Thus, we are left with monetary conditions which look to be too tight given the output gap. We also have US wage growth- on the most reliable data- back to pre-pandemic levels (and wages are the main driver of structural inflation), hence we think as GDP growth falls below trend the Fed could cut more than the market expects.

## 4) Positioning is high on some but not all measures

If we truly find ourselves in a regime shift in gold, this rally could have legs (or at least downside support) given the ‘implicit put’ in the form of Central Bank purchases. We find that the current rally barely registers compared to prior notable events, e.g. Dotcom bubble, GFC, oil embargo, etc. (Figure 31). While we could still be in the early innings of a rally, we likely need to see signs of investor exuberance behind the scenes to warrant a sustained leg higher.

We previously highlighted GDX upside as a catch-up trade to gold. This has since played out amidst signs of exuberance in GDX call optionality, and now the focus shifts back to the commodity itself in options land. If the implicit put thesis holds true, investors may consider selling GLD put spreads to fund upside call ratios. This position establishes both limited downside and upside biases, but with the upside breakeven well into undiscovered territory (upside breakeven ≈ +36%; downside breakeven ≈ -5%; max downside loss ≈ -9%; Figure 32).
