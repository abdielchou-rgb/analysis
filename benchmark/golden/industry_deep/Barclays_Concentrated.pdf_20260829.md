Navigating a concentrated equity landscape with only incremental broadening poses a unique challenge.  Yet, historically low correlation favors stock pickers. A concentrated market poses a unique challenge. Inter-sector and pairwise stock correlations remain at historic lows suggesting it is fertile ground for active management. Yet, market returns, earnings upside, and institutional investor exposure remain highly concentrated in Big Tech. Even earnings revision trends show a significant bifurcation favoring Big Tech. At the same time, progress on the much anticipated broadening has only been incremental, though we see some green shoots.

## So how does one navigate a concentrated market that also remains a stock-pickers

market? In our recent note ‘Heavyweight Champions’ we highlighted areas of fundamental strength underpinning Big Tech’s outperformance and their relative scarcity elsewhere in the US equity market. However, scarcity does not mean non-existent. To ‘broaden’ sources of returns we think seeking elements of the fundamental characteristics of Big Tech outside in the rest of SPX (and beyond) can be a good starting point to deal with a concentrated market.

We provide an optimized basket of 50 stocks outside of Big Tech to diversify sources of returns. These stocks are likely to provide diversification benefits along with sound fundamentals based on profitability, balance sheet strength, cash conversion, and growthadjusted valuation characteristics that closely resemble those of Big Tech.

## Japan Economics Focus

## BoJ dual guidance: Inflationary pressures to defy near-term slowdown Naohiko Baba, BSJL, Japan | Ryuichiro Hashimoto, BSJL, Japan

Excerpted from Japan Economics Focus: BoJ dual guidance: Inflationary pressures to defy nearterm slowdown, published on May 2, 2024

The BoJ’s recent communications around monetary policy indicate a sort of dual guidance. The first guidance is data dependent, based on the virtuous cycle between wages and inflation emphasized by the BoJ to date, while the second, we note, is outlook based, ie, an assessment of future inflationary pressures from current JPY depreciation.

In relation to the first, the BoJ has stressed the spread of wage hikes to SMEs and its passthrough to service inflation. The former is becoming evident from the results of the annual wage negotiations (aka “shunto”). In relation to the latter, our analysis finds the pass-through of wage hikes to services inflation is nonlinear depending on the size of wage hikes. Scheduled pay (on a common-establishments basis) has recently been stable at around 2%, reflecting the large shunto wage hikes in FY23. With even larger wage hikes in FY24, services inflation is likely to hold firm even with the decline in earlier cost-push factors.

As for the BoJ’s second guidance, we found that the inflation response to a 10pp JPY depreciation shock was statistically insignificant in services, but significant in goods, reaching 0.7pp at its peak. Hence, we expect the rapid JPY depreciation since the start of the year to once again boost goods inflation, presenting a non-negligible risk to the BoJ’s scenario of an improvement in real wages and thus consumption.

We have updated our inflation outlook with the BoJ’s dual guidance in mind. For now, we forecast a continued deceleration of the inflation driven by earlier cost-push factors (eg, JPY depreciation and the rise in oil and other commodity prices) and post-COVID economic reopening efects. However, we expect this to be eventually ofset by the pass-through of wage hikes to service prices, the upside to goods prices from renewed JPY depreciation, the rise in oil prices and the phase-out of energy subsidies. As a result, we expect BoJ core inflation to slow toward summer, but to solidify and finally become anchored around 2% y/y. In this context, we have revised up our FY24 forecasts by 0.1pp each to 2.8% for the core CPI and to 2.2% for the BoJ core CPI.

We maintain our base case for the BoJ to hike in July, then again next April. We believe it will be compelled to load “live ammunition” as early as July based on its second, “outlook-based” guidance. At the same time, since the details around SME wage-hike and price-setting behavior will be apparent by the July MPM, it should be able to confirm some progress in relation to the first guidance as well. In this fashion, we expect the BoJ to be able to develop communications based on both sides of its dual guidance.

On the political front, the ruling LDP efectively lost all three by-elections for the lower house held on 28 April, making it challenging for PM Kishida to dissolve the lower house and call a general election toward this summer and more likely to do so after the party’s presidential election in September. If so, we note that the BoJ could be exposed to political risk not only at its September MPM, but also at its October MPM, potentially leaving it unable to act until the year-end or later, if it fails to pull the trigger by July.

Value's outperformance over Growth from March to mid-April has reversed course over the last two weeks and we expect this to sustain. ## Stasis

Bradley Rogof, BCI, US | Dominique Toublan, BCI, US

Excerpted from US Credit Alpha, published on May 3, 2024.

The Fed stayed put with a dovish tilt, despite problematic signs from recent economic data and rising concerns about the consumer. Spreads remain range-bound amid constructive technicals and fairly strong Q1 corporate earnings.

The Fed is staying put, but still leans dovish. The path forward for rates looks less certain now than at the previous FOMC meeting (see May FOMC: Conspicuous omission). Chair Powell acknowledged the recent inflation data and the "lack of further progress" toward the 2% target. Still, he emphasized that the Fed's next move is unlikely to be a hike. Furthermore, he highlighted that the Fed's policy is restrictive and weighing on demand, pointing to softness in the labor market in particular. Our economics team continues to expect one 25bp cut in 2024.

But the latest inflation-related data remains problematic. First, ISM Manufacturing prices paid were higher than expected (60.9 vs. 55.4) and continued to increase from previous months (45.2 at YE23). This print was the highest since mid-2022. Second, the Employment Cost Index for civilian workers rose 1.2% q/q in 1Q, accelerating from 0.9% in the prior quarter. Finally, the preliminary unit labor cost report for 1Q24 was hot, at 4.7% q/q vs. 4.0% expected and 0.0% in 4Q23.

Still, there are nascent signs of consumer softness at the micro and macro levels. First, some consumer companies are indicating softness in more than the lower end of the income spectrum. Earnings calls from McDonald’s, Sysco, Starbucks, Restaurant Brands and Brinker all point to this softness. For example, McDonald's leadership noted that consumers are increasingly shifting their food spend to grocery stores instead of restaurants, given a drawdown in savings. See reports from our analysts here, here, and here.

Second, at the macro level, the Conference Board survey indicated that the consumer is close to the least optimistic about the future in the past 10 years. Furthermore, the JOLTS figures missed expectations and were the lowest since March 2021. This brought down the job openings/ unemployment ratio to 1.32x vs. 1.4x at YE23 and 1.20x on average just before COVID. Finally, quits are falling to below pre-COVID levels. Historically, this has meant lower pressure on wages (Figure 30).

1Q24 earnings have been solid overall. Our Earnings Scorecard for the first couple of weeks of earnings is the best we have seen since we started it 18 months ago. This scorecard is based on our analysts' views of companies' results and guidance. Similarly, 79% of S&P 500 companies have beat expectations on EPS, somewhat higher than the 76% average since the Global Financial Crisis (75% of S&P 500 companies have reported so far). Market structure: US bonds to start settling T+1 at the end of the month. On May 28, the SEC will shorten the settlement from T+2 to T+1 for all transactions in US cash equities, corporate debt and unit trusts (see SEC press release here). This is not the first time that the settlement cycle has been shortened. The T+3 to T+2 transition in 2017 resulted in lower transaction costs in credit, mostly for older bonds and in periods of elevated volatility (see T+1 Settlement: Strengths and Shortcomings). At the same time, new rules mean new logistical challenges, especially from a global perspective. However, we do not expect a significant market disruption, as all participants have had time to get ready for the transition. We also discuss longer-term market structure topics in our 2024 Equity Gilt Study. In particular for credit, we focus on The equitification of credit in the US and credit market transparency in Europe.

## Economic Outlook

## Powell + payroll = perfect

Christian Keller, Barclays, UK | Mark Cus Babic, Barclays, UK | Akash Utsav, Barclays, UK

Excerpted from Global Economics Weekly, published on May 3, 2024

A Fed that downplayed the risk of rate hikes, coupled with softer labour market data, was a goldilocks combination for financial markets, which could last at least until the next US inflation print. This should help central banks globally, including the BoE next week, and also the ECB, as Europe's growth recovery continues.

## Powell and payroll combine for soft-landing promise

Fed leans against perceived rate hike risk ...

The FOMC meeting this week evolved broadly as expected, albeit with a dovish tilt. The Fed kept rates unchanged, and the statement recognised the lack of progress on inflation, but saw little change otherwise, including on the guidance for rates. On QT, the FOMC announced that it would slow the reduction of Treasury holdings, starting in June, with the monthly redemption cap falling to \$25bn from \$60bn.

In addition, Chair Powell's communication was decidedly mixed, aimed at sustaining but not further tightening financial conditions. On the one hand, he strongly signaled that rates would be maintained higher for longer, for example by omitting the reference to rate cuts being likely "at some point this year." On the other, he insisted that policy is restrictive, pushed back against rate hikes ("I'd say it's unlikely [that the next move will be a hike]"), and maintained a benign assessment of the inflation trajectory, albeit with less confidence (Q: "[A]re there any signs /.../ looking at the reports from January or February or March, that suggested something more worrying than just expected bumpiness?" A: "Not really"). In efect, this mix of communication upheld recent market pricing, which lowered the probability of near-term rate cuts, but weighed on the probability of rate hikes.

It also reinforced our baseline that the Fed will deliver just one 25bp cut this year, at the earliest in September. However, a broad range of outcomes remains possible. The market prices July as live. We are skeptical, as we do not think the full Q2 data on inflation and employment will do enough to give the FOMC suficient confidence that inflation is moving sustainably toward 2%. At the same time, if core PCE inflation continues to print above 0.3% after May, alongside broad resilience, we suspect that discussions of rate hikes will intensify. We think the bar for a hike is elevated, and speculation about hikes will itself tighten financial conditions and mitigate the need to implement them, but we do not rule out the possibility that the Fed hikes again.

## ...while payrolls keep cuts on the horizon

This makes the incoming data all the more crucial, with the payrolls late in the week keeping our call for a September cut on track, as employment growth slowed to 175k, below consensus expectations for 240k. This is a notable — and welcome — deceleration from the c.270k average in the preceding three months, but not one that is so sharp as to stoke fears of a hard landing or call for imminent cuts. The rest of the labour market report was similar, with the unemployment rate inching up 0.1pp, to 3.9%, and average hourly earnings easing to 0.2% m/m (3.9% y/y).

Moreover, the jobs report comes on the heels of the JOLTS estimates earlier in the week, which showed fewer job openings than expected, a vacancies-to-unemployed ratio nearly back to the pre-pandemic norm, and less job market churn, suggesting that conditions remain in place to slow wage pressures. This could increase confidence that pay growth will not continue to run above the levels consistent with the inflation target, despite this week's employment cost index for Q1 accelerating to 1.2% q/q.

Also pointing to an orderly deceleration in activity, services and manufacturing ISM eased this week, with the former falling below 50 for the first time since 2022. In the services sector, this was accompanied by a softer reading for employment. However, sharply rising ISM price gauges temper some of the optimism prompted by otherwise generally benign data this week.

Next week, we will follow the Q1 SLOOS survey, which should evolve in line with recent trends showing retreating net tightening of lending conditions, and May flash UofMich survey.

## Shades of caution by other central banks

## BoE to soften communications, point to impending cuts

In contrast to the Fed, we think the BoE will next week take a step closer to beginning an easing cycle. The vote split is likely to remain 8-1 in favour of a hold for now, but we expect a change in the messaging to indicate that the MPC is inclined to begin cutting the Bank Rate, conditional on the data evolving in line with the updated forecast. This will be based on key indicators likely to decelerate in the coming prints and an updated forecast, which could show inflation below target in the medium term after penciling in the efect of yield curve tightening since February. The softening in communication could come via a conditional statement similar to the ECB's, indicating that if incoming data increase the MPC's confidence in inflation converging to target, it would be appropriate to scale back monetary policy restriction. That said, the shift is likely to be incremental and balanced by continued caution, in order to retain optionality, avoid markets getting carried away, and ensure buy-in from the more hawkish members.

Having set the stage at next week's meeting, we expect the BoE to begin to cut rates in June, given that, at the time of that meeting, headline inflation will be below target, core will be only fractionally above 3% and services inflation will have fallen more than 1pp, to below 5%. In addition, we think the BoE will acknowledge as soon as next week that policy is reasonably far from neutral, in line with the previously published estimates.

On the balance sheet, despite speculation that the recent increased usage of the BoE’s short tem repo facility might lead to an impending pause to active gilt sales, we expect the BoE to continue QT beyond September at a pace of £100bn over 12 months (Sep-Sep), of which about £13bn will be active sales.

## Japan defends the JPY ahead of a likely July BoJ hike

While the BoE is set to diverge from the Fed, Japan is contending with the consequences of deviating too much. After the JPY hit as low as 160 against the USD on Monday, Bloomberg analysis shows<sup>1</sup> the Japanese authorities likely intervened in the FX markets twice this week, spending a combined c.\$60bn to drive the JPY to 153 by the end of the week. This may be a sharp reversal on an intra-week basis; however, USDJPY remains where it was only three weeks ago. In the bigger scheme of events, halting the JPY's slide is likely to be an uphill battle, as the 10y real rate diferential is by far the largest it has been since at least 2007.

We still expect the BoJ to hike again in July, based on spot data regarding wage hikes among SMEs and an outlook for firmer inflation due to FX depreciation, the pass-through of wage hikes to services prices, and other factors. However, political developments may well constrain the BoJ's actions after July. The ruling LDP efectively losing all three by-elections on 28 April makes it challenging for PM Kishida to call a general election this summer and more likely for him to do so after the party's presidential election in September. In that case, the BoJ could potentially be unable to act not only at its September MPM, but also in October.

## Heightened caution by non-core central banks

Meanwhile, many smaller central banks are turning increasingly cautious. This week, Norges kept rates unchanged, but said tight policy may need to be maintained "for somewhat longer than previously envisaged."<sup>2</sup> Meanwhile, Riksbank's first cut remains on a knife's edge, with the market pricing over 50% probability of it coming at next week's meeting, but with the risk of its being delayed to June, given the recent krona weakness and the fact that the ECB is likely to begin cutting in June. The RBA also meets next week and is expected to remain on hold at least until September, but with last week's upside surprise in inflation raising the likelihood of a later cut.

In emerging markets, Latin American central banks continue with rate cuts, but in a more measured way. This week, Banrep (Colombia) cut 50bp, as expected, but did not accelerate the easing to 75bp, as some board members had signaled. Similarly, next week, we expect the BCB (Brazil) to cut 50bp, but given the repricing in Fed expectations and other factors, its members have recently weakened their guidance for such a cut, leaving the door open to a smaller, 25bp move. we expect the central bank of Peru to cut 25bp next week, with the risk of a hold. In contrast, we think Banxico (Mexico) will stand pat and keep a cautious tone. Outside of LatAm, external (Fed and commodity prices) and domestic influences have also pressured EEMEA central banks to opt for a more cautious stance. This week, the CNB (Czechia) cut 50bp, as expected, but we see the risk of an earlier end to the easing cycle, while next week, the central bank of Poland will likely stay on hold and maintain its hawkish rhetoric. In EM Asia, we see Bank Negara Malaysia keeping rates unchanged next week and for the rest of this year and next.

## Diverging outlooks for Europe and China

Besides the timing and magnitude of global monetary easing cycles, US growth outperformance remains a key theme in 2024. However, regionally, incoming data suggest this gap may be narrowing vis-à-vis Europe, as the euro area gradually wakes up after nearly two years of little growth, while China's activity continues to lag.

## Europe's recovery is materialising...

In Europe, the Q1 GDP data this week surprised sharply to the upside, with the euro area expanding 0.3% q/q (from -0.1% q/q in Q4). The positive news was broad geographically, as the biggest four economies all grew faster than expected, and the expenditure details available for France and Spain point to a recovery in domestic demand. While not all indicators point in the same direction, eg, domestic demand may have been less strong in Germany and Italy and the European Commission surveys were soft, the main takeaway remains that Europe's recovery from a long period of stagnation is underway. In the labour market, the March unemployment rate held steady at the lowest level on record, 6.5%. Next week, we will watch March retail sales and German industrial data.

At the same time, disinflation is proceeding roughly as expected. This week's April HICP showed headline inflation steady at 2.4% y/y, but core inflation fell 28bp, rounding to 2.7% y/y, compared with 2.9% y/y in March, driven by a notable step-down in services inflation. Moreover, sequentially, we estimate that core inflation is now running below 2.5% m/m SAAR 3mma. Thus, the April release reinforces our baseline view of a gradual services and core disinflation over the course of this year.

On balance, this leaves our baseline call for the ECB, which sees back-to-back cuts from June to September, unchanged. Chief Economist Lane's confirmation this week<sup>3</sup>  that interpretation of the incoming data at a non-projection meeting can boost confidence, with "implications for a rate decision at a non-projection meeting," afirms that July is on the table. However, growth accelerating faster than in the ECB's projections, the unemployment rate remaining low, and inflation not decelerating any quicker than expected mean a number of Governing Council members may see less urgency to implement sequential cuts.

## ...while China remains reliant on more policy support

In contrast, China's activity data remain soft, pointing to weaker domestic demand, which implies growth momentum is likely to fade quickly after the outsized gain in Q1. This week, the April NBS composite PMI retreated 1pt, to 51.7, the first deceleration this year, amid a sharp drop in services PMI and softer manufacturing, while the construction PMI moved broadly sideways. Such weaker PMI data are also consistent with April high-frequency indicators, ranging from contracting auto sales, cooling box ofice revenues, negative news flow about pay cuts and layofs, and moderating domestic flight activity to a deep contraction in property sales.

Against this backdrop of poor data, the Politburo meeting provided a boost to sentiment, as it signaled more policy support. First, it announced that the Third Plenum would take place in July, which might instill confidence in investors who saw the long delay as a sign of a lack of consensus and commitment. In addition, the meeting signaled a number of new support measures, including auto/consumer goods trade-in, equipment upgrades, a housing inventory reduction, fiscal implementation, and monetary easing. However, whether such statements translate into concrete policies remains to be seen and will be critical to lifting onshore sentiment and driving a sustained recovery.

Next week, we will look to the April trade data for confirmation of the better external dynamics seen in the improving Caixin manufacturing PMI and the April credit data.

A quarter that delivered a resounding beat to consensus and our estimates on solid revenue growth was overshadowed by the news UMBF would acquire HTLF (not covered) in an all-stock deal supported by a capital raise. Op EPS of \$2.22 was a 42c consensus beat and +40c to us driven by better spread income (+33c vs our est), fee income (+10c) and lower provision (+6c) ofset by higher expenses (-7). The acquisition is expected to close in 1Q25, and will add \$16+B to assets, pushing UMBF over \$50B. Due to purchase accounting math, the HTLF deal hits capital upfront, but will be highly accretive over 5 years after close. We view our upgrade to OW as a combination of three factors which support our positive view: 1) UMBF’s standalone business had a great quarter with much higher-than-expected NII and NIM, while DDAs were up significantly q/q, which was largely an outlier for our coverage this quarter; 2) the HTLF acquisition adds potential upside to our new FY26 EPS estimate (+7% from prior) when accounting for the accretion from loan and rate marks as well cost savings from the deal which are expected to be fully realized in FY26; and 3) there is likely upside to estimates from revenue synergies as UMBF expands fee income lines to the new footprint. As a result of the moving parts from earnings, the deal and the capital raise, our EPS estimates are adjusted for FY24/ FY25/FY26 to \$8.69/\$7.88/\$10.58 from \$7.31/\$8.04/\$9.91. We upgrade to OW and raise our PT to \$95 from \$91.
