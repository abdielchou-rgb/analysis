## US Economic Perspectives US Inflation Monthly: Some bumps ahead

## Inflation has continued to slow, but core disinflation to pause for a while

The July CPI, and PCE prices translation for July, continue to show progress consistent with lower inflation: We project a 15bps increase in core PCE prices in July, which would see 12-month core PCE inflation at 2.64% — roughly the same as last month and slightly below the FOMC's current year-end projection of 2.8%. Headline PCE prices are also projected to have increased 15bps in July with the 12- month change holding at 2.5%.

Preliminary August indicators also look consistent with inflation moderation: We forecast the headline CPI to increase 15bps in August (seasonally-adjusted; 6bp not-seasonally-adjusted) with core CPI prices rising 20bps.12-month headline CPI inflation would drop to 2.5% (from 2.9% currently) while core CPI inflation would barely round down to 3.1% (from 3.2% currently). We will review these projections as additional data becomes available over the next couple of weeks.

That said, we do not expect further downward movement in core inflation through the end of the year and expect monthly core price increases to pick up through October driven by three things: 1. higher used vehicle prices; 2. the effect of seasonal factors that have been overly-influenced by the pandemic; and 3. the pass-through of increases in international freight costs since December. We currently project monthly core CPI increases to peak in October somewhere between 30bps and 35bps — higher than the past few months, but below the pace of the first few months of this year.

 A detailed forecast spreadsheet is available on our website.

## Few changes to forecast, but a little higher in coming months

The biggest concern coming out of the July CPI was a pickup in housing rents. We think the pickup in OER from the smallest increase in nearly 3 years in June to the second smallest increase in July is little to worry about, but we expect OER to run mostly in the range of 30bps to 35bps per month for the remainder of the year. The pickup in tenants' rent in July is likely largely a one-off driven by a bounce back from prior low readings and overly sensitive seasonal factors.

 The biggest change to our forecast is a boost to used car prices over the September to December period, driven by somewhat stronger wholesale used vehicle prices and a re-evaluation of likely not-seasonally-adjusted changes.

 We also expect both higher freight costs and a bounce back in hospital costs will be upward pushes compared to the July CPI.

## Core PCE inflation forecast at 2.7% for 2024; disinflation resuming in Q1

 We have edged up our projection of core PCE inflation this year (Q4/Q4) to 2.7%. Our inflation forecast continues to be toward the lower end of current measures of consensus inflation expectations, though only slightly. However, our headline CPI projection for inflation a year from now is roughly 60bp above recent inflation swaps fixings.

While we expect disinflation to pause for the remainder of this year, we expect it to resume early next year as the strong increases from Q1 this year are replaced by more moderate increases. By the time the April core PCE price data is released in late May we expect 12-month core PCE inflation will be within a ¼pp of the FOMC's 2.0% inflation target. We continue to expect that moderating demand, easing supply conditions, slowing rents, and a softening labor market will hold down monthly price changes over much of the next year.

## Key inflation observations

The 17bp July core CPI increase was only 1bp below our projection in last month's US Inflation Monthly and 2bp below our projection just prior to the release. As a result we have made relatively few changes to our US inflation projection this month.

Key takeaways from the July CPI report and inflation concerns that have arisen since last month's US Inflation Monthly include:

## 1. Housing rents are unlikely to return above 40bp

For owners' equivalent rent (OER), a jump up from a 28bp in June to a 36bp in July still left the July OER increase the second smallest in nearly 3 years and 6bp below the next smallest increase going back to January 2022. (Figure 1) OER increases have clearly stepped down in the past two months. That said, our models do not expect much further slowing over the remainder of this year and perhaps even a slight uptick. (Figure 2) Our models, which were surprised on the downside June by 6bp and then on the upside in July by 7bp, suggest OER increases ranging between the high-20bps and high-30bps through early next year — a range that is above the 27bp average monthly OER pace from 2017 to 2019.

The 49bp July increase in tenants' rent was the largest since May of last year, and, at first glance, is significantly more concerning than the OER jump. However, we think the large increase in tenants' rent in July will mostly end up being a one-off that was driven by a combination of a bounce back from a weak January increase (the last time the July units were observed) and seasonal adjustment factors that have been swung around by the pandemic.

## 2. Used cars are likely to be strong later this year

The biggest change to our forecast this month is CPI used vehicle prices over the final four months of the year. Stronger wholesale prices, along with a closer look at the seasonal factors under the new methodology introduced in January, suggest a larger rise in CPI used vehicle prices than we had previously pencilled in. We now project that used vehicle prices will add roughly 3bp per month to the core CPI increase from September to December. By comparison they contributed an average of -3bp to core CPI monthly changes from January to July (and we expect they will still be a -2bp contribution in August).

Used cars will probably swing from subtracting 3bp per month from the core CPI over January-July to adding 3bp per month over September-December

## 3. Non-transportation core goods surprising weak in July

While we still think core non-transportation goods prices will rise, on balance, in the remainder of this year, its downward movement in two of the past three months leaves us less convinced than we were prior to the July CPI.

## 4. Not-seasonally-adjusted core CPI changes show no slowing in disinflation

In 20 of the past 22 months the not-seasonally-adjusted core CPI change has been below the change 12 months prior. Over those 22 months the average difference has been 15bp. In the past 3 CPI reports the NSA core CPI change has been 19bp, 15bp, and 9bp below the change 12 months prior. Currently there is little sign that the disinflation is slowing or that the "last mile" will be the hardest.

There is currently little sign that the "last mile" will be the hardest part of the disinflation...

That said, we forecast a notable break from that 22-month downtrend. In our forecast the not-seasonally-adjusted core CPI increases over the remainder of this year roughly match their pace from 12 months prior, which leads to a pause in the disinflation until early next year. A downside risk is that the trend of lower-than-last-year core CPI prints continues.

## Upcoming CPI prints

We forecast a 15bp increase in the headline CPI in August (seasonally-adjusted; +6bp not-seasonally-adjusted) with 12-month inflation dropping solidly to 2.5% (2.51%) from 2.9% (2.89%) currently. For comparison, 2.5% would be toward the upper end of the 1.6% to 2.9% range seen in the few years prior to the pandemic.

CPI inflation in now within the range seen in the few years prior to the pandemic

For the core CPI we forecast a 20bp increase in August with 12-month core CPI inflation edging down from 3.2% (3.17%) to 3.1% (3.14%). A 20bp core CPI increase would be larger than that in any of the past three months, but would still be smaller than in 31 of the 32 months prior to those past three. Compared to the past couple of months we expect less pushdown from airfares and used vehicle prices in August. We will refine this projection as additional data becomes available over the next couple of weeks.

We currently forecast a core CPI increase of 0.20% in August.

Looking further ahead than the August print, we forecast seasonally-adjusted core CPI increases will be notably higher in September and October before easing somewhat in November and December. In general the stronger prints later this year are a result of higher projected goods prices, particularly, but not only, for used vehicles. Additionally, the October core CPI increase should see about a 6bp push up from seasonal factors that we think have been overly-sensitive to the pandemic. Nonetheless, except for October, increases are projected to remain below the pace that prevailed in the first four months of this year. (Figure 29)

## Drivers of consumer inflation

## Inflation expectations

Long-run inflation expectations were unchanged in the preliminary University of Michigan survey for August. More importantly, those long-run inflation expectations are, and have been for the past couple of years, close to the levels they were at when core PCE inflation averaged 2%. In contrast, CPI inflation expectations from Philly Fed Survey of Professional Forecasters are below levels from when PCE inflation was, on average, at the FOMC's 2.0% inflation target. Either way, there is no sign from survey measures that inflation expectations are likely to keep inflation notably elevated.

10-year breakeven CPI inflation rates from swap markets have moved down a couple of tenths over the past month and are currently near 2.3%, closer to their pre-pandemic level (2.1%) than their 2022 peak (3.1%). 5-year breakeven inflation 5-years ahead derived from TIPS are between 2.1% and 2.2% — a tenth or so below the level consistent with the FOMC's 2.0% PCE inflation target.

Over the past 25 years inflation expectations in the Michigan survey are positively correlated with past inflation, but negatively correlated with future inflation.

5-year, 5-years ahead inflation compensation from TIPS are a little below the level likely consistent with the FOMC's 2.0% PCE inflation target.

## Exchange rates and import prices

After a significant jump in import prices for consumer goods in January, they have returned to a more normal pace in the past six months. Except for that January surge, consumer import prices have been roughly flat — a pace that is much closer to the prepandemic price trend (when CPI goods prices tended to decline) than the strong upward trend during much of the pandemic. (Figure 42) We are inclined to discount much of the January surge as it was related to medical goods where there is not a high correlation with the CPI. That said, medical commodities increased solidly for the few months prior to June and that could have been a result of the pass-through from the January import price jump.

Except for the huge January increase, import prices have been trending slightly downward.

Additionally, as noted in the front section of this report, upward pressure on goods prices could come from freight costs, which have increased sharply since mid-December. These costs are generally not included in US import price statistics and are an upside risk to goods inflation over the next handful of months.

## Resource utilization and the labor market

The unemployment rate rose to 4.3% (4.253%) in July — a moderate level, but up 0.9pp from its 3.4% trough early last year. Most of the increase in the unemployment rate in the past couple of months (as the unemployment rate rose from 4.0% in May to 4.3% in July) can be accounted for by rising labor force participation. That said, there has been some deterioration in the employment-population ratio over the past year (from 60.4% to 60.0%). We project labor slack to be essentially unchanged over the next 12 months.

A normal Phillips curve coefficient where 1pp in unemployment leads to anywhere from 0.1pp to 0.3pp of inflation would suggest that the level of unemployment can explain little of either the pick up or the slowing in inflation. However, alternative labor market indicators, particularly the job opening rate (shown later in the "Wages" section) suggest a somewhat larger role for labor market tightness.

## Box: Rules of thumb for impacts of key economic shocks

We usually rely on estimated time-series models to bring in the impact of various factors on our forecast. However, rules of thumb are often useful to give a rough idea of how important a shock is likely to be. Below are a few key rules of thumb that we use:

 1 percentage point increase in the unemployment rate pushes down core inflation by 8bp.

 Permanent \$10 per barrel level shift in the price of Brent crude pushes up the level of CPI by 40bp and core CPI by 4bp.

 A one percent decline in the dollar increases consumer import prices by 0.15 percent.

 A one percent increase in import prices results in a 0.12 percent increase in core consumer prices.

A one percent increase in CRB spot food index equals about 8bp in prices of food at home with about 2/3rds in the first six months and 1/3rd in the six months after that. (CPI food at home is 8% of the CPI basket.)

## Price inflation projection through 2026

We continue to project core PCE inflation to slow over the next year as services price increases diminish amid the pass-through of market rents and the slowing labor market. That said, base effects suggest it will be difficult to see much downward progress on 12- month core PCE inflation until the strong prints from early this year begin to drop out (unless a significant slowdown in the economy and/or stock market occurs before then). As a result we expect 12-month core PCE inflation will bounce between 2.6% and 2.7% through December before easing recommences early next year.

## Components of core PCE inflation

Figure 56 through Figure 60 show 3- and 12-month changes for the main components of our core PCE projection along with our forecasts and model projections. The models are estimated taking 2024Q2 as data and forecasted starting 2024Q3.

1. Core goods prices (25% of core PCE, Figure 56) have slowed considerably over the past year. We expect essentially no further disinflation, but for core goods inflation to move up temporarily before settling close to its pre-pandemic pace.

Goods price inflation is projected to remain not too far from its current level.

Our Phillips curve model for the category has adjusted down considerably over the past year and a half and now the model is roughly in line with our projection though the middle of this year and below it further out.

2. We forecast monthly housing services increases (essentially owners' equivalent rent plus tenants' rent, 20% of core PCE, Figure 57) will continue to slow over the next few quarters as the easing in rents for new leases filters into CPI/PCE housing rent inflation.

Our model for housing services inflation based on fundamental rent market factors (the unemployment rate, rental vacancy rate, and housing affordability) also shows slowing, but flattens out in the middle of next year at a level higher than our forecast.

The 12-month change in shelter rent inflation is past its peak, but still very elevated.

3. Price inflation for PCE health services (19% of core PCE, Figure 58) is projected to run a little above the pace of the past year as the price reductions from the ending of Medicare Covid reimbursement rate increases is past. However, the upward push is only moderate as wage growth in health care has been slowing for the past year and Medicare adjustment for wage increases (which appear in October for hospitals and January for physicians) are estimated by the Center for Medicare and Medicaid Studies to be a similar to recent years.

4. PCE food services inflation (6% of core PCE, Figure 59Fod servic) has been strong through most of the pandemic. We have seen slowing in recent months and expect further easing of the pace of increases with moderating wage increases.

5. Inflation for other core PCE services (31% of core PCE, Figure 60) has slowed in recent months and we expect the pace will ebb further as wage growth slowly eases. This is a small contrast to the model that has taken more signal from the strength earlier this year in these core services prices.

## Key risks to our modal forecast

We view our projection as the most likely (modal) scenario and see risks on both sides of the inflation forecast with the overall uncertainty surrounding the inflation projection remaining somewhat larger than usual over the past 20 to 30 years (though down considerably from a year or two ago). Over the next few months we see the risks as twosided but tilted to the downside now that the early-year surge is likely past.

We think that near-term inflation risks have shifted and are now skewed to the downside over the next few months

## Upside risks

1. Shipping costs pushing up core non-transportation goods prices: As noted earlier we think shipping costs will only have a modest effect on overall core prices, but if those shipping costs continue upward or the pass-through is greater than expected it is possible that there could be a much larger increase in core prices than we have penciled in.

2. Rents remaining strong: While the June and July CPIs have been a very positive step in slowing rents, it remains possible that Zillow's estimate of the amount of catch-up that CPI rents still need (around 6%) is more appropriate than taking the average across various measures, which suggest only a little additional catch-up. If Zillow is correct, CPI rents could continue to increase at roughly 45 to 50bp per month for the next 18 to 24 months.

3. Continued tight labor market passing into services prices: While we no longer forecast a recession, we do expect some easing in labor market pressure. If, instead, the economy does not gradually ease and wage growth remains elevated, it is likely that non-rent services inflation will be higher than we have forecast over the next year. In particular we see a notable upside risk to CPI airfares.

## Downside risks

1. A slowing economy: The recession that we had previously forecast starting this summer could still come to pass and have a greater slowing effect on inflation than we have anticipated. In particular, in recessions prices for PCE financial services, non-profit services, and travel-related services generally fall quite notably.

2. Rent weakness: Instead of the Zillow index being correct, it is quite possible that either the BLS NTR or Apartment List's index are right. If so, then OER and tenants rents in the CPI/PCE prices could be slowing sharply in coming months and be negative in CPI reports by next summer.

3. Stronger productivity growth: The past couple quarters have seen a pickup in productivity growth. While we see this pickup as a return to the long-run productivity trend after some time below that trend, it could be the start of a longer-run productivity boom. If so, such a move could lead to lower price inflation for the same level of wage growth. In the late-1990s, as productivity growth picked up, price inflation fell.

4. Lower natural rate of unemployment: Additionally, it is possible that the ability to work remotely has led to better employment opportunities pushing down the natural rate of unemployment. If so, the natural rate of unemployment could be closer to 3.7% than the 4.0% we assume. If so, this would subtract close to a tenth percentage point off of our inflation forecast.

## Comparison to the forecast models

The model forecasts for core PCE inflation this year (Q4/Q4) range from 2.9% to 3.0% as they have taken a greater signal from the strength in Q1 than we have. Much of that difference occurs in the current quarter where the models project core PCE prices increasing 2.4% to 2.8% in 2024Q3 (at annual pace). Our current tracking has core PCE prices increasing at 2.0% in the current quarter.

As with most Phillips curve style models, they were unable to forecast the inflation as it surged upward and, as expected, they have not been a good guide as inflation has fallen. (Note that in the early part of 2022 the three higher models below in Figure 61 projected core PCE inflation remaining above 4% throughout the forecast horizon.) In particular, the models do not include an effect for changes in the mix of demand and the supply constraints that hampered efforts to satisfy this changing mix. We believe the combined effects of the changing demand mix and supply issues were far more important in driving the inflation surge over the past 3 years than were the standard Phillips curve drivers of aggregate resource slack.

Our Phillips curve models have performed poorly during thus inflation experience.

Nonetheless, over the next couple of years we believe those unusual factors in this inflation surge will continue to diminish and and the models will continue to revise down. Our forecast is only slightly below the lower range of models by 2026.
