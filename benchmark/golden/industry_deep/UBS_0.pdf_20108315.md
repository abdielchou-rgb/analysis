# China Equity Strategy

# Capital market with Chinese characteristics I: Strategic importance of the A-share market

## The "People's" A-share market

We think capital appreciation aids common prosperity, as state-owned entities and ordinary citizens now hold over 62% of total A-share market cap. The 2024 "ninepoint" guideline calls this the "people's nature of the capital market". Therefore, we think the A-share market could serve as a new household wealth reservoir, especially during the property downturn, and that the market now has a higher strategic importance for the economy. We believe "new quality productive forces" and selfreliance cannot be fully developed without stronger financing support from the equity market. A re-rating of SOEs (see "Capital market with Chinese characteristics II: SOE valuation dilemma and potential solutions") could also help alleviate the pension shortfall via state-owned capital transfers (see "Capital market with Chinese characteristics III: How to support an ageing China?").

## Structural shift to a more investor-oriented A-share market

Wang Jianjun, vice-chairman of the Chinese Securities Regulatory Committee (CSRC) has said, "Investing and financing are two sides of the same coin" and "We need to build an investor-oriented market". China's IPO intensity had been higher than the US and Japan, and many investors hold the view that financing activities are draining the secondary market. However, the A-share market recently skewed towards shareholder returns along with tighter access to IPOs, rising dividends and share buybacks. A number of government agencies have since mid-January 2024 flagged their resolve to "safeguard" the capital market. The "national team" has also stepped up with ETFs recording strong inflows.

## Riding the policy tailwinds

As we highlighted in New momentum gathering, we believe the A-share market has overpriced economic concerns and underpriced policy tailwinds. China's equity market has rallied along with global fund inflows since 23 April 2024. We expect A-shares' valuation discount to other EMs to further narrow and believe that China has the potential to replicate Japan's success in restructuring the equity market.

## A-share market: financing- or investment-oriented?

There has long been a debate among investors over the function of the A-share market: should capital raising be prioritised or are investment returns in the secondary market more important?

The debate gets more intense when the market is sailing through choppy waters. On one hand, company owners and primary market investors insist that the capital market should serve the development of the real economy, as the central authority repeatedly stresses.

On the other, investors in the secondary market have blamed financing activities for draining the A-share market, while arguing that a stagnant or even failing secondary market cannot sustain the number of private companies going public year after year.

After reaching almost Rmb2trn in 2016, total funds raised hovered around Rmb1.5trn on an annual basis between 2015 and 2021. IPO and secondary offerings combined took the lion's share of total capital raised, while the former ballooned to Rmb588bn in 2022 from Rmb138bn in 2018, a CAGR of 44% and far outpacing the growth in dividend payouts.

Compared with other major markets, China's IPO fund raising intensity (as measured by the percentage of funds raised via IPOs as of average annual total market cap) is the largest by far since 2014. China's IPO fund raising intensity has been consistently above the 10-year rolling average since 2019 for five consecutive years.

The debate was muted when the A-share market was on an upward trend from 2019 to 2021 and quickly exacerbated when A-shares entered a prolonged slump from 2022. In the meantime, IPO fund raising intensity for US and Japan reached decade lows in 2022 and 2023 that coincided with their respective bull runs.

## A brief history

The A-share market was established in the 1990s to serve the financing needs of the large state-owned enterprises (SOEs). To meet this need in a targeted manner, a quota system was implemented for A-shares and each province was allocated a predesignated quota.

Since 2010, A-shares took on a new role of providing financing to start-ups and non-SOEs as the private sector started to boom. Annual capital raised through IPOs and the number of firms listed on the A-share market since then has consistently topped the world rankings.

While 3,000+ companies have gone public in the 10 years since 2014, coinciding with the boom in the primary market and portfolio companies of private equity/venture capital went public in droves, only 163 companies have been delisted in the same period of time. We believe the financing-oriented market did help boost China's economic growth to some degree.

However, the poor performance of the A-share universe is not only in stark contrast with China's economic success, but it has been accused of depriving investors of critical capital to reinvest, forming a bottleneck in the circulation of capital and even creating a negative wealth effect. Many secondary market investors have complained that investment returns have never been the priority of A-share companies.

## Why do we need an investor-oriented equity market?

As China's macro environment evolves, we believe a more investor-oriented A-share market is needed. Wang Jianjun, vice-chairman of the Chinese Securities Regulatory Committee (CSRC), commented in January 2024 that, "Investing and financing are two sides of the same coin" and "We need to build an investor-oriented market". We believe the investing function has the upper hand at this stage.

## New household wealth reservoir

We note that the prolonged property slump has been a drag on China's economic recovery over recent years. Based on UBS-S Economics team estimates, property and property-related companies amounted to 25% of total GDP. In addition, residential real estate makes up the largest portion by far of Chinese household wealth at around 60% in 2020, based on People's Bank of China (PBoC) estimates. A continued downturn will inevitably have a negative wealth effect and further dampen consumer confidence.

We therefore think China needs a new engine to create wealth in order to offset the negative impact of the property downturn. We expect Chinese households to continue to raise their equity exposure, and believe that the A-share market is the best wealth reservoir in the long run. A rising secondary market would create positive wealth effects and could as a result boost consumption.

China’s new asset management regulations require financial institutions to offer yields based on the net asset value of the product, which increases volatility of returns on fixed-income products. China has also tightened regulations on trust financing, which had offered investors higher 'risk-free' yields than traditional wealth management products.

Chinese households can also buy more insurance and pension products, while we think insurers and pension funds are likely to invest more in equities in a low interest rate environment.

We also note that local governments heavily rely on land sales: as of 2022, we estimate 25% of total local government revenue came from land sales. This represents not only government revenue and investment in public infrastructure, but millions of jobs and livelihoods.

Lacklustre land sales in recent years have severely hampered local government finances and created a constant drag on the economy from the bottom up. Local government revenue from land sales contracted to less than Rmb5.8trn in 2023, a 33.4% decline from the peak just two years ago.

Since the local governments hold a number of A-shares as the controlling shareholder, the healthy development of the secondary market should help ease the fiscal pressure.

## Common prosperity

If we break down A-share market cap by investor type, state and state-owned legal entities are by far the largest shareholders of the A-share market. We categorised the top-10 shareholders of every A-share company into either state (state-related entities, central SOEs, local SOEs, etc) or non-state (individuals, non-SOEs, foreign institutions, etc). We then summed their disclosed positions to gauge how large a presence the state has in China's equity market. We estimate the state holds at least Rmb27trn, over a third of the total market cap of A-shares.

As we summed only the top 10 shareholders' positions as a proxy for the state's ownership of the A-share market, actual holdings and the state's presence in the Ashare market is likely to be much larger. However, we note poor share price performance due to unsuccessful reforms or poor corporate governance could have a significant impact on the states' wealth and interest.

In addition, common prosperity is a fundamental principle of "Socialism with Chinese characteristics", which can be roughly divided into three stages according to the strategic arrangement of Chinese-style modernisation.

For example, the first stage takes place during the "14th Five-Year Plan" (2021-25). One of the main goals of this stage is to get household income growth into lock step with economic growth. Passive income, once overlooked and a supplement to labour income, will take on an increasingly critical role in total household income.

According to the CSRC, there are over 220m retail investors in China's stock market and an even larger number of mutual fund investors in China. It is reasonable to assume that the rise and fall of share prices will affect the majority of Chinese households' wealth.

If we total the stakes of the state, mass market retail investors, mutual funds, insurance funds and the national social securities fund and label it as the stake of "ordinary people" in the A-share market, the total amount reaches Rmb49trn, over 62% of total A-share market capitalisation. This amount dwarfs non-SOE strategic investors, entrepreneurs, high net worth individuals and hedge funds combined.

Therefore, it is plausible to suggest that capital appreciation is a priority for the state and would complement the common prosperity process. The 2024 nine-point guideline calls this the "people's nature of the capital market". We believe that letting everyone receive some capital returns is the easiest way to enhance the sense of gain among investors.

## Developing "new quality productive forces"

From a macroeconomic point of view, China's real GDP growth has been under pressure in recent years due to disruptions caused by the pandemic, a prolonged property downturn and geopolitical challenges.

The COVID outbreak in 2020 derailed the healthy growth trajectory of household income and consumption. As of Q124, the household savings rate was 2ppt above the pre-COVID level in 2019, indicating lingering cautious consumer sentiment and leading to the accumulation of excess savings of Rmb5.9trn. In addition, a prolonged property slump has been a drag on economic recovery in recent years.

To smooth the transition of the economic growth model, we believe efforts to foster new growth drivers could be accelerated to promote high-quality development, and developing "new quality productive forces" is a key priority for China.

First put forward in September 2023, the term "new quality productive forces" was highlighted at the Central Economic Work Conference in December 2023. During this year's National People's Congress, new quality productive forces was a well discussed concept. To "vigorously promote the construction of a modern industrial system and accelerate the development of new quality productive forces" was subsequently included in the government work report and listed as a priority task for the government in 2024.

The government has repeatedly mentioned the broader IT industry (i.e. the digital economy), renewables, EVs, high-end manufacturing and modern healthcare as key areas of focus among new quality productive forces that require further development as well as financial support, including but not limited to easier access to financing in the capital market.

Being listed has long been seen as the culmination of the success of a start-up, and we believe the listing of companies involved in new quality productive forces could provide sufficient resources for the next stage development.

We think a functioning and successful secondary market should not only provide abundant liquidity to primary market investors so that they can exit, but also correctly price an IPO company so that the candidate has enough resources for development.

If secondary market investors continue to make financial losses, start-ups will find it more difficult to access finance. Allowing secondary market investors to make money should lead to an increase in financing activities. Therefore, we believe innovative startups would be among the major beneficiaries of a healthy and strong secondary market from a micro perspective, and new quality productive forces would be developed from a macro perspective.

## Self-reliance and accelerated localisation

A longstanding concern for A-share investors has been geopolitical tensions; the past few years have seen escalations and de-escalations in the global power struggle and trade relationships. US-China trade tensions flared up in 2018. In addition to the hefty tariffs the US imposed on China, the latter has faced numerous obstacles and restrictions in areas of technology.

The US has tightened restrictions on China’s access to technology, including far-reaching export controls covering non-US companies. In the EV supply chain, the US Inflation

Reduction Act (IRA) requires higher localisation ratios for critical minerals and battery components to be eligible for subsidies.

Investors have also been concerned about the potential rollout of the Biosecure Act and its implications for China's healthcare industry.

Against this backdrop, the 20th National Congress report devoted an entire chapter to the subject of ensuring national security, echoing a global trend in many countries over the past few years of heightened security concerns.

We think the A-share market is likely to play a more important role in the development of cutting-edge innovation in China, as a healthy secondary market should be able to channel sufficient resources into high-tech development.

## Supporting an ageing China

China is getting 'old'. According to the UN the median age of China's population was 38.4 years in 2020, surpassing that of the US (38.3 years). The proportion of people aged over 65 years in China reached 15.4% in 2023, according to the latest disclosure from the National Bureau of Statistics (NBS), and is expected to rise to around 30% by 2050, according to the United Nations Population Division (UNPD).

China's ageing population is leading to a pension shortfall. This is partly due to lingering historical issues: workers who retired before 1997 received a pension even though they had not paid into a personal account. The gap is exacerbated by China's early retirement age.

According to the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences, the accumulative balance for the basic pension fund will peak in 2027F and be depleted in 2035F. As employers in China already bear a heavy burden of social benefit contributions, it could be difficult for them to further increase pension contributions to fill the shortfall.

Although postponing the age of retirement seems to be one solution, it faces numerous obstacles. A state-owned capital transfer was another option and was put into practice. In 2017, China began transferring state-owned capital to the social security fund and completed the transfer of Rmb3.3trn as of the end of 2020. We estimate up to Rmb400bn of cash dividends (9% of current annual basic pension income) could be used to supplement pension payments in 2030E. Given the widening pension deficit, further state-owned capital transfers seem inevitable (see "Capital market with Chinese characteristics III: How to support an ageing China?").

A rising A-share market would naturally create more resources for capital operations in the long-term. The development of China's third-pillar personal pension scheme (PPS) could support the development of China's equity market and increase institutional investor participation.

## "Nine-point" guideline paves the way for the next decade

On 12 April 2024, the State Council issued a guideline with nine main points aimed at strengthening supervision, preventing risks and promoting quality development of the capital market. This follows two earlier guidelines released in 2004 and 2014, all with nine main points. A once-a-decade document, the 'nine-point' guideline lays the foundations for financial work in the following 10 years and stresses the key areas of focus for the financial market. We summarise the three 'nine-point' guidelines and discuss their implications for China's capital market.

## 2004: non-tradable share reform

The first nine-point guideline stressed the importance of "vigorously" developing the capital market, which elevated the strategic significance. The proposal to solve the nontradable share issue ushered in a round of shareholding reform and paved the way for the H205-2007 A-share boom. The central authority explicitly called for ramping up direct financing, which was aimed at developing the private economy.

## 2014: registration-based IPO system and opening up

The second guideline's key points were registration-based stock issuance, developing private funds, strengthening professional institutional investors, and a further opening up of the securities and futures industries. These led to milestones like registration-based IPOs and Stock Connect. ## 2024: "investor-oriented" equity market

As we discussed in our report: 'Nine-point' guideline for overall development of A-share market in the next decade, the key points are:

1) Tighter IPO access. In Q124, IPOs slowed to a historical low of Rmb23.6bn, and many candidates have reportedly withdrawn applications recently. The CSRC has pledged to promote the balanced growth of the primary and secondary markets, showing a commitment to secondary liquidity. Higher IPO quality should also boost A shares' investability in the long run, in our view.

2) Improved shareholding reduction rules & supervision of cash dividends. Buyback plans this year are up sharply in number and value terms while dividends have trended up in recent years. This suggests A-shares are focusing on shareholder returns and market cap management.

3) Delisting supervision. The A-share market has had fewer delistings annually than developed markets in both number and market cap terms; we expect both M&As and delistings to accelerate.

4) Tighter trading regulation. Post the Q124 A-share volatility, new rules on program trading aim to better regulate high-frequency quant trading and improve extreme scenario response measures. We think this should help the market consolidate for long term growth.

5) Tapping mid-/long-term capital. ETF units recorded a Rmb114.4bn net gain in Q124, up 263% YoY. Insurers' stock investments are also rising. We expect strong growth of equity mutual funds, the promotion of index investing and the encouragement of long-horizon investors (insurers/social security/pension/bank WMPs) in the A-share market. We think institutionalization could help quality companies and high-dividend stocks to outperform.

6) Further reform & opening-up. We expect the potential cross-border interconnection to channel more resources to the IPOs and the IPO candidates will be able to raise funds overseas.

We think an investor-oriented A-share market is one of the government's top priorities for next stage development. Only a well functioning capital market where participants can achieve a decent return in the form of either capital appreciation or dividend payout can form a positive and sustainable self-reinforcing feedback loop that benefits investors and also stimulates corporate development, in our view.

# What has been achieved and what to expect next?

# The government safeguarded the capital market in early 2024

After the market corrections of the first half of January 2024, a number of government agencies have commented since mid-January on their resolve to safeguard the capital market. In addition to a number of positive policy signals from the CSRC, we note:

1) RRR and interest rate cuts by the PBoC.

2) Central Huijin Investment's announcement stating its full recognition of the allocation value of the A-share market (see report).

3) Vice Premier He Lifeng's proposal to increase support for high quality listed companies.

4) The State-Owned Asset Supervision and Administration Commission's (SASAC) incorporation of market cap management in its assessment of the heads of central SOEs.

5) A Government Work Report highlighting the need to strengthen the intrinsic stability of the capital market.

With these stock market supporting policies coming into play, market liquidity risk eased and the A-share market has quickly rebounded since 6 February. We think the coordinated actions provide strong support for the A-share market on a medium-term perspective.

## Dividends to increase

A-share companies have made substantial progress in terms of dividend payouts in the last 10 years. Total dividend payouts more than tripled to above Rmb1.7trn in 2023 from Rmb526bn in 2013, a CAGR of 12.5%. Although A-share earnings have been under pressure in recent years, the absolute amount of dividend payouts has been increasing, indicating listed companies' resolution to enhance investor returns.

Figure 19: Annual dividends paid by A-share companies
![](images/61e16519b0394a550cc02af0c5c3cd66121306c1cead9db6c327cb9befd113d6.jpg)

We compared the dividend yields of China's stock market with those of the US and Japan using the CSI 300, S&P 500 and Nikkei 225 as proxies, respectively. To our surprise, the CSI 300 actually leads the S&P and Nikkei in terms of dividend yield most of the time since 2010.

We believe tax rate differences between the three countries contributed to this outcome. The maximum dividend tax rate for A-share companies is 20% and under certain circumstances, such as holding for a period over one year, the individual income tax can be waived.

However, withholding tax rates for the US and Japan stand at 30% and 20%, respectively. Thus listed companies in both countries may be less motivated to reward investors through dividend payouts.

SOE investors have enjoyed above-market dividend yields since 2013. SOEs showed resilience while non-SOEs struggled during market downturns. In response to the central authority's call for a valuation system with Chinese characteristics (VCC) and SOE reform, the dividend yield of the former almost doubled over the latter and reached 3.4% in 2023.

## Share buybacks: developed from the ground up

Share buybacks by listed Chinese companies lag those of other major markets. If we calculate the total amount of share buybacks carried out as a percentage of total market cap, the percentage for the CSI 300 was 0.19% in 2023, less than a quarter of the Nikkei 225 and a tenth of the S&P 500. The S&P 500 leads its global peers in this area by a wide margin. The average annual amount of share buybacks as as a percentage of market cap for the S&P 500 reached above 2.9% in the past decade.

However, share buybacks are becoming increasingly common in the China market. The total amount of share buybacks carried out by listed companies grew from a mere Rmb8bn in 2013 to more than Rmb154bn in 2023, a CAGR over 34%.

In the four documents published by the CSRC on 15 March 2024 it called for high quality listed companies to actively carry out share buybacks, which we flag is already reflected in the number of share buyback plans announced so far this year. A total of 656 share buyback plans were announced in February alone, almost twice as many as the previous record in 2019.

Taking dividends, share buybacks, and capital raised into consideration, we note an improvement in the dynamics between financing activities and shareholder returns in recent years.

We note that annual capital raised has remained consistently over Rmb1trn since 2015 and averaging Rmb1.6trn a year. Although listed companies have rewarded investors through dividends and share buybacks amounting to a total of Rmb6.8trn in the seven years after 2015, Rmb11.4trn has been raised through IPOs, secondary offerings and preferred shares, leading to an accumulated shortfall of Rmb4.6trn over that time frame.

We feel it is plausible for investors in the secondary market to believe that financing activities have been 'draining the secondary market'. However, the situation has reversed since 2022 when financing activities as a whole were under immense pressure while A-share companies ramped up dividend payouts. In 2023, we believe the A-share market skewed more towards shareholders' returns.

## Valuation Method and Risk Statement

For various stocks across the industries we cover in the Hong Kong and mainland China stock markets, we use a variety of valuation approaches, including DCF models, Gordon growth model analysis and relative valuation analysis using various multiples such as PE, EV/EBITDA and P/BV.

We think the risks facing China's equities include a hard landing for the property market, a capital exodus associated with currency depreciation and slow structural reform progress. In our view, any government policies that do not adequately address these risks could result in a shock to the market. For example, an excess of stimulus policies could pose a risk to the transition from an investment-driven to a consumption-driven economy and increase the debt of government and state-owned enterprises.
