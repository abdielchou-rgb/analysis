# Fixed Income Asia Thematic Strategy Notes

# RMB: PBoC restart bond trading

In the recent annual Lujiazui Forum - a high-profile two-day financial conference in Shanghai, which usually attracts major financial regulators and institutions - PBoC Governor Pan Gongsheng delivered a candid and informative speech entitled “China's current monetary policy stance and the evolution of its future framework".

One of the topics he spoke about was to confirm that the PBoC would resume trading CGBs in the secondary market as a supplementary liquidity tool in its monetary toolkit. The discussion around this has picked up since March, following a South China Morning Post (SCMP) report quoting President Xi's speech at the Central Financial Work Conference last October, calling for “enriching the toolbox of monetary policy instruments and increasing Treasury bond trades in the open market". In an environment where the market is becoming increasingly concerned about China's growth outlook, particularly its medium-term trajectory, the new liquidity tool has raised questions on whether China is moving towards quantitative easing to support growth.

In this report, we touch upon a few key questions on this topic, including; (a) how PBOC has managed liquidity in the past; (b) why Treasury bond trading is now being considered as potentially a part of policy toolkit; (c) whether the PBoC is embarking on quantitative easing; and (d) implications for the market.

## How PBOC has managed liquidity

Liquidity management has always played an important role in China's monetary policy implementation, especially in its credit policy and money supply management. Its approach, however, has varied over time.

Prior to 2014, due to strong economic growth and capital inflows, PBOC relied mainly on FX reserve accumulation to inject base money into the economy. During that period, FX reserves accounted for the bulk of the PBOC's balance sheet expansion (Figure 1). As FX reserves accumulated faster than the money supply growth target, the PBOC kept increasing the reserve requirement ratio (RRR) during that period to sterilize its ongoing FX purchasing program. As such, the RRR went from 6% in 2000 to a peak of 21% in 2011, before beginning to decline continuously from 2014 onwards, when FX reserves peaked (Figure 2).

In 2014, with growth slowing and RMB starting to weaken, the PBoC started to inject more liquidity into the system actively, not only to achieve its economic target, but also to replenish the RMB liquidity it was taking out during its FX intervention activities to lean against RMB weakness. This started with RRR cuts and reverse repo operations, but the toolkit was gradually widened to various collateral-based liquidity facilities, including SFL, MLF and PSL (Appendix 1). As of writing, the outstanding size of the liquidity facilities is RMB10tn (Figure 3), which has been declining due to the reduction in PSL since February 2024.

Apart from the above, the PBoC also uses credit tools to inject liquidity into the system. Since the 1980s, it has used rediscount and relending facilities to provide cheap funding to banks that lend to the targeted sectors, such as the rural and SME sector. How? The mechanism is actually simple: commercial banks use loans they have made to customers as collateral to borrow from the PBoC, and then the PBOC issues loans to banks by increasing their reserves with it. From a cashflow perspective, the banks “swap” illiquid credit loans for liquid reserves after the transaction, which incentivises banks to lend more.

Over the past few years, this has been actively expanded. At the current juncture, there are 10 active rediscount and relending facilities for various purposes; and 6 targeted lending facilities to support activities and firms of national importance (Appendix 2). The total outstanding size of the PBOC's structural monetary tools at the moment amounts to RMB7.5tn (Figure 4).

Why the search for a new monetary liquidity tool?

There are two ways in which the PBoC's management of liquidity impacts money supply.

n The increase in base money through the accumulation of FX reserves and/ or the various liquidity facilities mentioned above.

n The money multiplier channel, which is impacted by the PBoC's adjustment of the RRR. After 2014, RRR cuts, pick-up in the money multiplier and the various liquidity instruments became the key in maintaining money supply growth (Figure 5).

However, after a decade of RRR cuts, the room for reductions has diminished. Although there is no rule on what the appropriate level of RRR should be (the Fed has had the RRR at zero since 2020, while Brazil has the RRR at 21%), in 2009 PBoC suggested that RRR should be between 5-7%. In fact, in recent years, despite universal RRR cuts, small financial institutions' RRR has been limited to 5% and have no longer been allowed to adjust lower (Figure 6). If so, we could be getting to end of the road on RRR cuts, with maybe appetite limited to another \~200bps. Given the more limited room on this front, exploring new policy instruments has become important, with central bank treasury bond purchases among the options.

With the ongoing capital market reforms over the past year, the CGB market is increasingly becoming more liquid, with the outstanding exceeding RMB30tn in April 2024 (\~23% of GDP, Figure 7). On top of that, with the President's request to better coordinate between fiscal and monetary policy, these additional tools can help ensure the smoothing out of government bond issuances whenever necessary.

## The history of the PBoC buying government bonds

According to China's central bank law (the Law on the People's Bank of China, passed in 1995 and amended in 2003), the PBOC “shall not provide overdraft for government and must not directly subscribe to or underwrite treasury bonds and other government securities (Article 29)", but the PBOC “may, on behalf of the fiscal authorities under the State Council, issue and redeem Treasury bonds and other government securities to financial institutions (Article 25)". In particular, the PBOC "may purchase and sell Treasury bonds or other government securities on the open market to implement monetary policy (Article 23)".

In other words, the PBoC is legally allowed to trade (buy or sell) Treasury bonds in the secondary market, but is prohibited to trade in the primary market. Looking back, there have been three occasions in which the PBoC has traded CGBs.

1 1998-2002. During this period, the yearly withdrawal of base money through open market operations on treasury bonds was RMB-70.1, -192, 81.7, 29.6 and 102.1bn, respectively. According to the authorities, the open market operations on treasury bonds had little impact either on banks’ excess reserves ratios, or on base money, as they possessed \~RMB2.5tn worth of CGBs and their monthly average excess reserves ranged between 540bn and 660bn.

n 2007. The PBoC indirectly bought RMB1.35tn worth of government bonds as part of the joint effort with MoF to set up CIC.

n 2017. In 2017, RMB696.4bn of 10Y bonds issued in 2007 matured. The maturities were refinanced via 600bn of 7Y and 10Y bonds directly placed with some commercial banks. The PBoC bought 600bn of those bonds from banks via open market operations conducted on the same day as the placement.

According to the PBoC, the claims on the government as part of PBoC's balance sheet – a proxy for the PBoC’s holding of treasury bonds – stood at RMB1.52tn in April (Figure 10), which is \~3.6% of total assets and \~1.2% of China’s GDP. The absolute amount of holdings has been roughly unchanged since 2008. The breakdown of PBoC bond holding suggests the majority of the holdings have a duration of less than 3 years (Figure 11) and, hence, giving limited room for PBoC to take a more active role in trading in the CGB market.

## Government bond trading is not new in Asia

Treasury bond purchases and sales in the open market have been a standard operation for many central banks well before QE was introduced. In fact, the Bank of England was the first central bank as far back as in 1694 o purchase government bonds. In more recent times, after the Global Financial Crisis in 2008, more central banks have been actively buying bonds, including in Asia.

Most central banks in the region are legally allowed to hold domestic government bonds on their balance sheets (Figure 12). However, there are some central banks, such as in China, India, and Japan, which are only allowed to purchase government bonds in the secondary market, but not in the primary market. Furthermore, there is no limit placed on central banks’ ownership of domestic government bonds, except in Malaysia, where the central bank restricts its purchases to 10% of the outstanding issuance, in order not to distort market prices.

During the pandemic years between 2020-2022, various Asian central banks started to experiment with quantitative easing (QE) in different forms, although none of the banks reached the zero bound of their respective policy rates. Why?

n Keeping liquidity flush. In 2021, the Philippines infused a large amount of liquidity into the banking system via direct market purchases to keep the overall banking system flush.

n Stabilizing the bond market. In 2022, Korea highlighted that direct KTB purchases were being made to moderate a temporary overshooting in KTBs as part of a market stabilization, and were not intended to change the overall direction of KTB yields.

n Keeping borrowing costs low. In India, RBI carried out Operation Twist to cap long-dated government bond yields and improve the policy transmission mechanism, steering effective borrowing costs lower. In Indonesia, BI and the MoF signed a burden-sharing scheme in order to manage the supply of bonds on the back of a wider fiscal deficit and also ease the interest burden for the MoF

Will this be the beginning of QE in China?

There are increasing signs that the PBoC will start its bond trading program soon. This has inevitably also led to an active debate on whether the resumption of its bond trading program will lead to QE. This, however, is not a view that the PBoC Governor subscribes to. In the Lujiazui forum, he stated clearly what the PBoC would not do: “It should be noted that incorporating bond trading into the policy toolbox does not mean quantitative easing.”

We agree with this view, at least for now. Why? (a) China still has room to ease using its traditional policy tools, including policy rates and RRR; (b) demand for CGB remains strong; and (c) the PBoC has sufficient toolkits to inject liquidity for now.

Furthermore, the President is not supportive of the PBoC doing QE either. In the same speech where the SCMP reported President Xi calling for an expansion of the PBoC's toolkit, he also stated that “stabilising money supply is an important foundation for ensuring good financial work. I have said before that all methods to ensure deleveraging are useless, if money supply is not controlled.” As such, we believe that implementing QE now would be contradictory to that idea.

## What is the potential size of Treasury trading if adopted?

Assuming that the PBoC is aiming to maintain steady M2 growth at \~8% (the average for this year), M2 growth in 2023 was \~RMB23tn and the money multiplier is kept stable at \~8 (the average of the past 12 months), this would imply a base money expansion of \~ RMB3tn per annum unless the PBoC were to cut the RRR further.

Assuming no other policy tools are used to inject liquidity into the system, the PBoC would need to buy \~RMB3tn of CGBs. This would be equivalent of \~10% of total Treasury bonds outstanding as of May and 75% of the total regular CGB issuances from the government this year. On the other hand, if the government continues to inject liquidity through alternative channels including the MLF and PSL, and fully utilizes all the quota for the various lending and re-lending facilities, the amount of bond purchasing could be much smaller (\~RMB1tn).

The latter is probably the more appropriate amount given PBoC would like prefer to pull multiple levers to support M2 growth target and also not to disrupt the bond market.

## Market implications

Reduction in interbank rate volatility. Given that the issuance of CGBs are not traditionally uniform (Figure 13), so as to allow local governments to issue their bonds first, this sometimes makes it more difficult for the PBoC to manage liquidity preemptively. For example, from September to November 2023 during peak issuances, interbank liquidity tightened notably (averaging around 20bp above the 7-day OMO) and the 5Y CGB tightened \~15bp. On the hand, during times when credit demand was weak and there wasn't enough government bond supply to drain liquidity, interbank funding tended to get very loose and significantly undershoot the policy rate anchor (7-day OMO). For the time being, the PBoC has managed this by adjusting its reverse repo and/or MLF activities. This additional toolkit should allow the PBoC to better manage interbank liquidity, thereby reducing any excess volatility.

Ensure CGB curve is in line with fundamentals. Given the PBoC can buy/sell CGBs in the secondary market and at different tenors, it should be be able to limit any excessive market moves that is not reflective of China's economic fundamentals. For example, over the past few months, the PBoC has been sending clear warnings to commercial banks, telling them to not excessively buy long-dated bonds (10Y and above) and have made it clear that the appropriate yields for 10Y and above should be between 2.5-3.0%. Despite the string of warnings, the 10Y CGB and above are now trading below the range, with the 10Y at \~2.25% and the 30Y at \~2.48% as of writing. A more active engagement from the PBoC could help drive yields higher (assuming they have sufficient inventories), and potentially help influence the curve shape.

Protect banks' NIM. Given the sizeable stock of CGB banks have, the ability to sell down their CGB holdings to PBoC could be a cheaper way to obtain liquidity while maintaining their NIM compared with using the various liquidity facilities PBoC provide.

Reduce potential market disfunction. Lastly, having the bond buying/selling mechanism in place would also give the central bank more leverage in dealing with episodes of unduly high market volatility. This has yet to happen in China, but had happened in the UK in 2022 due to the large unfunded tax cuts announced by the Chancellor at the time, which resulted in the BoE having to step in to the gilt market to buy government bonds so as to safeguard financial stability.

Macroeconomic fluctuations often account for most of the risks associated with exposures to instruments that promise to pay fixed or variable interest rates. For an investor who is long fixed-rate instruments (thus receiving these cash flows), increases in interest rates naturally lift the discount factors applied to the expected cash flows and thus cause a loss. The longer the maturity of a certain cash flow and the higher the move in the discount factor, the higher will be the loss. Upside surprises in inflation, fiscal funding needs, and FX depreciation rates are among the most common adverse macroeconomic shocks to receivers. But counterparty exposure, issuer creditworthiness, client segmentation, regulation (including changes in assets holding limits for different types of investors), changes in tax policies, currency convertibility (which may constrain currency conversion, repatriation of profits and/or liquidation of positions), and settlement issues related to local clearing houses are also important risk factors. The sensitivity of fixed-income instruments to macroeconomic shocks may be mitigated by indexing the contracted cash flows to inflation, to FX depreciation, or to specified interest rates - these are common in emerging markets. The index fixings may - by construction - lag or mis-measure the actual move in the underlying variables they are intended to track. The choice of the proper fixing (or metric) is particularly important in swaps markets, where floating coupon rates (i.e., coupons indexed to a typically short-dated interest rate reference index) are exchanged for fixed coupons. Funding in a currency that differs from the currency in which coupons are denominated carries FX risk.
