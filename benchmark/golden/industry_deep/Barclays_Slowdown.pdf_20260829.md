## Can 1Q24 Earnings Stave Of the Sellof?

Venu Krishna, CFA, BCI, US | Japinder Chawla, CFA, BCI, US | Rex Feng, BCI, US | Madhuresh Rai, CFA, BCI, US | Young Chen, BCI, US

Excerpted from U.S. Equity Insights: Can 1Q24 Earnings Stave Of the Sellof?, published on April 18, 2024

EPS estimates are drifting lower into the print while top-line remains stable, implying greater cost headwinds; low confidence in margins despite 2 quarters of inflection as expansion has yet to establish a broader base; options expect a quiet season; upside to full-year estimates remains reliant on Tech for now.

Estimates drifting lower into the print. Most major sectors have seen 1Q24 EPS estimates cut since the end of March. Communication Services and Tech are once again the primary exceptions. As a result, YoY EPS growth expectations for 1Q24 are modest, pricing in just 80bp of growth vs. the long-term average of just over +6%. Notably, top-line growth estimates have remained relatively stable, implying that downward revisions to EPS growth are coming from expectations of increased cost headwinds.

The Street is once again cautious on margins, perhaps because the SPX margin inflection was defended by just a handful of mega-cap Tech names last quarter, and is still considered tenuous. That said, estimates further out remain untouched, and continue to imply that SPX profit margins will rise to an all-time record this year even as 1Q24 estimates look for margins to shrink YoY for most major sectors. To us, this suggests low confidence in the margin outlook for 2024, and that consensus has adopted a wait-and-see approach until margin expansion establishes a broader base.

Tech remains the primary source of upside for full year estimates, though Financials and Energy have seen an upturn recently. These pockets of EPS improvement are being fully ofset by weakness elsewhere. Notably, the largest drivers of EPS revisions (whether upward or downward) over the last few months have been single names rather than sector- or industry wide trends, with downward revisions for smaller index constituents able to ofset the EPS upside coming from Big Tech. We think this leaves room for consensus to continue catching down to our own FY24 estimate as 1Q numbers begin flowing through to estimates.

## Science of Tomorrow: Natural hydrogen - drilling for gold

Amy Lian, Barclays, UK | Alex Stewart, CFA, Barclays, UK | Anil Shenoy, Barclays, UK | Alex Sloane, Barclays, UK | Michael Leithead, CFA, BCI, US

Excerpted from Global Chemicals: Science of Tomorrow: Natural hydrogen - drilling for gold, published on April 16, 2024

For years, experts believed hydrogen produced naturally within the earth was impossible to extract. But, some recent groundbreaking discoveries of huge natural hydrogen reservoirs are challenging this assumption. We explore potential implications for the hydrogen industry if natural hydrogen became mainstream.

It is widely assumed that the majority of hydrogen in the future will come from two methods of production: natural gas reforming with and without carbon capture, or electrolysis.

However, there is a relatively unknown but emerging method of hydrogen production that solves many of the issues related to blue and green hydrogen. This hydrogen occurs naturally in the earth and can be extracted using technologies similar to those currently used for oil and gas. Known as natural, white, or gold hydrogen, it was ignored by scientists for decades as a viable hydrogen source as many doubted whether it existed in large enough quantities to be recoverable. But, recent significant natural hydrogen discoveries are luring more scientists and companies to explore this production pathway. Crucially, unlike green hydrogen, natural hydrogen is a low carbon, primary energy source, as opposed to an energy vector.

Natural hydrogen is still a highly speculative application with no guarantees that it will reach commercial production. However, our Science of Tomorrow series is designed specifically to explore such topics which are not in the mainstream today but could disrupt incumbent technology in the future. In this report, we present an investor's guide on natural hydrogen and discuss the potential implications to our thesis that low carbon hydrogen will develop into a local market model, rather than a globally traded commodity, and the threat to existing blue/ green hydrogen assets. Both have important long-term implications for industrial gas companies such as Air Liquide, Linde and Air Products. In summary:

1) There is enough natural hydrogen in the earth to meet future hydrogen demand for hundreds of years, but how much of it is accessible and extractable is still unknown. Critics argue that it is unlikely large and extractable sources of hydrogen will be found, and examples of reported hydrogen flow at current oil and gas wells are negligible. Proponents believe though that this very assumption is fuelling a negative feedback loop where no one is searching for hydrogen in the right way as they don't believe it is worth searching for.

2) Natural hydrogen could be cost competitive with other forms of hydrogen. Detailed cost analysis is lacking as no large-scale commercial plants currently exist, but a well in Mali which has supplied residents with electricity since 2012 has been quoted as producing natural hydrogen for as little as \$0.5/kg<sup>1</sup> . Most natural hydrogen companies also seem to target a price of \~\$0.5-1/kg, which is competitive with grey (\~ \$1-3/kg), blue (\~ \$2-5/kg dependent on carbon pricing by 2030) and green projects (\~ \$0.5-6/kg by 2030).

3) We think any threat to existing blue/green infrastructure is likely to be localised rather than global, even if extremely large reserves are discovered. The cost to convert and transport hydrogen is such that transport between low and high cost regions is uneconomic (though we acknowledge some countries pursue a hydrogen strategy based on imports). Therefore, in the extreme case that very large scale reserves are commercialised, we think the risk of export competition is relatively limited. Instead, local demand for hydrogen in otherwise uneconomic use cases is likely to increase due to the lower cost of natural hydrogen. The risk of existing blue/green projects being cancelled due to natural hydrogen is currently low in our view due to the diferent commercialisation timelines, with the earliest natural hydrogen projects not expected to reach large-scale production until nearer 2030.

4) This theme impacts the following companies in our coverage: Air Liquide (OW), Linde (OW), Air Products (EW), Johnson Matthey (EW), and Umicore (EW). We also provide company profiles on selected private and listed pure-plays in the space.

Our Science of Tomorrow series highlights disruptive ideas across the chemical industry before they become mainstream. The aim of this series is to educate investors about nascent opportunities and those companies investing in them. For other reports in this series, see Microbiomes from early 2021, Biomethane and EV Battery Recycling from 2022 and the future of EV battery technology from 2023.

The pillars of the US equity rally YTD have been surprisingly durable through the recent bout of volatility. It's been a tough start to 2Q24 for risk markets, with the S&P 500 down nearly 5% MTD amid sticky inflation and a reset to Fed rate cut expectations, all before earnings season is set to begin in earnest. Many perhaps expected Big Tech to be square in the sights of de-risking after the group contributed nearly 42% of the S&P 500's YTD gains through the end of March, but Big Tech has been surprisingly resilient since the index rolled of its recent peak, driving a comparatively modest portion of the MTD sellof despite its substantial index weight. Overall, however, losses have been more evenly distributed than gains, and seem to indicate rally fatigue sparked by macro concerns rather than disorderly selling (Long & Short of It: Running Out of Steam, 16 Apr 2024). 1Q results are the next overhang, and Big Tech remains in the spotlight with a relatively high degree of analyst uncertainty around the 12m earnings outlook (U.S. Equity Insights: Can 1Q24 Earnings Stave Of the Sellof?, 18 Apr 2024).

## Macro vs. micro

Emmanuel Cau, CFA, Barclays, UK | Matthew Joyce, CFA, BBI, Paris | Magesh Kumar Chandrasekaran, CFA, Barclays, UK | Arihanth Bohra Jain, Barclays, UK

Excerpted from Equity Market Review - Macro vs. micro, published on April 19, 2024

Rates markets have aggressively recalibrated Fed expectations and equities have seen a fair amount of de-risking. With x-assets volatility now more reflective of the higher for longer narrative, earnings should take over from macro as the next driver of equities. Early Q1 results look pretty decent so far.

Markets have aggressively recalibrated Fed expectations. Remaining market complacency about the US rate cutting cycle has largely gone post the hot CPI print last week. The US OIS curve has now priced out most of the rates cuts for this year, in sharp contrast to the seven cuts expected at the start of year, and it is more hawkish than the (now dated) Fed dot plot forecasts. Equities have logically sold-of in this environment, and as we wrote last week (Broadening shaken but not stirred, 14 Apr), the Fed having to stay restrictive for longer raises the risk of a hard landing down the road. Powell's speech earlier this week confirmed that imminent cuts are indeed of the table, so the path to higher equity markets has arguably narrowed. However, with markets now more aligned with the higher for longer narrative, we believe that the bulk of the repricing higher in x-asset volatility may be done for now, which is a necessary condition for stocks to stabilize.

We flagged in our recent publications that market technicals were getting pretty toppish, making equities more vulnerable to unexpected shocks. Cash deployment into equities had picked up ytd and systematic positioning had become stretched too (see our Who owns What from 27 Mar). Our US strategists colleagues published a timely update on flows & positioning this week that confirmed positioning is indeed pretty full (Long & Short of It - Running Out of Steam, 18 Apr). We note however that the latest pull-back has quickly reduced complacency, with the AAII Bull bear index down to its lowest level since last November and equities seeing their biggest outflows since Dec'22 over the last two weeks. ## Americas

From dovish to hawkish

Bradley Rogof, BCI, US | Dominique Toublan, BCI, US

Excerpted from US Credit Alpha, published on April 19, 2024

The market sell-of continued as Fed commentary turned hawkish following elevated inflation prints. The recent weakness bears some similarities to October/September 2023, and if those similarities persist, spreads could have more downside in the near term. Positive technicals, however, should cap the widening.

Fed commentary took a hawkish turn this week. On Tuesday, Chair Powell signaled that recent inflation data have increased uncertainty about when and if lower interest rates will come later this year. “The recent data have clearly not given us greater confidence and instead indicate that it is likely to take longer than expected to achieve that confidence...Right now, given the strength of the labor market and progress on inflation so far, it’s appropriate to allow restrictive policy further time to work.” The hawkish shift was reinforced by comments on Thursday from NY Fed President Williams, who, when asked, indicated that a rate hike would be possible if the data warranted, but that it was not his base case.

At the same time, the economic growth picture remains strong. Data this week were somewhat light, but March retail sales on Monday came in well above expectations, with m/m growth of 0.7% versus expectations of 0.4% (and even higher growth when excluding autos and in the control group). This pushed the Atlanta Fed's GDPNow estimate for 1Q24 GDP to 2.8% from 2.4%, and then Tuesday's housing starts and industrial production data pushed it up further to 2.9%.

Markets have reacted negatively to the prospect of higher-for-longer rates. Strong growth, above-trend inflation, and a hawkish Fed have pushed out expectations of Fed rate cuts and helped shift the Treasury curve higher, with yields in both the front and the long end at least 35bp higher so far in April and now back at levels last seen in November 2023. Equities have also reacted negatively to recent developments, with both the S&P 500 and Nasdaq 100 down around 5% from recent highs and the Russell down more than 8%.

In credit, the CDX indices have continued to sell of, with IG and HY spreads 6bp and 40bp wider, respectively, so far in April. Cash had been more resilient to the weakness because of the typically negative rates-spread correlation, but both investment grade and high yield cash spreads have widened over the past week as well (albeit much more modestly for IG). Month-todate as of Wednesday's close, IG cash is 3bp wider and HY is 30bp wider. High yield has lagged in both derivatives and cash, leading to sharp decompression.

Recent price action is reminiscent of September/October 2023. During that period, risk assets also sold of as rates moved sharply higher, similar to the market dynamic today, especially with current rates at levels similar to those during that prior sell-of. Initially, cash spreads were resilient to the move higher in rates, but they ultimately started to widen as the VIX and MOVE indices increased sharply and the spread-rates correlation turned positive.

Using that earlier period as a guide, if rates continue to move higher rapidly, cash spreads could continue to widen modestly, as the speed of the rate move would likely give investors pause in the short term despite the allure of higher all-in yields. Ultimately, in Sep/Oct 2023, as discussed in more detail in this week's investment grade section, the long end and higherquality credit fared the best, and those are the parts of the market we currently favor. In high

yield, bonds and loans both sold of, and they have displayed some softness recently. As discussed in this week's high yield section, we think that higher rates should be a boon for loan investors, and we recommend swaps out of bonds into loans in the same capital structure to increase yield.

The credit derivative indices underperformed in September/October 2023 and are so far following a similar pattern. Roll-adjusted CDX.IG and CDX.HY spreads widened 15bp and 84bp, respectively, from the mid-September tights to the late-October wides. If markets follow a similar pattern, it would imply that we are a little less than halfway through the sell-of, with potential wides for the current move in the mid- to upper 60s for IG and \$103-103.5 for HY. We note, however, that we have seen little evidence of meaningful de-risking in the net investor positioning data (as of April 12). Anecdotal evidence this week suggests that de-risking has started, but so far, the reported positioning changes have been modest relative to Sep/Oct 2023, especially for IG (Figure 10).

Ultimately, favorable supply-demand dynamics should limit the widening in spreads. Conversations with clients continue to point to inflows and robust yield-driven demand for credit. At the same time, industrials supply is expected to slow, as a function of both earnings blackouts and heavy issuance in the first three months of the year. We think investors are likely to take advantage of higher all-in yields once rates stabilize, which should help cap the widening in spreads. The main risk to our view would be a weakening in growth, which continues to surprise to the upside.

## Europe

Rate vol to geopolitical vol

Søren Willemann, Barclays, UK

Excerpted from European Credit Alpha, published on  19 April 2024

In spite of heightened interest rate – and now geopolitical – volatility, European credit markets remain in a good place, with high yields attracting demand and the economic environment stabilising. For tail risk from the Middle East, we like hybrids over HY, and we still like to hedge cash with Main.

Market focus on the path of US monetary policy received meaningful competition from renewed geopolitical volatility out of the Middle East this week, with increasing concerns of an escalating conflict between Israel and Iran. From a market perspective, we view tail risk from the Middle East as stagflationary given the risk of meaningfully higher oil prices, as discussed in Darker clouds, fatter geopolitical tails.

As such, both US monetary policy uncertainty and Middle East tensions are of the 'higher rates for longer' downside variety. This has made it dificult to disentangle the market reaction to understand which has had the biggest impact. Stagflation would, in our view, be a HY/IG decompression event (economic downside and higher financing costs would be a doublewhammy for HY) but we see no real evidence of this.

Anecdotally, investors tend to see Middle East tails as a larger risk to Europe than the US, given Europe is an oil importer, in contrast to the US. The reality, as ever, is a bit more nuanced, in our view: first, the market for oil is global, so the US consumer would likely face similar prices as the European consumer; and second, the US consumer in general is more sensitive to oil prices, given the stronger reliance on cars for transportation. That said, with higher oil prices, the US does benefit from upside to its energy sector, including via potential investment and hiring.

Also on that angle, we see no real evidence of Europe underperforming the US. The only outlier is iTraxx Main, which has widened amid significant de-risking to pretty much everything: CDX IG, €IG cash (Figure 11) and equities. This also makes us see value in selling Main Payer options to buy SX5E puts.

More broadly, it simply appears that when faced with multiple uncertainties, the inclination is to de-risk in general. This also makes it harder to interpret the relative performance across credit since the weakness began on 8 April (Figure 12): BBs have underperformed while Bs have outperformed, CDS indices in general have underperformed, while hybrids in general have outperformed – in spite of IG supply being very low.

Arguably, it is dificult, if not impossible, to efectively position for a geopolitical tail event. As such, we instead focus on our base case and consider which parts of the market could be vulnerable to such a tail event.

The high yield environment remains conducive for demand for European credit, and the market consensus appears to remain for the ECB to cut in June. With economic numbers stabilising, this means that European credit as a whole is in a reasonably good place, and has some degree of protection from US rate volatility. Also, short term, IG supply should remain low amid the ongoing earnings season blackouts.

In case of a further Middle East escalation, we focus on a number of aspects: a stagflationary shock would be a HY/IG decompression event, in particular in cash, while relative positioning in Xover and Main could be a headwind to 'knee jerk' decompression. With AT1s remaining wide to

Bs and corporate hybrids wide to BBs, this makes us prefer hybrids in general to HY. Energy names, in IG and HY, could also outperform in such a scenario, and are currently not trading tight vs peers. Further, in spite of Main having underperformed recently vs cash – and everything else – with investors still fairly long risk, we still believe Main could widen quickly in case of further Middle East downside.

## Diverging...to a point

Christian Keller, Barclays, UK | Mark Cus Babic, Barclays, UK | Akash Utsav, Barclays, UK

Excerpted from Global Economics Weekly, (published on April 19, 2024

Fed policy is back to higher for longer, as disinflation has stalled and the economy continues to surprise to the upside. ## The Fed's 'higher for longer' means 'easing more carefully' for the rest

## The Fed is back to higher for longer

As the data pile up against the rate cuts penciled into the dot plot, the tone on C Street is tilting in a hawkish direction, with numerous speakers recently confirming that the FOMC is likely to hold rates higher for longer. This includes Chair Powell's commentary that "it is appropriate to allow restrictive policy further time to work and let the data and the evolving outlook guide us."<sup>2</sup> He added, "If higher inflation does persist, we can maintain the current level of restriction for as long as needed." In saying this, he did not repeat the remark from the March meeting that cuts "will likely be appropriate...at some point this year."

However, this week marks a new milestone in this shift, as the word "hike" returned to the conversation. Asked about the possibility of raising rates, New York Fed President Williams said this is not his base case, but failed to push back materially, answering that "if the data are telling us that we would need higher interest rates to achieve our goals, then we would obviously want to do that."

Rate hikes, of course, remain only a distant possibility, and Williams also maintained his benign view that "monetary policy is in a good place" as we remain on a bumpy path to the inflation target. Even so, that such a question would even be asked is symptomatic of the increased uncertainties once again facing markets and the Fed. Should the Fed have hiked more and is policy even restrictive? Is an only slightly restrictive stance enough to rein in price pressures in an economy in which demand remains strong? If so, how long of a hold will do the job? What if it fails?

For our part, we still think the combination of a reaction function that favours cuts and somewhat better inflation data over the summer will ultimately deliver the first cut in September. However, we are sceptical that policy is suficiently tight to ensure inflation continues to progress adequately to produce a second cut three months later. Instead, our forecasts see core PCE firming in y/y terms over the course of H2 and the Fed waiting for further confidence to cut again until March. However, these are nuances in an environment where the FOMC may also well wait until December to cut for the first time, and even scenarios where the Fed remains on hold through 2024 are not far from our current baseline.

Even if the Fed has an inclination towards a first cut – which we assume – it remains data dependent and the incoming numbers continue to stack up for the higher-for-longer argument. Following the strong NFPs and CPI, this week retail sales surprised sharply to the upside, suggesting consumer spending and broader economic activity remain very resilient, dispelling speculation about weakening after seasonal adjustment issues and weather-related disruptions produced a soft patch in January and February. Similarly, industrial production posted robust gains, reinforcing the strengthening in consumer goods demand and signaling a clear rebound in manufacturing. Taking this on board, we revise up our near-term outlook for growth, boosting projected GDP 0.5pp in Q2 (to 2.5% q/q saar) and Q3 (to 2.0% q/q saar).

We also look for an elevated Q1 GDP print next week, at 2.5% q/q saar, led by a thirdconsecutive quarter of robust household spending, as well as another 0.5% m/m increase in real PCE in March and a firm March core PCE print of 0.27% m/m.

## The ECB will diverge, but not without limits...

We believe the ECB is in a fundamentally diferent place as the Fed and will begin easing its policy rates in June. We expect three consecutive cuts over the summer (June, July, September). The first cut in June has been well telegraphed at last week's press conference and by a number of speakers since. Moreover, even as some indicators, including this week's 0.8% m/m gain in industrial production, support our forecast for a gradual economic recovery after 18 months of stagnation, we still see headwinds from forceful monetary transmission and only timid signs of growth. In addition, after folding in the latest energy prices, our profile for headline inflation is just slightly above the ECB's, and this week's March final HICP has increased our confidence in services disinflation. We forecast somewhat faster disinflation in core than the ECB.

After these first three cuts, we now change our call and expect the ECB to slow the pace of cuts to once per quarter, but still terminate at 2.5%, thus extending the easing cycle to June 2025. This is predicated on policy then being much closer to estimates of the neutral rate, growth improving in H2 24, and the ECB wanting to stave of large FX moves due to deviating too strongly from the Fed.

Yet this begs the question: can the ECB really diverge from the Fed even as much as we still expect, with the policy rate diferential peaking at 213bp next January, or will EUR depreciation stop it in its tracks? To answer this, in US - EA Monetary Policy Divergence: The transatlantic rift, we show that the divergence we forecast is neither particularly large nor unusual by historical standards. In addition, we see only a limited efect on inflation via the FX channel. First, the elasticity of European inflation to the exchange rate is not very big; our work suggests a 10% depreciation in EURUSD (ie, the EURUSD far below parity at c.0.95) produces only a 15bp tailwind on inflation in the year when the shock happens, though this boost could linger and

intensify later. For one, this is because as much as 40% of the euro area's imports are denominated in EUR. Second, the ECB will be focusing not just on the EURUSD, but also on the trade-weighted indices, which have fallen by less. While the EURUSD is currently c.2% weaker than in March,<sup>3</sup> the NEER is only c.0.4% lower. Third, the persistence of FX weakness matters, and we expect the policy diferential to narrow when the ECB slows its cuts and the Fed begins to ease.

Still, the exchange rate and the ECB's commentary on it bear watching, with speakers so far not suggesting it could derail the June cut, but keeping their options open. In addition, next week, we will follow March credit data for signals about monetary policy transmission, and the April PMIs, which we expect to continue to improve, rising to 50.9 from 50.3.

## China's growth worse than its appearance

China's Q1 GDP this week beat expectations by a wide margin, accelerating to 12.7% q/q saar from 3.2% q/q saar in Q4. However, we think these figures do not fully reflect the economic reality. China has a history of over-reporting GDP during periods of economic weakness, and we see discrepancies in the released data that are hard to ignore. Also, demand indicators such as retail sales have been significantly softer than production measures such as industrial production, but these are not incorporated in China's production-based GDP gauge. Americas Agribusiness (Neu)

A Herd of Rating Changes

16 April 2024

Benjamin M. Theurer, Rahi Parikh, Ryan Lavin, Reid Monahan

Americas Agribusiness

Following a volatile 2023 for protein – highlighted by worsening performance in beef – we expect a similar trend for the remainder of 2024. For beef, we expect the U.S. cattle cycle to continue its downward trend, as heifer retention has yet to hit herd-building levels, leading to continued profitability weakness. Pork profitability should improve as input costs drop – primarily in feed – and the hog oversupply corrects itself. Finally, we continue to prefer chicken as the price gap vs. beef widens and the supply/demand balance normalizes. With these dynamics at play we make a series of rating and price target changes. We upgrade TSN to OW (from UW) and raise its PT to \$69, we upgrade JBS to OW (from EW) with a new PT of R\$30, and Marfrig to EW (from UW) with a new PT of R\$11. We downgrade PPC to EW (from OW), given its relative outperformance YTD (PPC shares are up 27% vs. the S&P Consumer Staples Index +3.4%), but raise its PT to \$38. BRF (OW) and HRL (EW) remain unchanged.

Building the Pipes of AI, Initiate at OW

15 April 2024

Tom O'Malley, Scott Fessler, William Levy, Kyle Bleustein

U.S. Semiconductors & Semiconductor Capital Equipment

Astera Labs is uniquely positioned as a first mover/early market leader across a diverse set of data center connectivity products that sit at the intersection of nascent secular trends in AI and cloud computing.
