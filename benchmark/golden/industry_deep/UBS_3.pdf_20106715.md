# Global Equity Strategy 10 surprises for 2024

We have identified 10 potential surprises for 2024. These are events that are possible (i.e. where we think the risks lie) but are not the UBS or the marketimplied central case (as it is understood today). Should any of these surprises come to bear this year, we examine the investment implications of each scenario:

Surprise 1: S&P 500 rises 20% (core view: 5,400 S&P 500 by our US strategist, Jonathan Golub).

Surprise 2: China nominal GDP slows to 3% (core view is that nominal GDP growth accelerates to 5.1% in 2024 with real GDP growth of 4.6%).

Surprise 3: The yield curve steepens to 2% (3 month/10 years). Core view 60bp (3 month/10 years).

Surprise 4: Bonds cease to diversify again (core view is that they diversify).

Surprise 5: European pharma ends up being the best performing sector in Europe in 2023 (core view of European Equity strategy team is pharma is an underweight).

Surprise 6: Neither Biden nor Trump are elected President in 2024 (polls suggest Trump is the likely winner).

Surprise 7: Peace in Ukraine (no core view).

Surprise 8: Japan volatility rises sharply relative to US (core view is that it falls relative to the US).

Surprise 9: Apple's market cap falls below \$2trn in 2024 (core view is Apple is rated Neutral and price target implies a market cap of \$2.9trn).

Surprise 10: US rates fall but US corporate interest charge rises (core view: it falls).

As a reminder: Overall, we have a cautious view of markets (750 MSCI AC World), but see a 35% chance of a 15% rally in equities (based on productivity potentially rising to 2.5% under Gen AI resulting in a Nifty 50 style event). From a regional perspective, our focus is on emerging markets (Brazil, Taiwan and Korea) and Japan in dollar terms. Small cap are pricing in no economic recovery in the US, Europe and the UK. Quality normally outperforms as rates fall and is at valuation levels where it has historically outperformed. From a sector perspective, we favour defensives (healthcare in particular), but our largest overweight remains software. We also like Asian semis and select cyclicals (tyres, copper, European/Japanese banks, UK retailing). See our 2024 outlook for more details.

# Surprise 1: Generative AI does actually increase productivity growth to 2.5%, fuelling a 20% return from equities in 2024.

Core view: UBS US strategy team forecast 5400 on the S&P 500 year-end. We, on the global equity strategy team, are more cautious with a global MSCI AC World of 750 (but critically with a 35% chance of a 15% rise in global markets).

## Surprise view:

Gen AI is the ‘steam engine of the mind’, it is the ‘automation of the application of expertise’. We show in our outlook that there are copious examples of Gen AI already having a substantial impact (25% decrease in time spent with 40% increase in quality of work according to a study by Boston Consulting Group, to name but one of very many examples, see here).

Gen AI is capital-light and the infrastructure is already in place, and thus its diffusion rate can be abnormally quick. Randy Abrams, our Asia tech analyst, refers to 1.8bn monthly users of Chat GPT (see here).

The ICT revolution increased productivity by 1.5-2% and thus it's quite conceivable that investors will take the view that Gen AI can have a similar impact.

By the time we find evidence in the economic data for the productivity revolution, we could have had both the stock market boom and potentially the associated bust. In the Information Communication Technology (ICT) revolution, Alan Greenspan postulated (in his 17 October 1996 speech) that productivity was improving (namely, 'radical transformations in what we produce in the way of goods and services and how we produce them occur perhaps once or twice in a century at most'), but the 5-year moving average of productivity did not rise above 2.5% until February 2000, after the Nasdaq had tripled since Greenspan's speech and just before it fell by nearly 80%.

The danger is that investors use an improvement in the highly cyclical quarterly productivity numbers to embolden their view that Gen AI will boost productivity (with US Q4 productivity rising by 3.2%).

If productivity is 2.5% rather than the 1.5% that both the Fed and UBS economists assume, then:

i) Inflation very clearly undershoots. Wage growth of c4% at the moment would be consistent with unit labour costs of 1.5%, not 2.5%.

This means that the Fed are clearly undershooting their inflation target and can cut rates much quicker than the market expects to get back to a neutral rate (of 2.5%). The market is pricing in a trough Fed Funds rate of 3.6%.

ii) We would be mid-cycle not late cycle. This is because if inflation is undershooting, then full employment (i.e. the unemployment rate consistent with a 2% average inflation target) is way below the Fed estimate of 4.1% – and could easily be 2.5% to 3%.

iii) The margin squeeze would diminish.

iv) Most importantly, the ERP would be 1.2% higher at 5.1% (not 3.9%) versus a warranted ERP of 4.2% and thus equities would easily have c20% upside potential. If it took, say, 3 years for this productivity increase to come through, then the upside for equities would be around 17%.

These bubble periods get to a P/E of 45-72X (in Japan's case, this was the whole market, which at that stage was nearly 40% of global market cap) and an ERP for the whole market falling to 1.7%, and often lower.

In the TMT revolution, the P/E ratio of the whole market peaked at c25X 12-month forward earnings (versus 20.4X currently), even when the 10-year index-linked bond yield was c4%. As is the case recently, the stock market re-rated while the TIPS yield rose.

We have 5 out of 8 preconditions for an early-cycle bubble, namely:

i) perceived change in productivity brought often by technical change;

ii) happens with a minimum gap of 25 years from the previous episode;

iii) happens at end of secular bull market when aggregate profits come under pressure (as was the case in Figure 5 in the TMT period when national account profits fell and recorded EPS rose – but almost certainty recorded EPS would have fallen if shareholderbased compensation had been accounted for correctly);

iv) sees a more acute loss of breadth (because profits are under pressure)

A clear example of what happens in this environment was 1998-2000, when the advance decline line fell while the market rose, or Japan 1985-1990.

Namely, central banks might end up printing money (if the PBOC prints aggressively as in point 3 and the Fed stops QT once excess reserves fall below \$2.9trn, which may happen some time before October).

The other precondition that we are missing is heavy retail involvement. This could change.

So far, the net buying of equities has been corporates, but if rates fall with nearly \$6trn in money market funds, there could be a cash-to-equity switch.

Thus, potentially the only precondition we are missing for a bubble is to have an extended period of time with a limited drawdown (e.g. at the peak of the TMT bubble, the S&P 500 had not corrected by 20% or more for nearly 13 years). But even here, the Nifty 50 could provide a clue (the stock market fell 36% after the 1969 peak but then made a new high in mid-'72 (in nominal terms) and peaked in January '73 with the Nifty 50 peak (though in real terms the stock market never regained its previous peak).

The final upside risk is that we are in fact in a similar situation to 1995, 1998 and 2019, and hence this is a mid-cycle slowdown because the full employment rate is much lower than the Fed estimate (of 4.1%, or the CBO estimate of 4.3%).

On average, 4 months after the mid-cycle rate cuts of 1995, 1998 and 2019, markets were up 12%.

The evidence that we are mid-cycle would be that labour has lost pricing power even on a 3.7% unemployment rate.

The voluntary job quits ratio (the preferred measure of ex Fed Chairperson Janet Yellen) is consistent with wage growth falling to 3%.

If this is backed up by all the other measures (which so far it is not), this could potentially mean that full employment is c2.5%.

The conclusion would have to be that despite deglobalisation and higher minimum wages, labour has lost pricing power (and we are back to the days of a horizontal Phillips curve).

Investment implications: be long of equities and especially those areas that benefit from Gen AI (software, semis). We also think a bubble would potentially spread to other themes (electrification, automation and GLP 1). We know that when bubbles burst, prices can fall c80% and thus we believe if this was a credible scenario, investors should only invest until the equity risk premium falls (on 2.5% productivity) down to 4.2% (i.e. our warranted ERP).

## Surprise 2: China nominal GDP slows to 3%.

UBS view: China grows by 4.6% in 2024 (from 4.1% quarterly annualised in Q4 23) with nominal GDP accelerating to 5.1% from 4.6% in 2023.

Surprise view: Nominal GDP growth slows sharply.

Our China economics team has already explored this scenario (alternative scenario 2) in the November Economics and Strategy outlook.

China has had something of a triple bubble – credit, real estate and excess investment.

We can see this if we look at the build-up of credit – only Thailand (ahead of a severe recession in '98), Spain and Ireland (ahead of their severe recessions in 2011) saw bigger increases over a 10-year period. Private sector leverage is well above the US (as a % of GDP).

There are 6 main downside risks of GDP growth being slower than expected:

a) The excess capacity is more endemic and Chinese authorities are slow to shut it down. This causes ongoing deflation and means that despite rates being cut, real rates rise. Our China autos analyst, for example, estimates capacity utilisation in the autos sector at 60- 70%.

b) House prices fall below collateral values. Real estate is around 30% of bank assets but closer to 55% of collateral. Normally, the proportion of collateral supplied by real estate is underestimated.

The LTVs may well be much higher than assumed. The official LTVs are c28% (for assets, and probably when we consider collateral, closer to 50%) and hence there should be substantial leeway before property prices fall below collateral value, but the risk is, as always, that there is more hidden leverage in the system and thus LTVs are indeed higher.

Another 10% to 20% decline in real estate prices might push loans below collateral value (with anecdotal evidence that house prices are down c.25% in Tier 1 cities from their peak and down c30% from their peak in Tier 2 cities, substantially more than in the official data).

Moreover, the Bloomberg diffusion indicator for property or the price of property developers (relative to the market) are consistent with a further sharp fall in house prices. As shown already, property affordability still looks stretched.

We believe that if house prices fall 40-50% from peak, then we get in the territory of negative collateral.

If house prices fall below collateral values, then there is normally a substantial risk of a large rise in NPLs (not so much in real estate but on those 20% to 25% of non real estate loans that are collateralised by real estate).

This would require a bad bank to be set up (similar to Resolution and Trust Bank after the S&L crisis in the late 1980s in the US).

The risk is that the authorities might be slow to react which would then prolong a debt crisis.

c) The role of housing has been underestimated.

Housing is around 10% of GDP directly and c15% indirectly (with houses being warehouses for goods), c30% of local government revenue and at least c60% of household wealth.

In the case of the US, Spain, Ireland and Japan, within a year of falling house prices, we had a recession.

d) Much more restrictive trade policies.

President Trump has already mentioned a 10% tariff across the board. In the event of President Trump winning re-election, it’s quite possible that he might impose much higher tariffs on those products where the US perceives that there is unfair competition (we saw this in 2018 with steel and solar tariffs of 25%). Additionally, this time around, China may find it harder to export its excess capacity in Europe where already there is an investigation into rising EV imports and Europe could, for example, re-impose its 2013 anti-dumping duty on solar.

e) There is little fiscal room for manoeuvre without the PBOC buying bonds

The IMF highlights that the structural budget deficit is 7.1% of GDP in 2023 and the augmented net borrowing (which includes governmental guides funds and LGFVs) at 13.6% of GDP with an augmented government debt to GDP of 116%.

f) Policy is reactive.

Essentially a huge fiscal/monetary response is needed once economies start to see deflation. The risk is such a response is lagging. We saw this lagging response in particular in Japan in the 1990s.

## Impact:

i) Disinflation = bullish for global bonds. Export prices are already falling at 5% a year. This is clearly bad for global growth. China accounts for c17% of global GDP, but since 2010 has accounted for 36% of the increase in global GDP.

ii) The RMB weakens in response to both lower rates and the PBOC printing to finance more government debt. This again causes China to exports more dis-inflation and also poses a challenge to Asian trading partners.

iii) Big headwind for global capital investment. 52% of the increase in global capex since 2010 has come from China (with China accounting for just over a quarter of global capex). Hence this risk would be clearly very bad for mining, with China accounting for just over half of copper demand and nearly three quarters of seaborne iron ore demand. It would also be highly problematic for companies exposed to the China capital goods cycle (Kone, Schindler).

iv) It would hit cyclical sectors, especially luxury and semis.

Luxury has particularly high exposure. 60% of incremental growth and 35-40% of end demand is China-related and still has a surprisingly high revenue consensus for this year at 7.5%.

This is also a problem for global semis where up to c20% end demand is China-related, according to our analysts. Apple has nearly 20% of revenues from China.

We can see that there has been huge outperformance of the indirect China plays versus the direct. They are also very expensive on PE relative to the direct plays.

i) The short end falls more than the market expects (which would be in line with the UBS house view).

The four factors that could cause GDP to fall more than the consensus now expects (in line with the UBS view) are:

a) Excess savings have been largely used. They are now just \$0.75trn or 3% of GDP (having peaked at \$2.5 trn). Most of the excess savings are in the upper-income quintile, who will not spend it.

b) Fiscal tightening after big fiscal easing. UBS sees fiscal policy taking about 0.2% off GDP this year, having added 1% to GDP in '23, and in a recent note highlighted that the US fiscal deficit for 2024 by the CBO has been revised down sharply (\$2trn to \$1.6trn, after adjusting for booking issues – see here). It is quite possible that the delta change is even bigger, with the IMF predicting fiscal tightening of 1.4% of GDP this year after an easing of 1.9% of GDP in 2023.

The fall in excess savings and fiscal easing may have added as much as 5-6% to GDP growth in 2023. These two drivers are absent in 2024.

## ii) The fiscal position becomes more worrying.

Meanwhile the yield curve steepens because of the view that either a Biden/Trump clean sweep results in even more aggressive fiscal easing.

GDP would be 131% (from 99% currently and more than double that post-1974 average of 48% government debt to GDP) with the budget deficit never falling below 5% of GDP. Even to get the deficit in line with CBO’s pessimistic projections would require a very sharp cut in discretionary public spending (from 6.4% of GDP to 5.1%)

## Catalyst:

Why might bonds not diversify? Market could anticipate a permanent change in the attitude in the US and UK towards fiscal policy.

## Valuation Method and Risk Statement

Investing in global equities poses currency, country, industry, and company-specific risks. Valuations can be impacted by company-specific factors, investor risk appetite, as well as changes in the macroeconomic landscape and financial market stability.

Apple: Risks to our Apple thesis include (1) product delays or less innovative offerings, particularly a decline in iPhone unit shipments; (2) macro weakness dampening product demand, especially in China; (3) reduced product differentiation resulting in a successful smartphone attack from below; (4) governments looking to regulate Apple as its power increases; and (5) poor platform management.
