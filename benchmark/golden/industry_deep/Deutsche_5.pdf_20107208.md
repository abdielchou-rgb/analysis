## Our March 2024 global financial market survey had around 250 responses from around the world, conducted between the 20<sup>th</sup> and 22<sup>nd</sup> of March.

![](images/49860a28a83bb9b84425f6271838c146414971960184eccafae20dd5dd1e693d.jpg)

Our respondents are leaning slightly towards the ‘no landing’ (45%) over the ‘soft landing’ scenario (38%). Clearly the issue of persistent inflation remains the key uncertainty for the market, rather than fears of a recession, as only 17% believe the next phase for the US is a ‘hard landing’.

By the end of 2024, which phrase will best describe where the US economy will be?

More and more have pushed out expectations of a US recession beyond 2024, with only 13% now thinking it happens this year down from 62% who thought it would occur by YE 24 back in December. The ‘Don’t know’ result jumped from 11% to 19%.

When do you think the next US recession will occur? It will occur in…

Our respondents believe that equities and credit spreads are firmly pricing in a soft landing in the US, at 84% and 83% respectively, up from 76% and 71% in December. For the top 90-100% bucket, it's gone up from 18% to 35% for equities and from 15% to 35% for credit in just over 3 months.

Our respondents are almost uniformly split on the direction central banks should take next regarding inflation targets. 43% believe central banks should not compromise their 2% inflation target, while 47% think it's best to tolerate an extended overshoot before attempting to return to target. 10% think they should revisit their formal target.

![](images/3d34d8aeea814f523d17c27bd89a06e361846052a63b5248937c5c76fc403df0.jpg)

Even though ‘no landing’ was a popular answer earlier, longer-term inflation expectations have continued to fall even with recent US inflation stickiness. Average US 5yr inflation expectations have edged further down from 2.7% in Q4 to 2.6%, the lowest since Q3 ‘21. In Europe, they’ve fallen further from 2.4% to 2.1%, the lowest since Q1 ‘22.

On central banks, now that Japan has concluded its multi-year experiment with negative rates, 63% believe that we will not see another major economy return to negative interest rates within the next decade. 26% do though!

![](images/3f070d430d44272a58ebfae2c35c136c104e4f0cce1ae26d71a6170a9c2dfc28.jpg)

With US equities hitting record highs, and Bitcoin surging to new heights, we again asked readers about bubbles, for the first time in three years. The largest bubble risks appears in Bitcoin (7.9), but also in the Mag 7 (7.4). US tech equities (6.9) and US credit (6.2) are also above 6. Non-tech US equities (4.5), EU equities (4.0) and Asian equities (3.7) score low on the bubble watch.

Macroeconomic fluctuations often account for most of the risks associated with exposures to instruments that promise to pay fixed or variable interest rates. For an investor who is long fixed-rate instruments (thus receiving these cash flows), increases in interest rates naturally lift the discount factors applied to the expected cash flows and thus cause a loss. The longer the maturity of a certain cash flow and the higher the move in the discount factor, the higher will be the loss. Upside surprises in inflation, fiscal funding needs, and FX depreciation rates are among the most common adverse macroeconomic shocks to receivers. But counterparty exposure, issuer creditworthiness, client segmentation, regulation (including changes in assets holding limits for different types of investors), changes in tax policies, currency convertibility (which may constrain currency conversion, repatriation of profits and/or liquidation of positions), and settlement issues related to local clearing houses are also important risk factors. The sensitivity of fixed-income instruments to macroeconomic shocks may be mitigated by indexing the contracted cash flows to inflation, to FX depreciation, or to specified interest rates – these are common in emerging markets.
