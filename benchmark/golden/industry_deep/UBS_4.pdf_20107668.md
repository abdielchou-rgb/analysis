# European Economic Perspectives How will ECB balance sheet and liquidity evolve?

## We update our forecasts for the ECB balance sheet and Eurozone liquidity

In March, the ECB released its new "operational framework" for implementing monetary policy, choosing a "demand-driven" floor system, with ample liquidity. Based on the key features of the new framework, we can now estimate how the ECB balance sheet and Eurosystem (excess) liquidity will evolve over the coming years.

## Three key determinants of ECB balance sheet and liquidity

The ECB balance sheet and Eurosystem liquidity will depend on three key determinants: (1) the roll-off of the ECB's remaining TLTRO loans to Eurozone banks (€141bn), the quantitative tightening (QT) related to the Asset Purchase Programme (APP, €3trn) and the Pandemic Emergency Purchase Programme (PEPP, €1.7trn); (2) the growth of the liability side of the ECB balance sheet (mainly driven by autonomous factors); and (3) banks' demand for excess reserves (currently €3.5trn).

## Early 2027: Liquidity to get tight, ECB balance sheet to start growing again

Based on our projections for these three key determinants, we expect that, in early 2027, Eurosystem aggregate excess liquidity will have tightened from €3.5trn currently to around €1.25trn, and become "tight", i.e. fall below banks' comfort level. (The uneven distribution of excess reserves across the Eurozone implies that, in some countries, banking sectors or individual banks, partial liquidity shortages might emerge a lot earlier.) As a result, banks would use the ECB's refinancing facilities again more actively and the ECB would start to build up its structural bond portfolio. Around that time, having fallen from the current €6.6trn (46% of GDP) to around €5.3trn (33% of GDP), the ECB balance sheet would stop contracting and start growing again by around €100bn per year, while remaining broadly constant in terms of GDP.

## QT (APP/PEPP) and build-up of structural bond portfolio to overlap

We assume that the ECB will allow its QE portfolio (APP and PEPP) to largely expire over time, to reduce the ECB's footprint in crucial parts of the sovereign bond markets. According to our estimates, between now and end-2030, the APP and PEPP holdings will shrink by about two-thirds, from €4.6trn currently to €1.5trn, with further declines thereafter. However, as of 2027, when liquidity likely tightens and the balance sheet starts to grow again, the ECB − according to its new operational framework − would start building up its structural bond portfolio (SBP). At least initially, the SBP is likely to be considerably smaller than the ECB's current bond holdings. Mainly aimed at reserve creation, the SBP's portfolio and maturity structure might be different from the ECB's QE portfolio.

## ECB balance sheet to remain a lot larger than before the GFC

Despite the expected shrinkage over the coming years, the Eurosystem balance sheet is likely to remain larger in the future than before the Global Financial Crisis (GFC), given: (1) banks' structurally higher demand for central bank reserves amid tighter regulation and risk management; (2) the likely more frequent use of QE; (3) the risk of Eurozone fragmentation, which would require a more active role for the ECB and its balance sheet; and (4) the potential introduction of a digital euro.

## The ECB's new operational framework

On 13 March, the ECB released its new "operational framework" for implementing monetary policy. Although the new framework will not affect upcoming interest rate decisions, it will be of major importance for the future conduct of ECB monetary policy and determine: (1) how the ECB will steer short-term rates in the future; (2) what monetary policy instruments it will deploy; and (3) how far the ECB will be able to proceed with quantitative tightening (QT), and how Eurozone liquidity and the Eurosystem balance sheet will evolve over time. Based on the cornerstones of the new ECB framework, we can now update our forecast of how Eurosystem liquidity and the Eurosystem balance sheet will develop over the coming years.

On 13 March, the ECB presented its new operational framework for implementing monetary policy

We think the key features of the new operational framework reflect three key intentions on the side of the ECB:

 First, to maintain an elastic supply of central bank reserves over time, to prevent unwarranted tightening in liquidity conditions;

 Second, to prevent unwarranted interest volatility in a Eurozone where excess liquidity is unevenly distributed;

 Third, to encourage greater activity in interbank money markets.

## Key features of the ECB's new framework

Most importantly, the ECB decided in favour of a demand-driven floor system with ample liquidity. "Demand-driven" means that the ECB will allow banks to borrow as much liquidity as they want, against eligible collateral. As such, the ECB decided against a return to the (corridor) system of tight liquidity that was in place before the GFC. It also decided against the "supply-driven" floor system of the Fed, which provides abundant liquidity, but with the level decided at the Fed's discretion.

The ECB will deploy multiple instruments to provide short-term, medium-term and longer-term liquidity. The ECB will offer regular refinancing operations at the refi rate, using the fixed-rate full allotment mode, i.e. allowing banks to borrow as much liquidity as they want, as long as they can provide collateral. Initially, the ECB will mainly offer one-week operations (main refinancing operations, MROs) and three-month operations. At a later stage, "once the Eurosystem balance sheet begins to grow durably again", it will also offer refinancing operations with longer maturities and build up a structural bond portfolio, to provide liquidity on a more permanent basis. However, at least initially, we expect the structural bond portfolio to be considerably smaller than the ECB's current bond holdings. Since the structural portfolio will be mainly aimed at reserve creation, its portfolio and maturity structure might be different from the ECB's QE portfolio (e.g. focus on shorter maturities, green bonds or supranationals, no adherence to capital key, etc).

To prevent unwarranted interest rate volatility, the ECB will complement the demanddriven floor system with a narrow corridor. The depo rate (currently at 4.0%) will remain the key policy rate. The spread between the depo rate and the refi rate (which will be charged in the refinancing operations) will be reduced from currently 50bp to just 15bp. As a backstop, the ECB will also maintain the marginal lending facility, where banks can borrow overnight liquidity at short notice against collateral; the marginal lending rate (MLR) will be set 40bp above the depo and 25bp above the refi. With that, the corridor width will be reduced from currently 75bp to 40bp. The new interest rate corridor will be implemented on 18 September. According to the ECB, short-term money market rates should settle "in the vicinity" of the depo rate. As long as liquidity remains abundant, we would expect this to be very close to the depo rate. Once liquidity becomes scarcer, short-term rates could rise a bit above the depo rate towards the refi rate, but the structural liquidity operations would then ensure that this does not go too far. The relatively narrow corridor is intended to strike a balance between limiting interest rate volatility and stigma and setting an incentive for banks to use interbank markets again more actively (Figures 1 and 2).

## ECB balance sheet − history and outlook

## A short history of the ECB balance sheet

The Eurosystem balance sheet has seen dramatic growth since the global financial crisis (GFC), expanding from €1.1trn in early 2007 to a peak of €8.8trn in September 2022, before moderating again to €6.6trn most recently; from here, it will likely decline for another few years. In terms of Eurozone GDP, the balance sheet grew from a low-teens percentage in the early 2000s to 69% in 2021 before falling to 46% most recently (Figure 3).

The expansion of the Eurosystem balance sheet took place in four waves: the GFC (to 20% of GDP), the Eurozone debt crisis (to 32% of GDP), the 2015-18 period of the first QE programme (to 39% of GDP), and the Covid crisis (69%). The balance sheet expansion in the first two phases (GFC and Eurozone crisis) was mainly driven by the ECB's longer-term refinancing operations (LTROs), while the expansion from 2015 was due to asset purchases and longer-term refinancing operations (TLTROs). The recent shrinkage of the balance sheet has been dominated by the expiry of TLTROs, with quantitative tightening (QT) related to the APP playing a minor role. The balance sheet expansion went along with a large build-up of excess liquidity, which peaked at €4.6trn in October 2022, before moderating to €3.5trn most recently (Figure 4).

The Eurosystem balance sheet (in terms of GDP) is larger than the balance sheets of the BoE and the Fed, but smaller than the ones of the BoJ and SNB (Figure 5).

## Three determinants of the Eurosystem balance sheet

Within the context of the ECB's new operational framework, the future evolution of the Eurosystem balance sheet will depend on three key determinants:

Three factors to dominate evolution of Eurosystem balance sheet over time

## 1. The roll-off of TLTROs and QT related to APP and PEPP;

## 2. The growth of the liability side of the ECB balance sheet;

## 3. Banks' demand for excess reserves.

Of these three factors, the ECB will only be able to determine the first (QT), while Eurozone residents and governments will determine the second (growth of the liability side) and Eurozone banks the third item (demand for excess reserves). In other words, the ECB will not be in full control of the future evolution of the Eurosystem balance sheet.

We now look at the three determinants in greater detail.

## 1. Run-off of TLTROs, APP and PEPP

Since peaking at €8.8trn (69% of Eurozone GDP) in early 2022, the Eurosystem balance sheet has shrunk by around €2trn, to €6.6trn (46% of GDP). Around 90% of this shrinkage was due to the expiry of TLTROs, with QT related to the ECB's asset purchase programmes (APP and PEPP) accounting for most of the rest. Over the coming years, the expiry of TLTROs and QT related to the APP and PEPP will continue to dominate the shrinkage of the Eurosystem balance sheet. However, since more than 90% of the TLTROs have already matured, the relative importance of QT is going to increase, with assets related to the APP and the PEPP still amounting to around €4.6trn. Below, we look at the TLTROs, APP and PEPP in a bit more detail.

TLTRO repayments have dominated ECB balance sheet shrinkage in 2022- 24; QT related to APP and PEPP to dominate going forward

## (a) Expiry of TLTROs

The 10 auctions of the ECB's TLTRO-III "funding for lending" programme took place between September 2019 and December 2021. The volume of TLTRO-III funds peaked in Q4 2021, at €2.2trn, exceeding a quarter of the Eurosystem balance sheet, but has in the meantime moderated to "just" €141bn. Given that the TLTRO-III loans have a threeyear maturity, they will roll off the ECB's balance sheet by December 2024 at the latest, making the TLTROs the dominant driver of ECB balance sheet shrinkage in 2022-24 (Figure 6).

## (b) QT related to the APP

The ECB conducted net asset purchases under its Asset Purchase Programme (APP) from March 2015 to December 2018, and then again from November 2019 to June 2022, during which time net purchases fluctuated between €15bn and €80bn per month (Figure 7). The value of assets held under the programme peaked in June 2022 at €3.3trn (37% of the Eurosystem balance sheet) and have in the meantime fallen to €3trn. The APP consists of four individual programmes, the largest of which is the Public Sector Purchase Programme (PSPP), with current assets worth €2.4trn, or 80% of the APP; some 90% of these assets are sovereign bonds and 10% are supranational bonds. The average maturity of the sovereign bond portfolio is seven years. The other programmes are the Corporate Sector Purchase Programme (CSPP, €319bn, 10.7%), the Asset-Backed Securities Purchase Programme (ABSPP, €12.6bn, 0.4%), and the (third) Covered Bond Purchase Programme (CBPP3, €279bn, or 9.4%) (Figure 8).

From March to June 2023, the ECB reinvested only about 50% of the maturing APP securities, which led to a decline in the APP portfolio by around €15bn per month. In July 2023, the ECB stopped the APP reinvestment altogether, which accelerated the decline in APP holdings to around €26bn per month.

Based on ECB information on the APP redemptions over the next 12 months, we think the stock of APP securities will shrink by an average €28bn per month this year, to €2.7trn by end-2024. Our estimates for end-2025 and end-2026 are €2.3trn and €2trn, respectively (Figure 9).

## (c) QT related to the PEPP

The Pandemic Emergency Purchase Programme (PEPP) ran from March 2020 until March 2022. During this time, the ECB purchased assets worth €1.7trn, predominantly sovereign bonds (97% of the PEPP, currently valued at €1.6bn), but also corporate bonds (€46bn, 2.8%), covered bonds (€6bn, 0.4%) and commercial paper (all of which have now matured). The PEPP currently represents 24% of the ECB's balance sheet. The average maturity of the ECB's sovereign bond portfolio under the PEPP is 7.3 years.

In December 2023, the ECB announced that it would: (a) continue to reinvest the proceeds of maturing PEPP securities in full until June 2024; (b) scale back reinvestments to around 50% between July and December 2024 (which would cause the PEPP portfolio to shrink by an average €7.5bn per month); and (c) stop reinvesting entirely as of January 2025, so that all maturing PEPP securities would roll off its balance sheet. As a result, we expect the ECB's €1.7trn PEPP portfolio to shrink to €1.6trn by end-2024, €1.4trn by end-2025, €1.2trn by end-2026, and €0.9trn by end-2027 (Figure 13).

## 2. Growth of liability side of ECB balance sheet

Before the GFC, the size of the Eurosystem balance sheet was dominated by developments on the liability side of the balance sheet. As such, it was mainly a function of the liquidity needs of the banking system, i.e. minimum reserve requirements and autonomous factors (i.e. balance sheet positions that normally lie outside of the control of the ECB, such as banknotes in circulation, government deposits and net foreign assets (NFA)). However, the situation changed fundamentally with the GFC. Since then, the Eurosystem balance sheet has been dominated by developments on the asset side, particularly ECB liquidity injections through (T)LTROs, and QE. As the asset side grew dramatically, the corresponding entry on the liability side of the balance sheet was an increase in ECB liabilities vis-à-vis Eurozone credit institutions and (Eurozone and non-Eurozone) residents (essentially the entities from which the ECB had bought QE assets and the banks that had borrowed TLTROs and received ECB deposits in return) (see Figures 15 and 16, where a lot of the ECB's liabilities against credit institutions and residents are marked as excess liquidity, in grey).

Key drivers of the ECB's balance sheet: From the liability side (pre-GFC) to the asset side and eventually back to the liability side

Going forward, as the last TLTROs expire and the securities bought under APP and PEPP mature, the asset side will shrink and so will the corresponding liabilities. But eventually, the point will be reached when the balance sheet cannot shrink any further because the secular growth of the liability side, mainly autonomous factors, will "take over" again. At that stage, the Eurosystem balance sheet will start growing again. Accordingly, when we forecast the evolution of the Eurosystem balance sheet we need to consider key drivers on the asset side (TLTROs, APP, PEPP) and the liability side.

Eventually, the liability side (mainly autonomous factors) will "take over" again and cause the ECB balance sheet to grow again

In Figure 17, we show the development of autonomous factors (AF) and their subcomponents. AF saw a steady upward trend between 2002 and 2020, but then experienced a sharp increase in 2020-23; this was due to higher debt issuance during the Covid pandemic, which led to an increase in government deposits. More recently, government deposits have returned to (and even below) pre-Covid levels, so that autonomous factors have moderated again.

Long-term development of the liability side likely to be driven by autonomous factors, above all banknotes in circulation

How will AF develop from here? Historically, banknotes in circulation have been the key driver of autonomous factors, with a share of around 70%. Between 2008 and mid-2022, Eurosystem banknotes in circulation grew by an average 5.8% per year. In 2023, however, the value of banknotes in circulation declined (for the first time since 2002), which reduced the average growth over the past 15 years to 5.4% p.a. We assume that this was mainly due to higher interest rates − and hence temporary − but structural factors, such as the digitalisation of payments, might also have been at play.

Was the 2023 decline in cash circulation a one-off or the beginning of a new trend?

For our forecast (until 2030), we mechanically assume that the 5.4% trend growth rate will resume, and that the other subcomponents of AF will be constant. In this scenario, autonomous factors would grow by an average €100bn p.a. Ongoing growth in AF also seems to be the ECB's base-case assumption<sup>(5)</sup> However, we acknowledge significant uncertainty around this estimate, given the recently much slower growth (even contraction) in banknotes, which might not just be a temporary phenomenon, but could potentially be a new trend, due to digitalisation. Hence, we acknowledge that the liability side might grow more slowly than our base case scenario implies.

## 3. Banks' demand for excess reserves

The third determinant of the future growth of the Eurosystem balance sheet is banks' demand for excess reserves, which will depend on considerations related to regulatory requirements, risk management, and opportunity costs.

Central bank reserves are overnight balances held by commercial banks at the central bank and enable banks to meet their payment obligations. Together with currency in circulation, they are the most liquid and risk-free asset available in the financial system and play a pivotal role in settling payments. They therefore serve as the backbone of banks’ liquidity management.

Generally, banks' demand for central bank reserves is driven by three factors:

1. Minimum reserve requirements (MRR), set by the central bank;

2. Autonomous factors (as discussed: factors outside of the control of the ECB, such as banknotes in circulation, deposits by governments or supranationals, and NFA); and

How much excess reserves will banks want to hold in the future?

Key drivers of demand for central bank reserves: minimum reserve requirements, autonomous factors, and demand for excess reserves

3. Demand for excess reserves, i.e. banks' reserves holdings beyond required reserves.

As Philip Lane has argued<sup>(8)</sup> , the appropriate level of central bank reserves will likely rise over time, and is likely to be positively correlated with:

Reserve demand likely to rise over time

 The size of the banking system, the broader financial system, and the overall economy;

 The likelihood and potential severity of macro-financial tail events (the greater the perceived risk, the bigger the security buffer of central bank reserves that banks might want to hold);

 The tightness of micro- and macro-prudential regulation and supervisory oversight (regulation will determine the amount of high-quality liquid assets (HQLA) that banks need to hold);

 The scarcity of safe assets (good availability of HQLA reduces banks' need to hold central bank reserves);

 The mobility of deposits (the greater the risk of sudden deposit losses, the greater the demand for reserves as safety buffers);

 In addition, demand for central bank reserves will depend on the opportunity cost of holding reserves as opposed to other, higher-yielding assets; these will usually rise with the level of interest rates.

 Banks' demand will also depend on whether or not the central bank has a reputation of quickly and proactively expanding liquidity provisions in times of financial stress; the more agile a central bank tends to be, the less need for banks to hold large amounts of precautionary reserves.

 Lastly, banks' business models, risk management and risk appetite (vis-à-vis liquidity and volatility risk) will matter.

While the ECB had a good idea about banks' demand for reserves in the pre-GFC era, when it operated a (liquidity-tight) corridor system, nowadays banks' demand for reserves is uncertain. It is obvious that demand for reserves is now a lot higher than prior to the GFC, for structural reasons, such as tighter risk management and stricter financial regulation − but by exactly how much is difficult to estimate.

## At what reserve levels will liquidity become tight?

At what level of excess reserves will liquidity finally become tight? As Figure 18 shows, the relationship between excess liquidity and the spread between the deposit rate and €STR is not linear, but inverse. As excess liquidity drops from the currently still very abundant level of €3.5trn, the depo-€STR spread will initially move very little, or not at all. However, once excess liquidity has been sufficiently eroded, the incremental impact on €STR will become much stronger. Consequently, forecasting upfront how €STR will react as excess liquidity declines is difficult.

## What do the available estimates suggest?

According to an IMF study from 2018 (based on 1999-2018 data and hence partly influenced by the pre-GFC period), short-term interest rates are "un-anchored" (i.e. anchored neither to the refi rate nor the deposit rate) at excess reserve levels of €7- 400bn. (10)

In a study from 2021, the ECB estimated that, during the period from October 2010 to December 2013, when Eurosystem liquidity was relatively low compared with today, EONIA started to increase when excess liquidity was around €400bn. However, from January 2014 to December 2020, when liquidity had risen substantially, money market (11) rates started to take off when excess liquidity was around €1trn.

Last but not least, in a study presented at the 2023 ECB Forum in Sintra, Annette Vissing-Jørgensen estimated that banks' preferred holdings of excess liquidity amount to c.€1.25trn, up from €400bn in 2013.

Based on these results, and acknowledging substantial uncertainty, we would expect signs of liquidity tightness to emerge (and €STR to start rising above the depo) when excess liquidity falls towards €1.25trn.

However, the studies mentioned above estimate the level at which aggregate liquidity might get tight, without considering the uneven distribution of reserves across the Eurozone, which might lead to localised liquidity shortages already (well) before aggregate reserves have reached €1.25trn.

## 4. Combining it all

## Our forecast for the Eurosystem balance sheet and liquidity

Based on our projections for: (1) the pace of TLTRO and APP/PEPP run-off; (2) the secular growth of the liability side of the ECB balance sheet; and (3) banks' demand for excess reserves (presented above), we draw the following key conclusions about the future evolution of the ECB balance sheet and Eurozone liquidity conditions:

1. If the run-off of TLTROs, APP and PEPP were the only variables affecting the ECB balance sheet and liquidity, excess liquidity would be fully depleted around 2031. But liquidity might start to become tight − i.e. excess liquidity approaching €1.25trn − in late 2027 already.

ECB balance sheet and Eurozone liquidity: key conclusions of our forecast

2. The growth of the liability side of the ECB balance sheet (i.e. secular growth of autonomous factors) will likely bring forward the tightening of excess liquidity to early 2027. Around that time, the ECB balance sheet will likely stop contracting and start to grow again soon after. This would become evident in greater usage of the ECB refinancing facilities.

3. The estimates presented above refer to the Eurozone liquidity situation in aggregate. However, the uneven distribution of excess reserves across the Eurozone implies that, in some countries, banking sectors, or individual banks, partial liquidity shortages might emerge a lot earlier.

4. As regards the structure of the ECB balance sheet, we assume that the ECB will allow its QE portfolio (APP and PEPP) to largely expire over time, to reduce the ECB's footprint in crucial parts of the sovereign bond markets. According to our estimates, between now and end-2030, APP and PEPP holdings will shrink by about two-thirds, from currently €4.6trn to €1.5trn, with further declines thereafter. However, as of 2027, when liquidity might show signs of tightening, we would expect Eurozone banks to use the ECB's regular refinancing operation more actively and the ECB to start building up a structural bond portfolio. Under our (rather speculative) base case scenario, the SBP would grow from zero at the start of 2027 to around €1.3 trillion by 2030.

5. We estimate that the ECB balance sheet will contract from currently €6.6trn (46% of GDP) to around €5.3trn (33% of GDP) by end-2026 and then start growing again by around €100bn per year, while remaining broadly steady in terms of GDP. If autonomous factors (demand for banknotes) grow more slowly than in our base case scenario (e.g. due to digitalisation of payments), the balance sheet might grow more slowly.

6. Should the Eurozone encounter new severe shocks, the ECB would likely first cut rates towards the lower bound (potentially negative) before activating asset purchases again. In this case, the ECB balance sheet would end up larger than the estimates we provide as our base-case scenario.

## When will excess liquidity become tight?

If the run-off of the TLTROs, APP and PEPP were the only driver of the balance sheet, we would expect the ECB balance sheet to contract from currently €6.6trn to €6.2trn by end-2024, €5.6trn by end-2025, €5.1trn by end-2026, and €4.6trn by end-2027.

## Balance sheet and liquidity to evolve in two phases

We expect the future evolution of the ECB balance sheet and Eurozone liquidity to be subject to two distinctive phases:

In phase 1, between now and end-2026, the balance sheet will likely continue to contract, mainly driven by QT (APP and PEPP). Excess liquidity, which is currently still abundant (€3.5trn), would be steadily reduced over the course of phase 1. At the end of phase 1, liquidity would be just in line with banks' comfort level, presumably around €1.25trn.

In phase 2, which would start around 2027, excess liquidity would fall below banks' comfort level as the maturing APP and PEPP assets would reduce liquidity in the system while the secular growth of the liability side would lead to fresh liquidity demands by banks. At that stage, the liquidity absorption caused by QT (APP/PEPP) and the growth of autonomous factors would have to be compensated by banks' more active use of refinancing operations and/or the build-up of a structural bond portfolio by the ECB. As a result, the Eurosystem balance sheet would grow again, mainly due to the growth in autonomous factors.

Our simulations suggest that the ECB balance sheet will contract from currently €6.6trn (46% of GDP) to around €6.3trn (42% of GDP) by end-2024, €5.8trn (37% of GDP) by end-2025 and €5.3trn (33% of GDP) by end-2026, before growing again by around €100bn per year, while remaining broadly constant in terms of percentage of GDP (c.33% of GDP) (Figure 22).

## (b) More frequent use of asset purchases

Over the past 20 years, equilibrium real interest rates (r\*) saw a structural decline, and the recent period of higher inflation might have led to only a partial reversal, so r\* might still be lower than in the past. As a result, central banks might be constrained by the effective lower bound of interest rates more frequently than before the GFC. Also, over the past 10-15 years, QE has arguably been established as an almost normal monetary policy tool. This would suggest that central banks might also use QE in the future, which would keep the balance sheet larger than before the GFC. As Andrew Hauser, formerly with the BoE, put it: "[A] lower r\* implies a more active use of the central bank balance sheet to implement monetary policy." ## (c) Risk of Eurozone fragmentation

Financial market fragmentation can lead to a concentration of bank reserves and overall higher demand for reserves, which would go along with a (ceteris paribus) higher central bank balance sheet. This risk would seem to be accentuated in a diverse currency area such as the Eurozone. After all, fragmentation reduces the incentive for liquidity-rich banks to lend excess funds via the interbank markets. Fragmentation also raises the relative attractiveness of holding liquidity in the form of cental bank reserves rather than (17) the sovereign bonds of countries that are perceived to be subject to higher credit risk.

In the Eurozone, the process of monetary policy normalisation has raised concerns over unwelcome spread widening and fragmentation in sovereign bond markets. To reduce this risk and ensure that potential fragmentation would not force an early end to policy tightening (and acceptance of higher-than-desired inflation), the ECB, in July 2022, presented its "Transmission Protection Instrument" (TPI). Subject to conditions referring to fiscal, debt and broader macroeconomic stability, the TPI allows the ECB to intervene in the securities markets of countries that experience "a deterioration in financing conditions not warranted by country-specific fundamentals". Purchases are "not restricted ex ante", meaning they might potentially be very large, if required to calm markets. Obviously, activation of the TPI would have an expansionary impact on the ECB balance sheet – although this would presumably be temporary, not permanent.

## (d) Potential introduction of a digital euro

A fourth reason why the Eurosystem balance sheet will probably remain larger than before the GFC has to do with the potential introduction of a digital euro, i.e. a central bank digital currency (CBDC) for the Eurozone. In July 2021, following years of preparatory work, the ECB decided to launch a two-year investigation phase, to intensify the technical work on a digital euro. In October 2023, the ECB decided to proceed to the next stage ("preparation phase" of initially two years), at the end of which the Governing Council will "decide whether to move to the next stage of preparations, to (18 pave the way for the possible future issuance and roll-out of a digital euro."

to hold central bank money in the form of physical cash only, i.e. they did not have access to central bank money in digital form; this was the privilege of banks, which can hold central bank money in the form of central bank reserves. The digital money that households and corporates can hold is in the form of commercial bank deposits, but these do not constitute central bank money, but private money – the counterparty is not the central bank, but a commercial bank.
