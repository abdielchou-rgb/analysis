# What Companies Are Saying: Signs Of A Turn Up

This report presents a collection of management commentary from 130 earnings calls in the Q4 reporting season. We present the key themes that emerged below.

Companies continue to cite a tough macro backdrop but several now see signs of a turn up emerging. They are however keeping a lid on expectations and are at pains to note that they are not macro forecasters and given the continued pessimism and uncertainty around factors outside their control, they remain cautious. A key drag in recent quarters has been inventory destocking, but several companies now see light at the end of the tunnel with the process well advanced, even if restocking is not yet on the cards. The US continued to be a relative outperformer, Europe was mixed, while China a clear source of weakness. On the latter, many companies drew a contrast between near term cyclical headwinds and long-term potential. Most see cost and pricing dynamics as being in balance, indicating a return to normal. Companies are increasingly comfortable with the labor market even as they expect the pressure from wage inflation to persist. Many are of course counting on the strong labor market and wage growth to continue driving demand for their products. Part of their confidence stems from raising productivity to meet a variety of resource constraints and cost challenges, using a wide set of tools going well beyond AI. Debt reduction continues to be a priority amidst higher interest rates but given strong cashflows and substantial progress towards their deleveraging goals, many companies are increasingly deploying capital towards payouts in the form of dividends and buybacks, some notably for the first time.

## They are however keeping a lid on expectations

Companies are at pains to note that they are not macro forecasters and given the continued pessimism and uncertainty around factors outside their control, they remain cautious. Snap-On captured it as “it's not that things are bad today, but people worry about how things are going to happen” because “every day they get up and get bad news for breakfast.” O’Reilly said their “expertise is not in our ability to predict broader economic conditions” and that they “remain cautious in our outlook.” Robert Half said, “ongoing economic uncertainty continues to impact client and candidate confidence.” Fastenal agreed, saying “our customers remain cautious.” Cintas said that “growth is still good, momentum is good” but there still “is a little bit of uncertainty” and they “think it is wise to be prudent” as do Roper Technologies who “have not assumed that <a pickup> in our model” as they “want to see it before we load it in.” Pentair expected “growth to be slightly offset by the uncertainty that still exists regarding the macroeconomic environment.”

## Light at the end of the destocking tunnel

Inventory destocking has remained a broad and considerable drag for several quarters. Texas Instruments is still seeing “increasing weakness in industrial and a sequential decline in automotive, as customers work to reduce their inventory levels” while AMD said they “expect overall embedded demand will remain soft through the first half of the year, as customers continue to focus on normalizing their inventory levels.” However, many companies expect a positive inflection in volume trends soon, simply as the negative drag from destocking runs its course, even as restocking is not yet on the cards. Lamb Weston said that their “improving trend <in volume growth> largely reflects no further impact of the inventory destocking.” Similarly, PPG expects “Europe to stabilize, which really reflects a lack of a destocking” while Eastman Chemical said that “the key driver for pretty significant volume increase for us this year versus last year is really the sort of lack of destocking.” Seagate saw “solid progress in consuming excess inventory” while Dow Inc. thinks “the destocking that began in late 2022 has largely run its course.” Fedex believes “that the inventory destocking phase is over” but “the restocking phase is yet to begin in earnest,” a view echoed by International Paper, which said “we think destocking is obviously over” but they “have not seen restocking yet broadly.”

## China a drag on growth but companies maintain faith in the long-term potential

For most companies, China was a source of weakness, in keeping with the recent data and headlines. Colgate-Palmolive said “there's a real slowdown in China,“ OTIS Worldwide saw growth in other regions offset by the “the downturn in China,” Caterpillar said that “China is relatively weak” while Rockwell Automation continued to “see challenges in the Chinese manufacturing economy.” Corning does not expect “China, quote-unquote, sort of reverting to a more traditional demand cycle” and instead expects it to remain ”relatively below trend for the foreseeable future.” There are a few exceptions which have started seeing a cyclical turn up. For example, Western Digital is “encouraged by demand in China” while Dow Inc. sees “improving conditions, which could provide a source of demand recovery.“ Many companies drew a contrast between cyclical headwinds and longterm potential. Starbucks said, “despite the short-term headwinds, the long-term opportunity in China is clear” while McCormick said they “recognize there has been volatility in demand in China,” but “continue to believe in the long-term growth trajectory of our China business.” Procter & Gamble concurred that while the “nearterm is likely to present some challenges,” the “long-term China opportunity remains intact.” McDonald’s similarly said that despite near term challenges, “we're seeing strong growth there. We also built 1,000 restaurants in China.” Trane acknowledged the “headlines coming out of China,” but “the verticals that we're really strong in, continue to be strong” and that they are “excited about the opportunities that exist in front of us there.”

## Pricing as well as cost inflation dialing down in a return to normal

Companies are increasingly speaking of cost and pricing dynamics as being in balance, indicating a return to normal. O’Reilly Automotive described it as the “normal state of condition for our industry.” Unilever said they “have seen a steady decline of price growth rates as cost inflation eased.” Illinois Tool Works agreed saying “that big wave <of cost inflation> we've been dealing with, as we sit here today, we'd say, is largely behind us. And so, we're entering into a normal pricing environment across the portfolio.” Amphenol said they “don't necessarily think we're going to get necessarily the benefit of price” but also pointed out that “historically, that's not something that we would see anyways.”

## Companies comfortable with the labor market even as they expect wage pressures to persist

Most companies expressed comfort with the state of the labor market and are confident of being able to flex up or down as needed. While they expect this will ease wage pressures somewhat, they are not counting on significant relief. On the flip side, many companies are of course counting on the strong labor market and wage growth to continue driving demand for their products. IDEX Corp said “labor markets have eased up a bit. Frankly, it's easier to flex them” while Pulte Homes said they “don't see the industry stressing the labor availability.” Roper Technologies said that “the labor market generally loosening up has been advantageous for us.” Old Dominion Freight Line “currently believe that our workforce is appropriately sized for our current shipment trends, but we will likely need to add to our workforce this year as volumes begin to improve.” Cintas expects that “as the labor market eases and that will lessen pressure on wage growth as well.” However, many others echoed Procter & Gamble in saying that “wage and benefit inflation continues,” and PPG projected “higher expected wage inflation.” Mastercard said “low unemployment and rising wages” “remain key drivers of consumer spending.”

## But they are confident of raising productivity to offset resource constraints and cost challenges

Most companies are focused on raising productivity as an imperative to meet a variety of challenges. While cutting edge technology like AI garners all the headlines, corporates point to many other options in their toolbox, such as optimally aligning resources, traditional automation, simplification, standardization, waste reduction, defect control, supply chain optimization, and even simply employee training. Eastman Chemical captured it as “we have to have productivity every year where we're offsetting inflation so that we have the ability to invest in growth, deliver earnings growth and cash to the shareholders, all at the same time.” Darden Restaurants said, “our productivity enhancements were the difference between our wage inflation and our labor <per unit> inflation,” a view echoed by Union Pacific which said that a “solid level of workforce productivity mostly offset wage inflations as cost per employee only increased 1%.”

Payouts, both dividends and buybacks, are again ramping up even as debt reduction continues to be a priority.

Paying down debt amidst higher interest rates remains a priority for several companies. But given strong cashflows and substantial progress towards their deleveraging goals, many are increasingly deploying capital towards payouts, both in the form of dividends as well as share buybacks. Some companies are notably paying out for the first time. Pentair said they “continue to focus on debt reduction amid the higher interest rate environment with the potential for share buybacks.” United Rentals said they were “very pleased to announce” “plans to return nearly \$2 billion of cash to shareholders this year and a reduced leverage target of 1.5 to 2.5 times.” McCormick was also “pleased to be deleveraging faster than expected.” Halliburton said they “intend to continue buying back share” and “at the same time, as we did also in 2023, we intend to continue to retire debt.”

Macroeconomic fluctuations often account for most of the risks associated with exposures to instruments that promise to pay fixed or variable interest rates. For an investor who is long fixed-rate instruments (thus receiving these cash flows), increases in interest rates naturally lift the discount factors applied to the expected cash flows and thus cause a loss. The longer the maturity of a certain cash flow and the higher the move in the discount factor, the higher will be the loss. Upside surprises in inflation, fiscal funding needs, and FX depreciation rates are among the most common adverse macroeconomic shocks to receivers. But counterparty exposure, issuer creditworthiness, client segmentation, regulation (including changes in assets holding limits for different types of investors), changes in tax policies, currency convertibility (which may constrain currency conversion, repatriation of profits and/or liquidation of positions), and settlement issues related to local clearing houses are also important risk factors. The sensitivity of fixed-income instruments to macroeconomic shocks may be mitigated by indexing the contracted cash flows to inflation, to FX depreciation, or to specified interest rates - these are common in emerging markets. The index fixings may - by construction - lag or mis-measure the actual move in the underlying variables they are intended to track. The choice of the proper fixing (or metric) is particularly important in swaps markets, where floating coupon rates (i.e., coupons indexed to a typically short-dated interest rate reference index) are exchanged for fixed coupons. Funding in a currency that differs from the currency in which coupons are denominated carries FX risk.
