## Executive Summary

Focus on the US policies that can potentially change and the impact on EM: Work produced by the Morgan Stanley public policy team so far suggests scope for larger policy changes relative to the status quo under a Republican presidency compared to a Democratic one, with scenarios involving a united government across the White House and Congress also seeing potential for more meaningful impacts.

Thus, a Republican sweep is likely the scenario in which we see bigger impacts on USD and UST yields. It is in this scenario that we are likely to see not just changes to tariff and trade policy, but also meaningful changes to immigration policy and fiscal policy. We take our cue on the impact of these policies on the US economy from US Public Policy: Navigating a Noisy Election, June 14, 2024, where our economics team sees in Republican win scenarios wider uncertainty bands around the impact on US growth, with some downside risks.

We first assess the impact of such a scenario on USD and UST, with general thoughts on the feed-through to EM, before detailing the micro outcomes at a country level across five key policies. Finally, we review the key implications for each asset class and how to position for the potential policy changes.

## Most US policy macro factors lead to a stronger USD and hence weaker EM FX:

• Tariffs the most important factor in driving a stronger USD: Trade policy is a – if not the – central avenue through which the 2024 US election is likely to influence exchange rates and EM macro markets. Higher US tariffs on the rest of the world can weigh more heavily on economic activity abroad, strengthening USD by making the US look more attractive in comparison. Those trading partners that manage exchange rates could allow depreciation of their domestic currency to compensate for tariffs and the hit to growth, adding to upward pressure on USD.

• Fiscal policies also point to a stronger USD: An expansionary fiscal policy agenda in a Republican sweep outcome could raise US growth expectations and encourage capital flows into the US, boosting USD. There is an active debate on how rising US debt/GDP and fiscal expansion will interact with markets, and in particular if the dollar would weaken. Those downside risks to USD rise should there be any perceived changes to Fed credibility or independence.

• Changes to immigration policy less consequential for broad USD: Any changes in US immigration policy are likely to be more consequential for specific EM countries, especially in Latin America, than broadly for USD or the EM fixed income asset class in general. However, to the extent there are broad impacts on USD, they are likely to be negative overall as reduced net immigration would likely lead to reduced growth momentum.

## At the micro level, there are five key policies under a Trump presidency that could determine country outcomes:

1. Tariff and trade policy: While the focus is usually on the tariffs themselves, historical data and trade theory suggest that the more consequential impact comes from second-order effects driven by FX volatility and demand destruction reacting to higher price levels. These macro movements can have consequential implications for EM economies, but whether a shock is growth led or inflation led depends on the trade exposure of the economy. Of course also key will be whether tariffs are broad or targeted, and whether countries act in response. China, Asia ex Japan and Mexico would be most at risk, in our view.

2. Immigration policy: There is a high likelihood of even stricter migration policy. The primary negative spillovers to EM from this include weaker remittance flows that could even decline in a bear case scenario. The other tail risk scenario would be taxes on remittances, which would have a material impact on LatAm in particular.

3. Climate and energy policy: A targeted repeal of IRA is possible. Reduced investment in the US' green transition could reduce demand for key exports, or see additional tariffs on renewables equipment imports. Additionally, lower support for the green transition abroad via multilaterals and US agencies could reduce access to climate financing for EM countries. A potential carbon border tax would target high-emitting countries without a free-trade agreement with the US, primarily China. Finally, while the intention would be to boost domestic oil production, this appears less feasible in reality.

4. Overseas investment and aid policy: We would expect more focus on bilateral aid as a foreign policy tool to the detriment of multilateral aid. It could therefore mean less funding for MDBs and more focus on regional ex US financial arrangements. For the IMF, it could mean easier approval for perceived allies but also slower broader IMF reforms such as the quota increase, surcharges reduction and the broader quota reform.

5. Foreign policy: There is likely to be upside pressure on defence spending, especially in Europe. Ukraine support would be at risk, a clear negative, but then also increased talks about a ceasefire would be viewed positively, both for Ukraine and broader Europe. Middle Eastern geopolitical risk should on aggregate be defined by policy towards Iran that could become more hawkish, yet at a country level there could be certain beneficiaries in the region.

## Key EM local market views

• CNH the best expression of trade tensions, yet timing matters: China is clearly the most exposed to potential tariffs. The 2018-19 experience would suggest that USD/CNH could gain by around 80-100% of the increase in the tariff rate, but this time could be less given the large magnitude of the tariff hike floated. That said, if tariffs are not implemented immediately after the new president takes office and there is a period of negotiations first, CNH could have a window to rally in January-February 2025.

• Yet KRW, THB, MYR and TWD also vulnerable: Currencies of economies which are export-oriented, run a large trade surplus with the US, export products that are at higher risk of tariffs and are sensitive to CNH and risk appetite could also face higher risks.

• MXN risks also skewed to weaker outcomes: While there are scenarios in which Mexico benefits, we see risks to a weaker MXN. Mexico would be vulnerable to US tariffs on China only and the broader global tariffs. Also, USMCA renegotiation risks ahead of 2026 should not be underestimated. Finally, any negative impact on remittances would negatively impact growth, the current account and in turn MXN.

• Carry trade unwinds lead to value in USD/CNH: The divergence in USD/CNH versus USD/MXN in the past month implies, from a valuation perspective, that CNH is the best expression of trade tensions.

• EM policy responses should diverge to trade risks: Some EM central banks will likely focus on the hit to growth from tariffs, and lean into FX weakness with rate cuts (those with low inflation and external surpluses are more likely to take this approach). Others could worry more about inflation and financial stability risks (those with high/sticky inflation and low external stability), and either cut less or even hike depending on the extent of FX moves. Rates markets should diverge accordingly.

## Key EM sovereign credit views

• For trade, the main spillovers are likely on the importer side: FX is in general the better asset class to position for trade policies. However, within credit we would look for countries with large goods imports, particularly those running a large goods deficit, as they would be vulnerable to currency depreciation. This includes Morocco, Tunisia, El Salvador (despite being dollarised), Mozambique and to a lesser extent Senegal, Kenya and the Dominican Republic. Another potential negative spillover would be from the US pulling out of the Central America Free Trade Agreement (CAFTA-DR), in which case Costa Rica and the Dominican Republic are most at risk.

• Central America and the Caribbean at high risk due to immigration policies: Remittances from the US now play a significant part in external flows to the region, with them representing as high as 25% of GDP in some countries. Tough immigration policies, especially deportations, and/or proposals to tax remittances would thus be very negative. In this scenario, we would sell El Salvador bonds.

• Foreign policy should have a big say in the outcome for Ukraine and CEE bonds: If the policy is only to reduce aid to Ukraine, then both Ukraine and the CEE bonds should underperform. However, if at the same time there is a push towards a ceasefire, there is plenty of geopolitical risk that could be priced out. In this case we would buy Ukraine bonds.

• Venezuela policy may not be the same as under Trump's first term: Prior hawkish stances on Venezuela in the first Trump term raise risk. However, bond prices are already very low. Additionally, focus is now much higher on immigration and oil investment by Western oil companies, with an acknowledgement that the prior maximum sanction framework failed. In this scenario, we would buy Venezuela bonds.

• Bilateral and multilateral flows may skew more towards country allies: We do think that the market will view the outlook for both Argentina and El Salvador more positively under a Trump presidency given that it can help financial and investment flows into the country, including potentially easing the conditions of potential IMF deals. While this scenario thus suggests buying Argentina and El Salvador bonds, we caution that there are many more important drivers to make the bonds perform well. Hungary may also benefit from a closer ideological alignment.

• Geopolitical risk premia in the GCC could easily rise: Geopolitical risk premia in the Gulf have remained resilient despite regional events as the Saudi-Iran détente shielded the region from spillover. A resurgence of more hawkish US policy towards Iran could test the strength of this détente. Buying Qatar CDS is best for this risk, in our view.

• Lower oil prices on the back of higher US domestic oil production not a key risk: Despite the rhetoric, the ability to boost US production at a faster pace than the current one is limited, while reduced Iranian supply could also counteract the impact on overall balances. If a weaker demand profile nonetheless leads to lower oil prices, we would sell Saudi Arabia and Bahrain bonds.

# Assessing the Impact of Key Macro Policies on USD and EM

James Lord, Andrew Watrous

How will the US election results impact EM economies and asset prices? The question received a lot of airtime in our client conversations earlier in the year, particularly when the chances of a Republican Party victory were rising (as indicated by prediction markets), but have taken a back seat since the Democratic Party recovered some ground. Markets have also been more preoccupied with recent weak data from the US and the Bank of Japan rate hike.

The topic is very likely to rise up again on the agenda as the election date nears. Work produced by the Morgan Stanley public policy team so far suggests scope for larger policy changes relative to the status quo under a Republican presidency versus a Democratic one, with scenarios involving a united government across the White House and Congress also seeing potential for more meaningful impacts.

Thus, a Republican sweep is likely the scenario in which we see bigger impacts on US growth, USD and UST yields. It is in this scenario that we are likely to see not just changes to tariff and trade policy, but also meaningful changes to immigration policy and fiscal policy.

We see a Republican sweep as being most constructive for USD, and negative for EM currencies, with tariffs and fiscal stimulus the key drivers. Immigration policy is likely less consequential for broad USD, but to the extent that there is an impact, it will likely be negative for USD. Individual EMs are likely more exposed than the broad asset class.

A Republican sweep likely has a more nuanced impact on EM rates, given the uncertainties over whether the market will view the suite of policies in this scenario as positive or negative for US growth and thus bearish or bullish for USTs. Our US rates team sees some upside risks to UST yields from the fiscal stimulus that would likely result from a Republican sweep, but downside risks to yields from tariffs and immigration policy, with the negative growth implications offsetting inflation risks.

The scenario resulting in more downward pressure on EM local markets likely revolves around the sequencing of policy priorities in a Republican sweep. An early and simultaneous focus on tariffs and fiscal stimulus likely pushes USD up and EM currencies down. Though UST yield direction is more uncertain, our rates team believes that tax cuts and fiscal stimulus are likely to result in upward pressure on term premiums in USTs – potentially resulting in upward pressure on EM yields too.

The scenario resulting in a better outcome for EM local markets is likely a Democratic sweep, as this is the one that results in the least fiscal stimulus according to our economics team, and thus less upward pressure on USD and UST yields.

A divided government with a Republican president leaves EM exposed to a stronger USD and weaker EM FX that comes from the risk of higher tariffs, though this would not be a risk under a divided government with a Democratic president, which likely sees EM local markets perform better.

## US trade impact on global FX markets

A Democratic Party-led White House is unlikely to see tariff action with meaningful impacts for the US or global EM economies and their markets, leaving the status quo intact.

More meaningful changes are likely under a Republican Party-led White House, under both divided government and Republican sweep scenarios, where widespread or potentially more targeted policies are possible with the USMCA also up for review in 2026.

Trade policy is a – if not the – central avenue through which the 2024 US election is likely to influence exchange rates and EM macro markets.

As we explained in The Trump administration: A USD-trade policy case study , trade policy confrontation had a differentiated impact on USD during President Trump's term, largely due to the narrow country focus of the tariffs.

USD rose against the currencies of economies directly impacted by the tariffs (i.e., CNY and China), rose against currencies sensitive to risk appetite and global growth expectations (commodity currencies and many EMs), but fell versus EUR, JPY and CHF.

There are reasons to think that this differentiated performance of USD may be less apparent in the next cycle, resulting in broader USD strength.

First though, here are four key considerations on how we think the trade policies of the next administration may impact USD, taken from our recently published in-depth report (see The White House and the Dollar, August 6, 2024):

## 1. Impact on US growth expectations and inflation (net USD negative)

Net negative effects on US growth expectations: US exports could be affected by US tariffs if other economies impose tariffs in response, while tariffs also have the potential to disrupt the ongoing supply chain reorganisation, and affect the profitability of producers across the production chain.

Trade uncertainty arising from tariffs could reduce business investment, according to research from the Federal Reserve, while consumption can also be adversely impacted as tariffs rise, depending on how easily consumers are able to substitute the tariffed commodities and to what extent producers pass on higher input costs to consumers.

These negative effects are offset to some extent by higher tariffs' negative effect on gross US imports, but research suggests that the overall net effect on US activity is likely negative.

US inflation: Higher US tariffs raise domestic US prices. The effect on domestic prices may be mitigated by producers' decision to reduce profit margins or by USD strength (see point 3 below).

The September 2018 Fed staff assessment found that US tariffs would add “0.2 percent to the level of core PCE prices by the end of 2019”. However, as our economists wrote here, tariffs imposed in 2018 only boosted US CPI inflation modestly. The tariffs only had a limited effect on US inflation because only part of the goods tariffed were final consumer goods, CNY depreciated notably and there was fairly rapid trade diversion.

Risk of a differentiated response across EMs: For some EMs, the combination of broad USD strength, risk aversion and investor concerns about the local growth outlook is likely to lead to exchange rate weakness versus USD. Yet, currency weakness may cause central banks to be more cautious about easing monetary policy should they view the currency weakness as posing a risk to financial stability of their inflation goals. As highlighted in A) Tariff and Trade Policy , EMs that have experience of high inflation are more likely to risk inflation expectations being affected by currency volatility.

Some differentiation in the central bank policy response is therefore likely. Some central banks are likely to focus on the growth impact of higher tariffs and thus ease monetary policy – adding to downward pressure on their currencies. These markets are more likely to be those with more stable external and fiscal accounts.

Others are more likely to respond to the initial weakening in their currencies, driven by growth concerns, by focusing on inflation or financial stability risks and thus ease policy less.

Currency policy: In addition to monetary policy, foreign authorities may move to offset US tariffs by weakening their domestic currency against USD. In doing so, they may partially or fully offset the impact of higher US tariffs on the cost of imported goods and services for US importers.

The People's Bank of China set USD/CNY fixings significantly higher amid key events in the 2019 US-China trade confrontation, including when US-China trade talks ended without resolution and on the day when the US Treasury Department labelled China a currency manipulator.

Many authorities do not target their exchange rates explicitly and so exchange rate changes are likely to be an outcome of these policy and growth impacts and expectations.

## 4. Geopolitical implications (USD positive)

Anticipation of renewed trade confrontation under a Republican administration may also introduce a positive risk premium into USD due to geopolitical considerations. Investors may question whether trade policy objectives may supplant or undermine key US alliances with Australia and the UK (AUKUS), India, Japan and Australia (the Quad), NATO, and a coalition among the US, Japan and South Korea. A diminished role for these political and military relationships may heighten the market-perceived risk of expanded conflict.

These geopolitical considerations are more elevated at a time when 70% of respondents to a Bank of England survey report that geopolitical risks are among the most challenging they must manage. This proportion is far higher than in 2018 and 2019.

## The Trump administration: A USD-trade policy case study

During the Trump presidency, international policy confrontation was associated with USD gains against EM currencies and risk-sensitive currencies like AUD, and USD declines against low-yielding currencies like CHF and JPY.

On average, USD rose by 0.4% against CNH and MXN on days when trade-related actions occurred. Similarly, USD rose slightly against AUD (a highly risk-sensitive G10 currency).

These USD gains aligned with stock market declines and tighter TIPS breakeven yields observed on trade confrontation days. On average, the S&P 500 dropped by 1.1% and TIPS breakeven yields tightened on these days.

However, the USD strength also partly reflected policy decisions by trading partners to offset trade actions through currency weakness. For example, USD/CNY rose above 7.00 on August 5, 2019 – the same day that the US Treasury Department published a report finding that China is a currency manipulator.

## USD under renewed trade confrontation

We have shown that USD had a differentiated response to trade tensions during the Trump administration. Would it do so again in a renewed period of trade confrontation under another Republican administration?

The net effect on the broad USD would probably depend on the size and form of any potential tariff increase. For example, the breadth, magnitude and potential duration of the tariff increase, 10%? Is it universal? Is it policy-conditional or time-limited?

While lower US real yields would likely weigh against USD (all else equal), we see reasons to expect more pressure on currencies in the rest of the world, resulting in USD strength:

1. The stance of ECB and BoJ policy: One factor limiting EUR weakness stemming from broad trade confrontation was the European Central Bank's (ECB) policy stance in 2018- 19. At the time, policy rates were at or close to the effective lower bound, and the ECB was conducting substantial asset purchase programmes, limiting scope to ease further. The same was true for Japan ( Exhibit 13 ).

Interest rates in the euro area are now substantially higher than in 2018-19 (and the ECB has increased policy flexibility compared to 2018-19). As a result, we see significantly more room for ECB policy expectations to move in response to potential trade confrontation ( Exhibit 14 ). Today, the BoJ has raised interest rates and is no longer setting an upper limit for 10-year JGB yields.

This difference thus has the potential to see DXY stronger relative to the 2016-20 period, with knock-on impacts for the rest of EM.

2. Universal tariffs: As illustrated above, most trade actions and statements undertaken during the Trump administration were on products/sectors like aluminium and steel tariffs, or involved imports from individual countries like Mexico, Canada or China. However, unlike in 2018 and 2019, the Republican campaign has proposed universal tariffs rather than bilateral confrontation.

We expect a possible broader trade confrontation (i.e., the possibility of "universal baseline tariffs") to lead to less EUR and JPY strength than in 2018 and 2019. While countryspecific tariff action in 2018 and 2019 may have led investors to seek safety in European and Japanese assets, a universal tariff may directly influence European and Japanese growth and policy rate expectations to a greater extent than during the previous episode of trade confrontation.

3. Policy space from Chinese policymakers: Our Asia economics team notes that growth effects of the 2018-19 tariffs were substantially larger than the team initially expected – a regional GDP slowdown from a peak of 6%Y in 1Q18 to 3.9%Y in 4Q19.

Our economists wrote that the Chinese economy is now in a weaker state with reduced scope for easing compared to 2018-19. Unlike in that episode, China should not be able to stimulate the property sector to offset weaker external demand, and higher US tariffs would likely add to the deflationary pressures that China is already facing. Corporate confidence may not suffer to the same degree as in 2018-19 (because trade tensions have persisted for some time), but the effect would depend on the intensity of the trade measures taken.

## Differentiation is possible if trading partners accommodate US interests to avoid tariffs

While a universal baseline tariff of 10% is likely to result in broad USD strength in our view, some countries may be incentivised to make concessions to the US government to avoid tariffs. The concessions required would differ by country (for Mexico, more cooperation around immigration issues might be a factor. For China, addressing factors that are perceived to hinder American competitiveness could be on the table, such as the value of CNY and support for state-owned subsidies).

If any trading partner is able to successfully negotiate to avoid tariffs, rather than act in response, then the pathway to a weaker USD could open up on that bilateral cross.

## What's the best trade expression for tariff escalation?

CNH the best expression of trade tensions, yet timing matters: China is clearly the most exposed to potential tariffs. The 2018-19 experience would suggest that USD/CNH could gain by around 80-100% of the increase in the tariff rate, but this time it could be less given the large magnitude of tariff hikes floated. That said, if tariffs are not implemented immediately after the new president takes office and there is a period of negotiations first, CNH could have a window to rally in January-February 2025.

Yet KRW, THB, MYR and TWD also vulnerable: Currencies of economies which are export-oriented, run a large trade surplus with the US, export products that are at higher risk of tariffs and are sensitive to CNH and risk appetite could also face higher risks.

MXN risks also skewed to weaker outcomes: While there are scenarios in which Mexico benefits, we see risks to a weaker MXN. Mexico would be vulnerable to US tariffs on China only and the broader global tariffs. Also, USMCA renegotiation risks ahead of 2026 should not be underestimated. Finally, any negative impact on remittances would negatively impact growth, the current account and in turn MXN.

Carry trade unwinds lead to value in USD/CNH: The divergence in USD/CNH versus USD/ MXN in the past month implies, from a valuation perspective, that CNH is the best expression of trade tensions. ## Immigration policy – bigger country-level impacts

Any changes in US immigration policy are likely to be more consequential for specific EM countries than broadly for USD or the EM fixed income asset class in general. However, to the extent that the broad USD and the EM fixed income asset class will be affected, we see the following implications:

• Limited difference between a Republican and Democratic administration: Our colleagues in economics and public policy research believe that net immigration flows to the US would be reduced (see US Public Policy: Navigating a Noisy Election) with either a Republican or a Democratic victory. This catalyst should put downward pressure on growth and upward pressure on inflation.

• All roads lead to weaker growth and more dovish Fed policy: Reduced levels of immigration will likely have bigger and more lasting implications for economic activity than for inflation, however. As such, we see reduced net immigration leading to a more dovish Fed path and with it downward pressure on USD.

• EM fixed income could benefit at the index level: A more dovish Fed path and downward pressure on USD should all else being equal help EM fixed income. Front-end EM rates would look more attractive versus the US, while EM FX should broadly benefit from the weaker USD, helping to anchor the rates complex further.

• Soft landing at risk: Fast labour force growth associated with immigration has been a key factor behind our expectation for a soft landing of the US economy, and helping the declining trend in volatility (barring 2022 due to high geopolitical risk) and the strong performance of risk assets post-pandemic ( Exhibit 17 ).

• Weaker risk appetite is a possible outcome: A negative supply shock that puts the process in reverse risks destabilising risk appetite and would potentially put flows to EM at risk. However, we expect more dovish Fed policy and lower rates in generally to be the driving force for EM.

## Fiscal policy – our base case is for stimulus to boost USD

In The White House and the Dollar, we outline why we think that US fiscal policy under a Republication administration is likely to boost USD. The impact is likely to be most significant under a Republican sweep scenario, as this is the outcome more likely to result in the most fiscal expansion – with US\$0.5 trillion additional deficit expansion relative to a divided government scenario (with either Harris or Trump in the White House) over the next 10 years, through rolling forward more TCJA provisions.

At the same time, and as we describe further below, some investors worry about debt sustainability in the US and there are scenarios in our view where investor confidence could be impacted – it's just not our base case that this materialises anytime soon.

As described earlier, we think that trade policy and high tariffs likely boost USD by negatively impacting growth in the rest of the world by more than in the US. This theme potentially raises risk aversion and encourages a flight to quality assets.

However, in our base case, fiscal expansion in a Republican-led White House and Congress likely boosts USD by positively impacting US growth relative to the rest of the world. A faster pace of economic activity as well as potentially a more hawkish path for the Fed should help to suck in capital from the rest of the world, boosting USD.

The extent to which different EMs are impacted by this trend will depend on the extent of growth divergence which could result. EMs that benefit from strong export links to the US could outperform, whereas those without that export support may see their balance of payments and currency reactions dominated by the portfolio flow channel.

It's important to point out that the TCJA does not expire until 2026 and so any additional deficit expansion from this source in the scenario of a Republican sweep won't be impacting the economy until then. Our economists see similar outcomes in terms of the first-year impact of TCJA extensions from a Republican sweep or Democratic sweep. So, to the extent that this debate affects markets, it will be through the expectations channel, which is similar to how market were impacted in Trump's first term.

The outlook for 2026 growth is beyond Morgan Stanley's current forecast horizon. For 2025 GDP growth, the delta in our forecasts versus 2024 is worse for the US compared to many other economies ( Exhibit 19 ), in part because of a reduced fiscal impulse. And while a Republican sweep scenario may not result in fiscal policy changes that affect GDP growth in 2025 materially, markets should move in 2025 on the basis of expectations for 2026 and beyond.

As we wrote in King Dollar Lacks Challengers, April 18, 2024, we believe that USD and USD assets are likely to remain the primary reserve currency assets in the global economy and thus maintain the confidence of investors.

However, the size of deficit expansion under a Republican sweep scenario may well be big enough to raise questions once again about the risk. Our conversations with investors reveal that there are enough investors who believe that the US debt/GDP outlook poses a significant enough risk to make them bearish on USD, even though positioning for that risk has hardly been a fruitful strategy thus far, and some may attempt to reduce USD holdings or short USD on the back of a Republican sweep scenario – we can't rule out the possibility.

Our economics team also discussed the US debt/GDP outlook in Global Economic Briefing: Flashing Yellow: DM Sovereign Debt & Fiscal Flashpoints, April 11, 2024, and highlighted that, absent significant fiscal tightening, the debt/GDP path rises steeply into the end of the decade.

The Fed should be a key pivot point: At what point, if at all, fiscal risks emerge to materially impact investor sentiment is hard to say. Much depends on initial conditions around the level of inflation and whether the economy is in need of fiscal stimulus, as well as the level and cost of debt. However, one key pivot issue is likely to be the Fed, in our view.

If the Fed maintains credibility to deal with any inflation threat that comes from an expansion of the fiscal deficit, then this will go a long way towards ensuring investor confidence in USD and USTs. If the Fed's credibility is weakened, investor confidence would be at risk, and the USD response to expansionary fiscal stimulus could be negative. To be clear, our public policy strategists expect there to be, ultimately, enough checks and balances in the system to allow the Fed to execute monetary policy independently (see US Public Policy Brief: What If? Fed Independence & the US Election, February 6, 2024).

However, uncertainty and volatility could be generated along the way. Former President Trump has made a number of comments regarding Fed policy. Most recently, he stated that the president should have some say over interest rates and monetary policy (see, for example, this Bloomberg write-up on the topic). In other commentary, he has said he would not seek to shorten Fed Chair Powell's current term, stating "I’ve had my own disputes with him. But no, I would, I would let him serve it out especially if I thought he was doing the right thing."

If the Fed's independence were challenged, at a time when fiscal deficits were expanding, then markets would worry about inflation risk. Instead of heading up in response to growth-supportive fiscal stimulus, USD would likely go down, in our view. It is a scenario that would lead to lower real yields (as markets would likely assume an easier stance of monetary policy from the Fed), higher breakevens (as markets price in higher inflation due to Fed policy and fiscal stimulus) and a steeper curve. These are the ingredients for a weaker USD.

Would USD weaken across the board though? In this scenario, USD may not weaken across the board. It is likely to against other high-quality and high credit rating sovereign currencies such as EUR, JPY and CHF. But whether it would do against a range of different higher-yielding or growth-sensitive currencies is hard to say. The prospect of higher longduration UST yields driven by higher term premiums could generate uncertainty in the global economy with spillover effects for EMs dependent on foreign capital, undermining their currencies and bond markets. Risk appetite broadly speaking could be affected.

# US Public Policy: Tariffs

## Ariana Salvatore

Despite US membership in multilateral trade treaties and organisations, the executive branch maintains powers to institute tariffs with broad authorities: Former President Trump has made tariff implementation a key message of his re-election campaign, citing a variety of methods ranging from specific goods-based tariffs (in particular on items like EVs from Mexico) to a more broad-based approach (with the 10% universal baseline tariff, for example). Given these policy statements and the unilateral ability given to the president to direct trade policy, we contemplate the impact of these tariff scenarios.

The president derives his authority on trade from Congress, which has delegated it to the executive branch over time. In general, authorities tend to be more permissive in instances that invoke national security concerns: for example, President Nixon placed a 10% additional tariff on imports for a brief period of time in the 1970s, citing authority from the Trading with the Enemy Act (1917). Similarly, although challenged in courts, former President Trump's steel and aluminium (Section 232) tariffs on Europe were put in place and upheld based on the administration's declaration of a national security risk (a decision that was later upheld by the Biden administration).

The former president has not explicitly stated which authorities he would seek to leverage to implement the various tariff proposals he has floated on the campaign trail. Given precedent, we do believe it's plausible to expect court challenges to emerge in the event of tariff implementation – regardless of which path is taken – adding another layer of uncertainty to the tariff picture.

What did we learn in 2018-19? Take tariff talk seriously and literally: As we noted at the time, the US administration has tended to follow through on its tariff guidance. In this sense, we take its literal message of planned tariffs quite seriously. In 2018-19, most of the trade frictions focused on China and the associated Phase One negotiation, and the former president has frequently cited tariffs as a tool in negotiations. Pairing that idea with the

precedent of announcing tariffs then backing away from implementation (List 4b, 5% tariffs on Mexico) has led many investors to conclude that Trump's tariff proposals should not be taken literally. We disagree. Tariffs in theory and practice: Much of the initial price level shift up from tariffs is usually offset by a combination of FX and volume declines/reduced demand. The macro shifts in FX and volume have implications for EM economies. The FX channel can increase inflation risks for economies with large import baskets. The volume destruction from the end market implementing the tariff could negatively affect growth for economies with high exports/GDP.

Tariff policies: There are two avenues of tariffs that have been floated in campaigning by the Republican Party so far – narrow sector or country tariffs or blanket tariffs. The economic theory suggests that the effects are the same – the question is the scale. Clearly a wider, more encompassing tariff policy will have larger macroeconomic implications.

At-risk economies and regions: Asia ex Japan economies have the highest exports as a percentage of GDP and therefore are more likely to face growth risks from new tariff policies. African economies are less exposed on the export side of the equation because most regional exports are commodity-linked (where tariff passthrough effects are rapid) but face risks from imports in a stronger dollar environment. Emerging Europe has elevated export exposure but considerably less than Emerging Asia; within the region Turkey is most exposed to trade volatility. In Latin America Mexico is the most exposed economy, while other economies have a more neutral balance in their exposure to tariffs.

The scope of the tariffs will determine the specific winners and losers. Pre-existing tariff arrangements, such as NAFTA, add a layer of protection for participant members within the zone. As a general rule, we expect a continuation of the recent trends of nearshoring or friendshoring in global supply chains.

## Tariffs in theory and practice

Tariffs have first and second-order effects that have differentiated impacts on exporter and importer countries. Market narratives often focus on the first-order effects, but it is the second-order effects that tend to be more consequential for volatility and medium-term economic shifts in emerging markets: The first-order effect is the 'price' component of tariffs, which creates an initial level shift in prices that drives reactions in the market and the economy. The disruption to production and supply chains also constitutes a first-order effect. Declines in production, both for exporters and importers, can be consequential as the market readjusts to the new tariff-increased price. Second-order effects include externalities from the initial price/volume shift, such as foreign exchange rate movement, capex decisions amid restructuring of supply chains and interest rate adjustments. For macro stability in emerging markets, second-order effects pose material risks.

While a tariff-driven price rise may be colloquially described as inflationary, history shows us that it is not always certain that the price shift is more than temporary: Remember that inflation is defined as sustained price rises over time (which is why wages and/or price indexation are the usual drivers of inflation). From a tariff perspective, the path to inflation is far narrower. Much of the price rise is often mitigated by a range of factors, such as foreign exchange moves or margin absorption in the supply chain (why it matters if the exporter is consumer-facing or producer-facing). For inflation to be sustained, tariffs would need to be reciprocated and increased over time and, even then, it is unclear if the price level would remain elevated because of second-order effects. The distinction is important because there is an embedded assumption that tariffs lead to inflation which leads to higher US interest rates. Yet, central banks are often likely to look through a one-time price-level shift linked to tariffs on the view that it is transitory.

For emerging markets, there are greater risks of sustained inflation than in developed markets because of foreign exchange movements and related second-order effects: FX rates usually move in reaction to the tariffs so, for example, the exporter’s currency weakens to help to offset the price differential from the tariff. The weaker currency can have an inflationary effect because it affects the entire basket of imported goods. Furthermore, reduced capex and FDI in an emerging market as supply chains reorient can also lead to a weaker currency and higher inflation. Overall, there are higher incidences of indexation and exposure to goods price volatility, since a greater portion of their consumption basket is goods. Where inflation expectations may be less anchored or where real rates need to be held higher, there are higher risks of inflation persistence.

The easiest way to see the effect is to analyse the 2018 tariffs on approximately US \$350 billion worth of Chinese imports, which followed theoretical expectations closely. The price level rose in line with the tariff expectation, but the inflation was completely out of the tariffed goods one year later: The goods that saw price rises from tariffs were such a small part of the consumption basket that the overall inflation gauge barely budged.

Theoretically, a high ratio of exports/GDP implies a greater reliance on exports for economic growth, and naturally the pass-through of lower external demand brought on by tariffs to overall economic growth and labour markets is higher. On the other hand, higher imports relative to GDP imply that domestic consumption and/or production are heavily dependent on external inputs: Currency depreciation or supply chain disruption-induced increases in import prices are likely to have a larger impact on consumer and producer prices. Larger trade deficits place persistent pressure on currencies, while large trade surpluses could make a country more vulnerable to measures in response.

## Upside to nearshoring?

The nearshoring trend of recent years could accelerate but will likely depend on the shape of tariff policy: An all-encompassing tariff policy will solicit different reactions to a targeted tariff policy. Furthermore, how these tariffs are handled relative to existing free-trade agreements is also important. The differentiation should be consequential for both trade but also investment decisions in the medium term.

Heightened trade tensions that lead to targeted tariffs with China could expedite American companies' nearshoring plans to Latin America: Mexico, with its participation in NAFTA, is the most likely winner from such a scenario as nearshoring investment could accelerate. However, there is concern that China could try to use Mexico to bypass tariffs, with higher Chinese investment in Mexico recently, so there are risks that a Republican administration tries a broader tariff scope.

There are likely opportunities for a wider subset of Latin American economies, with Central America particularly in focus – Costa Rica, the Dominican Republic and Panama, and Guatemala to a lesser extent: For example, the Dominican Republic, Costa Rica and Panama are currently part of the Biden administration's Americas Partnership for Economic Prosperity and have been in discussions to take advantage of the chip manufacturing part of the US' CHIPS Act. We do not expect any changes to the CHIPS Act given its bipartisan support and see opportunities for the region to participate in downstream semiconductor production. Barbados, Colombia, Ecuador, Mexico, Chile, Peru and Uruguay are also members. Involvement in the Central America Free Trade Agreement (CAFTA-DR) should also help the region.

## Trade agreements at risk?

USMCA review in 2026 a risk to Mexico: The USMCA agreement includes a sunset clause that requires its three members (the US, Canada and Mexico) to certify in writing on July 1, 2026 that they intend to continue the agreement. For more details, see Mexico Focus – USMCA renegotiation risks should not be underestimated.

Risks to the Central American Free Trade Agreement (CAFTA-DR): This trade agreement was signed into law by the Bush administration in 2005 and all partner countries formally adopted it through 2009. It includes Costa Rica, the Dominican Republic, El Salvador, Guatemala, Honduras and Nicaragua. A former Latin American advisor to Trump has noted that he would like the US to withdraw from this agreement. During Trump's prior administration, he did not touch the agreement, likely because the US still has a trade surplus with all countries involved. Given Trump's focus on migration, we watch to see if he uses the agreement as a lever for further domestic action to prevent emigration as he did with bilateral aid during his last administration (see Bilateral aid : LatAm focus ).

The CAFTA-DR is a key agreement for American companies bringing operations into the region, so its rollback could have negative implications for countries set to benefit from this theme. We see Costa Rica and the Dominican Republic as potentially most negatively impacted in the event of a withdrawal from a nearshoring standpoint.

AGOA expiry in 2025 a risk to Africa: It is unlikely that the renewal of Africa Growth and Opportunity Act (AGOA) in 2025 will be high on Trump's list as he has previously preferred bilateral trade arrangements as opposed to collective programmes. The fact that it is non-reciprocal further raises the risks.

This poses downside risks to countries like Kenya, Nigeria and South Africa, which collectively have a high weight in the EMBIG-Div index and whose exports under AGOA as a proportion of total exports are relatively high. For Kenya, this is particularly important as the current account deficit remains high and external vulnerabilities have been a source of recent credit rating downgrades.

Status quo: Slightly stricter migration policy to materialise in 1H25, including stricter border enforcement via leverage of executive authorities. Remittance growth to slow but remain positive.

Potential Trump impact: High likelihood of even stricter migration policy, with three potential slower immigration scenarios seeing net immigration range from 0 to 1.4 million. ## Remittances at risk

Stricter US migration policy is a large risk to remittance flows in the medium term: In the years since the pandemic, we have seen a surge in remittances to Latin America, which we have attributed in part to the surge in migrant flows to the US, as outlined in EM Fixed Income Strategy, Economics & US Public Policy: Immigration Driving Remittances, April 12, 2024. We identify US growth, employment of Hispanic workers and inflation as the primary statistically significant variables that impact remittance growth in the US. Higher migration numbers have helped both US growth and expanding the Hispanic labour force in the US.

The US election outcome should have important impacts on future immigration numbers, and in turn remittances: Our US public policy strategists expect more stringent immigration policies to be implemented regardless of the election outcome, but for the most stringent immigration policies to be implemented under a Republican sweep election outcome. Our US economists outline five scenarios for immigration numbers next year based on different immigration policies being implemented ( Exhibit 56 ). They expect a negligible impact on inflation in these scenarios, but do see downside risks to growth and employment.

Any more stringent immigration policy should have a negative impact on US growth: In their baseline scenario with no policy change, they expect growth to come in at their current forecast of 2.2% for 2025, while in their most negative scenario for immigration, they expect growth to come in 1pp below that forecast. We note that other policies being discussed by the Trump administration, including the tariff policies outlined later in this report, could result in further negative pressures on US growth, but here we focus on only impacts from immigration policy.

The primary risk to the Hispanic employed population in the US is stricter deportations: Still, it is worth noting that with the record growth in migration and

Hispanic employment, even a slowdown in migration to 0 should keep a larger than prepandemic base of people sending remittances from the US.

## 1. The IRA

We view the chances of the IRA being fully repealed as low, even in the event of a Republican sweep: Bipartisan support for domestic manufacturing, nuclear power and the wind and solar tax credit gives us confidence that there is low risk of repeal. A few reasons explain why we see lower relative risk of repeal for these credits: 1) The solar ITC and wind PTC have been extended in the past even under Republican administrations (though we note that Trump specifically has taken aim at offshore wind); 2) There is growing bipartisan support for domestic energy supply and manufacturing, and increasing recognition around the value of nuclear power; and 3) \~80% of domestic manufacturing investments have been awarded to Republican-led districts, driving local job creation and further insulating 45X (the domestic manufacturing tax credit) from repeal risk as political incentives to claw back the funding wane with further deployment.

In the event of a Republican sweep, we have identified a handful of energy-related priorities that would only need a simple majority to pass, including those of particular concern to its trade partners:

• Much broader/stricter FEOC (Foreign Entity of Concern) language: Changes to FEOC interpretation in the IRA could impact manufacturing tax credits and the commercial EV credit. Specifically, a new administration could broaden the FEOC restrictions by utilising other tools (sourcing requirements, rules of origin, etc.) to change the way these credits are deployed.

• Enhancement of domestic content requirements (such as the 45X domestic manufacturing credit, which currently includes a subsidy even if the raw materials were not produced in the US). We would expect that this could be strengthened to limit the amount of IRA money flowing beyond the US border.

• Broad-based tariffs on China/SE Asia: We see elevated risk for the energy storage market in particular given the deep-rooted linkage to China. Additional tariffs put in place on clean energy equipment imports, particularly energy storage, are a potential threat given the sector's reliance on China – as it stands today, the majority of the battery supply chain (\~70%) is linked to China. More broadly, we see risk to other countries in SE Asia, including Cambodia, Malaysia, Thailand and Vietnam as part of the solar value chain. We note that Cambodia, Malaysia, Thailand and Vietnam accounted for 84% of US panel imports in 4Q23 according to S&P Global, though many manufacturers operating in these countries are Chinese-domiciled entities. Companies that can demonstrate supply chain independence from China despite being domiciled in South-East Asia will likely fare better than those with links back to China.

## 2. Carbon taxation

We view federal carbon taxation as unlikely though still possible under either a Harris or Trump administration, given historical bipartisan support across both sides of the aisle for the idea. A carbon border adjustment mechanism (CBAM) – essentially a tax at the border on embodied carbon in imports – is a policy structure that has the most support from both parties.

Proposals to tax carbon have been introduced, but a number of obstacles exist: Lawmakers have proposed myriad ways to achieve the ambitious climate goals set by President Biden from the start of his term in 2021. In particular, certain members of Congress have proposed establishing a carbon price or a carbon border adjustment to mirror that of the EU. For example, Senators Sheldon Whitehouse and Ron Wyden supported the policy as a potential offset for some of the spending associated with the Build Back Better agenda. Other bills (often co-sponsored by a group of bipartisan legislators) also propose taxing imports based on their carbon intensity exceeding the carbon intensity of equivalent domestically produced goods. Despite some support within the Democratic party, the current White House seemingly opposed the policy based on the idea that the administration would not support a tax increase for anyone making less than US\$400,000 per year (a similar rationale that drove opposition to the gas tax increase discussion during the bipartisan infrastructure bill negotiations).

Similarly, one of the key obstacles in implementing a carbon tax is determining what the price of carbon should be; currently, prices globally vary widely, and even domestically the EPA has changed its assessment of the carbon price under different administrations. Hence, without a robust carbon-pricing mechanism it would be difficult for the US to implement a carbon border adjustment similarly to that of the EU, though not impossible.

The Foreign Pollution Fee bill, introduced by Senators Bill Cassidy (R-LA) and Lindsay Graham (R-SC) in November 2023, is one of the more recent examples of how a US CBAM could work: This bill is explicit in that it would not tax any domestic production of carbon; rather, it "ensures the pollution intensity of products imported into the US is no dirtier than similarly produced goods" and "penalizes foreign countries, namely China, who willfully ignore environmental standards and international norms to undercut manufacturing" (from the Cassidy press release, here). Under the proposal, countries with a free-trade agreement with the US would be exempt as long as their pollution intensity of the good is within 50% of the US pollution intensity. Essentially, the

programme would be designed to tax any good from an industry for which the carbon intensity of that entire industry is more carbon-intensive than US production, meaning that individual installations that were carbon-efficient in a high-carbon country could not circumvent the rules.

Of goods scoped in, the FPF goes beyond the EU CBAM and seeks to cover aluminium, batteries, biofuels, cement, crude, glass, hydrogen, iron/steel, minerals, natural gas, petrochemicals, plastics, pulp/paper, refined petroleum products, solar cells and wind turbines.

We view China as the most at risk under a bill like the FPF, as well as Saudi Arabia, Brazil and India: Countries that have a free-trade agreement in place would be exempt from the bill if the pollution intensity is within 50% of US production; Mexico, Korea and Colombia could potentially pick up market share (in addition to developed countries like Canada).

## 3. The Paris Agreement

The Paris Agreement, signed in late 2015, officially entered into force on November 4, 2016: The original stipulations of the treaty were such that any signatory could not withdraw until at least three years had passed. President Trump announced in mid-2017 that he would withdraw from the agreement, but he therefore could not formally give notice until November 4, 2019. The treaty also required notice of one year for withdrawal, meaning that the US did not formally withdraw from the Paris Agreement until November 4, 2020, one day after the US presidential election. The newly elected President Biden announced that the US would rejoin the Paris Agreement once he became president, with the official rejoining of the treaty on February 19, 2021. This history lesson is important for two reasons: 1) A withdrawal now only requires that one-year notice and a new president would not have to wait those same four years; and 2) While Trump was effectively a nonparticipant during that time, we have seen in fact very little time during which the US was not a member of the agreement.

What does it mean to be part of the Paris Agreement? The agreement between 195 countries is the most comprehensive and collaborative effort to limit global warming to well below a 2C increase over pre-industrial levels. The agreement required that at least 55% of global emissions would be covered by signatories in order for it to be implemented. It is a legally binding agreement but differs from previous agreements, notably the Kyoto Protocol, in that each member state voluntarily sets its own nationally determined targets. Countries are therefore not legally obligated to actually decarbonise; rather, international law only compels them to report and review their goals. Put another way, countries are required by law to report on and monitor their goals but they are not under a legal obligation to make those goals strong enough to actually decarbonise or to set in motion the activities to decarbonise. It also does not require by law that companies hit these targets. An updated Nationally Determined Contribution, or NDC, is mandatory every five years. It is also not mandatory for developed countries to contribute to climate finance.

The US last submitted its NDC in April 2021, meaning that it will be required to submit another one if it stays in the treaty by April 2026. A new president could therefore announce its withdrawal from the agreement in January 2025 and formally withdraw in January 2026 in order to avoid publishing its next NDC.

We would expect that a Trump presidency could withdraw again from the Paris Agreement: Under Trump's previous administration, even prior to formal withdrawal, the US ceased its contributions to the Green Climate Fund (previously US\$3 billion per year) and overall climate finance fell to <US\$500 million per year. The US would not participate in the annual Conference of the Parties (COP) negotiations on climate.

It is also plausible that Trump could go a step further and withdraw the US from the Paris Agreement's governing treaty, the UNFCCC (the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change). The US views the Paris Agreement not as a formal international treaty, which requires a two-thirds Senate majority, but as an executive agreement, and hence President Obama was able to sign the Paris Agreement in 2016 without Senate approval (and Trump also could withdraw from Paris without Congress). President George H W Bush signed into the UNFCCC in 1992 with Senate approval; it is not clear if the president can unilaterally withdraw without Congressional approval but we do think that it will be up

for discussion. Moreover, if the US were to withdraw from the UNFCCC, readmittance would likely require a super-majority to re-enter the agreement. Withdrawing from the UNFCCC poses greater long-term complications for the US' participation in international co-operation on climate change.

For countries that remain in the Paris Agreement, a US withdrawal could create pressure to decarbonise more aggressively: The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) will take into account that the US will not try to decarbonise or hit its targets. The IPCC publishes its Assessment Reports (the sixth of which is from 2022) which include a section for policymakers and pathway modelling. We would expect that the US stepping away from its targets would therefore materialise in these scenario analyses, putting greater pressure on other nations to do more to mitigate emissions. With the US out of the agreement, there is also a chance of carbon leakage – meaning that emissions that are prevented in one area are moved into another that has more relaxed rules. For example, carbon-intensive steel production in a country mitigating its own emissions and trying to hit its target could move into the US instead where those emissions are not tracked or mitigated. Lastly, a US withdrawal could create a domino effect and compel other members to withdraw (if the US is not reducing its emissions, why should they?), though we view this scenario as lower probability given we did not see this knock-on effect last time around.

We may also see increased climate commitments from corporates, states and municipalities in response to the federal withdrawal: Following the announcement from Trump that the US would withdraw from Paris, 23 US states and three territories formed the United States Climate Alliance, a non-binding agreement between parties representing roughly 40% of US emissions. These states affirmed their commitment to the Paris Agreement. We may also see a resurgence in corporate commitments in light of a second US government retreat.

## 4. Climate finance

Under the previous Trump administration, climate finance programmes were heavily curtailed: His first proposed budget in 2017 eliminated payments to the UN Climate Change programmes, including the Green Climate Fund (part of the Paris Agreement, with the US pledging US\$3 billion per annum under Obama) and a reduction in funding for the multilateral development banks. We expect that a second Trump term would see more requests to cut spending on climate finance but would require Congressional approval. Trump also consistently proposed lower dollar amounts in foreign aid on climate that ultimately got distributed ( Exhibit 63 ). We view SSA and LatAm as the regions with the most to lose from a climate finance perspective under a second Trump administration ( Exhibit 64 ).

## 5. Oil markets

Morgan Stanley oil & gas teams have explored the impact that executive authority can have on energy policy and the associated impacts on oil and gas markets, as well as the energy and clean power sector (see Oil & Gas: How Could the US Election Impact Energy Policy? August 12, 2024). In short, they view it as unlikely that former President Trump could leverage executive authority to rapidly increase US production levels. Overall, they focus on four key issues:

1. Significantly accelerating US oil production is actually not easy: First, US oil production is already at an all-time high and continues to grow sharply. Second, there is tension between growing oil production and lowering prices at the same time given that breakeven levels on new wells will likely be in the US\$70s/bbl.

2. Potential for import tariffs would dampen commodity demand, due to the negative impact on global trade.

3. Iran oil exports skewed to downside: An implied potential imposition of secondary sanctions on buyers of Iranian oil could resume, as was done back in 2018. That could put several hundred thousand barrels per day of Iranian oil exports at risk, which could drive oil prices higher.

4. Potential removal of subsidies on EVs in the IRA would raise demand: In the short term, this would have only a modest impact on domestic oil demand, in our view. However, this could become more important over time.

All in, markets may view risks as skewed to lower oil prices: Indeed, Trump's own comments suggest that this is an aim. If factors 1 and 2 above become what the market focuses on, it would see oil price risks to the downside. However, as noted, it is not a clearcut case.

The impact on the Gulf credits: While the expansion of production has seen the US turn to a net oil exporter, the trade balance with the Gulf is still a deficit and nearly exclusively comprised of US imports of Gulf oil, at an average of 860kb/d in 2023. Saudi Arabia (440kb/d) and Iraq (320kb/d) are the largest suppliers, but both of these figures are small versus the size of Gulf exports heading to Asia.

The real risk comes not from falling demand from the US for Gulf oil as a result of further strength in domestic production specifically, but the effect of any announcement on supply increases on oil prices. The oil market is already entering a period of demand uncertainty, creating a weaker backdrop for further supply increases.

What's more, any further increase in US supply as a result of the election will come against likely increases in OPEC supply. The organisation is due to reverse some cuts from October 2023 onwards, which would see Saudi production rise from 9mb/d to 10mb/d over a period of 12 months. Following through with the normalisation in oil supply for Saudi Arabia will likely depend on oil market stability,

The Gulf credits still implicitly trade with an assumption of oil prices remaining resilient and not falling below US\$70/bbl for an extended period of time, in our view. Increased fears around a longer period of oil price weakness would hurt Saudi Arabia and Bahrain the most within the Gulf, in our view, given their higher breakevens. Saudi Arabia has a better capability to counteract some of this via accelerated increases in supply than Bahrain.

The Trump administration used foreign aid as a foreign policy tool: The administration used foreign aid as a way to further its policy priorities including reduced climate change financing and cutting aid to Central America in efforts to curb migration from the region into the US, and the establishment of regional aid initiatives focused on US priorities, including the Prosper Africa Initiative, the Indo-Pacific Strategy and the US Strategy for Engagement in Central America.

The establishment of the US International Development Finance Corporation (DFC) as a counterweight to China is an example of this: Trump's administration created the DFC in part as a response to China's Belt and Road Initiative (BRI). In June 2017, the administration's first budget for 2018 proposed an elimination of the US Overseas Private Investment Corporation (OPIC), one of the country's primary international development agencies. This then shifted direction when the administration began to see the agency as an important counterweight to China's BRI. Ultimately, the administration helped to create the bipartisan proposal for the DFC to coordinate OPIC and USAID's financing leg.

Foreign relation stances were also evident when looking at regional aid requests under recent administrations: Foreign aid requests were lower across all regions under Trump while they have increased across all regions under Biden. Under Trump, aid requested declined compared to that requested under Obama across all regions ( Exhibit 69 ). Under Biden, the aid requested has been higher across all regions, increasing notably for Europe, Latin America and East Asia.

• Latin America: Aid to Latin America has always been low compared with that to MENA and SSA. The aid requested in budgets declined under drafts by the Trump administration compared with the eight years prior, both as a share of total and outright. Congress blocked much of these requested cuts and aid to the region remained flat across his term. Under the Biden administration, requests for aid to the region have increased substantially, in part due to the focus on addressing the root causes of migration. Aid to the region has reached highs compared with history, although it has been lower than initially requested in budget drafts.

• MENA: The Middle East has received far more US assistance than Latin America, with only Sub-Saharan Africa seeing comparable levels. While overall aid requests fell dramatically during Trump's first presidency, the Middle East was insulated far more from this effect than other regions. As a result, the share of foreign aid to the Middle East was substantially higher during Trump's presidency than in both Obama's and Biden's terms.

• Europe: Foreign aid requests to Europe have historically been the smallest share of US aid budget requests, but Biden's administration has seen a notable increase both in the share of foreign aid requests and the actual end allocation. In fact, during Biden's presidency, Europe saw nearly 40% more aid disbursements than requested. The increase was largely driven by the Russia/Ukraine conflict and is comprised nearly exclusively of US assistance to Ukraine, with Poland a distant second.

Trump has historically favoured bilateral aid to multilateral aid: Even with the US as a primary shareholder at most major global multilateral development banks, Trump's preference has historically been for leaning more heavily on bilateral relationships. ## Tangible potential impacts under a Trump administration

• Pressure on new funding for MDBs: Any capital raises could become harder. This could also include any effort to rechannel the SDRs towards additional MDB lending, although this would not prevent other countries from doing it.

• More focus on regional financial arrangements: The trend towards a more multipolar world is likely to manifest itself via increased confidence in more regional global financial safety net arrangements, with entities such as $\mathsf { B R I C s } ,$ RSM, the Chiang Mai Initiative, Fed swap lines and PBOC swap arrangements gaining more prominence.

• Potential easier IMF programme approval for perceived allies: Given its 16.5% voting share, the US can veto individual programmes. However, the executive board tends to be more consensus driven. Thus, the bigger impact may be the US positively impacting the approval of potential programmes that it favours, with the large 2018 IMF Argentina programme an example. This time around, this may again include Argentina, and El Salvador to a lesser extent (see more below)

• Slower IMF reform approval: There are a few IMF reforms that could slow or be derailed entirely.

Quota increase delay likely: The most important near-term reform is the already approved 50% IMF quota increase that however still needs approval by all individual members, which includes each member country paying for the quota increase. The US needs to pay a sizeable US\$55 billion and has not paid yet. And without the US it will not go ahead given that it would not make it to 85%. However, it's worth noting that delays beyond the current end-2024 deadline are likely under a Democratic administration too, yet it may get more attention under Trump.

Surcharge reduction delay possible: Recent headlines out of the G20 suggest that more progress has been made on reducing the IMF surcharges – in other words reducing the penalty interest for large outstanding loans. In theory, we think that this should be able to proceed under either administration, yet it could be delayed.

Broader quota reform delay also possible: As part of the approval of the quota increase, it was agreed that the relative quota distribution would be looked at as well. Given that the aim would be to distribute it more fairly, including to China, this may well run into US resistance.

Finally, renewed US-China tensions could also impact debt restructurings: It is well documented that the addition of new bilateral creditors, in particular China, has made sovereign debt restructurings more difficult. Zambia taking 3.5 years is the clearest example. If US-China tensions were to increase under a Trump presidency, it may well also bring up questions of burden sharing between debt extended by China and broader bilaterals and multilaterals. That said, it's also worth noting that the current US Treasury has often talked about this issue already, meaning that it would not be entirely new. Also, luckily most of the current debt restructurings are coming to a conclusion. Perhaps an eventual Venezuela restructuring is where tensions could manifest given that both Western and Chinese creditors have larger exposures.

## Country specifics

## Upside pressure on defence spending, especially in Europe

Self-sufficiency and Ukraine to drive higher defence spending: Our European defence equity strategists have already noted the trend of higher European defence spending since 2017 ( Exhibit 80 ). And as they note, global conflicts and a desire for greater strategic autonomy should see this trend continue, independent of the US election outcome (see The European Defence Dilemma, April 5, 2024).

That said, it is likely that a Trump presidency would put even more front-loaded pressures on spending for a few reasons: 1) To continue providing support for Ukraine; 2) To backfill equipment sold or donated to Ukraine; 3) To structurally elevate readiness levels; and 4) To reduce US dependency.

Yet budgetary realities also a key headwind: However, our European economists also note that limited fiscal headroom due to high European government debt burdens means that the rhetoric for higher defence spending is not matched by budgetary reality. While the current NATO 2% of GDP commitment seems to be in sight, maintaining or going well beyond this may prove challenging barring a major further escalation in the geopolitical environment or harsher US rhetoric.

For the CEE region the increase is likely structural: Defence spending has generally increased as a result of the Russia/Ukraine conflict, and much of CEE and the Baltics now rank among the highest in terms of military budgets. The proximity to Russia clearly matters for this, and Poland stands out for its above-average spending on defence, even when considering its closeness to the conflict ( Exhibit 81 ). We think that this spending is structural and Poland would be one of the key sovereigns in favour of aiming to fill the gap left by any withdrawal of US funding to Ukraine.

Away from defence spending, some sovereigns within the region may also look to foster closer US ties under a Trump presidency where ideological alignment allows this. Hungary is a key example in the CEE where US flows could help to fill some of the gap from blocked EU disbursements, even if that role is currently filled by China.

## Ukraine support and ceasefire talks

Continued support for Ukraine at risk: Trump has often been critical of the aid sent to Ukraine. This is in contrast to the continued support offered by the current Biden administration and is likely to be continued under a Democratic presidency. Ukraine's need for support is significant, both financially and militarily. In case of no further financial support, it would be up to the EU and other allies to increase their share significantly. Overall, this would be a negative for Ukraine, just as it was early 2024 when US funding was being held up in Congress.

A question mark as to whether negotiations could actually end the conflict: Trump has also noted that "both sides will be able to come together and negotiate a deal that ends the violence and paves a path forward to prosperity", or end the conflict on day one. With no details provided and with Russia and Ukraine currently far apart in their conditions for ending the conflict, there will be clear question marks on how feasible such as plan is.

However, it is notable that public opinion in Ukraine has started to shift in 2024. The percentage of respondents to a poll from KIIS on who would accept territorial concessions to Russia for the sake of peace went up to 32% in May this year from 10% in May 2022 and 2023.

Talks of a ceasefire would clearly be positive for both Ukraine and Europe as a whole. Indeed, this is the only scenario where we really see Ukraine bonds outperforming from here as it would pull yields significantly lower and also increase the probability of the upside contingent scenario for the new bonds triggering (see Ukraine: Restructuring Update: Status Check Post a Second and Likely Final Round, July 23, 2024).

The CEE credits, especially Bulgaria, Hungary, Poland, Romania and Serbia, still face overhang from the conflict, both via geopolitical risk premia but also due to the fiscal impact and subdued growth profile in key western European export markets. Greater momentum on a ceasefire in Ukraine would be mostly positive for these credits, in our view, especially for Hungary given that Russia/Ukraine has commonly featured as a point of tension for this credit's relationship with the EU.

## Venezuela policy

A very uncertain situation in the near term: The elections at end-July were always the key catalyst this year and so far the outcome is unclear, with the opposition contesting the results. At this point the US has recognised that the opposition was the winner but without going the further step as back in 2019 to recognise the opposition as the official administration.

A Trump presidency could be more hawkish: The maximum sanctions policy was implemented under the first Trump administration. However, as detailed in Sanctions use , it's not clear that this will see the same focus this time around. Additionally, the now much more important immigration problem will also influence how hawkish Venezuela policy becomes under either a Democratic or Republican administration.

Chances of a debt restructuring the key driver for bonds: A key driver for bonds will be the possibility of conducting a debt restructuring. So far the uncertainty on the back of the elections has likely pushed the timing out, given that in order to conduct a debt restructuring sanctions will need to be eased (or licenses provided) on primary market trading. While impossible to assign a probability, we see a willingness from all sides to actually work towards an eventual restructuring, including Venezuela, the US and bond holders. That said, for now the issue at hand is the election outcome.

## Middle East and North Africa

Iran policy could see Middle East geopolitical risk premia remain higher for longer: One of the key drivers of Middle East risk premia in a Trump presidency is the likelihood of more hawkish policy with regard to Iran. This has been emphasised repeatedly both by Trump and Vance, with Trump mentioning the effectiveness of sanctions against Iran in his first term.

Unlike during the prior Trump presidency, where Saudi-Iran relationships were comparatively less favourable, this change in US policy towards Iran would come in the context of normalised relationships between Saudi Arabia and Iran. The normalisation, and the resulting improvement in Yemen, had helped to bring down regional risk premia – as measured by QATAR CDS – to new lows.

While geopolitical risk premia have already increased notably since October 2023, the Saudi-Iran détente has been one of the key reasons why Gulf credits have remained comparatively resilient in the face of regional geopolitical risk. Any change in this status quo, or greater pressure on the Gulf states to dial back Iran ties, could create a downside risk to these credits.

Both presidencies are likely to pursue a de-escalation in Gaza, but support for further regional actions varies: Both presidential candidates have expressed the need to ramp down the Israel-Hamas conflict, but Trump has talked more about leaving the resolution of the conflict to Israel. While the conflict is the most near-term focus, a Trump presidency may also be more receptive to Israel engaging Iranian-linked entities in the Middle East. Meanwhile, Harris has mentioned focusing on diplomatic solutions.

## Sub-Saharan Africa

• Kenya stands out as one of the few African countries that had positive engagement with Trump during his first term. This could improve given Kenya's strategic importance to the US in East Africa. Kenya is important in keeping extremist religious insurgency at bay in the region.

• South Africa is at risk of seeing relations with the US deteriorate given its nonaligned policy about Russian aggression and its recent litigation of Israel (US ally) at the International Criminal Court. However, the recently formed Government of National Unity in South Africa opens the door for a resetting of relations with the US as the new government may be more amenable to a more moderate stance on key issues.

## Sanctions use

US sanctions have been used by both administrations and had material market impacts: Both the Biden and Trump administrations put significant sanctions in place on other countries, namely Venezuela under Trump and Russia under Biden.

Further bond-specific sanctions less likely under either administration, in our view: Both administrations included sanctions on trading sovereigns bonds in the secondary market. While Russia's are still in place, Venezuela's bonds can now be traded in the secondary market again. Specifically, OFAC amended two general licenses to remove the secondary trading ban on certain Venezuelan sovereign bonds and PdVSA debt. At the time, the comment from OFAC was that "Treasury assesses that this would have the positive effect of displacing nefarious players in this market, and with negligible financial benefit to the Venezuelan regime". On the back of this, it seems much less likely that secondary bond trading will be targeted in the future by any administration.

uncertain: While sanctions enacted on Russia on the back of the invasion of Ukraine showed a significant willingness of the US to use the full force of sanctions, including freezing the FX reserves of Russia, the Biden administration also recently eased sanctions on Venezuela. Under a Democratic administration, sanctions use will therefore likely remain an important tool that can be dialled up and down.

Under Trump, there is some evidence, although incomplete, that sanctions may not always be the favoured tool. First, Trump expressed doubts that the maximum sanctions path taken together with Guaido was the right one (see Axios article). Second, in his recent Bloomberg Business week interview, he noted that "I don’t love sanctions". Third, Elbridge Colby, who worked on Trump's national security team and may have a role in a potential second administration, also noted that he doesn’t like sanctions in a recent interview.

We think this does suggest that a potential reduction in sanctions, including unfreezing Russia's FX reserves, could be part of negotiations to end the conflict (although it's worth noting that most of the frozen assets are in the EU, not the US). It also suggests that a return to the maximum sanctions policy on Venezuela is less likely.

Asia most impacted by trade policy: As discussed in the sections above, there are many moving parts in the US election. For Asia markets in particular, any changes in tariff and trade policy should be the key focus given the export-oriented nature of many economies in this region. Our economists highlight that it is possible that the negative impact of tariffs on corporate confidence and capex could be smaller than in 2018-19, given that trade tensions have persisted for some time, but ultimately it will depend on the intensity of the trade measures implemented.

## How much could CNH weaken by if tariffs are imposed?

Looking at the 2018-19 experience... One of the measures that could help to mitigate the impact of tariffs on exports is through the currency, i.e., by allowing RMB to weaken as part of the balance of payments adjustment process. Indeed, in 2018-19, when the US announced tariffs on China imports, RMB weakened both against USD and on the CFETS basket.

...suggests that CNH could weaken by the magnitude of the tariff hike… Back then, the US announced four tranches of tariffs on China imports, spread over approximately 1.5 years. Altogether, US\$370 billion of China's exports to the US were tariffed at rates ranging from 7.5-25% ( Exhibit 84 ). The total tariff bill amounted to US\$71.5 billion. As China's total exports to US in that year were nearly US\$550 billion, the average tariff rate was about 13%.

Between May 2018 and September 2019, which covers most of the episodes of trade tensions, RMB weakened around 14% versus USD and around 7% on the CFETS basket. This experience shows that: 1) RMB could be allowed to weaken to offset the tariff impact; and 2) USD/CNH gained to a similar extent as the tariff amount.

...or 80% of it: This result is broadly consistent with that if we compare the gains in USD/ CNH with the rise in the US trade-weighted average tariff on Chinese imports during that period ( Exhibit 85 ). From May 2018 to September 2019, the US' trade-weighted tariff on Chinese imports rose by 17.2pp from 3.8% to 21%. With the 14% gain in USD/CNH over that period, this implies that the rise in USD/CNH was about 80% of the increase in the tariff rate.

This time it could be less: The 2018-19 experience would therefore suggest that USD/ CNH could gain by around 80-100% of the increase in the tariff rate. However, it is unlikely that CNH would depreciate to the same extent this time, given that the size of the tariff hike that has been floated in this election campaign is at least double that seen in 2018-19. Furthermore, China's rewiring of exports to new geographies could help to mitigate the impact of tariffs from the US.

Precedence of trade tensions for India and Thailand: India and Thailand have also seen trade tensions with the US rise under the previous Trump administration. In March 2019, the US removed India from the Generalised System of Preferences (GSP) programme, which allows eligible economies to export certain products to the US tax-free. Thailand similarly saw some of its trade preferences under the GSP programme being suspended in 2019-20.

In his recent Bloomberg interview, Trump has also mentioned India tariffing a US motorcycle manufacturer, repeating a comment which has been made several times last year and during his administration.

When it comes to the currency impact of trade tensions, we think that there are three layers to consider:

• Whether it is a blanket tariff on all of the US' trading partners or a more targeted, bilateral tariff on specific trading partners;

• Sensitivity of currencies to moves in USD/CNH; and

• Risk sensitivity of currencies.

In the event of blanket tariffs, expectations of slower global trade could weigh disproportionately more on the currencies of export-oriented economies like SGD, MYR, TWD, THB and KRW. Risk appetite would also likely sour, which should weigh on high-beta currencies like KRW, THB, IDR and TWD.

In the event of more targeted, bilateral tariffs, currencies of economies which are export-oriented, run a large trade surplus with the US and export products that are at higher risk of tariffs could face higher risks. In this regard, CNH, KRW, THB and MYR could see greater pressure. Given that THB, KRW, MYR, SGD and TWD have a high beta to CNH, any weakness in CNH could also spill over to these currencies.

Put together, KRW, THB, MYR and TWD appear to be more vulnerable to any changes in trade or tariff policy on the back of the US election, in addition to CNH: However, the THB weakness could be cushioned to some extent by a rise in gold prices if risk appetite weakens.

While India also runs a large trade surplus with the US, its more domestically driven economy, coupled with the RBI's reaction function on the currency, may help to cushion the effect on the currency. INR could thus weaken alongside the stronger USD but is unlikely to underperform relative to the rest of Asia.

# Mexico: More Vulnerable than You Think

## Ioana Zamfir

Mexico's economic integration with the US has continued to strengthen over the last four years via trade and remittances: The topic of nearshoring has dominated Mexicorelated investor conversations for a few years now. Anecdotally, optimism about Mexico's growing importance within the supply chain network of the US remains quite high.

As shown below, since 2020 the rebound in Mexico's exports has far outpaced the recovery in domestic consumption, implying that Mexico's economy has become more of a beta play on the state of the US economy than it had been in recent years. Another important contributor to the incremental economic integration between the two countries has been the sharp rise in Mexico's remittances (90% of which come from the US). As shown below, since 2020 remittances have increased by almost 50% on a 12-month rolling basis and have recently surpassed US\$60 billion cumulatively.

However, we believe that these two factors have actually just increased Mexico's reliance on external sources of economic growth (in the absence of meaningful domestic policy measures), making it extremely vulnerable to potential US policies that target remittances or trade, even if not specifically aimed at Mexico.

## 1. Taxing remittances would have a multifaceted impact on Mexico

The main factors behind the substantial rise in remittances have been somewhat trickier to pinpoint and drivers often quoted include a rise in electronic transfer options, higher levels of US immigration (though as explained here formal Mexican immigration to the US has slowed in the last years) and generous US fiscal stimulus in 2020-21 followed by a sharp recovery in Hispanic employment in the US (90% of Mexico's remittances come from the US).

As discussed in Remittances at risk , recent headlines have flagged a bill introduced in late 2023 by vice-presidential candidate JD Vance to apply a 10% tax on remittances. The idea of taxing remittances is not new – in 2017, another Congressional bill (The Border Wall Funding Act of 2017) floated the idea of applying a 2% tax on remittances to Latin American and the Caribbean.

Taxing remittances has not been a highlight of the current presidential campaigns so far (as of August 2024). That said, we think that Mexico would be quite vulnerable to such a measure, if it were to be implemented, via three channels:

1. MXN: Over the past years, the very strong ongoing economic integration between Mexico and the US has led the peso to behave like 'the dollar of EM'. It is no coincidence that when NFPs surprise to the downside, MXN tends to lag its EM peers and sometimes even underperform versus USD, as the currency is likely perceived as a high-carry EM proxy for the state of the US labour market (given remittances). Remittances from the US represent a substantial inflow of USD to the country, providing implicit support for the peso and contributing to supporting what we have flagged as relatively expensive valuations. To the extent that the structural trend higher in remittances is reversed by taxes, we would expect MXN's valuation to structurally correct as well, to pre-2020 levels. In other words, 20.00 could become the new 17.00, though the actual 'fair value' would depend on the size and scope of such a measure and on the ability of 'informal' channels to absorb some of the flow.

2. Growth: Though hard to quantify their exact impact on growth in Mexico, remittances have undeniably provided economic support for locals, amid a lack of domestic fiscal support or growth-enhancing reforms. To the extent that the US remittances flow is curbed by taxation, we would likely see some decoupling between the two economies and a break in their recent integration. As the reform agenda of the incoming administration continues to lack substantial productivity or growth-enhancing measures, we would expect the Mexican economy to start to lag the US economy growth-wise, though once again the impact would depend on the details of such measures.

3. Current account: Remittances to Mexico have ensured some stability in the country's external accounts, supporting a healthy current account profile and offsetting deficits in other line items. Notably, the recent improvements in Mexico's trade balance (both oil and non-oil) have lowered some of the pressure on remittances to support the current account balance. However, we think that the first-round effect of taxes could be a widening current account deficit, assuming that the net amount of USD that reaches Mexico falls.

## 2. USMCA renegotiation risks should not be underestimated

A pick-up of USMCA-related noise into its 2026 sunset clause is very likely (both short term and medium term): The USMCA agreement includes a sunset clause that requires its three members (the US, Canada and Mexico) to certify in writing on July 1, 2026 that they intend to continue the agreement. As noted by our public policy team, at that point the three members can extend the agreement for another 16 years, or they can agree to a series of annual reviews to potentially renew the agreement before it expires in 2036.

In a more volatile scenario for Mexico, a future US president could use the sunset clause as an opportunity to renegotiate some USMCA terms. In this scenario, Mexico and Canada would likely focus on limiting the amount of concessions they would have to make, but the headlines would likely be quite noisy. This would be a very important risk for Mexican assets, as investors would be quite sensitive to potential threats to the very strong economic integration between Mexico and the US.

USMCA headline noise could pick up around September, once campaigning for the US presidential elections picks up more meaningfully. We generally expect 3Q and 4Q to be quite volatile for MXN, especially as the country's 2025 budget will also be presented.

That said, we think that USMCA is likely to remain a live topic in 2025 as well, especially if the US decides to request additional concessions from its trading partners. It is still a bit too early to trade this topic and uncertainty is bound to persist, but we think that traderelated risks for MXN over the next two years are likely underestimated by investors, anecdotally.

## 3. Mexico would be vulnerable to US tariffs on China (or global tariffs)

Mexico is not exactly capitalising on gaps left by China in US imports... As described here, Mexico's market share of US manufacturing imports is now hovering around 17%, from \~13% in late 2020. Over the same period, China's market share has declined from \~20% of US manufacturing imports to \~11%.

That said, Mexico has more or less forged its own path in terms of growing its trade relationship with the US and did not exactly provide substitutes for items previously imported from China in greater quantities. Below we show the cumulative 12-month value of US manufacturing imports from Mexico and China as of January 2016 and as of May 2024.

On the China front, computers and electronic product imports to the US dropped by almost 25% in total USD value (from \~US\$170 billion to \~US\$120 billion) and were arguably the category most impacted by trade tensions. Mexico's exports to the US in the same category increased by \~40% versus 2016, but the delta only makes up for roughly half of the gap left by China, in USD terms.

Mexico also did not capitalise on other manufacturing imports where China lost market share with the US, such as apparel or leather and allied products.

Mexico really gained market share with the US in the transportation equipment category (which includes vehicles and vehicle parts), which almost doubled since 2016 on a 12- month cumulative basis. However, on this front, China was never really a big exporter to the US, implying that Mexico did not exactly fill a void left by China, but mostly ended up in a position to meet growing demand in the US auto sector.

Mexico also gained US imports market share in the electrical appliances imports category, though China has continued to increase its market share as well.
