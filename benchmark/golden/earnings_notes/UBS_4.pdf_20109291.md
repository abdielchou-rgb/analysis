# Cloud Infrastructure

# 2Q24 Preview of AWS, Microsoft Azure and Google Cloud – Spending Trends Appear Solid

## Summary

As we approach the 2Q24 earnings calls for Microsoft, Amazon and Google, we share our thoughts about their cloud segments Azure, AWS and Google Cloud, respectively, after speaking to \~30 customers and partners. Bottom line, overall cloud infrastructure spending appears to be stable if not slightly improving, a welcome relief post several Software firms posting weak results and citing tighter IT spend over the last few months. As a result, we see upside potential to investor growth bogeys. We’re hosting an investor call on Thu Jul 18<sup>th</sup> at 8:30am ET to discuss our updated views.

## Key Insights From Our Checks

Reflecting on our checks this quarter, we conclude: 1. While our checks made frequent references to a still-tight spending backdrop (it’s not a party out there), we did NOT hear anecdotes of any material incremental spending tightness in April/May a la peers in the Software sector. Rather, the overall consensus was “stable” if not slightly improved given a) the ramp in AI spend and b) early evidence of a pick-up in on-premise to cloud migration activity. 2. In terms of share shifts among the Big 3, a consistent theme was the number of customers and partners that cited share gains by Microsoft resulting from its early lead on the AI front. This has been a recurring theme from checks over the last 6-12 months. That said, we’ve spoken to several very large AWS customers that appear content to build their AI stack on AWS (less switching than some might think). 3. Historically, on-premise to cloud migration activity has been a major driver of AWS, Azure and Google Cloud growth, and the pace of migration activity has clearly slowed over the last 18 months. We’re now hearing more signals of some pick-up in cloud migration activity. In particular, several checks flagged an early improvement in data migrations, spurred-on by AI and the desire to “unlock value” from their on-premise data.

## Read-through to Microsoft, Amazon and Google

In our view Street expectations for AWS, Microsoft Azure and Google Cloud growth appear to be in a good place, with a strong potential for modest (but not material given the still-tough spending backdrop) upside to investor bogeys. For Microsoft Azure, we believe that the investor consensus is for 32% c/c Azure growth in 4Q/Jun (a 1-point beat off the high end) and guidance for \~30% growth in 1Q/Sept. We don’t see material upside potential to this estimate but conclude that 32% is doable. For Amazon (covered by our Internet team), investors are now expecting 2Q24 AWS growth of 18% (a 1-point acceleration) while we are conservatively modeling 16% growth. For Google Cloud, the Street is modeling a slight deceleration to 27% growth in 2Q24, while our Google coverage team is modeling more conservative 24% growth.

## Net, What Did We Learn?

Let’s now reflect on all \~30 customer/partner checks above and call out common points and patterns. We would highlight the following:

## 1. Budgets Still Tight, but Cloud Spend Resilient

The last several months have not been good for the Software sector. As we outlined in a recent note (see link to our “What’s Going on in the Software Sector?” note here), a number of companies (Salesforce, Workday, MongoDB) have missed and cited a downturn in the overall spending environment in April/May, with enterprise CIOs flagging some “AI crowding out” as well as heightened spend rationalization. As we conducted these Cloud customer and partner checks, we were listening intently for anecdotes of incremental spending weakness in April/May that resulted in partners landing below their AWS/Azure practice growth targets and customers even gently tapping on the spending brakes.

To be clear, the anecdotes above include frequent references to a still-tight spending backdrop. It’s not a party out there. Our checks spoke to persistent belt-tightening, a continued effort to find more Cloud spend optimization savings and one check cited likely 2H election-driven spending uncertainty. Partner 14 above summed it up by saying “It’s still a tough spending environment out there, many of our clients remain in costcontrol mode and are asking for clear ROI before making further commits”. That said, we did NOT hear anecdotes of any material incremental spending tightness in April/May a la peers in the Software sector. Rather, the overall consensus was a “still-tough but stable” spending backdrop. While this feedback likely caps the probability of a material beat relative to Street AWS/Azure growth expectations, it points to a relatively low risk of a miss and in our view implies that a “modest beat” in 2Q24 and the coming quarters is probably the most likely outcome.

## 2. AI Interest is Very High, Spend Is Early-Stage

We’ve authored a number of reports (see our latest here, our recent AI Survey of \~125 enterprises) describing the pace of enterprise AI go-lives (AI applications going inproduction at scale) as being modest, with 4Q24 or 1H25 likely being the period of real acceleration. If anything, we were slightly more encouraged after this round of cloud checks, for the simple reason that almost every single conversation with AWS, Azure and Google Cloud partners was largely or partly about AI adoption. To say that it is top-ofmind is probably an understatement, as it infused almost every conversation that we highlighted above such that one would objectively conclude that it is inevitable that real AI-related spend will come, it is just a matter of time. ## 4. Data Migration Activity Picking Up?

Historically, on-premise to cloud migration activity has been a major driver of AWS, Azure and Google Cloud growth, and in our view the pace of migration activity has slowed over the last 18 months during this tighter IT spending backdrop. As such, we’re listening very carefully for signals that it may be starting to come to life again. While the signals are not yet powerful, on the margin these checks above did suggest some pickup in cloud migration activity. Note the commentary from Partner 13 above that many enterprises have under-spent against their cloud commits, and are now accelerating their migration activity as a result. Another partner above said “overall, the cloud spending environment feels better than in 2023. Customers can’t cut forever, at some point the show must go on and indeed, we’re seeing some uptick in on-prem to cloud migration activity”. We asked this specific migration question in our recent AI Survey and show the responses in Figure 2 on the following page. The responses validated this thesis, as a full 66% of respondents expect AI initiatives to positively impact migration plans.

In particular, several checks flagged an early improvement in data migrations, spurredon by AI and the desire to “unlock value” from their on-premise data. ## The OpenAI-Oracle Deal

One material catalyst for Oracle shares post the 4Q/May print was the announcement of the OCI contract with OpenAI. On this deal, Oracle said: “OpenAI selected Oracle to run deep learning and AI workloads on Oracle Cloud Infrastructure, because it is the world's fastest and most cost-effective AI infrastructure.We're building a very, very large data center…primarily for training, we're doing masses and masses of training”. The confusing thing about this OpenAI deal announcement is that the press release from OpenAI sent a different message and was very specific about Microsoft’s involvement, saying that Oracle, Microsoft and OpenAl are partnering to extend the Microsoft Azure Al platform to OCI. Indeed, as if to remove any misunderstanding, the next morning OpenAI tweeted that:

 We want to clarify some inadvertent confusion. Our strategic cloud relationship with Microsoft is unchanged. The partnership with OCI enables OpenAI to use the Azure AI platform on OCI infrastructure for inference and other needs. All pretraining of our frontier models continues to happen on supercomputers built in partnership with Microsoft.

The reason that this is important for Microsoft investors in particular is that Microsoft has communicated that it has an exclusive partnership with OpenAI, and that all OpenAI model training and inference is done on Azure. This cannot be the case if OpenAI signed a deal with Oracle OCI for training. It seems likely to us that Microsoft is technically deploying Azure software on Oracle OCI servers and that OpenAI and Microsoft are utilizing Oracle in part because Microsoft is capacity-constrained. This is important for investors, for at least two reasons. First, it signals that Microsoft remains supplyconstrained, consistent with Microsoft’s messaging about Azure AI demand exceeding supply. Second, if indeed Microsoft is deploying Azure software on OCI, then Microsoft may be recognizing Azure revenues from the OpenAI-Oracle deal, as they are when offloading workloads to other 3rd-party infrastructures such as CoreWeave. If so, then the existing supply constraints may not be a material a drag on reported Azure growth.

## CoreWeave Commentary

Privately-held GPU cloud infrastructure provider CoreWeave has become an increasingly relevant “check” on the demand trends that the hyerscalers are seeing, although CoreWeave (as well as Lambda and other peers) is only a readthrough to the highgrowth AI or GPU-based infrastructure demand. Below are observations from our last few conversations with CoreWeave (over the last few months) that are relevant for investors in Microsoft, Amazon and Google:

While CoreWeave’s GPU demand in 2023 was dominated by AI start-ups, CoreWeave is now saying that the majority of its committed backlog is from enterprise and software customers (a shift that began in earnest in 4Q23), with a still-strong portion from AI startups (“day-zero tech adopters”) that CoreWeave argues are “not getting the engineering solutions they needed” from AWS, Google Cloud and Oracle OCI. CoreWeave was clear that Microsoft is not underdelivering but note that Microsoft is a large customer that in our view has acquired material GPU capacity from CoreWeave. CoreWeave is seeing larger commits from enterprise customers with “substantial interest in non-blocking InfiniBand fabrics at large scale”.

CoreWeave argues that it has an available GPU capacity advantage, as it was early to secure data center and GPU capacity through the end of 2025, such that it has “an immense amount of capacity that you can’t get your hands on in that timeframe today. Anyone who didn’t get that is going to be severely bottlenecked regardless of their demand. It’s a highly competitive data center market through 2026”.

 In terms of capacity constraints, CoreWeave points to the lack of availability of “power-related infrastructure, sheer power capacity” and (this was new for us), a shortage of “step-down voltage transformer equipment”. Note that CoreWeave is not flagging a shortage of GPUs or land.

 On the training/inference demand, CoreWeave is signaling a shift, from \~90%/10% in 2023 to perhaps \~70%/30% today based on utilization (a pick-up in inference demand from the GPU cloud providers is a key signal that AI workloads are moving into production).

CoreWeave is utilizing a 6-year depreciation schedule for GPUs. If Microsoft and Amazon are using a similarly long useful life for Nvidia H100s, this obviously mitigates the magnitude of the near-term depreciation expense drag.

## Data Center Industry Expert 1

Demand is strong as it’s ever been from the hyperscale community for data center space. It’s becoming more challenging to categorize these companies because the lines are blurring between cloud, hyperscale, AI, etc. There’s tremendous demand from the largest tech companies in the world. Your more pure-play AI companies are still in the smaller tranche, 10MW data centers, with the exception of the bigger one or two realizing they need to move up the stack to get product, companies like a CoreWeave or a Lambda Labs. If there’s smaller 10-20MW opportunities they’ll take it, but they’re moving up the chain. They’re not taking down a 350MW space like Microsoft but they’re getting larger. There’s a tremendous amount of demand for 2-6MW from enterprise companies that have shifted their business because of AI. These are your Workdays, things like that, companies that are in the business of trading on data. The more they can sell, that boosts data center demand to boost that revenue. Demand is strong for 2MW to 300MW RFP requirements.

 Power is definitely not getting better. I think we’re seeing the power conundrums are for things that are 3-5 years out. Anything that’s coming up within 24 months has extreme competition.

The portion of high-density (AI) workloads is going up overall, closer to 30%, but I talk to several operators who are saying that there is a top down, cognizant effort for most DC providers to keep their pureplay AI business under 10%. They have such a tremendous amount of demand from AI companies but there’s of course credit-worthiness considerations. Those deployments under Microsoft, AWS, Oracle, Google, those are increasing. Those like CoreWeave and Lambda have demonstrated robust demand, not a problem. The rest are struggling to complete deployments as the lease providers are looking to keep exposure down. There are few DC vendors who want to take the risk on that.

There are increased costs as it relates to what I’d call a more purpose-built AI facility. There was one AI company deal that was presented with a \$180’s rate because of credit worthiness concerns but they had to pay it because it’s their business.

 There’s a tremendous amount of AI landing in second and third generation data centers because those are turnkey with power and water already in them. Floor loads and ceiling heights might be less optimal, but they have what they need to make it work.

## Commentary from Smaller Cloud “Proxies”

The combined revenue run rates of the cloud segments of Amazon, Microsoft and Google - \$215 billion in 1Q24 – are so large that cloud consumption shifts cascade into several corners of the technology market. Let’s look for clues into recent demand momentum shifts from other relevant tech firms, namely Snowflake, Datadog and MongoDB that derive a large part of their revs from products optimized for and running on the public cloud infrastructure and (in the case of Snowflake and MongoDB) that are on April quarters and hence had another month to report on relative to Amazon, Microsoft and Google.

## Imperfect Correlation to AWS/Azure

Before discussing the results and commentary from the smaller “cloud proxies”, it’s worth putting their results in context, specifically about the degree of correlation to AWS/Azure spend. In mid-May, we hosted the management team of Datadog for a virtual investor call and touched specifically on the degree of correlation between Datadog’s revenue results and those of AWS/Azure. While in our view the correlation remains high (one can see this is the timing of when Datadog revs started coming under pressure in 2022 and when they started stabilizing and improving in 2023. The timeline matches up very well with what AWS and Azure saw). That said, the correlation has weakened somewhat over the last few quarters, as AWS and Azure revs growth rate has accelerated and yet the smaller cloud proxies are on average posting stable revs growth (modest acceleration for Datadog, modest deceleration for Snowflake and MongoDB).

Why the growing divergence? On Datadog in particular, we can think of a few things (that may also apply to Snowflake, MongoDB and Confluent):

 Datadog is highly correlated to new on-prem to cloud migration activity. This is improving but is only one AWS/Azure growth driver.

 AWS/Azure could be seeing an uptick in cloud services that Datadog has no exposure to, such as AI inference and data management and database. As noted above, we are hearing more data points suggesting that AWS, Azure and Google Cloud may be taking share from the pure-play data software firms, obviously weakening the correlation to Snowflake, MongoDB and Confluent.

 Timing of new bookings/commitments could be a bit different, as could companyspecific product cycles.

Datadog’s CEO, in response to the question about AWS/Azure correlation, has said:

Overall, our success is very tied to cloud. The end-all trend is cloud. Cloud migration is going to keep going for years in the future, and we’re still pretty early into it. That’s going to be a large driver of growth for times to come. That being said, we’re not one to one with the hyperscalers. If you look at it on a q/q basis, there are lot of things that add noise. Some things involving rev rec, pricing changes and other factors make the correlation numbers difficult. Customer commits can also be different. One thing I’d say – we’ve seen very strong performance with Azure this quarter and over the last few quarters. This is the fastest growing part of the business. Azure is accelerating, and we’re growing even faster than that. Whatever trends are underpinning all of this, we’re exposed to the exact same trends.

## Recent Results and Messaging

The key and relatively consistent message from these “cloud proxy” stocks is that usage or consumption growth trends bottomed in 2Q23 or 3Q23 and stabilized or modestly improved over the last two quarters, consistent with the messaging from Amazon and Microsoft. However, the 2Q24 and 2024 guides from these firms do NOT point to any material near-term recovery but rather to stable demand trends or perhaps a modest improvement. In general, the feedback from these smaller cloud firms is consistent with (or perhaps modestly worse than) the growth acceleration signals from AWS and Microsoft Azure, perhaps due to a) the bigger AI lift at the core infrastructure level and b) share gains, especially at the data layer.

Snowflake: In its 1Q/Apr quarter, Snowflake, one of the largest single customers of AWS, posted a higher-than-normal revs beat, with product revs growth accelerating slightly to 33.8% from 32.9% after 9 straight quarters of decelerating growth (mirroring the same recovery we’ve seen in AWS/Azure growth, albeit with a lag). Sequential revs growth was UP y/y for the 3rd straight quarter, similar to the trend we’ve seen from the Big 3 cloud providers. However, the outlook for 2Q/Jul was a bit less encouraging. Assuming a “normal” beat, estimated 2Q/Jul product revs growth would decelerate to \~30% and importantly, sequential revs growth would be BELOW that of the year-ago period, breaking this recent trend. Snowflake didn’t explicitly flag any source of pressure, apart from saying that 1Q/Apr got a boost from a number of large customer ramps.

Datadog: Infra/apps observability firm Datadog posted a modest growth rate acceleration in 1Q24 to 26.9% from 25.6% in 4Q23, with sequential revs growth of \$22m higher than in the year-ago quarter for the 3rd straight quarter but still below the 1Q22 levels. With a “normal” beat, Datadog’s growth rate would accelerate again (we model 27.5% in 2Q24) with sequential revs growth above the year-ago period for the 4th straight quarter. While Datadog’s commentary about a solid March quarter-end was encouraging, Datadog only inched up its full year 2024 revs growth guide slightly to 22.6%, not signaling any material demand recovery.

MongoDB: In its 1Q/Apr quarter, database software firm MongoDB posted a modest deceleration in Atlas cloud-hosted database revs growth to 32% from 34%, with sequential revs growth DOWN y/y after two straight quarters of improving y/y trends. More importantly, MongoDB cut its full year revs growth guide to just 13% and cited broad-based “macro headwinds” that weighed on apps usage growth and therefore the need for incremental Atlas database capacity. This was a major miss by one of the prominent cloud proxy firms.

DigitalOcean: Operating at significantly smaller scale (1Q/Mar ARR of \$749 million), DigitalOcean offers core infrastructure and platform services primarily to small and midsize organizations with 2024 investments targeting AI use cases and GPU capacity. Total revenue growth was just 12% y/y in 1Q/Mar (\~9% organic growth), below the hyperscalers but directionally was a slight acceleration from 4Q/Dec. Net expansion improved slightly as well to 97% (from 96%), largely from slightly less contraction but notably not from an uptick in expansion rates. While the company stands to benefit from an increase in AI revs (with a guide for their recent Paperspace acquisition to add 3pts of revenue growth to an 11% 2024 midpoint), the heavy SMB exposure currently drives uncertainty in the nearer term pace of consumption growth.

## A Shift From “As Needed” Cloud Pricing?

One of the hallmarks of the cloud infrastructure model, at the root of the AWS value proposition, was that customers only need to pay for what they use, with no need to over-provision capacity and under-utilize it (a common on-premise pain point for customers). Back in October 2023, Oracle was the first to signal that it was employing a different revenue-recognition model on large GPU-centric OCI commitments given persistent GPU constraints and the demand-supply imbalance. According to Oracle, the revs rec starts when customers begin utilizing a large (say, 10,000-GPU) OCI cluster, as the commitment begins amortizing in a straight-line fashion from the get-go, regardless of whether the customer is actually utilizing the GPU cluster. We depict the different relative to a “normal” CPU-centric workload, which ramps according to actual usage or consumption, in Figure 4 below.

What this means is that the revs ramp on GPU cluster go-lives is immediate, with a stairstep pattern. As Oracle put it, as large-scale GPU supply comes online, it is like “a boulder landing”. This is a phenomenon unique to large-scale high-performance compute (HPC) cloud environments, in contrast to the steady drip of small 500-GPU clusters coming online and to the pay-as-you-go ramp of non-AI CPU-based commits. Readers will recognize, as we did, that this could serve to “inflate” AI revs reported by the hyperscalers as customers are getting charged for GPU capacity that they may not be fully utilizing. Our understanding is that CoreWeave is employing the same revs rec model.

We bring this up again because we spoke with one of the largest AWS customers in late-May, who called-out some gross margin pressure because AWS was requiring them to buy/commit to blocks of GPU capacity (in the form of a reserved instance) that they didn’t initially need, resulting in low near-term utilization rates while customer demand for GPUs was still ramping for this customer. This is interesting, as it suggests that AWS might also be doing the same. This is obviously not in the interest of customers (who are forced to over-provision) but good for the cloud provider. Note that this anecdote is from one of the largest AWS customers, such that if THEY were required to over-commit, one can imagine that many smaller customers are required to as well. This is a key point to keep in mind, as y/y comps could get tough if/as the GPU constraints ease in the coming years and the hyperscalers presumably back off this contract model.

## Focus on Backlog Integrity as AI Start-Ups Stumble

Amazon, Microsoft and Google haven’t disclosed what portion of their cloud backlogs come from pre-IPO AI start-ups executing model training and inference on their cloud AI infrastructures.  xAI: Just last week, Elon Musk’s xAI start-up announced that it would not be moving forward with a proposed \$10 billion cloud commitment (with Oracle, perhaps among others) and the start-up will instead build its own data center infrastructure for model training.

## UBS Bottom Line

Let’s now step back and absorb all the data points described above, and draw some conclusions as we approach the June quarter reports for Amazon, Microsoft and Google.

## Cloud Growth Poised for Modest Acceleration

As illustrated in Figure 5 below, the combined 1Q24 growth rate of the Big 3 cloud providers accelerated for the 2nd straight quarter to 24%, after growth stabilized at \~19% in 2Q23 and 3Q23. Combined sequential revenue growth of \$2.24b was up significantly (+178%) y/y against a very tough year-ago period and even well above sequential revs growth of \$1.82b in 2Q22, before customers started to ruthlessly eliminate wasteful cloud spend. Needless to say, these improving trends are very encouraging and have been a major driver of Microsoft and Amazon shares. As we look ahead to the 2Q24 prints, the Street is assuming that overall Big 3 revenue growth stabilizes at this 23-24% level for the next several quarters. Below are the key growth bogeys that we’re hearing.

AWS Growth of \~18%: The Street consensus growth rate estimate for AWS growth in 2Q24 is 17% and in our judgement the investor growth bogey is 18%, a one-point acceleration. We’d note that a) 2Q24 represents a 4-point easier compare and b) AWS growth of 18% implies 2Q24 AWS sequential revs growth of \$1.1 billion, well above the 2Q23 (depressed) level of \$786 million but still below 2Q22 sequential growth of \$1.3 billion.

Azure Growth of 32%: Microsoft guided to 4Q/Jun 2024 Azure c/c growth of 30-31%. We believe that the buy-side bogey is for a 1-point beat to 32%. Azure c/c growth of 32% implies sequential Azure revs of \$1.6+ billion, up materially from \$1.2 billion in the year-ago June quarter (before Azure AI revs really kickedin). While not a slam-dunk, in our view 32% growth is doable, and we outline our rationale in the Microsoft Azure section below.

## Investor Growth Estimates Look Reasonable

Bottom line, the Street is assuming stable Big 3 cloud growth rates in 2Q24 following two quarters of acceleration. In our judgement, this appears to be slightly too cautious a view. It’s hardly a party out there on the overall IT spending front, but as noted above, we did NOT pick up any strong signals that cloud spending growth moderated in the April-Jun timeframe as it appears to have for several Software firms. AI activity is earlystage but is clearly gaining momentum and there is anecdotal evidence that a) on-prem to cloud migration activity might be starting to recover and b) enterprises are consolidating IT spend (especially at the data layer) with their primary cloud infrastructure partner. While in each of the last two quarters we’ve landed in the “in-line or skinny beat” camp for the upcoming prints, this time we might be more firmly in the “modest beat” camp, with AWS in particular appearing to have the best upside potential relative to Street revs growth estimates.

## Amazon Web Services

Let’s now discuss the company-specific trends and estimates, starting with the largest of the cloud players, Amazon Web Services (Amazon shares are covered by our Internet research team, led by Stephen Ju).

## Investor Outlook for 18% Growth

AWS posted 1Q24 y/y revs growth of 17% (with a one-point lift from the leap year, so 16% growth normalized) to \$25.0 billion, a material 4-point acceleration following 3 straight quarters of 12-13% growth although still down materially from 40% growth roughly two years ago. Despite the recent acceleration, sequential AWS revenue growth is still not back to the 1H22 levels, just before the period of cloud spend optimization efforts and slower new workload growth began. On its 1Q24 call, Amazon said that a) “companies have largely completed the lion's share of their cost optimization and turned their attention to newer initiatives” (a view supported by our checks and by the smaller cloud proxy firms), b) “before the pandemic, companies were modernizing their infrastructure, moving from on-premises infrastructure to the cloud, the pandemic and uncertain economy that followed distracted from that momentum, but it's picking up again” and c) “we see considerable momentum on the AI front where we've accumulated a multibillion-dollar revenue run rate already”.

## AWS AI Revs Reach “Multi-Billions”

Let’s focus for a moment on Amazon’s “multibillion-dollar AI revenue run rate” comment, the first AI financial disclosure that Amazon has made. That equates to perhaps \$500-700 million of AI revs in 1Q24. Assuming modest AI revs in the year-ago period (we’re unclear whether Amazon is including pre-GenAI products such as Sagemaker in the base), then AI workloads could have contributed say 2 points of y/y AWS growth in 1Q24 (compared to 7 points for Microsoft Azure). If we ex out the 1- point lift from leap year and a 2-point lift from AI revs, then normalized core or ex-AI AWS revenue growth would have been 14%. If AWS AI revs were up by perhaps \$150- 200 million sequentially in 1Q24, then the total “core” or ex-AI AWS sequential revs growth in 1Q24 would be closer to the level reported two years ago in 1Q22 (still strong, but not blow-out). Our view it is highly likely that the AWS AI revenue stream continues to ramp at a solid clip, as a) model providers such as Anthropic ramp training and inference spend on AWS and b) as large AWS customers (most of whom have elected to build their AI apps on AWS despite the investor perception that AWS is lagging) go into production with these initial AI apps, driving inference spend.

## UBS Bottom Line

The Street consensus growth rate estimate for AWS growth in 2Q24 is 17% (UBS's estimate is slightly lower at 16%, as shown in Figure 6 below) and in our judgement the investor growth bogey is 18%, a one-point sequential acceleration. To repeat, a) 2Q24 represents a 4-point easier compare and b) AWS growth of 18% implies 2Q24 AWS sequential revs growth of \$1.1 billion, well above the 2Q23 (depressed) level of \$786 million but still below 2Q22 sequential growth of \$1.3 billion. While not a slam-dunk, in our view the investor AWS growth bogey of 18% looks doable as AI revenues ramp and core cloud spending appears to be at a minimum stable, if not picking up modestly. Looking beyond the 2Q24 quarter, investors are debating whether AWS revs growth can continue to accelerate towards 20% by year-end 2024. As noted in Figure 6 below, our Amazon research team is conservatively modeling a 2024 exit growth rate of 16%.

## Backlog Growth Moderated, but 4Q Was So Material

As we show in Figure 7 below, AWS backlog growth improved materially in 4Q23, both in terms of dollars added (a remarkable \$23 billion, by far the highest ever) and growth (to 41% y/y). ## Margins Are Back to 30%+

As noted in Figure 8 below, AWS posted very strong operating margins of 37.6% in 1Q24, well north of investor expectations and a level that skeptics likely wouldn’t have thought possible 5+ years ago (when a key concern was price/margin compression and infrastructure commoditization). Amazon has highlighted “increased leverage on our headcount costs”, given constraints on AWS headcount growth over the last year as growth decelerated. One key variable going forward will be the extent to which higher AWS capex (in part to address AI-related demand) will flow through to COGS and therefore AWS gross margins. Indeed, on the 1Q24 call, Amazon said that “we expect the combination of AWS's reaccelerating growth and high demand for GenAI to meaningfully increase year-over-year capital expenditures in 2024, we don't spend the capital without very clear signals that we can monetize it”. ## Microsoft Azure

Let’s now turn to a discussion of Microsoft Azure and its growth momentum.

## 4Q/Jun Bogey for 32% c/c Azure Growth

Microsoft guided to c/c Azure revenue growth of 30-31% for 4Q/Jun, stable with 31% reported growth in 3Q/Mar and up materially from 27-28% in each of the previous 3 quarters. In our view, the investor bogey is for a 1-point beat to 32%. To get there, sequential Azure revs growth in 4Q/Jun would have to be \~\$1.6 billion, up materially from \$1.0-\$1.2 billion of sequential revs growth in each of the previous 4 quarters and by far the highest sequential revs growth that Azure has ever posted. Let’s examine if that high bar for Azure is achievable, by examining both the AI and non-AI pieces.

## Expected 8-Point AI Boost to Azure Growth

The 7-point AI lift to Azure growth in 3Q/Mar was up modestly from 6-points in 2Q/Dec, 3-points in 1Q/Sept and 1-point in 4Q/Jun. Microsoft did not even commit to disclosing this metric in 4Q/Jun (we believe that they will, as Microsoft understands that “return on capex” investor concerns can only be quelled by disclosing the demand side of the equation, Azure AI revs) but we’d peg the investor bogey at an 8-point boost, in part because Microsoft hinted that this AI boost would improve in 4Q/Jun. Note that an 8- point boost to Azure growth in 4Q/Jun implies Azure AI revs of \~\$1.7 billion or \$6.8 billion annualized. While Microsoft doesn’t disclose Azure AI revs and hence our numbers could be off, an 8-point growth rate boost implies sequential Azure AI revs of \~\$375 million, up from \$225 million in 3Q/Mar and \$460 million in 2Q/Dec. Given plenty of anecdotes about enterprises beginning to put their initial AI applications into production (obviously driving inference demand for Azure), this estimate seems doable. The Information reporting that OpenAI has hit \$3.4 billion of annualized revs (see link here) makes us feel marginally better with the Azure AI outlook.

As a reminder, Microsoft actually MISSED the Street’s Azure AI revs estimate (for an 8- point growth rate boost) last quarter and while this outcome resulted in some consternation, investors ultimately gave Microsoft a pass. Why the “miss” then and has anything changed? The “AI miss” was surprising given a steady improvement in enterprise AI spend, and because this AI KPI includes GitHub Copilot, which continues to see very strong traction. We wondered whether infrastructure scaling bottlenecks skimmed off some growth and/or whether ChatGPT use slowed. In our mid-May conversation, it felt as if Microsoft was slightly down-playing the capacity bottleneck concerns, saying that they are “a little” constrained, that “it is easing”, that (GPU) order lead times are not an issue and that the CFO comments on the 3Q/Mar call were meant to signal that demand is exceeding supply (a good problem), not that Microsoft has material capacity bottlenecks.

## Outlook for Stable Non-AI Azure Growth

If the investor consensus is indeed for 32% c/c Azure growth with an 8-point lift from AI, then core Azure or non-AI growth would have to be 24%, stable with 3Q/Mar. Let’s unpack this non-AI figure, which has two key segments. In our view the SaaS or nonconsumption portion of Azure is likely to keep decelerating, we estimate to 16% from 17% last quarter. That means that the rest of the non-AI Azure revenue stream – core consumption-based Azure – needs to hold steady at 27% and post 4Q/Jun sequential revs growth of \$1.24 billion, up from \$1.0 billion in the year-ago 4Q/Jun 2023 quarter. Is this achievable? We conclude YES. While our checks pointed to a still-constrained cloud spending backdrop, the checks also flagged an early-stage recovery in cloud migrations activity as well as Azure share gains, especially in data services.

## UBS Bottom Line

Pulling together the above thoughts/analysis, we conclude that 32% c/c Azure revs growth in 4Q/Jun is doable. Looking forward, we must keep in mind that a) we’re still in a tight IT spending backdrop and b) in 1Q/Sept, Microsoft starts lapping a larger Azure AI contribution (which jumped in a 3-point contribution in the year-ago quarter). As such, we conclude that many investors would likely be content for Azure c/c revs growth to continue to track in the low-30% range and we’re now hearing fewer investors suggesting that guidance tracking to mid-30% Azure growth is necessary for the stock to work. We estimate that guidance for 30% Azure growth in 1Q/Sept (with upside, low-30%) might be enough given the backdrop of concerns about the budget environment.

## Interview With CTO Kevin Scott

Last week, Microsoft’s CTO Kevin Scott (widely credited with leading Microsoft’s partnership with and investment in OpenAI) did an interview with Sequoia Capital. In our view the highlights for investors were:

 Supply > Demand: “the inference stack, a large fraction of the overall compute footprint, is constrained, we have more demand than supply at the moment”. This statement echoes what the CFO said on the last earnings call but it is encouraging, and hints at strong Azure AI growth as supply constraints are lifted.

Continued Azure Infra Investments: “despite what other people think, we’re not at diminishing marginal returns on scale up, it just takes a while to build supercomputers and then train models on top of them, the next sample is coming”. This signals a continued investment in training infrastructure for OpenAI models, although the twist here is that Microsoft doesn’t report OpenAI training revs/infra capex in its financials (capex could be even more than what is reported).

Cost Efficiencies: “I think Nvidia is doing a good job, in terms of the efficiency of the scale up, each hardware generation is better price-performance wise, there’s just an embarrassing amount of parallelism, we’re getting better and better at extracting that architecturally in the hardware, there’s a bunch of innovation happening on the network side as well, you just have very, very powerful incentives to go optimize the software stack to squeeze more performance out of it”. This is expected but encouraging, implying the AI cost curve can slope down.

Copilot Success: “it is easier to think about how to go from rough frontier model capability to useful tool when you’re narrowing it down to a domain, that’s been a reasonable deployment path, we’ve got a handful of our Copilots that have real market traction right now, GitHub Copilot we’ve talked most about, it’s been a hit. The more general the Copilot is, the harder it is to have it actually take very high precision action”. This statement about Copilots implies that domain-specific AI products such as GitHub Copilot have stronger traction that general-purpose AI products, perhaps signaling that Office Copilot adoption has not inflected yet.

Microsoft’s total current commercial backlog or cRPO was flattish for three consecutive quarters at \$95-96 billion, then grew by a record \$9 billion in 3Q/Mar. Microsoft also discloses commercial bookings growth, which we show in Figure 11. In 3Q/Mar, bookings growth materially accelerated to 31%. As noted in the AWS discussion above, it is tough to read too much into backlog/bookings growth as an input into near-term Azure growth, but it’s hard not to interpret these metrics as being bullish medium-term Azure growth signals.

## Capex Spend to Remain Elevated

From a base of all-in (PP&E plus capital leases) capex of \$14.0 billion in 3Q/Mar, Microsoft again guided to a “material” sequential increase in total capex in 4Q/Jun and to “higher” capex (which almost all investors are assuming anyway) in FY25. We’re modeling 4Q/Jun all-in capex \$16.5 billion and to reach \$68 billion for the full year FY25 (in our view, the consensus investor estimate is higher, at \$70-75 billion). We doubt that Microsoft specifically guides to full year capex, such that investors will likely annualize the 4Q/Jun capex and assume some sequential growth. At what level would FY25 appear “too high”? In our view, if \$80+ billion looked likely, without a positive Office/ M365 Copilot adoption disclosure, this could become a problem for the stock, igniting too many “return on capex” concerns.
