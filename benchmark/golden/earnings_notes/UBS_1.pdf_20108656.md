## 10 (+1) Key Macro themes for H2'24

## 1- What should we expect from the ECB if the FED does not cut? (Figure 1-2)

After a strong labour market report in the US (NFP 272k vs. 180k expected), the market is now pricing only 35bp of cut for the FED in 2024 (from \~160bp in January). Clients have been asking a lot about a scenario where the FED does not cut this year, and the impact it would have on the ECB. In our view, the ECB can still cut up to 3 times in 2024: First, the drivers of inflation are very different - with services inflation/OER for Europe/US, respectively. Second, the impact on the EUR would not be an immediate concern for the ECB as the pass-through has declined over time and a lot of goods are actually priced in EUR rather than foreign currency. Lastly, some GC members made an argument that if the FED stay restrictive, that also implies more restrictive financial conditions in Europe, which should actually support a move in the opposite direction. Overall, we think there are more downside risks for cuts in 2025 (current baseline 4 cuts) than 2024.

## 2- Which idiosyncratic risk could prevent the ECB to cut further? (Figure 3-4)

As expected, the ECB kicked off the easing cycle last week. However, they revised up their inflation projections for both 2024 and 2025 by 0.2% (to 2.5/2.2%, respectively), and attributed these upward revisions to higher-than-expected inflation outcomes in recent months, especially services inflation, and higher inflationary pressures from energy and labour costs. Against that backdrop, clients are concerned by wages (4.3% for Q1) and the impact on services inflation (4.1% in May). Having a look at the breakdown, inflation across wage sensitive services is currently above non-wage sensitive services - although the weight of wage-sensitive services are too small to have an impact overall.

## 3- Contagion from US hot data/'no landing’ to EU rates/credit (Figure 5-6)

Probably one of the most asked questions in Europe. Historically, from a credit perspective, EU HY spreads tend to do better than their US counterparts in surprise US monetary shocks (although valuation cheapness probably played an important role). As EU spreads (especially IG) are driven mostly by rates/inflation, we think that potential compression remains limited tactically, as long as the data is hot in the US. On the flip side, all else being equals, we think that the contagion would fade if the FED provides guidance on/delivers its first cut.

## 4- Can the FED deliver more than priced in and in which scenario? (Figure 7-8)

Outside of our baseline for a soft-ish landing (\~55-60% probability), we think there are only 2 scenarios where the FED could deliver more: a recession (\~10%) or what clients have characterized as immaculate disinflation (\~10%) - the balance would would be a 'no landing' scenario. While we see signs of credit stress emerging in the US economy, especially in LL/privates as well as office CRE and credit cards, trends and levels are more in line with a softening not a recession. We would need to see much further deterioration in US labour market/growth for the FED to react. With 5 meeting left, acknowledging June is a hold and November is out of the table, only a recession could really trigger more severe cuts in magnitude.

## 5- Reverse Yankees issuance - risk or opportunity? (Figure 9-10)

In May, clients have been asking a lot about Reverse Yankees as we have seen issuance skyrocket despite earnings season (\~56bn YTD). US banks only represent \~17% as they don't need EUR funding despite its attractiveness relative to USD (most of the business they do with EU companies is in USD). As the market has been flooded with issuance and given client's focus on the several daily primary deals, this has created RV opportunities for both EU and US investors, especially within Financials and Consumer

## 6- Investors are not ready to implement decompression trades (Figure 11-12)

Given the significant spread tightening we had YTD (EU IG/HY -29/-51bp at 110/328bp), cash decompression trades have started to look attractive. Having said that, only few clients have implemented these trades (which partly explains higher beta parts of the EU credit market have remain resilient). As we do not see any imminent catalyst for a widening in our baseline, we think switching from BB to BBBs/Hybrids to Senior is too early given the high level of carry. Historically, spreads have traded in a tight range for much longer (sub-90bp in the US from 93' to 97' and 04' to $0 7 ^ { \cdot } - \mathrm { i n }$ the first quartile in Europe in 07' [12 months], 14' [10 months] and 18' [16 months]). Said differently, valuations are not a strong enough catalyst per se to trigger a widening in our view.

## 7- Rangebound environment or room for compression? It depends on the quality (Figure 13-16)

We see IG/HY spreads close to \~110/325bp as fair valued/slightly expensive, with a little bit of room for compression in the short term for high grade. According to our models, IG spreads sensitivity to growth and financial conditions remains elevated which means that the pace and extent of future CBs cuts as well as growth recovery will be crucial for credit risk premium in Europe. HY credit risk premium sensitivity to lending remains elevated but has declined significantly vs. 3m ago as easier financial conditions seems to be almost fully priced in spreads and risk has shifted to the upside now. As we see both US Core PCE and EU Core CPI declining to the [2.25-2.5%] area by year-end, pushing the FED to start easing monetary policy this year, we maintain our spread targets at \~105/325bp for IG/HY in December.

## 8- US vs. EU credit - investors seem to all agree (Figure 17-18)

Clients seem to all agree on that front - away from the pure valuation story, EU credit should see better days than the US credit given the divergence in macro data and stronger technicals in Europe. Financials remain our top pick for both regions, although we like it for potential compression in Europe vs. US (on top of carry). In HY, we do not see much opportunities away from idiosyncratic distress stories and names/sectors still trading wider than their 2021 levels, especially as EU HY spreads ex-distress are now trading at 236bp (#2 tightest level since GFC, with 220bp in 2017).

## 9- Technicals: are flows still supportive for credit? (Figure 19-21)

We have seen arbitrages continue in the ETF space between rates and IG credit which has pushed IG ETFs flows to stabilize at a lower level. IG MFs flows increased which has probably helped the huge amount of primary deals to be absorbed in May. In HY, spreads remain heavily supported by both ETFs and MFs flows given 1) all-in yield attractiveness (carry); 2) low default expectations and despite net issuance picking up. Having said that, client discussions suggest that the demand for duration has picked up drastically and money has started to rotate from short to longer dated funds.

## 10- Away from a shock, what could push spreads wider? (Figure 22-23)

From \~50 client meetings, we only got a few contrarian/less bullish views on credit. The tone was more mixed on duration though. Historically, US spreads get wider on the back of unexpected monetary tightening, systemic shocks (CRE, private credit, consumer), and growth slowdown (severe). As of today, clients tend to worry more about potential US debt sustainability/fiscal and elections as well as small defaults/issues in isolation and market complacency around recession risks. In Europe, a fast easing cycle triggering a steepening of the curve could push fast money - a much larger part of the investor base today vs. previous cycles - to leave the asset class. For context, IG/HY ETFs size went up \~30/35% in \~2yrs (IG ETFs peaked at +50% in Feb'24).

## 10+1- European Elections - what could be the impact for credit? (Figure 24-25)

As President Macron's call for snap elections following the large far-right wing victory at the elections, clients are increasingly worried about the impact on risky assets. Our view is that spread widening should remain broadly limited, probably similar to 2022 (first round) - on a relative value basis, banks and retail should widen the most. Having said that, if the FED and/or inflation disappoints and France gets a far-right PM, a sustained and significant sell-off is more likely, especially as volumes tend to be lower during summer.
