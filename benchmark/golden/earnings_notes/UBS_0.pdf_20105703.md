We polled our analysts across Asia to identify stocks where they expect a positive or negative surprise vs consensus in the Dec-23 results. ## Sell-side ratings: Where is the street turning bullish and bearish quickly?

Compared to EPS upgrades and downgrades, sell-side ratings changes on stocks are less frequent and potentially indicative of material change in the analyst's thought process on a stock, and bigger than incremental EPS changes. They also encapsulate not only fundamental earnings information, but also the valuations context. Our framework (first introduced here) aggregates bottom-up sell-side analyst views on stocks into views on markets/sectors. Tracking this over time gives a good overview of big changes in sell-side mood across the broader market (Figure 10). Over the past 3 months, we can see that the sell-side has turned most bullish on India, Thailand, and Malaysia, and slightly bearish on China and Philippines (Figure 9). It has turned bullish on Energy, Financials, and Software services, while downgrading Utilities and Materials. This framework also highlights opportunities heading into 3Q results: stocks/sectors with a sharp rise in sellside ratings but at risk of missing earnings next quarter (and vice versa). We highlight BYD (1211 HK) as stock where the sell side has been turning negative, but for which UBS analysts expect positive surprises in results. Similarly, while sell side has been turning positive on GlobalWafers (6488 TT) recently, UBS analysts expect negative surprise in results.

## Buy-side crowding positioning

We use the UBS Quant team's proprietary factor score to identify the most crowded markets/sectors in Asia. In 2022, our UBS Quant team launched the crowding factor to identify crowded positioning in stocks. Simply speaking, crowding score indicates how many market participants are holding similar positions in stocks. Crowding can occur on both long and short positions.

The crowding factor framework leverages a combination of internal/external data including prime brokerage data, stock loan data, 13F filings, and other proprietary UBS inputs. This is a much more effective approach than attempting to define crowding through incomplete data sets or secondary approaches such as price movements, factor spreads, or various correlations.

Crowding as Factor: Back testing results show that crowding factor generates 4.0%/- 5.9% on Long/Short sides of the portfolio. This, especially when juxtaposed with analyst earnings insights, took the portfolio performance to 5.9%/-10.8% on Long/Short sides (See Q-Series report).

Crowding as Risk: Alternatively crowding could also be seen as a risk-control metric. Crowded positions and strategies can outperform for significant stretches of time if the level of overlap keeps increasing and flows continue in the space. They can also drift along up or down without catalysts or events that would trigger flows in any direction. However, if there is a time when many market participants try to exit at the same time, things can turn ugly quickly. During market dislocations or periods of heavy de-risking (either reducing leverage or exiting the position entirely), many people are trading in the same direction with sometimes limited liquidity. This can result in large price impacts that are directly attributable to crowding. Therefore Crowding awareness in certain names, sectors, markets may help mitigate poor performance during periods of market stress.

## Sell-side ratings Net Score framework

We introduced a sell side ratings Net Score framework (Net Score), which is a bottom-up way to aggregate sell-side analysts on individual stocks into a sell-side view on markets/ sectors. Unlike incremental EPS changes, analyst ratings (Buy/Hold/Sell) change less frequently, and carry information about significant changes in the sell-side view on a stock (that captures not just the earnings change information, but also valuation information at the time of the change). We define the Net Score for a given stock as: (number of Buy ratings - number of Sell ratings) \* 100 / total no of ratings.

As an illustration: 1) a Net Score of 100 implies all sell-side analysts have a Buy rating on the stock, while 2) a score of 0 implies Buys and Sells are equal in number. The more bullish sell-side analysts are collectively on a stock, the higher the Net Score and vice versa. To ensure quality of data, we restrict our analysis to those stocks that have at least 10 analyst ratings.

Once the stock-level Net Scores are available, it is a simple extension to indices. We calculate sector/market-level net scores by aggregating Net Scores of individual stocks using free-float market cap weights.

We notice that sell-side ratings aren't equally distributed between Buys and Sells (the 10- year average Net Score for EM Asia is 67, as against an unbiased score of 50). We compare the current Net Score value with its five-year average to control for any systematic sell-side bullish bias. We also introduce Net Score momentum, defined as the 3 month change in Net Score value.
