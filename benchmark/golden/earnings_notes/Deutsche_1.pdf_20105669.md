## December, Q4 and 2023 Performance Review

2023 was an incredibly eventful year in markets, with plenty of forces for investors to navigate. In March, there was significant turmoil following the collapse of Silicon Valley Bank, leading to some of the biggest bond market moves in decades. In May, we saw growing excitement about the potential growth impact from AI, leading to a major outperformance from big tech stocks. Then from the summer, the prospect of interest rates remaining higher for longer led to a major bond selloff, which briefly pushed the 10yr Treasury yield above 5% for the first time since the GFC. Geopolitical events remained in focus too, particularly after Hamas’ attack on Israel in October. But from late-October onwards there was an astonishing rally across several asset classes, as declining inflation led investors to grow increasingly excited about a soft landing. That then got further momentum in December, particularly after the Federal Reserve signalled 75bps of rate cuts for 2024.

So despite several ups and downs along the way, most assets put in a strong overall performance in 2023. For instance, the S&P 500 posted a +26.3% gain in total return terms, supported by a +107% gain from the Magnificent 7. Bonds were also back in positive territory after their losses over the previous two years, thanks to the late recovery over the final two months of the year. And finally, with central banks staying hawkish for much of the year, risk-free rates in 2023 were the highest they’d been in a long time. For instance, US T-bills returned +5.1%, marking their strongest performance since 2000.

## Year in Review – The high-level macro overview

As January began, concerns about a recession were prominent after the losses of 2022. In fact, in the first week of 2023, the ISM services index for December came in at 49.2, marking the first time since May 2020 that both the ISM services and manufacturing prints had been below the 50-mark. But after that brief wobble, the growth picture began to look a lot more promising. For instance, European natural gas prices came down by -24.8% in January, and then another -18.6% in February, which marked a big relief after the high prices seen in late-2022. Meanwhile in China, easing Covid restrictions led to growing optimism about their economic performance. And in the US, the initial reading for the January jobs report saw nonfarm payrolls up by a very large +517k, with unemployment falling to a 53-year low of 3.4%.

But with growth coming in stronger than expected, February once again saw growing fears about more persistent inflation. That was particularly so after US CPI data from late-2022 was revised upwards, with the 3m annualised rate of core CPI for December moving up from 3.14% to 4.25% as a result. was growing talk about a ‘no landing’ scenario. Unlike the hard or soft landings, this scenario had inflation staying high whilst growth remained strong, meaning that the Fed would need to hike rates even more to bear down on inflation. At the start of March, that saw Fed Chair Powell say in his semi-annual congressional testimony that “we would be prepared to increase the pace of rate hikes”, which explicitly opened the door to larger 50bp hikes again. Shortly afterwards on March 8, 2yr Treasury yields closed at a post-2007 high of 5.07%, and expectations of the Fed’s terminal rate stood at a new high for the cycle of 5.69%.

However, that all changed swiftly with the collapse of Silicon Valley Bank, which raised fears about broader contagion across the financial system. For instance on March 13, yields on 2yr Treasuries saw their largest daily decline since 1982, surpassing the daily moves during the GFC, after 9/11, and on Black Monday in 1987. Meanwhile in Europe, Credit Suisse then came under investor scrutiny and saw large deposit outflows, which culminated in a purchase by UBS that included guarantees from the Swiss government. That led to major bond moves in Europe too, and on March 15 as concern grew about Credit Suisse, the 2yr German yield saw its largest daily decline in data going back to reunification in 1990. US banks saw a massive underperformance as a result, and the KBW Bank index was down by -24.9% in March in total return terms. And more broadly, the MOVE index of Treasury volatility rose to levels last seen at the height of the GFC in 2008. This concern lingered for some weeks, and on May 1, First Republic Bank was closed and JPMorgan acquired most of its assets. But by June, markets had calmed for the most part, and the VIX index of volatility was at a post-pandemic low.

Otherwise in Q2, a big story was growing excitement about the potential for AI, particularly after Nvidia raised their outlook far above expectations thanks to demand for AI processers. This meant that the Magnificent 7 were one of the top equity performers in 2023, with a total return of +107% over the year as a whole. Nvidia itself saw the strongest performance in the entire S&P 500, with a gain of +239% over the year, whilst Meta also saw a very strong +194% advance as well. That group of stocks helped the S&P 500 record a +26.3% gain over 2023, although it’s worth noting that the advance was driven by a relatively narrow group of stocks. Indeed, the equal-weighted S&P 500 was only up +13.9% over the year, which is the biggest underperformance for the equal-weighted version compared to the S&P 500 since 1998 during the dot com bubble.

Alongside this, from April there was another round of concerns about the US debt ceiling as the latest deadline approached. There was a clear market reaction in response, including a noticeable widening in CDS spreads for the United States. Moreover, yields on T-bills that matured around the X-date in early June briefly traded with a 7 handle, showing how investors were demanding extra compensation to hold those bills at risk of default. But a deal was eventually reached on May 27, and was signed into law by President Biden on June 3.

The continuation of positive economic news meant that central banks retained their focus on persistent inflation, contrary to speculation about a pause after the regional banking turmoil. For instance, the Fed hiked again in May, and in June their dot plot still signalled two more rate hikes for 2023. That came as the data remained very positive, and at the time they showed nonfarm payrolls had grown by +294k in April and another +339k in May. Then in July, the Fed hiked once again, taking rates up to a 22-year high.

That prospect of rates remaining higher for longer led to a major bond selloff in Q3. Indeed, the Fed’s September dot plot saw the 2024 dot move up to its highest yet, at 5.1%, suggesting there was little rush to pivot towards rate cuts. Alongside that, inflationary pressures were still present, and oil prices were up by over \$20/bbl over Q3 as a whole. And as that was going on, investors became increasingly focused on budget deficits, not least after Fitch Ratings downgraded the US credit rating from AAA to AA+.

By late-October, that backdrop meant the US 10yr yield surpassed 5% intraday for the first time since 2007. Equities also continued to struggle, and by October, the S&P 500 had seen 3 consecutive monthly declines for the first time since the pandemic turmoil of March 2020. The landscape looked pretty tough, and the data had weakened considerably in Europe as well. For instance, the composite PMIs for the Euro Area were consistently in contractionary territory by this point, and on the last day of October, we also found out that the Euro Area economy had seen a -0.1% contraction in Q3.

Geopolitical issues were also back in focus around this time, which followed Hamas’ attack on Israel in October. From a market perspective, the initial question was whether this might lead to a broader escalation, and Brent Crude oil prices were up by +7.5% in the week immediately following the attack. Investors moved into haven assets in response, and October also saw a +7.3% rise in gold prices. Meanwhile in Israel, the TA-35 index was down -10.7% over October, marking its worst monthly performance since March 2020.

But around late-October, the market narrative swiftly began to turn. In part, that was thanks to several downside surprises for inflation, which led to growing hopes that it would finally be back around target levels. Central banks themselves also began to sound more dovish, and the Fed’s dot plot in December took investors by surprise after it signalled 75bps of rate cuts in 2024. For markets, this led to significant optimism that a soft landing was coming into view, whereby inflation would be back at target levels without a recession taking place, which in turn would allow central banks to cut interest rates.

All that led to a major cross-asset rally, and November marked the best month for Bloomberg’s global bond aggregate since December 2008 at the height of the financial crisis, with a +5.0% return. That was then followed up in December by a +4.2% gain, and it meant that the index ended the year up +5.7% in total return terms. That was a big turnaround from the situation by the end of October, when it had looked like bonds were set for a third consecutive annual decline. Meanwhile for equities, there was also a very strong year-end rally, and the S&P 500 saw its fastest advance since it rebounded following the initial Covid selloff. That included a +9.1% gain in November in total return terms, which was then followed up by a +4.5% gain in December, which left the index up +26.3% for the year.

## Which assets saw the biggest gains of 2023?

Equities: After significant losses in 2022, equities bounced back again in 2023, with gains for the S&P 500 (+26.3%), the STOXX 600 (+16.6%) and the MSCI World index (+24.4%). Tech stocks were the major outperformer on a sectoral basis, with the NASDAQ (+44.7%) seeing even larger gains, whilst the Magnificent 7 (+107%) saw one of the top performances of any financial asset in 2023.

Credit: 2023 was also a more positive year for credit, with HY outperforming IG. For instance, US HY was up +12.9% on a total return basis, whilst US IG non-fin was up +8.8%.

Sovereign Bonds: Thanks to the year-end recovery in November and December, sovereign bonds put in a decent performance over 2023, beginning to recover from their losses over 2021 and 2022. For instance, US Treasuries ended the year up +4.1%, whilst Euro sovereign bonds were up +7.2%.

Gold: Although most commodities struggled in 2023, gold was an exception with a +13.1% gain. The best quarterly performance for gold came in Q4, with an +11.6% advance over the quarter as investors priced in a growing likelihood of central bank rate cuts in 2024.

## Which assets saw the biggest losses of 2023?

Energy Commodities: Oil prices saw their first annual decline since the pandemic year of 2020, with both Brent crude (-10.3%) and WTI (-10.7%) posting declines. In both the US and Europe, it also marked the first annual decline in natural gas prices since 2019, with European natural gas futures ending the year down -57.6%, whilst US natural gas futures fell -43.8%.

Japanese Yen: Among the G10 currencies, the Japanese Yen was the weakest performer, with a -7.0% decline against the US Dollar in 2023. That came as the Bank of Japan remained one of the only central banks to maintain a dovish stance, continuing with its negative interest rate policy, even as others such as the Fed and ECB kept hiking rates.

Macroeconomic fluctuations often account for most of the risks associated with exposures to instruments that promise to pay fixed or variable interest rates. For an investor who is long fixed-rate instruments (thus receiving these cash flows), increases in interest rates naturally lift the discount factors applied to the expected cash flows and thus cause a loss. The longer the maturity of a certain cash flow and the higher the move in the discount factor, the higher will be the loss. Upside surprises in inflation, fiscal funding needs, and FX depreciation rates are among the most common adverse macroeconomic shocks to receivers. But counterparty exposure, issuer creditworthiness, client segmentation, regulation (including changes in assets holding limits for different types of investors), changes in tax policies, currency convertibility (which may constrain currency conversion, repatriation of profits and/or liquidation of positions), and settlement issues related to local clearing houses are also important risk factors. The sensitivity of fixed-income instruments to macroeconomic shocks may be mitigated by indexing the contracted cash flows to inflation, to FX depreciation, or to specified interest rates - these are common in emerging markets. The index fixings may - by construction - lag or mis-measure the actual move in the underlying variables they are intended to track. The choice of the proper fixing (or metric) is particularly important in swaps markets, where floating coupon rates (i.e., coupons indexed to a typically short-dated interest rate reference index) are exchanged for fixed coupons. Funding in a currency that differs from the currency in which coupons are denominated carries FX risk.
